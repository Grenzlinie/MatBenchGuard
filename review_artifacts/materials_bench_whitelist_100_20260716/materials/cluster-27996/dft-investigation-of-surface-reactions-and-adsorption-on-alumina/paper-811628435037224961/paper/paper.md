# Interactions Between Free Radicals and a Graphene Fragment: Physical Versus Chemical Bonding, Charge Transfer, and Deformation

Fengting T. Wang,${}^{[a]}$ Lei Chen,${}^{[b]}$ Chuanjin J. Tian,${}^{[a]}$ Yan Meng,${}^{[a]}$ Zhigang G. Wang,$^{*[a]}$ Ruiqin Q. Zhang,$^{*[a,c]}$ Mingxing X. Jin,${}^{[a]}$ Ping Zhang,${}^{[a]}$ and Dajun J. Ding${}^{[a]}$

The adsorption of six free radicals (FRs) respectively on a graphene fragment was studied using a density functional tight-binding method with the inclusion of an empirical dispersion term in total energy. The results indicate that the different interaction paths between the FRs and the graphene lead to different forms of physical (PA) or chemical adsorptions (CA). The CA appears only in the condition where some of the nonhydrogen atoms are closer to the graphene, with the deformation occurring in the latter. The charge transfer increases with the increase in adsorption energy in every FR-graphene system. Although the deformation in the graphene is negligible in all PA cases, the FR is closer to the graphene and the graphene deformation is clearer in all CA cases, with all atomic displacements being larger than 0.1 Å. Our findings are useful not only for FR scavenging but also for studying the interaction between general molecules and material surfaces. © 2011 Wiley Periodicals, Inc. J Comput Chem 32: 3264–3268, 2011

Keywords: graphene fragment · free radicals · physical adsorption · chemical adsorption · density functional tight binding method

## Introduction

In recent years, intense investigations have been performed on free radical (FR) scavenging activity on nanomaterials, including graphene and carbon nanotubes, impacting both science and technology areas.${}^{[1-5]}$ Graphene,${}^{[6]}$ which was obtained by Geim and Novoselov in 2004,${}^{[7,8]}$ is the thinnest graphite layer, with a one-atom-thick hexagonal mesh structure. It can act as a substrate of adsorbing FRs,${}^{[9,10]}$ DNA,${}^{[11-15]}$ protein,${}^{[16]}$ water${}^{[17]}$ and so on.

As is well known, FRs play an important role in many human physiology processes, and excessive FRs are highly damaging to human health.${}^{[18-20]}$ FRs may be formed by single electron oxidation or the reduction of an atom or molecule. They can be atoms, molecules, or ions containing unpaired electrons on an open shell configuration, and they may carry a positive, negative, or zero charge. Unpaired electrons provide radicals with high activity,${}^{[21,22]}$ causing complex interactions between FRs and material surfaces. Both the type and the orientation of the FR on a material surface determine the conformational change, charge transfer, and other aspects in the system. In fact, the interaction between a molecule and a material surface is closely related to molecular orientation, which may determine if the interaction is a physisorption or chemisorption process.${}^{[23]}$

To study the interaction between FRs and material surfaces, we must adopt a calculation method that can describe not only the intramolecular chemical bonding effect but also weak intermolecular interactions.${}^{[24]}$ It has been proved that conventional density functional theory (DFT) is not qualified to handle the latter. However, with an additional empirical $R^{-6}$ term added in the total energy, the DFT method has been used to study the structure and the interaction energies between small molecules and graphene.${}^{[25,26]}$ Similarly, with a similar empirical dispersion term in total energy, a recently developed density functional tight-binding (DFTB-D) method${}^{[27]}$ has been effectively applied to study material surfaces adsorbed with different molecules through weak interactions. This method is able to describe accurately the geometrical and electronic structures of both isolated molecules and weak interaction molecular systems.

Here, we study the systems of six-representative FRs adsorbed respectively on a graphene segment surface based on DFTB-D calculations and explore the possible physisorption and chemisorption structures in these systems. We find that both physical (PA) and chemical (CA) adsorptions are possible, and that both involve some trends in adsorption energy, charge transfer, and conformational change. Our findings are useful not only for FR scavenging but also for studying the interaction between general molecules and material surfaces.

## Computational Details

We investigated the interactions between six FRs (CH, $CH_3$, $C_2H$, $C_2H_3$, $C_2H_5$, and OH) and a graphene segment with the DFTB-D method using the DFTB+ code.$^{[28,29]}$ We also analyzed the molecular orbitals and charge transfers. DFTB is a straight-forward extension and successful implementation of standard tight-binding (TB) theory to operate in a self-consistent charge mode based on a second-order expansion of the Kohn-Sham total energy functional as calculated within DFT.$^{[30,31]}$ The second-order terms are approximated by a simple distribution of atom-centered point charges estimated by Mulliken population analysis. To describe the van der Waals interaction between FRs and the graphene, we adopted the DFTB-D method, which uses an empirical dispersion term added into the DFTB total energy.$^{[32,33]}$ This method has been used successfully to study the interaction between organic molecules and materials,$^{[34,35]}$ the adsorption of water clusters on a graphene surface$^{[36]}$ and so forth.

First, we built a fragment model of the graphene containing 54 carbon atoms with 18 edge saturating hydrogen atoms as the substrate in the interaction system, which is called $C_{54}H_{18}$. For ease in analyzing the data, we initially placed the graphene segment strictly in the x-y plane as shown in Figure 1. We placed the FRs above the central area of the graphene segment surface, with the different orientations of each FR on the graphene segment being well considered in this system. We performed geometric optimizations for 144 carefully designed initial FR-graphene conformations, to search for the possible local minimum geometries and obtained a number of stable structures with the DFTB-D method. Subsequently, the identified favorable structures with different bonding characters or different orientations between FR and graphene were selected to study their interaction characters.

These structures are shown in Figure 2. The results show that both PA and CA could exist in these systems. The PA and CA are further distinguished by their significant differences in adsorption energy, with the PA adsorption energy being below ~10 kcal/mol and the CA adsorption energy being above 20 kcal/mol as shown in Table 1.

Figure 2 and Table 1 show that all the CH, CH₃, C₂H₅, and OH form two PA structures and one CA structure on the graphene segment, whereas the C₂H forms only one PA structure and the C₂H₃ one PA and one CA structure. In each FR-graphene system, we selected the isomer with the maximum binding energy as the representative for analyzing the differences among these systems including the charge transfer and the deformation. In Table 1, the absolute value of the CA binding energy between the FR and the graphene follows a trend of CH(IV) > C₂H > OH(III) > C₂H₃(II) > CH₃(III) > C₂H₅(III) and the PA a trend of OH(II) > C₂H₃(I) > C₂H₅(I) > CH₃(II) > CH(II). Obviously, the PA is weaker than the CA. In Figure 2, the CA appears only in the condition that the nonhydrogen atom is close to the graphene; this is because the unpaired electrons of the FRs always locate near the nonhydrogen atom.

Table 1 also gives the charge transfers for the PA and CA cases. It shows that the order of the PA charge transfer is OH(II) > CH(II) > C₂H₃(I) > CH₃(II) > C₂H₅(II), whereas the order of the CA charge transfer is CH(III) > OH(III) > C₂H > C₂H₃(II) > CH₃(III) > C₂H₅(III). For the CA, we clearly see that the charge transfer between the FR and the graphene is associated with the number ratio of hydrogen/nonhydrogen atoms. The larger the ratio, the weaker is the electron attraction capability of the FR. So, both CH₃(III) and C₂H₅(III) give their electrons to the graphene, respectively. It is obvious that the charge transfer of the CA is greater than that of the PA in most systems. But C₂H₃ is an abnormal case, showing an opposite trend. When compared with the PA, the charge transfer of the CA is one order of magnitude smaller for C₂H₃. In Figures 2 and 3, the hydrogen atoms in different positions of C₂H₃ are all close to the graphene. This increases the contact between the positive areas (green; hydrogen atoms of C₂H₃) and the negative areas (red; carbon atoms of graphene) in the differential charge isosurface (Fig. 3), and the charge transfer in this PA system becomes clearer than that in the other PA systems.

Analyzing the data of the adsorption energies and charge transfers, we found that the charge transfer increases with the increase in adsorption energy in every system, whether PA or CA. For most FRs (except $C_{2}H_{5}$), the adsorption energy with a nonhydrogen atom close to the graphene is larger than that with a hydrogen atom close to the graphene in the PA. To understand the result of this particular $C_{2}H_{5}$, we depicted the unpaired electronic highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) for both cases in Figure 4 with an isosurface plot, showing their respective bonding characteristics. These HOMOs indicate that partial wavefunction overlap between the $C_{2}H_{5}$ and the graphene may decrease the system energy (i.e., increase the adsorption energy) in $C_{2}H_{5}$(II), whereas there is no overlap in other FR-graphene systems, such as the $CH_{3}$-graphene system.

Next, we describe the deformation in the studied systems. In Figure 1, the initial z-coordinate of the graphene is set to zero, and the adsorptions of the FRs cause the graphene deformation mainly along the z direction. So, the graphene atomic displacement in the z-direction simply measures the deformation of the graphene. Accordingly, the deformation trends of the graphene can be examined for the PA and CA cases. To better understand the interaction between graphene and FRs, we further studied the change in distance between them. For both the PA and CA, we considered the distance from the nonhydrogen atom (C or O atoms) in the FR nearest the graphene (see Fig. 1, for on-top, bridge, and hollow sites) to the graphene itself to be the distance between the FR and the graphene.

As a test, we fixed the geometry structure of the graphene and found that the CA never appears in the FR-graphene system. This result contradicts the fact that the graphene can chemically adsorb the FR. This test shows that the deformation of graphene takes place essentially in the CA process between the FR and the graphene.

Our results show that the order of magnitude of graphene deformation in all PA cases is not larger than $10^{-2}$ Å. In contrast, in the CA cases shown in Table 1, the displacements are all larger than 0.1 Å. The displacement in the CA case is $CH(II) > C_{2}H > C_{2}H_{5}(III) > C_{2}H_{3}(II) > CH_{3}(III) > OH(III)$. The interaction between different FRs and the graphene makes a clear difference not only in graphene deformation but also in the distance between the FRs and the graphene. The displacements follow the order of $C_{2}H_{3}(I) > CH_{3}(I) > C_{2}H_{5}(I) > OH(I) > CH(I)$ in the PA case, and $C_{2}H_{5}(III) > CH_{3}(III) > C_{2}H_{3}(II) > OH(III) > C_{2}H > CH(III)$ in the CA case. Overall, the distance between the FR and the graphene in the PA case is larger than that in the CA case, whereas the graphene deformation in the PA case is smaller than that in the CA case. Moreover, for all CA cases, when the distance between the FRs and the graphene is about 1.5 Å, the graphene atomic displacement reaches 0.8 Å; when the distance between the two is about 1.2 Å, all graphene atomic displacement is about 1.0 Å; for the CH(III) state, the graphene atomic displacement reaches 1.84 Å. Thus, the closer the FR is to the graphene, the clearer the graphene deformation.

## Summary

In summary, we studied the physical and chemical interactions between six FRs and a graphene segment, respectively, by theoretical calculation at the DFTB-D level of theory. The results show that both the CA and PA states can exist if the FR approaches the graphene surface from some directions, although the interaction energy of the PA is smaller than that of the CA. In most systems, the charge transfer of the CA is more than that of the PA and is associated with the number ratio of hydrogen to nonhydrogen atoms for the CA; also, the atomic displacement in the CA cases is larger than $0.1$ Å, and clear deformation is a necessary condition for all CA cases to occur. The analysis of the molecular orbitals and charge differences was presented to achieve better understanding of the various interactions. We hope that the results will be helpful for studying complex interactions between FRs and materials.
