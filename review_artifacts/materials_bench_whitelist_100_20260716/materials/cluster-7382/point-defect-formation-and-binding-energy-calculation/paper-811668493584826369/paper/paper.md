## ABSTRACT

First principles calculations based on gradient corrected density functional theory are carried out to understand the electronic structure and mechanisms responsible for desorption of hydrogen from Ti doped and vacancy containing sodium-alanate (NaAlH₄). The energy necessary to remove a hydrogen atom from Ti doped NaAlH₄ is significantly smaller than that from pristine NaAlH₄ irrespective of whether Ti substitutes the Na or the Al site. However, the presence of Na and Al vacancies is shown to play an even more important role: The removal of hydrogen associated with both Na and Al vacancies is found to be exothermic. It is suggested that this role of vacancies can be exploited in the design and synthesis of complex light metal hydrides suitable for hydrogen storage.

## INTRODUCTION

The success of a new hydrogen economy¹⁻⁵ relies on our ability in finding storage materials with high gravimetric and volumetric density as well as fast kinetics⁶ and favorable thermodynamics.⁷ Unfortunately there are currently no materials that can satisfy all these requirements. In some inter-metallic alloys, the volumetric density of H is larger than that of liquid hydrogen, however, the gravimetric density of hydrogen seldom exceeds 3 wt %. To store hydrogen with a 10 wt % capacity, one has to consider host materials that are composed of light elements such as Li, Na, B, C, N, and Al. Unfortunately, hydrogen in these materials are held by strong covalent and/or ionic bonds and desorption of hydrogen requires rather high temperatures. Thus, ways of changing the chemistry of these light complex metal hydrides has to be found so that they can be considered as viable hydrogen storage materials.

A class of materials that has attracted considerable attention in recent years is called alanates. These materials have the formula unit [M(AlH₄), M=Li, Na, K; Mg(AlH₄)₂] where the [AlH₄]⁻ unit is ionically bonded to the metal atoms. Although these materials can store hydrogen up to 18 wt %, desorption of hydrogen requires rather high temperatures and the materials are not ideal for applications. Recently NaAlH₄, which has a theoretical hydrogen content of 7.5 wt %, has attracted the most attention. This is due to the discovery by Bogdanovic and coworkers⁸ that addition of TiCl₃ not only makes the hydrogen storage capacity of sodium alanates reversible, but also significantly reduces the hydrogen desorption temperature. Several experiments have been recently carried out to understand the mechanisms for hydrogen desorption and the role Ti precursors play in the process. However, the results are not conclusive as an atomic understanding of where Ti atoms reside and how they influence the bonding

between hydrogen and metal atoms are lacking. For example, while some experiments⁹ suggest that Ti occupies the surface sites, others¹⁰ show that Ti occupies the Na sites in the bulk lattice. Presence of NaCl¹¹⁻¹³ and TiAl₃⁹, ¹⁴⁻¹⁹ has also been seen which suggests that there could be Na and/or Al vacancies in the sodium alanate crystal.

A few theoretical studies of the atomic and electronic structure of pure sodium alanates have been recently carried out which show that the bonding between Al and H atoms is covalent and the [AlH₄]⁻ unit is bound to Na⁺ by an ionic bond. The material is an insulator with a band gap of 4.6 eV. Studies by Iniguez *et al.*²⁰ show that Ti prefers to substitute the Na site in sodium alanate. In addition, calculations on clusters²¹ and crystals²⁰,²²,²³ show that Ti substitution weakens the bond between the neighboring H and Al atoms, thus making it possible for hydrogen to desorb at lower temperatures. Recently, Lovvik *at al.*²⁴, on the other hand, suggest that Ti prefers to occupy the Al site. We will show in the following that this discrepancy is due to the reference energy one uses in calculating the preferred site. We will also show that desorption of hydrogen from Ti doped NaAlH₄ is easier than that from pristine NaAlH₄ irrespective of whether Ti substitutes the Na or the Al site. No studies have been carried out that probes the role of vacancies on hydrogen desorption. Note that Na vacancies can be created as Na atoms from the sodium alanate crystal react with TiCl₃ to form NaCl. Similarly, Al vacancies may exist when Al atoms from the alanate lattice combine with Ti to form TiAl₃.

In this paper we present the electronic structure of pure and Ti doped NaAlH₄. In addition we probe the role of Na and Al vacancies on the electronic structure of sodium alanate and the mechanism for hydrogen desorption. In the following we describe our computational procedure and discuss our results.

## CALCULATION DETAILS

The calculations are carried out using the density functional theory and generalized gradient approximation for the exchange and correlation potential.²⁵,²⁶ We have used a (2x2x1) supercell consisting of 96 atoms (Na₁₆Al₁₆H₆₄) (see Fig. 1) to model the substitution of Ti in tetragonal NaAlH₄.¹⁰ Ten supercells are considered for the calculation of the total energy and electronic structure. We have performed our calculations in two steps: First, we compare the cohesive energies between (TiNa₁₅)Al₁₆H₆₄ and Na₁₆(TiAl₁₅)H₆₄ to determine whether Ti substitutes the Na or Al site as it is *a priori* not known which site Ti would occupy. Second, we calculate the structure and total energy of Na₁₅Al₁₆H₆₄ and Na₁₆Al₁₅H₆₄ supercells that simulate vacancies at the Na and Al sites respectively. Third, we compute the energy cost to remove a H atom from Na₁₆Al₁₆H₆₄, (TiNa₁₅)Al₁₆H₆₄, Na₁₆(TiAl₁₅)H₆₄, Na₁₅Al₁₆H₆₄ and Na₁₆Al₁₅H₆₄ suprecells to model the hydrogen desorption process from pure, Ti doped, Na vacancy and Al vacancy containing alanate. We have used the projector augmented plane wave (PAW) basis²⁷ as implemented in the VASP code²⁸ for these calculations. The periodic supercell approach is employed and the energy cutoff was 300 eV. We have verified that using a larger energy cut off (500 eV) does not affect our conclusions. The optimization of the geometry has been done (ionic coordinates and c/a ratio), using the Hellmann-Feynman forces on the atoms and stresses on the supercell. For sampling the irreducible wedge of the Brillouin zone we used k-point grids of 3x3x3 for the geometry optimization and 5x5x5 for the final calculation at the equilibrium volume. The structures are optimized fully without any symmetry constraint. In all calculations, self-consistency was achieved with a tolerance in the total-energy of at least 0.1 meV/atom. The calculated lattice constants a=4.95 Å and c=10.89 Å as well as the Na-Al and Al-H distances of

3.50 Å and 1.64 Å compare very well with the corresponding experimental values of 4.98 Å,
11.15Å, 3.52 Å and 1.63 Å²⁹ respectively. In Fig. 1 we identify the Na and Al sites that were
used to either substitute Ti or create a vacancy. The energies necessary to remove a hydrogen
atom from these supercells were calculated by reoptimizing Na₁₆Al₁₆H₆₃, (TiNa₁₅)Al₁₆H₆₃,
Na₁₆(TiAl₁₅)H₆₃, Na₁₅Al₁₆H₆₃ and Na₁₆Al₁₅H₆₃ supercells.

# RESULTS AND DISUSSION

We first present the results of the site preference of Ti in crystalline sodium alanate so
that these can be compared with earlier calculations²⁰,²⁴. To determine the preferential site for Ti,
we compare the cohesive energies of (TiNa₁₅)Al₁₆H₆₄ and Na₁₆(TiAl₁₅)H₆₄. In Table 1 we list the
cohesive energies (with reference to isolated atoms) of Ti doped and vacancy containing NaAlH₄
supercells relative to that of the pristine NaAlH₄. Based on these results one can conclude that Ti
would prefer to occupy the Na site as it is 0.925 eV more favourable than that when Ti
substitutes the Al site. However, one can argue that the correct reference energy to be used
should be that of the cohesive energies of hcp Ti, bcc Na, fcc Al, and the binding energy of the
H₂ molecule. These energies are found to be -5.508 eV/atom, -1.102 eV/atom, -3.498 eV/atom
and -4.511 eV respectively from our spin polarized calculations. We should emphasize that spin
polarized atomic energy correction is important especially for the isolated Ti and H atom. For
example, without the spin polarized energy correction, cohesive of H₂ molecule is as large as -
6.737 eV, which is in agreement with -6.781 eV calculated by Ke et al.³⁰. One can redefine the

energy gain/cost in replacing Na(Al) site by Ti in terms of the cohesive energies of bulk Ti, Na, and Al as:

$$\Delta\mathrm{E_{Ti\rightarrow Na}} = \mathrm{E[(TiNa_{15})Al_{16}H_{64}]_{coh} + E(Na_{bcc})_{coh} - E(Ti_{hcp})_{coh} - E[Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{64}]_{coh}}$$
$$\Delta\mathrm{E_{Ti\rightarrow Al}} = \mathrm{E[Na_{16}(TiAl_{15})H_{64}]_{coh} + E(Al_{fcc})_{coh} - E(Ti_{hcp})_{coh} - E[Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{64}]_{coh}}$$

We find $\Delta\mathrm{E_{Ti\rightarrow Na}} = 0.031$ eV/atom and $\Delta\mathrm{E_{Ti\rightarrow Al}} = 0.015$ eV/atom. The positive energy values indicate that doping is energetically unfavorable and that the least unfavorable site for Ti to substitute is the Al site. These results agree well with 3.0 kJ/mol per atom (0.031 eV/atom) and 1.5 kJ/mol per atom (0.016 eV/atom) obtained by Lovvik *et al.*²⁰ Even though the above energies are positive, it is not unlikely for Ti to occupy substitutional sites since the ball milling of TiCl₃ with NaAlH₄ introduces external energy into the system.

We now calculate the energy needed to remove a hydrogen atom from pristine and Ti doped sodium alanate. These energies are defined as:

$$\Delta\mathrm{E^{H}} = \mathrm{E[Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{63}]_{coh} - E[Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{64}]_{coh}}$$
$$\Delta\mathrm{E^{H}_{Ti\rightarrow Na}} = \mathrm{E[(TiNa_{15})Al_{16}H_{63}]_{coh} - E[(TiNa_{15})Al_{16}H_{64}]_{coh}}$$
$$\Delta\mathrm{E^{H}_{Ti\rightarrow Al}} = \mathrm{E[Na_{16}(TiAl_{15}) H_{63}]_{coh} - E[Na_{16}(TiAl_{15})H_{64}]_{coh}}$$

The calculated hydrogen desorption energies from pristine sodium alanate and from Ti substituted at Na and Al sites in sodium alanate are given in Table 2, namely, $\Delta\mathrm{E^{H}}= 3.96$ eV, $\Delta\mathrm{E^{H}_{Ti\rightarrow Na}}= 1.86$ eV, $\Delta\mathrm{E^{H}_{Ti\rightarrow Al}} = 2.47$ eV. Note that the energy to remove a hydrogen atom from Ti doped sodium alanate is smaller than that from pristine sodium alanate irrespective of whether Ti substitutes the Na or the Al site. This is caused by the change in the electronic structure as Ti is substituted at the Na or Al site²²,²³. As a Na atom is replaced by Ti, the covalent bond between Al and H atom nearest to Ti is weakened as can be seen from the increase in the Al-H bond from 1.64 Å to 1.70 Å. As an Al atom is substituted by Ti, the bond length between Ti-H is 1.80 Å and consequently leads to the lowering of the hydrogen desorption energy.

We will now show that the creation of Na and Al vacancies plays an important role on the electronic structure and hydrogen removal energy. These desorption energies are calculated by taking the cohesive energy differences as defined below:

$$\Delta \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Na}_{\mathrm{v}}}^{\mathrm{H}}=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{Na}_{15} \mathrm{Al}_{16} \mathrm{H}_{63}\right]_{\text {coh }}-\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{Na}_{15} \mathrm{Al}_{16} \mathrm{H}_{64}\right]_{\text {coh }}$$

$$\Delta \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{v}}}^{\mathrm{H}}=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{Na}_{16} \mathrm{Al}_{15} \mathrm{H}_{63}\right]_{\text {coh }}-\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{Na}_{16} \mathrm{Al}_{15} \mathrm{H}_{64}\right]_{\text {coh }}$$

We see from Table 2 (column 2) that it only costs 0.24 eV of energy $[\Delta E_{Na_{v}}^{H}]$ to remove a hydrogen atom from Na₁₅Al₁₆H₆₄ supercell. This is substantially smaller than that from Ti doped sodium alanate. More importantly, the removal of hydrogen from the vicinity of a Na vacancy is exothermic as the hydrogen atoms combine to form a H₂ molecule. The situation is more complicated in the case of an Al vacancy. Unlike in the case of a Na vacancy where the four hydrogen atoms remain in dissociated form attached to the Al atom, in the case of Al vacancy, four hydrogen atoms form two hydrogen molecules. Removal of a single hydrogen atom from the vicinity of the Al vacancy is still exothermic, but it is much less than that associated with the Na vacancy. The energy cost to remove one of the hydrogen molecules formed after the creation of an Al vacancy is 4.87 eV. However, the energy cost is smaller, namely, 2.94 eV if one were to remove two H atoms attached to different Al vacancy sites separately. This is because once a H atom is removed, the remaining H atom binds to the AlH₄ unit and the system gains energy as a result. Thus by comparing the energy cost to remove a H from Na₁₆Al₁₆H₆₄, (TiNa₁₅)Al₁₆H₆₄, Na₁₆(TiAl₁₅)H₆₄, Na₁₅Al₁₆H₆₄ and Na₁₆Al₁₅H₆₄ supercells, we come to an exciting conclusion that the presence of vacancies can enhance hydrogen desorption far better than Ti substitution into the alanate lattice can. It is worth noting that NaAlH₄ is a material where Na⁺ is bonded to (AlH₄)⁻ by an ionic bond. Thus removal of Na⁺ would raise the question of charge imbalance and the compound would charge balance by creating defects that compensate for the lack of positive charge due to the missing Na⁺. In our calculation, we removed a neutral Na(Al) atom to create a Na(Al) vacancy. Thus, the compound is maintained charge neutral after the creation of a vacancy. The structure is then fully optimized allowing the atoms in the super cell to have the freedom to relax and compensate for the missing atom.

Our task now is to understand the origin of this substantial reduction in the hydrogen removal energy following the creation of a vacancy. We concentrate on the Na vacancy. The

initial thinking was that the presence of a vacancy will deprive the adjacent $AlH_4$ unit from the extra electron it needs for stabilization. However, the structure of $AlH_4$ unit adjacent to the vacancy given in Fig. 2 is nearly the same as that in $NaAlH_4$ or $(Ti,Na)AlH_4$. In fact, the Al-H bond in a vacancy containing $NaAlH_4$ is somewhat shorter than that even in the pure alanate. To understand this paradox, we show in Fig. 2 the geometries of the $AlH_3$ units following the removal of a H atom. Note that while the bonding between Al and H in the $AlH_3$ units in $Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{63}$ and $TiNa_{15}Al_{16}H_{63}$ remains nearly tetrahedral, that in $Na_{15}Al_{16}H_{63}$ it is very different-namely, it is nearly planar with an Al-H bond length of 1.62 Å.

We have seen from earlier calculations $^{21}$ on $AlH_n$ clusters that the geometry of the $AlH_3$ cluster is planar and that the Al-H bond lengths are 1.60 Å. In addition, $AlH_3$ is a magic cluster, (i.e. the most stable among the $AlH_n$ series). The relative stability of a "planar" $AlH_3$ is primarily responsible for the reduced hydrogen desorption energy from $Na_{15}Al_{16}H_{64}$. Thus, the role of vacancy manifests in the formation of a stable $AlH_3$ unit following H desorption. In the $Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{64}$ crystal, $AlH_4$ units behave like anionic $AlH_n^-$ clusters. It is known that $(AlH_4)^-$ anion is the most stable cluster among $AlH_n^-$ species. This is consistent with what we have found in the alanate crystal where the energy cost to remove an H from $Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{64}$ is 3.96 eV. Once a Na vacancy is created in $Na_{16}Al_{16}H_{64}$ no electron can be transferred from Na, thus the $AlH_4$ unit becomes neutral. The energy cost to remove a H atom from the neutral $AlH_4$ unit is only 0.24 eV. This shows that much insight of hydrogen desorption can be obtained from studies of small atomic clusters although for quantitative understanding calculations in crystals are needed.

## CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have used density functional theory and super cell approach to study the Ti doping as well as Na and Al vacancies in $NaAlH_4$. We have found that Ti prefers to substitute the Na site or the Al site depending on different reference energy one uses. The energy needed to remove a hydrogen atom is reduced from 3.96 eV in pure sodium alanate to 1.86 eV when Ti is substituted at the Na site, while it is reduced to 2.47 eV when Ti is substituted at the Al site. More importantly, we found that the presence of Na and Al vacancies plays an even lager role in the hydrogen desorption. Not only the hydrogen removal energies in these systems are dramatically decreased compared with perfect crystal, but also the process is exothermic. These results are attributed to the unusual stability of the neutral $AlH_3$ cluster. Thus the dominant role of Ti in reducing hydrogen desorption temperature may be an indirect one - it leads to vacancy formation which then reduces the hydrogen desorption energy. This work suggests that a catalyst suitable to reduce hydrogen desorption temperature may be the one that can easily create vacancies in the alanates.
