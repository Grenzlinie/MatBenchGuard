# Short-range dislocation interactions

S.J. Zhou $^{*}$, D.L. Preston

Applied Theoretical and Computational Physics Division, Los Alamos National Laboratory, Los Alamos, NM 87545, USA

## Abstract

Short-range dislocation interactions are inherently atomistic and can only be investigated at present by means of atomistic simulations. Large-scale molecular-dynamics (MD) simulations can provide a great deal of quantitative information about the generation, motion, and interaction of dislocations, which are processes not easily accessible at present to experiments. In this paper, we will show the necessity of atomistic simulations in investigating short-range dislocation interactions by analyzing junction formation and jog formation in the process of dislocation intersection. ©1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

**Keywords**: Dislocation; Jog; Junction; Intersection; Atomistic simulation

## 1. Introduction

Dislocations [1] are involved in many aspects of the mechanical response of metals and intermetallics, including fracture [2–5], dry friction [6], and of course plastic deformation. The development of realistic models of deformation and fracture, as well as 3D discrete dislocation dynamics (for more information about 3D discrete dislocation dynamics simulations see [7–10]), require information about short-range dislocation interactions (see e. g. [11]). In the simplified case of two dimensions, Zhou et al. [12] have shown with atomistic simulations that the force between a pair of dislocations with planar cores, separated by a distance $r$, can be described by a continuum theory $1/r$ form down to 2 to 3 lattice spacings; dislocation core–core interaction corrections are significant at smaller distances. However, the short-range interactions among dislocations in three dimensions involve many new phenomena such as jog, kink, and junction formation, cross-slip, and dislocation intersection that appear when dislocations have the freedom to bend as well as twist. These phenomena are intrinsically atomistic, so continuum dislocation theory provides only an approximate description, and existing experimental techniques afford little information about them. Large-scale molecular-dynamics (MD) naturally encompasses the dislocation core-scale physics as well as bending and twisting. Its utility for investigating 3D dislocations, cracks, and their interactions has been demonstrated [5,13]. Recently Zhou et al. [14] carried out MD simulations of the intersection of two perpendicular repulsive dissociated dislocations in copper, one $60^{\circ}$ dislocation (a $60^{\circ}$ angle between its Burgers vector and its sense vector) and one screw dislocation, and found that this type of intersection process is complicated, involving junction formation and jog deviation. ## 2. Short-range dislocation interactions

The beakdown of continuum dislocation theory at distances within a few lattice spacings of the dislocation core preclude its application, except in an approximate way, to short-range processes such as junction and jog formation. However, accurate quantitative data on short-range interactions can be acquired by means of atomistic simulations. For example, the entire sequence of complex 3D configurations that occur during the intersection of extended dislocations has been recently simulated via MD by Zhou et al. [14]. In the following we will contrast the paucity of approximate results based on continuum theory with the large amount of detailed information already generated by atomistic simulations.

### 2.1. Dislocation reaction criteria in the continuum theory

For a straight mixed dislocation with its Burgers vector inclined at an angle $\beta$ to the dislocation line, the energy per unit length within an isotropic cylindrical medium of radius $R$ is

$$
\frac{W}{L}=\frac{\mu b^{2}}{4 \pi}\left(\cos ^{2} \beta+\frac{\sin ^{2} \beta}{1-v}\right) \ln \frac{\alpha R}{b}=E(\beta) \ln \frac{\alpha R}{b}.
\tag{1}
$$

This equation shows that the energy of a dislocation is proportional to $b^{2}$, which is also valid for a curved dislocation line such as a circular loop and for a dislocation at large bow-out [1]. Even without considering the variation of the dislocation energy with $\beta$, the possibility of a dislocation reaction can be determined from the values of $b^{2}$ before and after the reaction, the so-called the Frank energy criterion. For example, a perfect dislocation with Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}_{1}$ can dissociate into two Shockley partial dislocations $\mathbf{b}_{2}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{3}$ if $b_{1}^{2}>b_{2}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}$.

A general criterion for dislocation reaction can be written as

$$
E_{\text {initial }}>E_{\text {final }},
\tag{2}
$$

where $E_{\text {initial }}$ and $E_{\text {final }}$ are the total energies, including self-energies and interaction energies, before and after dislocation reaction, respectively. In principle, Eq. (2) can be used to determine whether the dislocations can react or not. In practice, $E_{\text {final }}$ is difficult to calculate. There are two reasons: (1) the reaction products are unknown since the energy landscape can be very complex for 3D multiple-dislocation interactions; (2) even if the reaction products are known, it is a challenge to calculate $E_{\text {final }}$ with linear elasticity theory since the 3D dislocation configuration is very complicated. The breakdown of linear elasticity near the dislocation cores make the problem even more formidable.

There have been some theoretical studies of 3D dislocation reactions (e.g. [15-19]). However, those analyses are questionable since the core energies, self energies, and interaction energies of the dislocation lines were not accurately calculated.

With the advent of powerful computers, some of these problems can be solved with large-scale molecular dynamics simulations.

### 2.2. Atomistic simulation results

#### 2.2.1. Methodology of molecular dynamics simulations

Here we briefly summarize the methodology used for our MD simulations of both repulsive [14] and attractive dislocation intersection in copper. The atoms interact via a realistic embedded atom method (EAM) many-body potential [20,21]. Throughout, we use as units the atomic mass $m$, the distance $r_{0}$ between nearest neighbors in the zero-temperature and zero-pressure crystal, and the bond energy $\epsilon$; hence the unit of time $t_{0}$ is given by $t_{0}=r_{0} \sqrt{m / \epsilon}$. The time step is $0.01 t_{0}$. For the Cu EAM potential, $\epsilon=0.59 \mathrm{eV}$, $r_{0}=2.556 \AA$, and $t_{0}=2.7 \times 10^{-13} \mathrm{~s}$ ($\sim 1.5$ Einstein vibrational periods). The relaxed stacking fault energy

is $36.1 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ for EAM Cu [22]. In f.c.c. metals of relatively low stacking fault energy, including copper, glide dislocations on the close-packed $\left\{\begin{array}{lll}1 & 1 & 1\end{array}\right\}$ planes are in extended configurations, consisting of two Shockley partial dislocations separated by a stacking fault ribbon. For the EAM Cu potential used here, the equilibrium separations calculated with elasticity theory are $5.7 \mathrm{~nm}\left(\approx 22 r_{0}\right)$ for the $60^{\circ}$ dislocation and $2.9 \mathrm{~nm}\left(\approx 11 r_{0}\right)$ for the screw dislocation, respectively. In our MD simulations the separations of the partials differ from the equilibrium values since there are other nearby dislocations and the dissociated dislocations are moving. In this paper, we study junction formation, jog formation, and dislocation intersection without the complications of thermal fluctuations, hence the initial temperature is set to nearly zero $(\sim 0.01 \mathrm{~K})$. Although the kinetic energy $E_{\mathrm{KE}}$ of the computational cell increases during the intersection process, the corresponding effective temperature, $2 E_{\mathrm{KE}} / 3 N k_{\mathrm{B}}$, where $N$ is the total number of atoms in the MD region, never exceeds $2 \mathrm{~K}$.

A schematic of the dislocation glide-plane geometry for repulsive intersection is shown in Fig. 1. In this paper, the notation introduced by Thompson (Hirth and Lothe, 1982) is used to describe Burgers vectors and glide planes. The dislocations $\mathbf{B A}$ and $\mathbf{D B}$ were embedded by initially displacing atoms according to the linear isotropic continuum elastic solution. Free boundary conditions are employed in the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-directions. We apply loading in the following way. A homogeneous compressive elastic strain in the $z$ direction only is imposed on the system at a constant elastic strain rate until a predetermined value of the elastic strain $\varepsilon_{z z}$ is attained at time $\tau$. Subsequently, $\varepsilon_{z z}$ is maintained throughout the computational cell by permanently fixing the positions of the atoms in a few layers on the top and bottom of the cell at their locations at time $\tau$, but allowing all other atoms to move according to the equations of motion.

We first allow the system to relax about $2 t_{0}$. The BA screw dislocation and the DB $60^{\circ}$ mixed dislocation immediately dissociate, respectively, into two $30^{\circ}$ partials, $\delta \mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B} \delta$, and into a $90^{\circ}$ partial $\alpha \mathbf{B}$ and a $30^{\circ}$ partial $\mathbf{D} \alpha$ (see Fig. 1). Under compressive loading $\left(\varepsilon_{z z}<0\right)$ the dissociated $\mathbf{D B}$ dislocation moves down (in the $\left[\begin{array}{lll}0 & \overline{1} & \overline{1}\end{array}\right]$ direction) and intersects the BA screw dislocation. The intersection results in an extended jog, or jog line, on the $\mathbf{D B}$ dislocation and a contracted jog on the BA dislocation.

### 2.2.2. Junction formation

It is a common assumption that two repulsive dislocations simply cut through each other without reaction. That is because elasticity theory cannot predict what happens when two dislocations are within a few lattice spacings of each other. With our MD simulations [14], we have shown the partial $\alpha \boldsymbol{B}$ of $\mathbf{D B}$ and the partials $\delta \mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B} \delta$ of $\mathbf{B A}$ actually react to form a junction line by bending themselves along $\left[\begin{array}{lll}1 & 0 & \overline{1}\end{array}\right]$, the intersection line between their glide planes $a$ and $d$ (Fig. 2 and Fig. 2 of [14]). The reaction between $\alpha \mathbf{B}$ and $\delta \mathbf{A}$ is $\alpha \mathbf{B}+\delta \mathbf{A}=\alpha \delta / \mathbf{A B}=\frac{1}{6}\left[\begin{array}{lll}0 & \overline{1} & 3\end{array}\right]$, while the reaction between $\alpha \mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{B} \delta$ is $\alpha \mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B} \delta=\alpha \delta=$ $\frac{1}{6}\left[\begin{array}{lll}\overline{1} & 0 & 1\end{array}\right]$. Since the reactions occur between the segments of $\mathbf{D B}$ and $\mathbf{B A}$ in the same direction [10 $\overline{1}$ ], we can use the $b^{2}$ criterion to examine the reaction.

From the above analyses, we can see that the $b^{2}$ criterion can be used for the reaction between two parallel dislocations.

A 3D dislocation dynamics simulation of the bending event preceding junction formation could be used to check whether its segmented dislocation lines are unphysically stiff, as is often the case.

The reconfiguration of dislocation lines has been observed in the dislocation dynamics simulation of Schwarz [23]. It would be most effective to investigate 3D dislocation reactions by using both dislocation dynamics and atomistic simulation. Given that long-range elastic interaction could determine the short-range configurations of interacting dislocations, a dislocation dynamics simulation with realistic bending and twisting flexibilities could be used to identify the configurations of interacting dislocations that need to be investigated with atomistic simulations.

### 2.2.3. Jog line formation
The repulsive intersection between $\boldsymbol{D B}$ and $\boldsymbol{B A}$ results in the formation of jogs on both $\boldsymbol{D B}$ and $\boldsymbol{B A}$. With elastic theory, however, it is difficult to predict the glide planes on which the jogs are located, the directions in which the jogs are oriented, and the kinds of jog configurations (contracted or extended). These characteristics of the jog determine its effect on the mobility of the jogged dislocation [24].

With MD simulations [14], we found that the junction line forms along $[10 \overline{1}]$, which could prevent the jog line from forming along the same direction $[10 \overline{1}]$. Consequently, the jog line forms along $[0 \overline{1} \overline{1}]$.

This example clearly shows that atomistic simulations are needed to find the correct reaction path if the problem involves a complicated energy landscape.

### 2.2.4. Critical breaking angle for repulsive dislocation intersection
Under the applied compressive strain, the edge partial $\boldsymbol{\alpha B}$ bows around the almost stationary screw dislocation $\boldsymbol{B A}$. When the two segments of $\boldsymbol{\alpha B}$ meet at the critical breaking angle $\phi_{\mathrm{c}}=70^{\circ}, \boldsymbol{\alpha B}$ passes the screw dislocation $\boldsymbol{B A}$. About 5 ps later the trailing partial $\boldsymbol{D \alpha}$ passes through $\boldsymbol{B A}$ without bending and at nearly constant speed [14]. Hence the critical configuration for complete intersection is attained when the two segments of $\boldsymbol{\alpha B}$ meet at $\phi_{\mathrm{c}}$. Unzipping a junction line along $[10 \overline{1}]$, and forming a jog line along $[0 \overline{1} \overline{1}]$ are two factors that determine $\phi_{\mathrm{c}}$. The critical breaking angle measures the strength of $\boldsymbol{B A}$ as an obstacle to $\boldsymbol{D B}$.

### 3. Conclusions

By analyzing the processes of both repulsive and attractive dislocation intersections, we have demonstrated that short-range dislocation interactions must be investigated with atomistic simulations since their energy landscapes are quite complicated. Atomistic simulations can find the physical path in the complicated energy landscape. Essential information for macroscopic/mesocopic deformation modeling, such as a critical breaking angle for dislocation intersections, can be accurately determined with atomistic simulations.
