## ABSTRACT

Dense doped ceria films are promising candidate electrolytes for solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) operated at intermediate temperature ($\sim$500 °C). In this work, detailed microstructural features of Gd-doped ceria (GDC) electrolyte film supported by Ni–GDC cermet anode were studied. Formation of inhomogeneous microstructures in the GDC film was observed, which was enhanced in the region near the film/anode interface due to the Ni diffusion from the anode. Then, the mechanism by which Ni affects the microstructure was explained by computer simulation of formation and development of defect clusters.

### 1. Introduction

Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) operated at intermediate temperature ($\sim$500 °C) has attracted much attention because reducing the operating temperature of a SOFC to below 800 °C can reduce the degradation of cell components, improve the flexibility in cell design, and lower the cost of material and manufacture by using cheap and readily available materials [1,2]. Currently, yttria-stabilized zirconia is the most commonly used electrolyte material, which shows high ionic conductivity at high temperature (>800 °C). Associated with lowering the operating temperature, one problem is an increase in electrolyte resistivity. This problem can be overcome either by decreasing electrolyte thickness, i.e. by using thin film electrolyte, or by using alternative material with higher ionic conductivity at lower temperature [3]. Rare-earth-doped ceria has relatively high ionic conductivity at intermediate temperature so that dense doped ceria films are promising candidate electrolytes for intermediate-temperature SOFCs.

For the purpose of achieving high quality electrolyte films, the microstructural characteristics of electrolyte films and electrolyte/electrode interfaces have been studied. Because high-temperature treatments cannot be avoided in fabrication processes of SOFCs, interdiffusion of cell components may take place [4–7]. Furthermore, in sintered bulk samples of various types of rare-earth-doped ceria, inhomogeneous microstructures (i.e. nano-sized domains) containing segregated dopant cations and locally ordered oxygen vacancies have been widely observed when the doping concentration is higher than 10–15 at.% [8–17]. Therefore, it is possible that these inhomogeneous microstructures may also form in the electrolyte films. In our recent work [18], the formation of inhomogeneous microstructures in 20 at.% Sm-doped ceria (SDC) film has been confirmed. Moreover, it was observed that the diffusion of Ni from the Ni–SDC cermet anode into the film can enhance the formation of inhomogeneous microstructures in the region near the film/anode interface. Because the inhomogeneous microstructures may block the migration of oxygen vacancies and decrease the concentration of freely mobile oxygen vacancies, they have a negative effect on the ionic conductivity of doped ceria [8–11]. Therefore, it was suggested that the diffusion of Ni into the film can lead to a degradation of the ionic conduction of the film near the film/anode interface and, hence, can lower the effective ionic conductivity of the electrolyte film [18]. Such an effect will become more significant as the thickness of electrolyte film is reduced or the thickness of interfacial region is increased by the enhanced interdiffusion. Consequently, the experimental result that the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte film decreases with decreasing film thickness can be explained [18,19].

However, in our previous work [18], a detailed microstructural analysis on the electrolyte film is still lacking and the mechanism by which Ni diffusion affects the microstructure has not been explained. Therefore, further study on the inhomogeneous microstructures in doped ceria electrolyte films is required.

In this work, detailed microstructural features of 20 at.% Gd-doped ceria (GDC) film supported by Ni-GDC cermet anode were studied mainly by using transmission electron microscope (TEM). The effect of Ni diffusion from the anode on the microstructure of the GDC film was observed. Then computer simulation was used to understand the effect of Ni on the microstructure.

## 2. Experimental procedure

### 2.1. Sample preparation and characterization

$Ce_{0.8}Gd_{0.2}O_{1.9}$ films were deposited on NiO-GDC composite substrates by electrophoretic deposition (EPD) method. The preparation of the substrate and the condition of EPD have already appeared in Ref. [20] though Sm-doped ceria was used in that study. The GDC nanopowder for the substrate preparation and film deposition was prepared by ammonium carbonate coprecipitation method [21] followed by calcination under flowing oxygen gas (200 mL/min) at $800\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 2 h. After EPD, the samples were co-sintered at $1400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 6 h in air. For the reduction of NiO into metallic Ni, the sintered samples were subsequently heated at $500\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 4 h under flowing $\text{H}_{2}$ (50 mL/min, $10\%\text{H}_{2}$ in He). For comparison, a GDC bulk sample was also prepared by sintering at $1400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 6 h.

The microstructures in both the samples before and after the reduction treatment were first observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS). Then the detailed microstructures were characterized using TEM. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were obtained by a JEOL JEM-2000EX TEM. The high resolution TEM (HRTEM), EDS and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) analysis were performed on a JEOL JEM-2100F TEM equipped with EDS and Gatan imaging filter (GIF). To prepare TEM samples, the cross sectional samples were ground to about $60\ \mu\text{m}$ followed by ion-milling.

### 2.2. Atomistic simulation methodology

As suggested in our previous work, the inhomogeneous microstructures in rare-earth-doped ceria are formed from the development of defect clusters containing dopant cations and oxygen vacancies [10,22]. Therefore, the effect of Ni on the formation of inhomogeneous microstructures in GDC electrolyte film was explained by atomistic computer simulation of defect clusters. The simulation was based on a classical Born model description of ionic lattice [23]. The lattice energy is the sum of long-range Coulombic interactions, which are summed using Ewald's method [24], and short-range interactions described by parameterized pair potentials, $S_{ij}$. The short-range interactions are modeled using a Buckingham potential form $S_{ij} = A\mathrm{exp}(-r_{ij}/\rho) - Cr_{ij}^{-6}$, where $A$, $\rho$ and $C$ are adjustable parameters. The values of these parameters are listed in Table 1. The short-range interactions are set to zero beyond a cut-off of $20\ \text{\AA}$. The effect of ionic polarizability of $\text{O}^{2-}$ and $\text{Ce}^{4+}$ ions is included via the shell model [29]. In this model, the ions are comprised of a massive core with charge $X|e|$ and a massless shell with charge $Y|e|$. The overall charge state of each ion is equal to $(X + Y)|e|$. The core and shell are connected by an isotropic harmonic spring of force constant $k$. These shell parameters are listed in Table 2.

To predict how a lattice accommodates defects, the Mott-Littleton two-region approach [30] was used for the simulation of the defect formation energy, which is coded in the GULP program [31]. The detailed methodology of this approach is described elsewhere [32]. In this approach, the lattice for energy minimization is partitioned into two regions: a spherical inner region I, at the center of which the defects are introduced, and an outer region II, which extends to infinity. To ensure a smooth transition between regions I and II, an interfacial region IIa is introduced. In this study, we used an inner region I with a radius of $3.0a_{0}$ ($a_{0}$ is $5.411\ \text{\AA}$, which is the lattice constant of ceria) and an interfacial region IIa with a radius of $6.0a_{0}$.

## 3. Experimental results

### 3.1. Microstructural characterization

The samples were first observed by SEM. As shown in Fig. 1, the thickness of the dense GDC films is about $10\ \mu\text{m}$. By comparing the images of samples before and after the reduction treatment, it can be seen that the reduction treatment has caused transformation of relatively dense NiO-GDC substrate into porous Ni-GDC anode.

The detailed microstructures of the films were further characterized by using TEM. In the HRTEM image of the GDC film before the reduction treatment, inhomogeneous microstructures enclosed by dashed lines can be seen (Fig. 2(a)). They are nano-sized domains, which have the same features as those in sintered bulk samples [8–17]. Because the lattice of the domains is slightly different from the fluorite-structured matrix, there is lattice mismatch between the domains and the matrix. These domains can be distinguished from the matrix by Fast Fourier transform (FFT) and inverse FFT (IFFT) as explained in our previous work [11]. Besides the domains, superstructure can be observed in the region near the film/substrate interface. As shown in Fig. 2(b), fringes parallel to ($\overline{2}\overline{2}0$) plane can be seen with repeated distance of $0.75\ \text{nm}$, which is four times of ($\overline{2}\overline{2}0$) plane spacing. In the SAED pattern (Fig. 2(c)), besides the reflections of fluorite structure, diffuse scattering related to the domains can be seen but no extra reflection corresponding to the superstructure was observed. Since the SAED pattern was taken from a large area of the sample ($\sim0.2\ \mu\text{m}$), it is insensitive to the small deviation of the microstructure. Therefore, FFT was performed on the HRTEM images by the software Digital Micrograph 3.7.4. In the FFT image corresponding to Fig. 2(a), only the reflections of fluorite structure can be seen (Fig. 2(d)), while in the FFT image corresponding to Fig. 2(b), extra spots related to the superstructure can be seen (Fig. 2(e)). Similar superstructure was observed in 50 at.%Tb-doped ceria [12]. It was suggested that the superstructure is developed from the domains and has a C-type-related structure [12]. Because the superstructure was not observed in $\text{Ce}_{0.8}\text{Gd}_{0.2}\text{O}_{1.9}$ sintered bulk sample [14], the formation of the superstructure indicates an enhancement of microstructural inhomogeneity in the GDC film near the film/substrate interface.

The sample after the reduction treatment was then observed (Fig. 3). Superstructure can also be observed in the region near the film/substrate interface. Corresponding to the formation of the inhomogeneous microstructures, diffuse scattering in the diffraction pattern and extra spots in FFT image can also be seen. ### 3.2. Interdiffusion between GDC film and substrate

Because of the sintering and reduction treatment at high temperature, it was expected that interdiffusion between the electrolyte film and substrate may take place. The interdiffusion was first examined by EDS line scan across the film/substrate interface. As shown in Fig. 5, aggregation of Ni behind the interface or segregate at the interface can be seen. However, considering the spatial resolution of EDS (about $1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$), it is hard to confirm the Ni diffusion from Fig. 5. Therefore, TEM–EDS point analysis was performed. As an example, Fig. 6 shows the EDS spectra taken from the sample before the reduction treatment. The analysis points are in GDC film and NiO grain near the film/substrate interface. Interdiffusion can be seen from the spectra. When the parameter $d$ is small ($0$–$0.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$), the Ni concentration $\lbrack\mathrm{Ni}/(\mathrm{Ce}+\mathrm{Gd}+\mathrm{Ni})\rbrack$ is about 17–19 at.% before reduction, and increases to about 26–30 at.% after reduction. However, the Ni concentration decreases dramatically with increasing $d$. When $d$ was larger than $0.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, Ni was hard to be detected. In contrast, the Ce and Gd concentrations in NiO or Ni grains $\lbrack\mathrm{Ce}/(\mathrm{Ce}+\mathrm{Gd}+\mathrm{Ni}),\mathrm{Gd}/(\mathrm{Ce}+\mathrm{Gd}+\mathrm{Ni})\rbrack$ are relatively low, which are 2–3 at.% and 0–1 at.% before reduction, and 7–11% and 0–2% after reduction, respectively.

Comparing the Ni diffusion with the HRTEM and EELS observation results, it can be seen that the enhancement of the formation of inhomogeneous microstructures and high ordering level of oxygen vacancies near the film/substrate interface should be attributed to the Ni diffusion. In the previous work of SDC film supported by Ni–SDC cermet anode [18], similar observation results of HRTEM, EELS and Ni diffusion were reported and it was also suggested that the Ni diffusion can enhance the formation of inhomogeneous microstructures and ordering level of oxygen vacancies in the film. As suggested in the previous work [18], an interfacial region at electrolyte side is formed due to the Ni diffusion and the enhancement of inhomogeneous microstructures and ordering level of oxygen vacancies. Since the inhomogeneous microstructures have negative effect on the ionic conductivity of doped ceria [8–11], this interfacial region can result in a degradation of the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte film [18].

## 4. Computer simulation

The effect of Ni diffusion on the microstructure can also be understood by computer simulation of defect clusters because inhomogeneous microstructures in doped ceria are formed based on the development of defect clusters [10,22].

When rare-earth dopant is introduced into ceria, it is widely accepted that vacancy compensation is the favorable solution mechanism. For example, in GDC, the substitution of $Gd^{3+}$ cations for $Ce^{4+}$ is accompanied by the formation of one oxygen vacancy for every two $Gd^{3+}$ cations. In the case of $Ni^{2+}$ cations dissolved in $CeO_2$, the charge imbalance can be compensated in two different ways:

(1) An oxygen vacancy can be created. The equation and the reaction energy are
$$NiO \stackrel{CeO2}{\rightarrow} Ni_{Ce}'' + V_{O}^{\cdot \cdot} + O_{O}^{x} \tag{1}$$

$$E_{\text{Reaction}} = E_{CeO_2}^{\text{Lattice}} + E_{Ni_{Ce}''} + E_{V_{O}^{\cdot \cdot}} - E_{NiO}^{\text{Lattice}} = 5.00\mathrm{eV}. \tag{2}$$

(2) A Ni interstitial can be formed in the body center of an empty cube. The equation and the reaction energy are
$$2NiO \stackrel{CeO2}{\rightarrow} Ni_{Ce}'' + Ni_{i}^{\cdot \cdot} + 2O_{O}^{x} \tag{3}$$

$$E_{\text{Reaction}} = E_{CeO_2}^{\text{Lattice}} + E_{Ni_{Ce}''} + E_{Ni_{i}^{\cdot \cdot}} - 2E_{NiO}^{\text{Lattice}} = 9.46\mathrm{eV}. \tag{4}$$

## 5. Conclusions

In this work, the detailed microstructural features of GDC electrolyte film were studied. The formation of inhomogeneous microstructures including domains and superstructure was observed. Moreover, Ni diffusion, which mainly takes place during sintering, can enhance the formation of inhomogeneous microstructures in the electrolyte film near the film/anode interface. Such effect of Ni diffusion on the microstructure is possibly because Ni can act as dopant in GDC and, therefore, the total concentration of dopant including both Ni and Gd is increased. The effect of Ni diffusion on the microstructure can be further explained by computer simulation of the formation and development of clusters containing $Ni^{2+}$ cations and oxygen vacancies.
