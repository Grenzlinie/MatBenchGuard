# A means of obtaining a full kinetic rate expression for the oxidation of a solid substrate from a single criticality data point

J. C. Jones
Department of Engineering, Fraser Noble Building, University of Aberdeen, King's College,
Aberdeen AB24 3UE, UK

(Received 23 January 1998; revised 22 May 1998; accepted 24 May 1998)

A method whereby kinetic rate parameters for the oxidation of a material such as powdered coal or shredded wood can be obtained from a single criticality data point—determined, for example, from oven heating tests with a 10-cm cubic gauze container—is developed theoretically and applied to two bituminous coals. © 1998 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved

(Keywords: spontaneous heating; kinetic parameters)

## INTRODUCTION
The basket heating approach to studying spontaneous combustion of solids such as coal or wood¹ requires, for the determination of the activation energy $E$, that critical ambient temperatures of ignition shall be determined for a number of sample (basket) sizes and the results plotted according to the Frank–Kamenetskii (F-K) model of thermal ignition. A data point can then be substituted into the expression for the critical F-K parameter in order to obtain the pre-exponential factor $A$, this requiring values for quantities including the exothermicity and the thermal conductivity. A weakness in adaptations of approaches which use only a single sample size²·³ is that they assume, not necessarily justifiably, a single value of $E$.

All of this is well known. The aim of this paper is to describe how a value of the activation energy can be rigorously obtained from a single criticality data point. Once this value has been obtained, an $A$ value can be found by substitution; therefore, a series of tests to determine the critical ambient temperature of a solid substrate at one sample size only can yield a full rate expression. Results reported previously for two Scottish bituminous coals⁴ will be used to illustrate the approach.

## THEORY
Criticality is marked by fulfilment of the condition⁵:

$$
\delta_{\text{crit}}=\frac{r_{\mathrm{o}}^{2} Q E \sigma A \exp \left[-E / R\left(T_{\mathrm{o}}\right)_{\text{crit}}\right]}{k R\left(T_{\mathrm{o}}\right)_{\text{crit}}^{2}} \tag{1}
$$

$=2.57$ for a cubic reactant shape,
where$T_{\mathrm{o}}=$ ambient (oven) temperature (K), $r_{\mathrm{o}}=$ reactant dimension (m), $Q=$ heat of combustion (J kg⁻¹), $\sigma=$ bulk density (kg m⁻³), $k=$ thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) and $R=$ gas constant (8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).

Now, for a reactant at criticality:

$$
Q A \exp \left[-E / R\left(T_{\mathrm{o}}\right)_{\text{crit}}\right]=c[\mathrm{d} T / \mathrm{d} t]_{\mathrm{o}}, \tag{2}
$$

where $c=$ heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) and $[\mathrm{d} T / \mathrm{d} t]_{\mathrm{o}}=$ rate of temperature rise at the stage where the sample reaches oven temperature (K s⁻¹).

The significance of $[\mathrm{d} T / \mathrm{d} t]_{\mathrm{o}}$ has been fully discussed previously⁴. Substituting from eqn (2) into eqn (1) gives:

$$
\delta_{\text{crit}}=\frac{r_{\mathrm{o}}^{2} E \sigma c[\mathrm{d} T / \mathrm{d} t]_{\mathrm{o}}}{k R\left(T_{\mathrm{o}}\right)_{\text{crit}}^{2}}. \tag{3}
$$

Rearranging:

$$
\delta_{\text{crit}}\left[\left(T_{\mathrm{o}}\right)_{\text{crit}} / r_{\mathrm{o}}\right]^{2} \times\left\{k R / \sigma c[\mathrm{d} T / \mathrm{d} t]_{\mathrm{o}}\right\}=E. \tag{4}
$$

Now, the quotient in the first pair of square brackets on the right of eqn (4) is directly calculable from a data pair, and $[\mathrm{d} T / \mathrm{d} t]_{\mathrm{o}}$ can be measured from the recorder trace. The density $\sigma$ is of course known, and $k$ and $c$ can be reliably estimated for materials such as wood and coals. A value can therefore be obtained for $E$.

Common values of $k$ $(0.143\ \text{W m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1})$ and of $c$ $(1260\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1})^{4}$ will be used. Substituting the criticality data points into eqn (4) gives, for the Rosslynlee coal:

$$E=66\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

and for the Dalquhandy:

$$E=68\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}.$$

Now, the multi-point values$^{4}$ are respectively $54\pm5$ and 74 $\pm4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. The single-point method has therefore delivered the goods in terms of the relative magnitudes and the actual values to within about 10–15%. The pre-exponential factor $A$ can be obtained by substitution of the data point, as shown previously using a multi-point value of $E^{4}$; therefore, the way is open for determination of full kinetic rate expressions for a solid substrate from a single criticality data point.

The purpose of this paper is to explain the principles of the approach and illustrate it by means of an example. Measures to improve the precision, such as more accurate values of $k$ and $c$, are a matter for future users of the approach to introduce as necessary. Even without such improvements, the measured value of $E$ is sufficiently accurate for judgement to be made as to whether the activation energy is in the neighbourhood of that assumed in the standard tests for self-heating which use a single sample size$^{2,3}$, in the range of about $77-90\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, depending on the safety margin built in. There is, however, one obvious refinement to the experimental technique: use of a recorder with a first-derivative channel would enable $[\text{d}T/\text{d}t]_{o}$ to be obtained to a higher degree of precision than hand measurement from the temperature trace itself.

## CONCLUDING REMARKS
Values for the kinetic parameters of a solid substrate can be determined from a single criticality data point using the approach described herein. This is particularly valuable when one of the standard tests for spontaneous combustion using a 10-cm basket is applied, since a value for $E$ can be obtained from the temperature traces close to criticality and it can be ascertained how close this is to the range of values for which such tests are reasonably reliable. This eliminates the high degree of uncertainty entailed in application of such tests with no knowledge of the activation energy. If good values of $k$ and $c$ for the particular substrate are available or can be determined, very accurate values of the kinetic parameters can be deduced. The principles outlined in this paper open the way to these benefits as an extension of an already widely used experimental approach.
