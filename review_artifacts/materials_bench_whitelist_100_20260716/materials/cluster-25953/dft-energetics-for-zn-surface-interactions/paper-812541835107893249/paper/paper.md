# Spontaneous Formation of a ZnO Monolayer by the Redox Reaction of Zn on Graphene Oxide

Seungwoo Son, $^{\parallel }$ Yeonchoo Cho, $^{\parallel }$ Hyo-Ki Hong, $^{\parallel }$ Jongyeong Lee, Jung Hwa Kim, Kangsik Kim, Yeongdong Lee, Aram Yoon, Hyeon-Jin Shin, $^{*}$ and Zonghoon Lee*

**ABSTRACT:** Graphene-based two-dimensional heterostructures are of substantial interest both for fundamental studies and their various potential applications. Particularly interesting are atomically thin semiconducting oxides on graphene, which uniquely combine a wide band gap and optical transparency. Here, we report the atomic-scale investigation of a novel self-formation of a ZnO monolayer from the Zn metal on a graphene oxide substrate. The spontaneous oxidation of the ultrathin Zn metal occurs by a reaction with oxygen supplied from the graphene oxide substrate, and graphene oxide is deoxygenated by a transfer of oxygen from O-containing functional groups to the zinc metal. The ZnO monolayer formed by this spontaneous redox reaction shows a graphene-like structure and a band gap of about 4 eV. This study demonstrates a unique and straightforward synthetic route to atomically thin two-dimensional heterostructures made from a two-dimensional metal oxide and graphene, formed by the spontaneous redox reaction of a very thin metal layer directly deposited on graphene oxide.

**KEYWORDS:** ZnO monolayer, graphene-like ZnO, graphene oxide, spontaneous redox, transmission electron microscopy

## INTRODUCTION

Two-dimensional (2D) materials are fascinating both for the discovery of new physics and properties in the 2D limit and for many fascinating applications. Currently, atomically thin 2D materials with many features are available; their unique structure and properties make them attractive for optoelectronic devices, especially in photodetectors, $^{1}$ transistors, $^{2}$ and sensor $^{3}$ applications. In particular, forming metal oxide semiconductors on 2D materials has become a new integration method in the fabrication of optoelectronic and flexible electronic devices with high charge carrier mobility and wide band gap energy. $^{4-6}$

Among the candidate metal oxide semiconductors, zinc oxide (ZnO) has been used in UV photosensors, $^{7}$ LEDs, $^{4,5}$ and flexible electronic devices $^{8}$ as forms of ZnO grown on graphene or hexagonal boron nitride. The wurtzite structure is thermodynamically the most stable form of $ZnO,^{6}$ but when thinned to few layers, ZnO with the wurtzite structure can transform into a stable 2D monolayer honeycomb structure similar to graphene. $^{9-11}$ This graphene-like form of ZnO has unique electrical and mechanical properties and has been investigated both theoretically and experimentally. $^{12,13}$ In particular, because depositing a graphene-like ZnO monolayer on graphene itself is potentially interesting for various applications, the growth of thin ZnO layers on graphene has been extensively studied. $^{14,15}$

Various methods including hydrothermal, $^{16}$ metal-organic vapor phase epitaxy, $^{17}$ and atomic layer deposition (ALD) $^{15}$ have been employed for depositing ZnO semiconductors on graphene. In particular, it has been shown that a graphene-like ZnO monolayer can be formed on graphene by ALD. However, due to the hydrophobic nature of graphene, the deposited material usually forms small islands of Zn or wurtzite ZnO on the graphene substrate.

Here, we introduce a simple method to obtain graphene-like ZnO monolayers on graphene oxide that involves the deposition of Zn metal on graphene oxide and O-containing functional groups on the graphene oxide function as solid oxygen sources for the oxidation of the deposited Zn metal. In this work, by combining atomic resolution transmission electron microscopy (ARTEM) with image simulations and first-principles calculations, we are able to reveal the metal-to-semiconductor metal oxide transition of the Zn metal on graphene oxide. Thus, our study proposes a new synthetic route to atomically thin 2D heterostructures comprising a 2D metal oxide and graphene by the spontaneous redox reaction of a very thin metal layer on graphene oxide.

Deposition of Zn on Graphene Oxide. Deposition of Zn on graphene oxide was conducted in a precision etching and coating system (PECS; Gatan). Characterization. Raman spectra were acquired using a WITec confocal Raman microscope (helium−neon laser, 532 nm). XPS analysis was performed using the K-alpha system (Thermo Fisher Instruments Inc.). UV−vis−NIR spectra were acquired using Cary 5000 (Agilent Instruments Inc.).

Atomic Resolution TEM Imaging and STEM-EELS. TEM analysis was performed using an aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (Titan G2 Cube 60-300 kV (FEI) at the UNIST). To minimize beam exposure to the sample, images were collected at a low operating voltage of 80 kV while adopting a minimal beam exposure approach. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM)− electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was performed using a Gatan Quantum 965 dual EELS system with an energy resolution of 0.2 eV.

Image Simulation. Image simulations of ARTEM images were performed using MacTempasX under the microscopy conditions (Titan G2 Cube (FEI) at 80 kV). The simulated structure was constructed using Vesta and Matlab with an interlayer distance of 3.556 Å, estimated from density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Figure S3b). Brightness normalization and profiling were performed using Matlab software.

DFT Calculations. We calculated the energetics of oxygen transfer processes using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package. We used the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof functional with Grimme's D3 correction. The cutoff kinetic energy of the plane wave basis set was 500 eV. The simulation box contained a vacuum of at least 15 Å, and the dipole correction was applied in order to minimize spurious interactions between slabs. Ions were relaxed until residual forces were less than 0.02 eV/Å.

In Figure 2a, the pristine graphene oxide sample with its O-containing functional groups is shown; the corresponding fast Fourier transform in the inset shows blurred spot patterns of graphene oxide due to lattice distortion of graphene induced by the presence of a large number of different O-containing functional groups. During the deposition of Zn metal, nanoclusters of ZnO starts to form on these O-containing functional groups of graphene oxide. Figure 2b shows that the ZnO monolayer clusters gradually grow up to 2−3 nm in diameter, after which the crystallites grow in the lateral direction and show same hexagonal lattice with the increased deposition time. Generally, ZnO is crystallographically misoriented by $30^\circ$ with respect to the graphene substrate to minimize the density of dangling bonds. The two misorientation angles of 0 and $30^\circ$ were explained to be due to the heteroepitaxial relationship between the ZnO monolayer and graphene,¹⁵ where the lowest energy configurations correspond to when the $[10\overline{1}0]$ or the $[11\overline{2}0]$ crystallographic direction of ZnO matches the $[10\overline{1}0]$ direction of graphene.¹⁸ In our experiment, the ZnO monolayer does not show a clear heteroepitaxial relationship on graphene oxide. This is because, unlike graphene, there is a substantial number of O-containing functional groups on graphene oxide, and owing to the high reactivity of these O-containing functional groups, the dangling bonds of the ZnO monolayer are terminated by bonding with these O-containing functional groups rather than by binding to the surface of graphene oxide maintaining a graphene lattice. This interaction of O-containing functional groups with the dangling bonds of the ZnO monolayer may perturb the heteroepitaxial relationship between the ZnO monolayer and the graphene oxide substrate. After Zn metal deposition for 60 s, coalescence takes place over large areas on the graphene oxide and the ZnO monolayer grains are merged, resulting in the formation of grain boundaries (Figure 2c). The presence of several sets of spot patterns in the Fourier transform (the inset of Figure 2c) confirms the formation of a nanosized polycrystalline ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide.

To investigate the electronic structure of the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide, as well as that of the pristine graphene oxide film, EELS was performed. Figure 2g shows the low-loss EEL spectrum of pristine graphene oxide and the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide after subtracting the zero-loss peak. Plasmon excitations are seen in the spectra of the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide. The band gap energy of the ZnO monolayer is estimated from the spectra using a power law and linear fit method;¹⁹,²⁰ our results yield a band gap energy value of 4 eV for the formed ZnO monolayer, which is significantly larger than that of bulk ZnO (3.3 eV). We attribute this result to quantum confinement effects expected to prevail in a nanosized ZnO monolayer and the graphene-like structure of the ZnO layer.¹⁴,²¹,²² Figure 2h shows the C K-edge spectrum recorded from the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide and the pristine graphene oxide film. The positions of the different peaks that were identified in the C K-edge suggest the presence of both sp² and sp³ bonds in graphene oxide. The fine structure of the C K-edge in the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide is slightly different from that in pristine graphene oxide, but the peak energies are very close to those in pristine graphene oxide, suggesting little damage to the graphene oxide layer during the deposition of the Zn metal. Figure 2i shows the comparison of the EEL spectrum of the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide and that of pristine graphene oxide, which confirms the presence of both Zn and O in the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide. The inset in Figure 2i shows the fine structure of the O K-edge where peaks located around 560 eV corresponding to oxygen atoms bonded to Zn in the ZnO monolayer are seen.²³

ARTEM images show graphene oxide with a ZnO monolayer residing on the surface. Following the deposition of Zn metal on graphene oxide, the ZnO monolayer is formed by a spontaneous

redox reaction of the Zn metal on graphene oxide, and the ZnO monolayer so formed can be further grown by electron beam irradiation. Figure 3 shows time-elapsed ARTEM images taken during the formation of the ZnO monolayer on the graphene oxide surface triggered by electron beam irradiation. First, the ZnO monolayer is not perfectly formed with empty regions among the ZnO layer (Figure 3a). Next, Zn and O present on the graphene oxide surface bind to form a ZnO monolayer (Figure 3b,c). The uncrystallized Zn and surrounding O on the graphene oxide undergo self-organization with the edge of the ZnO monolayer (Figure 3b,c) and result in the formation of a uniform monolayer sheet of ZnO on graphene oxide (Figure 3d). With extended electron beam irradiation, the O atoms in graphene oxide are progressively consumed, creating a hole on the graphene oxide layer as indicated by the solid white line in Figure 3d; the stepwise evolution of this process can be viewed in real time in the Supporting Information Movie, which images the lateral growth of a ZnO monolayer on a graphene oxide substrate at atomic-scale resolution. The time-elapsed ARTEM images show layer-by-layer growth of the ZnO monolayer, which implies the surface energy of the ZnO monolayer is lower than that of the graphene oxide surface; when this is not the case, the deposited material forms three-dimensional islands,following the Volmer-Weber growth mode. $^{24}$

Figure 3e,g shows, respectively, the raw ARTEM images of the graphene-like ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide and the corresponding simulated image under the imaging conditions. A schematic of atomic modeling of the ZnO monolayer on graphene for image simulations is shown in Figure S3. Figure 3f,h shows the intensity profiles acquired from the experimental image and the simulated image, respectively. In Figure 3e-h, Zn, O, and C atoms can be clearly distinguished by variation in intensity arising from their different atomic weights (Zn > O > C). This variation of intensity proves that the formed ZnO is a monolayer of graphene-like ZnO in which Zn and O atoms show the trigonal planar coordination instead of the bulk tetrahedral configuration of the wurtzite ZnO. $^{14}$ When a graphene-like ZnO monolayer is formed by adatom adsorption on the surface of graphene, the O-terminated zigzag edges of the ZnO monolayer are more stable when compared with Zn-terminated zigzag edges or armchair edges, $^{15}$ but the growth of graphene-like ZnO on the graphene oxide surface does not always match with the trend of growth on graphene. A comparison of the raw ARTEM image (Figure 3e,f) with the simulated image (Figure 3g,h) reveals that the formed ZnO on graphene oxide has a monolayered graphene-like structure and several types of edges including O-terminated zigzag edges.

Various defects such as vacancies and grain boundaries, invariably present in materials, have been investigated using aberration-corrected TEM. $^{25-29}$ In this study, our ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide is ideal for atomic resolution imaging of the different defects, mainly due to its monolayer nature and also because of multiple grain boundaries. The defects of the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide were investigated using an aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope, and the results are shown in Figure S4.

The oxidation of Zn on graphene oxide is not abnormal because the standard electrode potential of Zn is -0.76 V, while that of Pd is 0.92 V, meaning that Zn is more easily oxidized than a noble metal. This trend can also be demonstrated from first- principles total energy calculations. If it is assumed that the oxygen atoms supplied by graphene oxide originate from epoxide groups, the reaction energy of bulk Zn oxidation is -3.85 eV per atomic oxygen, while that of bulk Pd oxidation is only -1.86 eV per atomic oxygen. Because the oxidation of Pd on graphene oxide has already been reported, $^{30}$ there is no doubt that Zn will be oxidized on graphene oxide. In addition, we found that an epoxide oxygen atom from graphene oxide can migrate to the Zn surface without crossing any energy barrier and the reaction is exothermic by 5.5 eV (Figure 4a).

Oxygen atoms belonging to graphene oxide can be easily transferred to the surface of metallic Zn and to a surface oxygen vacancy in ZnO (Figure 4a,b). In order to prove this, we used first-principles density functional calculations (DFT) to estimate the barriers and energies necessary for different oxygen transfer scenarios. Metallic Zn and ZnO with a surface oxygen vacancy were considered, since an oxygen atom from graphene oxide is first transferred to metallic Zn and there on to ZnO. Two most common oxygen-containing functional groups on graphene oxide were considered: hydroxyl and epoxide groups.

We found that in all the cases, oxygen transfer from graphene oxide to Zn or ZnO is both thermodynamically and kinetically preferred at any thickness of Zn and ZnO (Figure S5). The oxygen transfer processes are also found to be highly exothermic. The least exothermic is the transfer of a hydroxyl group to metallic Zn, which is accompanied by release of a high energy of ~4 eV. Moreover, the oxygen transfer processes are kinetically facile and the energy barrier of oxygen transfer to the surface oxygen vacancy of ZnO is negligible for both hydroxyl and peroxide groups in graphene oxide. The processes of spontaneous oxidation of the Zn metal and reduction of graphene oxide are illustrated in Figure 5. Figure 5a shows the schematic image of pristine graphene oxide, deposition of the Zn metal on graphene oxide, and the formation of ZnO on the partially reduced graphene oxide. The oxidation of Zn metal occurs spontaneously during its deposition on graphene oxide by the reaction with oxygen supplied from the graphene oxide substrate. Consequently, graphene oxide is partially reduced by transfer of oxygen from its O-containing functional groups to the Zn metal. XPS and Raman analysis were conducted to determine the extent of reduction of graphene oxide. Figure 5b shows the XPS spectrum, where the C 1s spectra of the pristine graphene oxide, the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide, and graphene oxide after etching the ZnO monolayer are compared. Graphene oxide with etched ZnO was also analyzed to investigate whether it was possible to maintain graphene oxide in a partially reduced state without the ZnO layer on its surface. The C 1s spectra of the pristine graphene oxide show a main peak at 284.5 eV originating from sp²-hybridized C; the peak around 286.7 eV indicates the presence of oxygen on pristine graphene oxide, attributed to the C−O bond, as in C−O−C (epoxide) or C−OH (hydroxyl) groups.³¹ However, the XPS spectra of the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide show a decreased intensity for C−O binding. The XPS spectra of graphene oxide after etching ZnO are similar to the spectra of the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide. This result implies that the partial reduction of graphene oxide due to ZnO formation maintains its bonding configuration even after etching the newly formed ZnO. The peak ratios of C−C, C−O, and O−C═O in pristine graphene oxide to those in the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide based on the C 1s XPS spectrum are summarized in Figure 5c. The listed data clearly indicate the decreased intensity of peaks arising from oxygen bonded to carbon following the deposition of the Zn metal. This change in peak ratios is matched by a corresponding increase in oxygen bonded to Zn.³² Figure 5d shows the Raman spectra of pristine graphene oxide, the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide, and graphene oxide after etching of ZnO. Pristine graphene oxide shows intense D and G bands and a flat 2D region; the D band is more intense than the G band ($I_D/I_G = 1.16$). However, the $I_D/I_G$ of graphene oxide is lowered to 0.97 in the presence of a ZnO monolayer on its surface. Thus, both the G and D bands are modified following the partial reduction of graphene oxide due to a decrease in O-containing functional groups with sp³ carbons.³³ The partial reduction of graphene oxide is additionally confirmed by the TEM image and optical absorbance data of graphene oxide after etching the ZnO monolayer (Figure S7).

■ CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we present a unique approach to form a ZnO monolayer on graphene through a spontaneous redox reaction of Zn deposited on graphene oxide. Oxygen for the formation of ZnO is supplied by O-containing functional groups present on graphene oxide. ARTEM enabled the direct observation of ZnO monolayer self-formation on graphene oxide at an atomic scale, and its graphene-like structure and the edge configuration were further investigated by image simulation. Furthermore, we demonstrated the in situ growth of a ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide under electron beam irradiation, resulting in

layer-by-layer growth of the ZnO monolayer. DFT calculations explain that the migration of oxygen from graphene oxide to the Zn surface occurred without any energy barrier. Moreover, our experimental measurement shows that the ZnO monolayer on graphene oxide has a wide band gap energy of up to 4.0 eV, which is significantly larger than that of bulk ZnO. We attribute this band gap increase both to the quantum confinement effect and to the graphene-like structure of the ZnO monolayer. Notably, graphene oxide is partially reduced by the formation of ZnO. XPS and Raman analyses showed that graphene oxide is deoxygenated by transfer of oxygen from its O-containing functional groups during the oxidation of Zn. This study demonstrates the potential of a unique synthetic route to generate atomically thin 2D heterostructures formed from a 2D metal oxide and graphene, resulting from the spontaneous redox reaction of an ultrathin metal layer on graphene oxide.
