T.SAMESHIMA
Sony Research Center, 174 Fujitsuka-cho, Hodogaya-ku, Yokohama 240, Japan.

## Abstract
Phase transition between crystalline and amorphous states was studied through 30ns-pulsed XeCl laser induced melting of silicon films. Crystallization occurs through interface controlled growth for laser energy above surface melting threshold. Grain size is smaller than 100nm because of short melt duration (<80ns). Amorphization is observed in silicon films thinner than 40nm when the silicon films are completely melted then solidified homogeneously. The amorphized films have a large defect density ($\sim10^{20}cm^{-3}eV^{-1}$), which is remarkably reduced by hydrogen plasma treatment for 1 minute. This paper also discusses the application to fabrication of thin film transistors with a high carrier mobility (>100cm²/Vs) at a low temperature of 250⁰C.

## Introduction
Pulsed laser-induced melt followed by regrowth is a powerful technique for surface modification of semiconductor such as crystallization activation of ion implanted species and amorphization. Many researchers have been working to clarify these phenomena[1-8]. We have reported that pulsed laser-induced melt followed by regrowth is also useful for crystallization of thin silicon film formed on a glass substrate. This method made fabrication of polycrystalline thin film transistors (poly-Si TFTs) at temperature below 300⁰C possible[9,10]. Moreover, we recently reported thin poly-Si films fabricated on quartz substrates can be amorphized by irradiation using a XeCl excimer laser with a long pulse of 30 ns [11-15].

This paper discusses the characteristics and mechanism of pulsed laser-induced crystallization and amorphization of silicon films, and also electrical and optical characteristics of these films. It also discussses fabrication of poly-Si TFTs and amorphous silicon(a-Si) TFTs using laser crystallization and laser amorphization.

## Pulsed laser-induced melt-regrowth of silicon films
Irradiation with a XeCl excimer laser with a 30ns pulse (full width at half maximum) provides rapid heating of hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si:H) formed on glass substrates. Undoped a-Si:H films with thickness from 6 to 36nm deposited on quartz substrates at 250 ⁰C using radio frequency glow discharge (rf-GD) were irradiated with XeCl excimer laser pulses at room temperature in a vacuum chamber. The laser energy density was increased from 100 mJ/cm² in steps of 10 mJ/cm² in order to release hydrogen atoms without explosive eruption. The pulsed laser-induced melting of silicon films can result in the formation of crystalline and amorphous states following solidification. Figure 1 shows threshold energies for crystallization and amorphization as a function of film thickness. The crystallization threshold energy was almost independent of film thickness between 10 and 36nm. Silicon films are heated to the melting point of a-Si:H during irradiation and crystallization initiates[10]. Figure 2 shows electrical conductance change in a 30nm-thick film during and after laser-induced melting with increasing laser energy. The conductance change is associated with change of melt depth. The similarity in shape of the conductance curves indicates that the melt-regrowth occurred in the same way at energy density between 160 and 240mJ/cm². The solidification velocity is obtained by the down-slope of the conductance. It was about 0.6m/s.

On the other hand the amorphization threshold energy is much larger than the crystallization threshold energy, as can be seen in Fig.1. The amorphization threshold increases as film thickness increases. Figure 3 shows the conductance change in a 20nm-thick 2%-phosphorus doped-laser crystallized silicon films during and after irradiation with 2 pulses. The surface was melted by irradiation with a low energy of 240mJ/cm². The conductance of the film increased rapidly upon initiation of the melt. It rapidly decreased 70 ns after the initiation of melt. The decrease of the conductance was completed within 5ns (=time resolution). The conductance in molten silicon changed gradually near the maximum conductance. These results show that the silicon film was melted completely by the irradiation and then the molten silicon

After the termination of melt, the electrical conductivity decreased to $1 \mathrm{x} 10^{-2}$ $\mathrm{S} / \mathrm{cm}$, while the original polycrystalline film had a high electrical conductivity of $1.5 \mathrm{x} 10^{3} \mathrm{~S} / \mathrm{cm}$. These results show that the silicon film was partially melted by the irradiation with an energy lower than the amorphization threshold and then the molten silicon was slowly solidified to the crystalline state.

### Electrical and optical properties of crystallized and amorphized films
a-Si:H films are melted and crystallized for laser energy between crystalline threshold and amorphous threshold. Grain size is increased as laser energy increases. Transmission electron micrograph (TEM) measurements revealed that the grain size is distributed between 10 and 60nm as shown in the bright field TEM photograph of Fig.4. The small grains resulted from the short regrowth time as can be seen in Fig.2. The grains had random orientation and were roughly round. After crystallization, the film had no hydrogen atoms although the a-Si:H film has a hydrogen concentration of about 10 atomic percent as shown by infrared transmission spectra. All of hydrogen atoms were released during laser induced melting. Figure 5 shows the temperature dependence of electrical conductivity for 20 nm-thick undoped and 2%-P-doped silicon films. The conductivity activation energy of the undoped crystallized film and the recrystallized film increased by 0.45 eV as the temperature increased from 180 to 320 K, as can be seen by curves "a" and "c", respectively. On the other hand, the P-doped crystallized film had a large conductivity of 1300S/cm, which did not change with temperature between 100 and 350 K, as shown by curves "d" and "f" in Fig.5 because phosphorus atoms are activated through laser induced melting and degenerated band structure were formed.

On the other hand, silicon films are amorphized by laser irradiation above the amorphization threshold energy. The conductivity for undoped and P-doped amorphized films changed with temperature, but the change was less than for the undoped crystallized films. The conductivity was insensitive to phosphorus doping, as shown by curves "b" (undoped) and "e" (P-doped). Using the a variable range hopping conduction model, the density of localized states is estimated as $5.3 \times 10^{19} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} \mathrm{eV}^{-1}$ for the undoped amorphized silicon and $1.06 \times 10^{20} \mathrm{~cm}^{-}$ $^{3} \mathrm{eV}^{-1}$ for the P-doped amorphous silicon. Amorphization and crystallization are reversible phenomena. They are governed by laser energy density. The conductivity was measured during the reversible transition from crystalline to amorphous and back to crystalline as shown in Fig.5.

The electrical and optical properties of amorphized silicon films strongly depend on film thickness. Figure 6 shows the electrical conductivity of amorphized and crystallized films as a function of film thickness. The electrical conductivity of amorphized films was higher than $1 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~S} / \mathrm{cm}$ for films thinner than 18 nm. On the other hand, the electrical conductivity was reduced to $1 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~S} / \mathrm{cm}$ as the film thickness increased to 36 nm. The electrical conductivity for the 36 nm-thick film is close to that of crystallized films, which was independent of the thickness. Raman scattering spectra for films thinner than 18 nm showed a TO-phonon peak around $500 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and a tail on the lower-wavenumber side, which are assigned to the amorphous mode[16] as shown in Fig.7. On the other hand, the Raman spectrum of the 24 nm- thick film showed an additional TO-phonon peak around $513 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, which is assigned to the crystalline mode[16] as well as the amorphous peak. Furthermore, the sharp TO-phonon peak was dominant in the spectrum of the 36 nm-thick film. The spectrum displayed almost the same behaviour as TO-phonon spectra of polycrystalline films. Measurements of electrical conductivity and Raman scattering spectra clearly showed that films thinner than 18nm were completely amorphized. On the other hand, amorphous and crystalline states coexisted in the 24 nm-thick films. The crystalline state was dominant in the 36 nm-thick film.

The laser amorphized films have a large amount of dangling bonds because there is no hydrogen atoms in the films. The dangling bonds can cause localized defect states in the band gap. The logarithm of electrical conductivity for laser amorphized films is nearly proportional to $(1/temperature)^{1/4}$ for temperature. This means that the electrical conductivity is caused by variable-range hopping. Using the variable-range hopping conduction model[17,18], the density of localized states at the Fermi level for 20nm-thick amorphized films was estimated as about $1x10^{20}cm^{-3}ev^{-1}$. The electrical conductivity of laser amorphized films can be changed by hydrogen plasma treatment. The dark conductivity in 12nm-thick film was remarkably reduced from $2x10^{-3}S/cm$ (initial) to $2x10^{-9}S/cm$ by the hydrogen plasma treatment with 30W for only 1min., as shown in Fig.8. It was close to that of a 12nm-thick a-Si:H film fabricated by rf-GD. On the other hand the photoconductivity in 12nm-thick amorphized film was increased to $1.5x10^{-5}S/cm$ (AM1, $50mW/cm^{2}$) by the hydrogenation. It was much higher than $3x10^{-6}S/cm$ of the 12nm-thick a-Si:H. Figure 9 shows the change in the optical absorption coefficient of the 12nm-thick laser amorphized film with duration of the hydrogenation. The optical absorption coefficient was large over the range of photon energy between 1.4 and 2.2 eV. It was reduced by the plasma hydrogen treatment, especially in the low photon energy region. However, It hardly changed for hydrogenation longer than 30sec. After the hydrogenation for 1 minute, the optical absorption coefficient of the laser amorphized films was still larger than that of a 12nm-thick a-Si:H film fabricated by rf-GD at $250^{o}C$, as can be seen in Fig.9. Fig.10 shows the relation between the optical band gap and the width of the Urbach tail. The optical band gap was obtained by a Tauc plot[19] of the absorption coefficient and the width of the Urbach tail was obtained from the exponential slope of the absorption coefficient spectra in the low energy region. The optical band gap increased from 1.3eV to 1.73eV as a result of the hydrogenation and the width of Urbach tail was reduced from 0.19eV to 0.06eV, which is comparable to the tail width of a-Si:H fabricated using rf-GD. Furthermore, the optical band gap of the laser amorphized film increases slowly as the width of Urbach tail reduces compared with a-Si:H, which was reported by Cody [20]. Optical and electrical data on hydrogenation of laser amorphized films shows that localized defect states in amorphized films are effectively reduced to a level as low as hydrogenated amorphous silicon films by hydrogen plasma treatment. The high photoconductivity ($1.5x10^{-5}S/cm$) of the 12nm-thick hydrogenated amorphized film results from the higher optical absorption caused by a lower optical band gap than that of a-Si:H. Hydrogen concentration in the films after the hydrogenation was less than 1 atomic percent, which was estimated by fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). Laser amorphization and the post hydrogenation could therefore be methods to fabricate amorphous silicon films and low defect density with a smaller amount of hydrogen atoms than ratio frequency glow discharge.

Mechanism of laser induced melting followed by solidification

Required laser energy, solidification velocity, and optical and electrical properties are different between laser crystallization and amorphization of silicon films. These results show that there are two ways of solidification from laser-induced liquid states. One is interface controlled growth, in which a liquid/solid interface is formed by laser irradiation with an energy lower than the amorphization threshold and the solidification velocity is governed by degree of heat diffusion into the substrate. The low heat diffusion coefficient of the glass substrate and the long pulse width (30ns) can reduce the heat dissipation ratio to a level of $10^{10}K/s$. The short melt duration caused by lower energy irradiation would keep the temperature gradient large enough to form a liquid /solid interface in the silicon film and cause the slow interface controlled growth with the solidification velocity of 0.6m/s, as shown in Fig.2. Low solidification velocity can lead a high energy state of liquid silicon to a lowest energy state of crystalline silicon. Optical and electrical measurements revealed that silicon films after solidification for laser energy densities lower than the amorphization threshold were composed exclusively of crystallites, as shown in Figs.4, 5, and 7. Grain growth is limited by the short melt duration (<80ns). The grain size in crystallized films is therefore smaller than 100nm and dendroid growth does not occur, as is often observed in solid-phase crystallization in a furnace[21].

Numerical analysis of the unidimensional heat flow equation[22,23] was conducted using the experimental results of Figs 1 and 3 to estimate the melt duration and the temperature gradient prior to solidification.

Figure 11 shows the calculated temperature gradient in liquid silicon at the Si/quartz interface as a function of melt duration with difference film thickness. The temperature gradient reduced as melt duration increased for each film thickness. The temperature gradient is about $1x10^5$ K/cm (dashed curve) at the critical melt duration for amorphization threshold energy for each film thickness. The longer melting for thicker film is therefore evidence that amorphization occurs through homogeneous solidification under a uniform temperature distribution. The result of Fig.11 suggests that a temperature gradient above $1x10^5$ K/cm can cause interface controlled growth of silicon and crystallization can be realized.

Measurements of electrical conductivity (Fig.6), and Raman scattering spectra (Fig.7) clearly showed that the laser amorphized films were completely amorphized for films thinner than 18 nm. The crystalline state was dominant in the 36 nm-thick film. The crystalline nucleation rate in the homogeneous nucleation was estimated using these data. If we assume that crystalline grains grow spherically, the minimum nucleation density is $(4\pi(d/2)^3/3)^{-1}$, where d is the film thickness, because the grain growth is limited by film thickness. If the 36nm-thick film is assumed to be completely crystallized, the minimum nucleation density is $4x10^{22}\ m^{-3}$. This value is one magnitude higher than the crystalline nucleation density which Stiffler recently reported in pulsed laser induced melt regrowth of 150 nm-thick crystalline silicon films formed on quartz substrates[24]. The large crystalline nucleation density is probably caused by the higher cooling rates than Stiffler's case. The transition period from molten silicon to the amorphous solid state was less than 5 ns (detection limit), and using this as an upper limit, the minimum crystalline nucleation rate was obtained as $8x10^{30}\ m^{-3}s^{-1}$. The amorphization of thin films ($\leq$18 nm) occurs through the fact that grain growth is reduced as the film thickness decreases. The reduction of grain growth would result from the limitation of grain growth by film thickness and the reduction of recalescence. The crystalline area would be rapidly reduced proportionally to (thickness)$^3$ as the film thickness decreases and amorphous state would dominate for thin films. Latent heat energy, which is released during solidification of liquid silicon, reheats newly solidified silicon and may cause partial melting. Recalescence can cause crystalline grain growth in solid phase or through liquid-solid interface growth. Thicker film is heated by recalescence to higher temperature because the latent heat energy of molten silicon per unit area increases proportionally as the film thickness increases.

If grain growth is suppressed during solidification, the amorphous state should be dominant even for thicker film. In contrast, if grain growth is enhanced, the crystalline state should be observed for thinner film. In order to control the degree of recalescence, amorphization of silicon films coated with an SiO$_2$ layer was studied. Formation of an SiO$_2$ layer on a silicon film allows additional heat diffusion into the SiO$_2$ layer and reduces recalescence at solidification. The electrical conductivity of laser amorphized silicon films increased from $2x10^{-5}$ to $3x10^{-4}$S/cm as the thickness of SiO$_2$ increased from 0 to 800nm, as shown in Fig.12. The Raman spectrum showed a broad TO-phonon peak for amorphized silicon films coated with 800 nm-thick SiO$_2$, and it also included a sharp TO-phonon peak of the crystalline mode, as shown in Fig.13. These results show that the 30 nm-thick silicon film capped with 800nm-thick SiO2 film was completely amorphized. The degree of heat diffusion is doubled and recalescence is reduced to one half for the sample with 800 nm-thick SiO$_2$ film because SiO$_2$ is thicker than the heat diffusion length prior to the solidification, which was determined by the fact of that the threshold energy for amorphization ($320mJ/cm^2$) did not chang with thickness of SiO$_2$ above 600 nm. It should be noted that the cooling rate in silicon films coated with the SiO$_2$ layer prior to solidification is the same as that for non-coated sample if the solidification temperature is not changed. Heat energy symmetrically diffuses into the SiO$_2$ layer and quartz substrate, and the temperature gradient in the SiO$_2$ layer and quartz substrate are the same as that for Si/quartz. The same temperature gradient gives rise to the

same cooling rate according to one dimensional heat flow theory [22]. The $SiO_2$ layer therefore reduces only recalescence. The results in Figs. 12 and 13 show that the recalescence caused grain growth in the 30nm-thick silicon film and the amorphous state was maintained by reducing the recalescence effect by increasing heat dissipation using $SiO_2$ layers. The recalescence effect was reduced to one-half by forming the 800 nm-thick $SiO_2$ layer. That is the same degree of that in the uncoated 15 nm-thick silicon film. The electrical conductivity of the 30nm-thick film coated with an 800 nm-thick $SiO_2$ layer was however lower than that of the 15 nm-thick amorphized film, as shown in Figs. 6 and 12. This result shows that reduction of the recalescence effect is not sufficient in itself to form an amorphous solid state and that the limitation of grain growth due to film thickness is also an important process for amorphizing silicon films.

On the other hand, the crystalline state was observed in 15nm-thick laser amorphized film in the $Si(15nm)/SiO_2(20nm)/Si(15nm)/quartz$ structure. A 15 nm-thick a-Si:H layer was deposited on a quartz substrate using rf-GD. It was crystallized by laser irradiation in vacuum. A 20 nm-thick $SiO_2$ layer followed by a 15 nm-thick a-Si:H layer were subsequently deposited on the crystallized film using rf-GD. The top silicon film was also crystallized by laser irradiation in vacuum. Finally laser irradiation was provided from the rear side of the sample in order to amorphize the silicon layers. For the poly-Si/$SiO_2$/poly-Si/quartz sample, only the bottom silicon layer was amorphized at an energy density of $185\ mJ/cm^2$. The top and bottom silicon layers were amorphized at energy densities above $200\ mJ/cm^2$. On the other hand, the silicon layer was amorphized at an energy density above $180\ mJ/cm^2$ for the $SiO_2$/poly-Si/quartz. The electrical conductivity of the amorphized layer for the $Si(15nm)/$ $SiO_2(20nm)/Si(15nm)/quartz$ sample was $5x10^{-5}S/cm$, which was lower than that for the $SiO_2(20nm)/Si(15nm)/quartz$ sample, as shown in Fig.14. The $E_2$ peak, which is caused by the transition at point "X" in the Brillouin zone of crystalline silicon [19], was also observed in the optical reflectivity of the silicon films in the layered structure, as shown in Fig.15. Latent heat energy is released during solidification from the top and bottom silicon layers when they are melted completely. Both layers can be heated by the recalescence, via heat exchange through the thin $SiO_2$ layer. The degree of recalescence is therefore double, which is the same degree as that for the 30 nm-thick silicon film. The reduction of the electrical conductivity and the observation of $E_2$ peak in the optical reflectivity show that recalescence can cause crystalline grain growth even in thin amorphized silicon films.

Rapid solidification occurs for laser energy above the amorphization threshold as shown in Fig.3. On the other hand, gradual solidification was observed for laser energy below the amorphization threshold. In homogeneous solidification, the degree of heat dissipation has an important role in the reduction of recalescence, which can cause grain growth during solidification in order to realize complete amorphization. Crystalline grain can grow when recalescence is large ( as in the case of thicker film formed on quartz substrate).

## Application of crystallized and amorphized films to thin film transistors

Laser crystallization is a powerful technique in order to fabricate poly-Si TFTs in the low temperature process because crystallization is successfully achieved without heating the whole substrate. A 20nm-thick phosphorus doped hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si:H,P(2%)) film was first deposited on the glass substrate at $250^oC$ using rf-GD. After removing the a-Si:H,P film in the channel region, a 20nm-thick undoped a-Si:H film was deposited using rf- GD. The silicon layers were crystallized by laser irradiation with an energy density of $200mJ/cm^2$. Phosphorus atoms in the patterned a-Si:H,P layer were incorporated into the top silicon layer during the laser crystallization so that a region with a resistivity lower than $10^3\Omega/sq$ was formed. After the irradiation, poly-Si islands were formed. The silicon layer was hydrogenated using hydrogen plasma with 30W for 5min. at $250^oC$ in order to reduce defect state. The hydrogen plasma treatment is effective to improve the electrical characteristics in poly-Si films as well as amorphized films. Although the poly-Si film hardly had no photoconductivity just after the crystallization, the photoconductivity was increased by a brief of 30sec hydrogenation step. In order to investigate the possibility of applying laser amorphized films to electron devices, a-Si TFTs with a bottom gate were fabricated. A 20nm-thick silicon film was used as the channel region. A 80nm-thick 2%-phosphorus doped silicon film was used to form the source and drain regions. After irradiation with an energy density below 240mJ/cm² in order to remove hydrogen atoms in the films and perform crystallization, irradiation with an energy of 250mJ/cm² was provided in order to amorphize the silicon film in the channel region. The phosphorus doped film was thick enough to prevent amorphization occuring, so the source and drain regions have a low resistivity below 10³Ω/sq. ## Conclusion
Crystallization and Amorphization were studied through pulsed laser-induced melting followed by solidification of silicon films formed on quartz substrates induced by irradiation with a 30 ns-XeCl excimer laser. Grain growth increased through interface controlled growth with a velocity of 0.6m/s as the laser energy density increased from melting threshold up to amorphization threshold. On the other hand, amorphization occurs thorough homogeneous solidification with a temperature gradient lower than 1x10⁵K/cm caused by longer melting with higher laser energy. The transition from amorphous to crystalline was reversible and governed by laser energy density. Crystalline states were not observed in amorphized silicon films thinner than 20nm. On the other hand coexistence of crystalline and amorphous states was observed in 24 nm-thick silicon films. These results demonstrate that film thickness is an important parameter for amorphizing silicon films. The crystalline state was dominant in the 36nm-thick film. This result gave the crystalline nucleation density of 4x10²²m⁻³ under homogeneous solidification. Amorphization of the 30nm-thick film coated with the 800nm-thick SiO₂ film provided a complete amorphous state. On the other hand, crystalline state was observed in the 15nm-thick amorphized film coated with 15nm-thick Si/20nm-thick SiO₂. These results show that recalescence has an important role to play in laser amorphization and it can cause grain growth in the newly solidified amorphous silicon if films are thick (>20nm) enough to enlarge recalescence. A 12nm-thck laser amorphized silicon film has a large amount of localized defect states on the order of 10²⁰cm⁻³eV⁻¹. The defect state was reduced to a level as low as that of a-Si:H by hydrogen plasma treatment for only 1 minute. As result of the hydrogenation, the electrical conductivity was reduced from 2x10⁻³ to 2x10⁻⁹S/cm, the optical band gap was increased from 1.3 to 1.73eV and the width of Urbach tail was reduced from 0.19 to 0.06eV.
