# Study of the Wurtzite-Type Binary Compounds. II. Macroscopic Theory of the Distortion and Polarization*

F. KEFFER AND A. M. PORTIS†
University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania

(Received June 7, 1957)

The slight departure of wurtzite-type binary compounds from ideal $c/a$ ratio and ideal sublattice displacement parameter $u$ is accounted for by the forces arising from partial polar binding. These long-range forces are calculated in terms of a postulated charge $\pm fe$ per ion and certain lattice sums which we evaluate. When balanced against the elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric constants of the crystal, the forces account for both the direction and the order of magnitude of the distortion. The compounds AlN, BeO, and ZnO are considered in detail. An experimental value of 8.5 is given for the dielectric constant of AlN, but in the absence of precise values for elastic and piezoelectric constants only an order of magnitude check of the theory can be made at this time. Some remarks are appended on the general problem of finite lattice sums in complex lattices which have dipole and/or quadrupole moments in the unit cell.

## I. INTRODUCTION
$I^{N}$ an earlier paper under the same general title, $^{1}$ Jeffrey, Parry, and Mozzi have shown by x-ray analysis that the wurtzite-type compounds AlN and BeO are slightly distorted from a regular tetrahedral distribution of bonds. In this paper we shall attempt to correlate the observations of JPM with a theory of wurtzite structures which assumes partially ionic and partially covalent binding.

Although our principle goal is an understanding of wurtzite structures, we make a number of observations in the appendix which are applicable to complex ionic structures in general. We discuss in particular the effects of the presence, as in wurtzites, of dipole and quadrupole moments arising from the ion distribution in the unit cell.

The ideal wurtzite structure, shown in Fig. 1, is based on two hexagonal-close-packed (hcp) sublattices displaced from each other by a distance $(3/8)^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{0}$ where $a_{0}$ is the nearest neighbor distance for the hcp sublattice. This displacement is the distance from atoms 3 to 4 shown in the figure. The over-all structure is expected to have a $c/a$ ratio equal to that for the hcp lattice, $c_{0}/a_{0}$$=(8/3)^{\frac{1}{2}}=1.633$. Each atom on a given sublattice will then be at the center of a tetrahedron formed by the atoms of the other sublattice. In terms of a parameter $u$, such that $uc$ is the relative displacement of the two sublattices, we expect $u=u_{0}=0.375$.

It has been known for many years that the actual $c/a$ ratios of wurtzite structures range between 1.59 and 1.635. Table I, which is taken from a recent compilation by JPM, lists the known values of $c/a$ for the wurtzite-type binary compounds. We see from the table that, generally speaking, the compounds with greatest electronegativity difference have the largest departure from ideal $c/a$.

can give rise to net ionic-like charges around each atom. We shall assume an ionic charge centered at each atom of $\pm fe$ where $e$ is the magnitude of the electronic charge, and we shall explore the effect of this partial ionic binding on the lattice structure.

According to Table I the compounds of most nearly ideal $c/a$ also exist in the zinc blende structure. This structure is similar to wurtzite except that the two interpenetrating sublattices are face-centered cubic instead of hcp. No distortion of the zinc blende structure is possible without destruction of the cubic symmetry. The nearest neighbor (tetrahedral bond) arrangements in zinc blende and in ideal wurtzite are identical, the two structures differing only in the positions of distant neighbors. The Madelung constants for the two structures, in units $c_{0}=1$, are²: zinc blende, 4.368; wurtzite, 4.375. Thus the ionic binding of the ideal wurtzite structure is slightly the greater of the two, and as we shall see, the ionic binding of the distorted wurtzite structure is even stronger. From our ionic assumption, then, we can conclude that the compounds of largest ionic binding can gain sufficient energy by distortion of $c/a$ that they cannot profitably exist in the cubic zinc-blende structure.

A further kind of distortion is possible in the hexagonal structure and forbidden in the cubic. The two sublattices may be slightly displaced with respect to one another so that the $u$ parameter is no longer the ideal 0.375. That is, the wurtzite structures have the requisite crystal symmetry for pyroelectricity. Recently Jeffrey and his co-workers¹,³ have measured the $u$ parameters in AlN and BeO, obtaining 0.385 and 0.378 respectively. Such a displacement, if ionic charges are present, will give rise to a dipole moment. As we shall see, this effect also lowers the polar energy.

Part of the shift in the $u$ parameter, however, may simply be due to nearest neighbor bond bending or bond stretching caused by the change in $c/a$. We may place limits on this part of the shift. First, if the bond stretching constants are much larger than the bond bending constants, the central atom will move to a position in which it is equidistant from the atoms on the distorted tetrahedron, and hence
$$u=(1 / 4)+(1 / 3)(a / c)^{2}. \quad (1)$$

On the other hand, if the bond bending constants are larger, the central atom maintains tetrahedral angles with all four neighbors and
$$u=(1 / 2)-(1 / 24)^{\frac{1}{2}}(a / c). \quad (2)$$

For AlN, with $c/a=1.600$, the two extremes given by (1) and (2) are $u=0.380$ and 0.372, respectively. The measured value of 0.385 suggests that other than nearest neighbor bond energies may come into play. For BeO, with $c/a=1.623$, the two extremes are $u=0.377$ and 0.374, and the measured value is 0.378.

We shall assume that the introduction of ionic charges $\pm fe$ causes both the changes in $c/a$ and in $u$. To determine if the magnitude and direction of the changes can be thus accounted for, we balance the effect of this ionic charge against the macroscopic crystal compliance coefficients, namely elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric constants. We take account of electronic as well as ionic polarization, but we neglect any direct effect of charge transference which might accompany the distortion. That is, we assume the ionic charge to be identical in the ideal and distorted lattices. Some indirect effects of charge transferrence are of course contained in the piezoelectric constants.

## II. EFFECTS OF POLAR ENERGY ON LATTICE DISTORTION

We shall be interested in the strains $\epsilon_{1} \equiv \epsilon_{x x}, \epsilon_{2} \equiv \epsilon_{y y}$, $\epsilon_{3} \equiv \epsilon_{z z}$, and the polarization $P_{3} \equiv P_{z}$. Corresponding to these will be the stresses $p_{1} \equiv p_{x x}, p_{2} \equiv p_{y y}, p_{3} \equiv p_{z z}$, and the electric field $E_{3} \equiv E_{z}$. For convenience we introduce the general labels $\epsilon_{4} \equiv P_{3}$ and $p_{4} \equiv E_{3}$. That part of the compliance matrix relevant to our discussion will be of the form (hexagonal symmetry):
$$\begin{array}{ccccc} 
& p_{1} & p_{2} & p_{3} & p_{4} \equiv E_{3} \\
\epsilon_{1} & s_{11} & s_{12} & s_{13} & d_{13} \\
\epsilon_{2} & s_{12} & s_{11} & s_{13} & d_{13} \\
\epsilon_{3} & s_{13} & s_{13} & s_{33} & d_{33} \\
\epsilon_{4} \equiv P_{3} & d_{13} & d_{13} & d_{33} & \eta_{33}.
\end{array}\qquad(3)$$

Here the $s_{i j}$ are elastic compliance constants, the $d_{i 3}$ are piezoelectric strain constants, and $\eta_{33}$ is the (axial) dielectric susceptibility. We use the general notation $C_{i j}$ for an element of this matrix.

In elastic theory it is customary to expand the crystal energy density $U(\epsilon_{i})$ in a Taylor series about the equilibrium crystal configuration. However, since the actual wurtzite compounds are but slightly distorted from an ideal lattice, we find it convenient to center our Taylor expansion about the ideal wurtzite configuration, and to measure all $\epsilon_{i}$ from zero values in the ideal configuration. Thus,
$$\begin{aligned}
U\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)=U_{0}+ & \sum_{i}\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial \epsilon_{i}}\right)_{0} \epsilon_{i} \\
& +(1 / 2) \sum_{i, j}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} U}{\partial \epsilon_{i} \partial \epsilon_{j}}\right)_{0} \epsilon_{i} \epsilon_{j}+\cdots, \quad(4)
\end{aligned}$$

where the partial derivatives are to be taken with all other $\epsilon_{k}$ held constant and to be evaluated in the ideal lattice.

The central assumption of this paper is that, due to the presence of an effective charge $\pm f e$ on each ion, the crystal is strained and polarized away from the ideal wurtzite structure. Thus in the absence of the polar energy, $U_{P}$, the crystal would be in equilibrium in the ideal structure and the first derivatives in (4) would vanish. Therefore,
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial \epsilon_{i}}\right)_{0}=\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{i}}\right)_{0}. \quad(5)
$$

The values of the $\epsilon_{i}$ in the actual lattice may be determined to second order from the set of equations
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial \epsilon_{i}}\right)=0=\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{i}}\right)_{0}+\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} U}{\partial \epsilon_{i} \partial \epsilon_{j}}\right)_{0} \epsilon_{j}. \quad(6)
$$

These equations have solutions
$$
\epsilon_{i}=-\left|U_{i j}\right|^{-1} \sum_{j}\left(\partial U_{P} / \partial \epsilon_{j}\right)_{0} M_{i j}, \quad(7)
$$
where $|U_{i j}|$ is the determinant of the strain matrix $U_{i j}=(\partial^{2} U / \partial \epsilon_{i} \partial \epsilon_{j})_{0}$ and $M_{i j}$ is the minor of $U_{i j}$. We have
$$
M_{i j}\left|U_{i j}\right|^{-1}=\left(\partial^{2} U / \partial p_{i} \partial p_{j}\right)_{0}=C_{i j}, \quad(8)
$$
where $C_{i j}$ is an element of the compliance matrix, (3) above, evaluated in the ideal lattice. Changes in this matrix in going from the ideal to the real lattice will be of higher order in the strains and polarization, and therefore we shall use experimentally determined values of $C_{i j}$ in what follows.

From (7) and (8) we obtain
$$
\epsilon_{i}=-\sum_{j} C_{i j}\left(\partial U_{P} / \partial \epsilon_{j}\right)_{0}. \quad(9)
$$

This set of equations has a very simple physical inter- pretation. The polar forces, as represented by the partial derivatives of the polar energy, act as effective stresses and fields to distort the lattice away from the ideal wurtzite structure. The distortions may be related to the parameters measured by JPM by means of the following equations:
$$
\frac{c}{a}=\frac{c_{0}\left(1+\epsilon_{3}\right)}{a_{0}\left(1+\epsilon_{1}\right)} \approx \frac{c_{0}}{a_{0}}\left(1+\epsilon_{3}-\epsilon_{1}\right) ; \quad(10)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon_{4} \equiv P_{3} & =P_{I}+P_{E} \\
& =N f e c\left(u-u_{0}\right)+P_{E}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $P_{I}$ and $P_{E}$ are the ionic and electronic polarizations, $\pm f e$ is the assumed effective charge on each ion, and $N$ is the number of ions of one sign per unit volume.

Strictly speaking, all that can be said of the ideal lattice in which the partial derivatives are to be evaluated is that it has the ideal $c_{0} / a_{0}$ ratio and that $P_{3}=0$. We shall assume that both the ionic and electronic polarizations are zero, i.e., that the $u$ parameter is also ideal.

From (9), (10), and (11) we now obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(c / a)-\left(c_{0} / a_{0}\right)}{\left(c_{0} / a_{0}\right)}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(s_{3 j}-s_{1 j}\right) & \left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{j}}\right)_{0} \\
& -\left(d_{33}-d_{13}\right)\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial P_{3}}\right)_{0} ; \quad(12 \mathrm{a})
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
N f e c\left(u-u_{0}\right)+P_{E}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3} d_{j 3} & \left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{j}}\right)_{0} \\
& -\eta_{33}\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial P_{3}}\right)_{0}. \quad(12 \mathrm{~b})
\end{aligned}
$$

In a polar lattice with NaCl or zinc blende structure the symmetry is such that the terms in Eq. (12) can give rise only to a uniform compression. Such a compression may also take place in the wurtzite lattice; we shall assume it to be small and therefore we shall evaluate the partial derivatives in an ideal lattice of the same density as the real lattice. The important thing about the wurtzite lattice, as we shall show, is that its lack of cubic symmetry does indeed give rise to a distortion and polarization which, when calculated from Eq. (12), is in the observed direction and is of the observed order of magnitude.

The polar energy is given by
$$
U_{P}=U_{I}+U_{E}, \quad(13)
$$
where $U_{I}$ is the ionic or Madelung energy, and $U_{E}$ is the energy of the induced electronic polarization in the ionic field. We shall neglect any depolarizing effects from the crystal surface. This amounts to assuming either (A), a crystal shaped long and thin along the $z$ axis; (B), the presence of surface charges canceling the effects of surface polarization, or (C) the presence of domains. There seem to be indications that single crystals of AlN either satisfy (A) or tend to twin and presumably satisfy (C).

The ionic energy is
$$
U_{I}=N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right) \epsilon_{t^{\prime}} \sum_{t}^{\prime} \epsilon_{t} / r_{t t^{\prime}}. \quad(14)
$$

Here $c r_{t t^{\prime}}$ is the distance between the ions $t$ and $t^{\prime}$, and the signs of the ions are given by $\epsilon_{t}= \pm 1$. We use the usual notation of a prime on the summation sign to indicate omission of the term with vanishing de- nominator.

The lattice sum in Eq. (14) is to be taken over an infinite lattice and evaluated by the Ewald method. The justification for replacing all of our finite lattice sums by their corresponding infinite Ewald sums is given in Appendix II. In particular it is shown that the condi- tions to be met for such a replacement are (A), (B), or (C) assumed above.

We now proceed to evaluate the partial derivatives in Eq. (12) in terms of crystal lattice sums. Introducing $\zeta \equiv u-u_{0}$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\epsilon 2 \epsilon 3 \epsilon 4}= & \left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\epsilon 2 \epsilon 3 \zeta} \\
& +\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \zeta}\right)_{\epsilon 1 \epsilon 2 \epsilon 3}\left(\frac{\partial \zeta}{\partial \epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\epsilon 2 \epsilon 3 \epsilon 4}, \quad(15)
\end{aligned}
$$

with similar equations for $\epsilon_{2}$ and $\epsilon_{3}$. All derivatives are to be taken in the ideal lattice. The principal contribution to (15) will be the large rate of change of monopolar energy with crystal strain, and thus we may write

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\epsilon 2 \epsilon 3 \epsilon 4} & \approx\left(\frac{\partial U_{I}}{\partial \epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\epsilon 2 \epsilon 3 \zeta} \\
& =\sum_{l^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\partial U_{I}}{\partial r_{1, t t^{\prime}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial r_{1, t t^{\prime}}}{\partial \epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\epsilon 2 \epsilon 3 \zeta} \\
& =\sum_{l^{\prime}}\left[-N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right) \epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \epsilon_{l} r_{1, t t^{\prime}} / r_{t t^{\prime}}{ }^{3}\right]\left(r_{1, t t^{\prime}}\right) \\
& =-N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right) \alpha^{11}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Equation (16) and similar equations in $\epsilon_{2}$ and $\epsilon_{3}$ define $\alpha^{i i}$ which, as shown in Appendix II, may be evaluated as infinite Ewald sums. We note that

$$
\alpha^{11}+\alpha^{22}+\alpha^{33}=\alpha,
$$

the crystal Madelung constant. It is illuminating to think of $\alpha^{i i}$ as elements of a "Madelung tensor." In a lattice with cubic symmetry the three diagonal elements are equal, but in general only the relation (17) will hold, and differences among the $\alpha^{i i}$ will lead to electrostatic crystalline distortion. We shall see that part of the wurtzite distortion comes from this sort of anisotropy.

The remaining partial derivative in Eq. (12) is given by

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial \epsilon_{4}}\right)_{0} \equiv\left(\frac{\partial U_{P}}{\partial P_{3}}\right)_{0}=-E_{I},
$$

where

$$
E_{I}=-\left(f e / c^{2}\right) \sum_{l}^{\prime} \epsilon_{l} r_{3, l l^{\prime}} / r_{l l^{\prime}}{ }^{3},
$$

is the ionic field present at the lattice points in the idea structure. Equation (18) may be obtained most simply by an examination of Eq. (4). The energy term linear in $P_{3}$, i.e., in the polarization away from the ideal lattice, must contain only that field present in the absence of $P_{3}$. The field (19) is not zero because of the lack of axial reflection symmetry in wurtzite. The field has the same sign and magnitude at all lattice points under the assumptions (A), (B), or (C) above; and, as shown in Appendix II, we may evaluate (19) as an infinite Ewald sum.

On inserting (16) and (18) into (12) and invoking hexagonal symmetry, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(c / a)-\left(c_{0} / a_{0}\right)}{\left(c_{0} / a_{0}\right)}= & N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right)\left[\left(2 s_{13}-s_{11}-s_{12}\right) \alpha^{11}\right. \\
& \left.+\left(s_{33}-s_{13}\right) \alpha^{33}\right]+\left(d_{33}-d_{13}\right) E_{I} ; \quad(20 \mathrm{a})
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
N f e c\left(u-u_{0}\right)+P_{E}= & N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right) \\
& \times\left(2 d_{13} \alpha^{11}+d_{33} \alpha^{33}\right)+\eta_{33} E_{I} . \quad(20 \mathrm{~b})
\end{aligned}
$$

The lattice sums $\alpha^{11}, \alpha^{33}$, and $E_{I}$ are given in Appendix I, Eq. (40). We insert these values into (20) and split (20b) into ionic and electronic portions, obtaining

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(c / a)-\left(c_{0} / a_{0}\right)}{\left(c_{0} / a_{0}\right)}= & -N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right)\left[1.428\left(2 s_{13}-s_{11}-s_{12}\right)\right. \\
& \left.+1.518\left(s_{33}-s_{13}\right)\right] \\
& +0.104\left(f e / c^{2}\right)\left(d_{33}-d_{13}\right) ; \quad(21 \mathrm{a})
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
u-u_{0}= & -\left(f e / c^{2}\right)\left[2.856\left(d_{13}\right)_{I}+1.518\left(d_{33}\right)_{I}\right] \\
& +\left(0.104 / N c^{3}\right)\left(\eta_{33}\right)_{I}, \quad(21 \mathrm{~b})
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{E}=N\left(f^{2} e^{2} / c\right) & {\left[2.856\left(d_{13}\right)_{E}+1.518\left(d_{33}\right)_{E}\right] } \\
& +0.104\left(f e / c^{2}\right)\left(\eta_{33}\right)_{E} . \quad(21 \mathrm{c})
\end{aligned}
$$

Here the subscripts $E$ and $I$ refer to electronic and ionic parts of the piezoelectric constants and dielectric susceptibility. This split-up is not rigorously correct, since ionic and electronic effects are intermixed; we shall however neglect such complications.

Since no direct measurements of $P_{E}$ have been obtained, we must neglect Eq. (21c) and confine our attention to (21a) and (21b). They relate the $c / a$ and $u$ parameters measured by JPM to the elements of the compliance matrix. The ionic part of the susceptibility may be obtained from

$$
\left(\eta_{33}\right)_{I}=\eta_{33}-\left(\eta_{33}\right)_{E},
$$

where the electronic susceptibility, in accordance with standard theory, is given by

$$
\left(\eta_{33}\right)_{E}=\left(n^{2}-1\right) / 4 \pi \text {. }
$$

Here $n$ is the optical index of refraction in the $z$ direction (extraordinary index). The ionic part of the piezoelectric constants can at present only be guessed. The remaining quantity not readily measurable is the assumed charge per ion, $f e$. Since we have two equations involving this unknown, however, it should be possible to make a quantitative check of the theory.

Unfortunately, very few of the elements of the compliance matrix for wurtzite structures have been measured. Thus at present we can only test our theory in a very rough way. Before proceeding to such a test for $\mathrm{AlN}$ and $\mathrm{BeO}$, we wish to report a measurement of $\eta$ of AlN.

### III. MEASUREMENT OF THE DIELECTRIC CONSTANT OF AIN

The dielectric constant measurements were made on a disk of sintered polycrystalline AlN about 1.5 inches in diameter and 0.1 inch thick. Electrodes were pressed closely onto the disks, with a thin layer of apiezon grease being used to attach the electrodes. The capacity of this assembly was then measured over the audio range using a General Radio 650-A Impedance Bridge. It was found necessary to make a correction for the packing fraction.⁴ The sample density was found to be 0.748±0.002 of the x-ray density. On making in addition the usual edge correction, we determined the dielectric constant of AlN as κ=8.5, and hence the dielectric susceptibility η=0.60. We do not expect an appreciable variation of η with orientation; our theory actually neglects part of the variation since we assume that the elements of the compliance matrix are unchanged by the distortion. (The remainder of the variation may be inherent in the ideal wurtzite structure.) Thus we shall use the value of η in place of η₃₃. For consistency we shall also use the average value of the index of refraction.

### IV. ROUGH CHECK OF THE THEORY FOR AIN, BeO, AND ZnO

In Table II we list the known quantities, for AlN, BeO, and ZnO, which are involved in Eqs. (21a) and (21b). The last two rows of the table are, respectively, the second term on the right side and the left side of (21b). It is seen that for AlN and BeO the direct effect of the ionic field accounts for roughly two-thirds of the sublattice displacement, the remainder presumably coming from the indirect effect of the c/a distortion combined with the piezoelectricity, that is, from the first term on the right side of (21b). (We assume, as seems most probable, that the Von Hippel value of η in BeO is the more accurate of the two quoted figures.) We should be surprised, however, if our macroscopic theory were to be of great accuracy, and we are satisfied to obtain correct orders of magnitude. Also it is to be recalled that we have neglected any direct consideration of the effect of charge transference.

It is seen that, with f∼1, values of the piezoelectric constants ∼10⁻⁸ statcoulombs/dyne can account for the remainder of the change in u parameter. These values are reasonable, being about the same order of magnitude as in quartz. We assume the electronic portion of the piezoelectric constants to be of the same or smaller order of magnitude as the ionic portion.

On inserting values of ∼10⁻⁸ for d₃₃ and d₁₃ into Eq. (21a), we see that the second term on the right is ∼10⁻⁴ and hence incapable of explaining more than about one percent of the change in c/a. Thus, as seems reasonable, the anisotropy of the Coulomb forces [first term on the right of (21a)] is the primary cause of lattice contrac- tion, and the accompanying piezoelectric effect (second term) plays only a minor role.

We may now approximate Eq. (21a) by
$$A \approx B f^{2}\left[1.428\left(2 s_{13}-s_{11}-s_{12}\right)+1.518\left(s_{33}-s_{13}\right)\right] ; \quad(24)$$
here, for AlN and BeO the values of A are 0.02 and 0.006, respectively, and the values of B are 1.94×10⁻¹¹ and 3.82×10⁻¹¹.

In the absence of values of the elastic constants we can make only extremely rough guesses beyond this point. It is probable that s₁₂ and s₁₃ are small compared to s₁₁ and s₃₃. ## APPENDIX I. EVALUATION OF INFINITE LATTICE SUMS

The two types of infinite sums we shall be concerned with are
$$\alpha^{j i}=\epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \sum_{l}{ }^{\prime} \epsilon_{l} r_{j, l l^{\prime}}{ }^{2} / r_{l l^{\prime}}{ }^{3},\qquad(25)$$
and
$$E_{I}=-\left(f e / c^{2}\right) \sum_{l}{ }^{\prime} \epsilon_{l} r_{3, l l^{\prime}} / r_{l l^{\prime}}{ }^{3}.\qquad(26)$$

These infinite sums are independent of the position of the lattice point $l'$, since the symmetry in wurtzite is such that, to within a rotation of the lattice about its axial direction, all atoms see the same environment. It should be noted, however, that many complex lattice structures lack this symmetry.

The sums, if taken over a finite lattice, will be position dependent. In Appendix II we discuss this dependence.

The lattice sums are slowly convergent and are most readily evaluated by a technique, originally developed by Ewald,⁷ which uses generalized theta functions and their transformation formula. Essentially this method splits the sums into two parts, one over the direct lattice and one over the reciprocal lattice. The near-in structure of the reciprocal lattice reflects the distant structure of the direct lattice, and hence when split this way the lattice sums converge with extraordinary rapidity.

As applied to a very general class of lattice sums, the Ewald method has been expounded most elegantly by Born and his students.⁸⁻¹⁰ These authors consider first the infinite-lattice sum
$$S_{n}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{l}{ }^{\prime} \epsilon_{l} r_{l}{ }^{-n} \exp \left(-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{l}\right) ;\qquad(27)$$
the formulas for the lattice sums of interest to us may be obtained from the formula for the sum (27) by taking various derivatives with respect to $k_{j}$ and letting $k \to 0$.

In particular
$$\alpha^{j i}=-\epsilon_{l} \lim _{k \rightarrow 0}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial k_{j}{ }^{2}} S_{3}(\mathbf{k})\right] ;\qquad(28)$$

$$E_{I}=-\left(f e / c^{2}\right) \lim _{k \rightarrow 0}\left[i \frac{\partial}{\partial k_{3}} S_{3}(\mathbf{k})\right].\qquad(29)$$

We measure all distances in multiples of the lattice parameter $c$, and we place the lattice point $l'$ at the origin.

The formula for the sum (27) is given by Eq. (4.7) of Born and Bradburn⁹—restricted, however, to a simple Bravais lattice of ions of a single sign. The modification of their result to a lattice with a number of different sign ions per unit cell is not difficult. Let the vector to the $l$th lattice point be given by
$$\mathbf{r}_{l}=\mathbf{r}_{l}+\mathbf{s}_{m},\qquad(30)$$
where $r_{l}$ is the vector to a corner of the $l$th unit cell and $s_{m}$ is the vector from that corner to the $m$th ion in the unit cell. Let $\mathbf{b}_{l}$ be the vector to the $l$th lattice point of the reciprocal lattice, and define a structure factor
$$f_{l}=\sum_{m} \epsilon_{m} \exp \left(-2 \pi i \mathbf{b}_{l} \cdot \mathbf{s}_{m}\right),\qquad(31)$$
where the sum is over all the ions of the unit cell of the direct lattice. Then the Born and Bradburn formula becomes
$$S_{n}(\mathbf{k})=\left[(\pi \tau)^{\frac{1}{2} n} / \Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2} n\right)\right]\left[S_{A}+S_{B}-(2 / n)\right], \quad(32)$$
with
$$S_{A}=\sum_{l}{ }^{\prime} \epsilon_{l}\left[\exp \left(-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{l}\right) \varphi_{\frac{1}{2} n-1}\left(\tau r_{l}{ }^{2} \pi\right)\right],$$

$$S_{B}=v_{b} \tau^{-\frac{1}{2}} \sum_{l} f_{l} \varphi_{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2} n}\left[\left(2 \pi \mathbf{b}_{l}-\mathbf{k}\right)^{2} / 4 \pi \tau\right].$$

In this expression $S_{A}$ is summed over the direct lattice, $S_{B}$ is summed over the reciprocal lattice. The latter has volume $v_{b}$ per unit cell. The sum $S_{n}(\mathbf{k})$ is independent of the choice of Ewald's parameter $\tau$, the idea being to choose this parameter for equally rapid convergence in the sums over the direct and reciprocal lattice. The $\varphi$ functions (introduced by Misra⁸) are defined by
$$\varphi_{m}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty} \beta^{m} e^{-\beta x} d \beta.\qquad(33)$$

These functions satisfy the recurrence formula
$$\varphi_{m}(x)=\varphi_{0}(x)+(m / x) \varphi_{m-1}(x) ;\qquad(34)$$
and, in particular,
$$\begin{aligned}
\varphi_{0}(x) & =e^{-x} / x ; \\
\varphi_{-1}(x) & =-E i(-x) ; \\
\varphi_{-\frac{1}{2}}(x) & =(\pi / x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[1-\Phi\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right].
\end{aligned}\qquad(35)$$

Here $E i(-x)$ is the exponential integral, and $\Phi(x)$ is Gauss' error function. Furthermore, we have the relation
$$(\partial / \partial x) \varphi_{m}(x)=-\varphi_{m+1}(x),\qquad(36)$$

## APPENDIX II. EVALUATION OF FINITE LATTICE SUMS

We consider first the sum $\alpha^{j j}$. The finite lattice sum
$$
\alpha^{j j(V)}\left(\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}\right)=\epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \sum_{l}{ }^{\prime V} \epsilon_{l}\left(r_{j, l}-r_{j, l^{\prime}}\right)^{2} /\left|\mathbf{r}_{l}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}\right|^{3}, \quad(41)
$$
unlike the infinite sum, is dependent upon the position in the sample $\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}$ from which the sum is evaluated. We may obtain the finite sum by subtracting the sum over lattice points outside the sample from the infinite sum,
$$
\sum^{V}=\sum^{\infty}-\sum^{\infty-V}\qquad(42)
$$
and converting the sum $\sum^{\infty-V}$ into an integral, an approximation valid for large $V$ and for points $\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}$ not within the first few atomic layers of the surface. For convenience of integration we choose the sample shape as spherical; the application to arbitrary ellipsoidal shapes will become obvious.

To preserve electrical neutrality we take the finite sum over an integral number of unit cells; the effect of surface charges will be considered later. Let all position vectors be defined as in Eq. (30). Then the finite sum becomes
$$
\alpha^{j j(V)}\left(\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}+\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right)=\alpha^{j j}-I_{3}{ }^{j j(\infty-V)}\left(\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}+\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right),\qquad(43)
$$
with
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{3}{ }^{j j(\infty-V)}=2 N c^{3} \epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \sum_{r=R}^{\infty} d \mathbf{r} \nu^{-1} \sum_{m} \epsilon_{m} & \\
& \times \frac{\left(r_{j}+s_{j, m}-r_{j, l^{\prime}}-s_{j, m^{\prime}}\right)^{2}}{\left|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right|^{3}}, \quad(44)
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\nu$ is the number of ions per unit cell. Convergence of the integral is assured by charge neutrality, that is, by the relation (39). The integral, however, is exceedingly difficult to evaluate without interchange of summation and integration, which interchange is impossible since the separate integrals are divergent. We may get around this problem by the following artifice, closely related to our method of obtaining the infinite sums:
$$
I_{3}{ }^{j j(\infty-V)}=\lim _{k \rightarrow 0} \epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \nu^{-1} \sum_{m} \epsilon_{m} I_{\mathbf{k}},\qquad(45)
$$
where
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\mathbf{k}}=2 N c^{3} \int_{r=R}^{\infty} d \mathbf{r} & \frac{\left(r_{j}+s_{j, m}-r_{j, l^{\prime}}-s_{j, m^{\prime}}\right)^{2}}{\left|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right|^{3}} \\
& × \exp \left[-i \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\right]. \quad(46)
\end{aligned}
$$

The interchange of summation and integration is permissible in (45) since $I_{\mathbf{k}}$ converges for all nonzero values of $\mathbf{k}.^{11}$ The evaluation of the integral $I_{\mathbf{k}}$, as well as that of an integral which will be needed for the sum $E_{I}$, is facilitated by the introduction of two integrals:
$$
J_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(2 N c^{3}\right) \int_{r=R}^{\infty} d \mathbf{r} \frac{\exp \left(-i_{\mathbf{k}} \cdot \mathbf{r}\right)}{\left|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right|} ;\qquad(47)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(2 N c^{3}\right) \int_{r=R}^{\infty} d \mathbf{r} & \frac{\left(r_{j}+s_{j, m}-r_{j, l^{\prime}}-s_{j, m^{\prime}}\right)}{\left|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right|^{3}} \\
& × \exp \left[-i \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\right]. \quad(48)
\end{aligned}
$$

We note that
$$
K_{\mathbf{k}}=\exp \left[-i \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{s}_{m}-\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\right]\left(\partial / \partial r_{j, l^{\prime}}\right) J_{\mathbf{k}} \quad(49)
$$
and
$$I_{\mathbf{k}}=i\left(\partial / \partial k_{j}\right) K_{\mathbf{k}},$$
hence all integrals may be obtained from $J_{\mathbf{k}}$. We let $\theta$ be the angle between $\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}+\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m}$ and obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
J_{\mathbf{k}}=4 \pi\left(2 N c^{3}\right) \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{n} & P_{n}(\cos \theta)\left|\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}+\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m}\right|^{n} \\
& × k^{n-2}\left[j_{n-1}(k R) /(k R)^{n-1}\right]. \quad(50)
\end{aligned}
$$

11 A similar artifice may be used for the evaluation of finite-lattice dipolar sums; see M. H. Cohen and F. Keffer, Phys. Rev. 99, 1128 (1955).

As already mentioned, it is essential that the integral be evaluated for nonzero $\mathbf{k}$, that is, for $kr\to\infty$ as $r\to\infty$; this means that the upper limit of the integral makes no contribution. This is a correct procedure since it corre- sponds precisely to the way the infinite lattice sums have been treated at infinity, and hence the subtraction in Eq. (43) is valid. Physically this amounts to allowing no contribution, either to the infinite sum or the integral, from a surface at infinity.

The integrals $K_{\mathbf{k}}$ and $I_{\mathbf{k}}$, as power expansions in $\mathbf{k}$, are readily obtained from $J_{\mathbf{k}}$. Using the charge neutrality condition, Eq. (39), and taking the limit $k\to0$, we find
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{3}{ }^{j j(\infty-V)}=-4 \pi\left(2 N c^{3} / \nu\right) \epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \epsilon_{m} & \left\{(2 / 15)\left(r_{j, l^{\prime}}+s_{j, m^{\prime}}-s_{j, m}\right)^{2}\right. \\
& \left.-(1 / 10)\left|\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}+\mathbf{s}_{m^{\prime}}-\mathbf{s}_{m}\right|^{2}. \quad(51)\right.
\end{aligned}
$$

If we now define dipole and quadrupole moments per unit cell by the relations
$$
\mathfrak{v}=c \sum_{m} \epsilon_{m} \mathbf{s}_{m}, \quad(52 \mathrm{a})
$$
$$
q_{j k}=c^{2} \sum_{m} \epsilon_{m} s_{j, m} s_{k, m}, \quad(52 \mathrm{~b})
$$
we note that (51) contains both dipole and quadrupole terms. Because of charge neutrality, however, the quadrupole contribution disappears in a summation (or average) over the unit cell, and we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nu^{-1} \sum_{m^{\prime}} I_{3}{ }^{j j(\infty-V)} & \\
& =4 \pi\left(2 N c / \nu^{2}\right)\left\{(4 / 15)\left(\mu_{j}\right)^{2}-(1 / 5) \mu^{2}\right\}. \quad(53)
\end{aligned}
$$

The dipole contributions in (53) can be traced to the depolarizing fields from the sample surface. To see this, we consider the Madelung constant for a finite lattice,averaged over the unit cell:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle\alpha^{V}\right\rangle_{N} & =\alpha-\nu^{-1} \sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}} I_{3}{ }^{j j(\infty-V)}\right) \\
& =\alpha+(4 \pi / 3)\left(2 N c / \nu^{2}\right) \mu^{2}.
\end{aligned}\qquad(54)
$$

In terms of the polarization per unit volume, $P=2N\mu c/\nu$, it is seen that the finite lattice term is simply the depolarizing energy per ion for a sphere.

The argument has been carried through for a spherical sample. For arbitrary ellipsoidal shapes the factor $4\pi/3$ will go over into the appropriate combination of depolarizing factors.

If we invoke now any of the assumptions (A), (B), or (C) listed in following Eq. (11) in Sec. II, we have a sample without depolarizing fields. Hence the correct sum to use in the finite lattice will be the infinite Ewald sum.

Turning now to the finite sum $E_{I}$ we see that the integral to be subtracted from the infinite sum is given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
I^{(\infty-V)}=-\left(f e / c^{2}\right) & \lim _{k \to 0} \nu^{-1} \sum_{m} \epsilon_{m} K_{k} \\
=-(4 \pi / 3) & (2 f e N c / \nu) \\
& \quad × \sum_{m} \epsilon_{m}\left(r_{3, l^{\prime}}+s_{3, m^{\prime}}-s_{3, m}\right). \quad(55)
\end{aligned}
$$

It is seen that the finite sum contains, in addition to the infinite sum, the depolarizing field produced in a sphere by the dipole moment (52a). In the absence of de- polarizing fields this contribution will vanish, and the infinite Ewald sum will be correct.

We note that if (53) had not been averaged over a unit cell, and even if depolarizing fields were not present, the quadrupole contribution would remain
$$
\alpha^{V}\left(\mathbf{r}_{l^{\prime}}\right)=\alpha+(4 \pi / 3)\left(2 N c^{3} / \nu\right) \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_{l^{\prime}} \sum_{j}\left(q_{j j} / c^{2}\right). \quad(56)
$$

This quadrupole contribution, however, is of no conse- quence to the lattice energy, since it disappears on an average over the unit cell. That is, it has alternate signs at $\pm$ions. We note that this "erroneous" term in the Madelung constant is not picked up in the infinite Ewald sum; it came from our considerations of finite sums. The term will, however, be picked up in an infinite sum evaluated by the Evjen method. $^{12}$ Consider, for example, the CsCl lattice. If we take the unit cell to be a body-centered cube of length $2c$ with negative charge at the center and $\frac{1}{8}$ positive charge at each corner, we have
$$
\sum_{j}\left(q_{j j} / c^{2}\right)=3 ; \quad\left(2 N c^{3} / \nu\right)=\frac{1}{8},
$$
which leads to
$$
\alpha^{V}=\alpha \pm(\pi / 4).
$$

This peculiar effect has been noted by Krishnan and Roy, $^{13}$ who point out that the choice of a unit cell with electrical neutrality in all the faces will avoid the trouble. They ascribe the effect to double layers at infinity. We would prefer to think of it in terms of a net quadrupole moment in the unit cell. To avoid the trouble one can either (1) pick a unit cell without a quadrupole moment (but frequently, as in wurtzite, this is im- possible), (2) average the sum over the unit cell, or (3) abandon the Evjen method and perform an infinite Ewald calculation.
