# Electronic energy bands and optical properties of Cr and Fe by the KKR method

N C Debnath, M Ray Chowdhuri and S Chatterjee

Department of General Physics and X-rays, Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science, Jadavpur, Calcutta 700 032, India

Received 20 December 1984

Abstract. Electronic energy bands and optical absorption spectra have been calculated by the KKR method for the two BCC transition metals Cr and Fe. The method of Segall and Yang was used for efficient calculation of the structure constants and the muffin-tin form of the crystal potential has been used to calculate the logarithmic derivatives. From the band structure so obtained, the density of states and $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectra were calculated for both metals for photon energies up to 6 eV. The various structures found in the spectra were compared with the latest experimental results and good agreement was observed between the calculated and the experimental values. The structures in the absorption spectra have been successfully interpreted in terms of various band-to-band transitions inside the Brillouin zone.

## 1. Introduction

The study of the electronic energy bands of transition metals has been the focus of intensive research over the last two decades (Wood 1962, Loucks 1965, Callaway and Wang 1977, Rath and Callaway 1973) because of their technological importance. The elements of the third-row transition metals, specifically Ti, Cr, Fe, Ni and Cu, deserve special attention as they constitute the basic components in a large number of engineering materials. As a continuation of the sustained interest and efforts in this field new results, both experimental and theoretical, are being reported periodically about the electronic structures and other important physical properties that can be derived from them, namely the densities of states (DOS) and $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectra.

With the advent of photoemission spectroscopy (UPS and XPS) and automatic ellipsometry it has been possible to get more refined data on the band structure of solids, the energy distribution curve (EDC) and the optical absorption spectroscopy and thus these experiments provide a good test to verify the one-electron model of the solid.

The motivation for the present calculations of the electronic and optical absorption properties of Cr and Fe came from the desire to interpret the recent experimental data on those two metals. The UPS data on Cr reported by Sugawara *et al* (1984) and optical absorption data of Bos and Lynch (1970), Lenham and Treherne (1966) and the recent optical conductivity data of Nestell and Christy (1980) are quite interesting and relevant in this context. Although the photoemission data on iron oxides are quite numerous (McIntyre and Zetaruk 1977) the data on pure iron are relatively scarce. Of the few experimental data on Fe the UPS data of Pessa *et al* (1976), the optical absorption data of Mcbee and Kruger (1969) and the most recent experimental data of Chen *et al* (1979) are

worth mentioning. On the theoretical front a number of results on the electronic structure of Cr and Fe are reported in the literature of which special mention must be made of the works of Kulikov and Kulatov (1982), Callaway and Wang (1977) and Moruzzi et al (1978). Various types of methods and potentials were used in these works and more emphasis was put on the discussion of the DOS, the Fermi surface parameter and magnetic ordering in ferromagnetic Fe and antiferromagnetic Cr. However, the optical properties of these metals were not studied. Since the theoretical calculation of the optical absorption spectra depends on the band structure of the solid at a large number of points inside the BZ and in a relatively large energy range, new band-structure calculations of Cr and Fe in a larger energy range and using closer mesh points inside the BZ are necessary. But as the first-principles methods become very expensive a number of interpolation schemes (Hodges et al 1966, Muller 1967, Smith and Mattheiss 1974) were developed to obtain the energy values at intermediate points. Recently, however, a number of workers have shown that a first-principles calculation of the band structure of solids at a large number of points inside the BZ over a relatively wide range of energy is possible at least for the BCC and FCC metals through a computationally improved KKR method (Segall and Yang 1980) and recently applied by Ray et al (1982a, b, 1983) to calculate the electronic and optical properties of 4d transition metals. In the present work, we have used this improved version of the KKR method in conjunction with a muffin-tin form of the crystal potential to calculate the electronic band structure, the density of states (DOS) and the $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectra of BCC Cr and Fe and have made an attempt to interpret the experimental EDC and optical absorption spectra of these two metals.

## 2. Outline of the methods

The KKR method is quite well documented in the literature and here we just give the working formulae for the calculation. The secular determinant of the KKR (Kohn and Rostoker 1954) method from which the energy values are calculated is given by

$$
\operatorname{det}\left\|A_{l m, l^{\prime} m^{\prime}}^{k}(E)+\varepsilon \delta_{l l^{\prime}} \delta_{m m^{\prime}} \frac{n_{l} L_{l}-n_{l^{\prime}}}{j_{l} L_{l}-j_{l^{\prime}}}\right\|=0
\tag{1}
$$

where

$$
A_{l m, l^{\prime} m^{\prime}}^{k}(E)=4 \pi \sum C_{l m, l^{\prime} m^{\prime}}^{L M} D_{L M}(\boldsymbol{k}, E)
\tag{2}
$$

in which $C_{l m, l^{\prime} m^{\prime}}^{L M}$ are the Gaunt coefficients, $L_{l}$ are the logarithmic derivatives and $D_{L M}$ are structure constants defined by the expression

$$
D_{L M}(\boldsymbol{k}, E)=\frac{4 \pi}{\tau} \frac{1}{j_{l}\left(\varepsilon R_{i}\right)} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{j_{l}\left(k_{n} R_{l}\right) Y_{L M}\left(\hat{k}_{n}\right)}{E-k_{n}^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4 \pi} \delta_{L 0} \cot \left(\varepsilon R_{i}\right)
\tag{3}
$$

where $\varepsilon=\sqrt{E}$. For practical calculation $R$ is assumed to be very small, $j_{l}(\varepsilon R)(\varepsilon=\sqrt{E})$ can be replaced by $|\varepsilon R|^{l}$ and hence equation (3) has a singularity not only at $E=\left|\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{K}_{n}\right|^{2}$ but also at $E=0$. Since the range of $E$ required in a band-structure calculation does not go beyond a small number of reciprocal lattices Segall and Yang (1980) proposed writing equation (3) as

$$
D_{L M}(\boldsymbol{k}, E)=\frac{4 \pi}{\tau} \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\frac{k_{n}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{L} \frac{Y_{L M}\left(\hat{k}_{n}\right)}{E-k_{n}^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-L} f_{L M}(\boldsymbol{k}, E)
\tag{4}
$$

where the sum $(N)$ is over those few $k_{n}$ leading to poles at the energy of interest. Equation (4) proved to be very convenient for fitting $f_{L M}$ in terms of polynomials as functions of both energy and $|k|$ on a particular axis. These polynomial coefficients were stored and subsequently utilised to determine the structure constants for a particular energy and at a particular point on an axis, which thus helped to find the eigenvalues from the secular determinant given by equation (1). The structure constants for BCC metals were calculated by the numerical techniques of Davis (1971) and the best results were obtained with $\eta=$ 0.25. The numbers of reciprocal and real wavevectors were 43 and 20 respectively and the squares of the largest reciprocal and direct vectors used were 6 and 27 respectively.

## 3. Results and discussion

The crystal potential has been constructed in the usual way (Papaconstantopoulos and Slaughter 1974). The total charge density $\rho(r)$ is found by superposing the atomic charge densities by the $\alpha$-expansion of Löwdin (1956). The atomic charge densities in turn were generated by using the Hartree-Fock-Slater programme of Herman and Skillman (1963). The Coulombic part of the potential was obtained by solving Poisson's equation and the exchange potential $V_{\mathrm{ex}}(r)$ was calculated from the expression

$$
V_{\mathrm{ex}}(r)=-6 \alpha\left(\frac{3}{8 \pi} \rho(r)\right)^{1 / 3}
$$

where $\alpha$ is a parameter ranging between 1 and $\frac{2}{3}$. Thus the total potential is

$$
V(r)=V_{\text {Coul }}(r)+V_{\text {ex }}(r) .
$$

In the present work, the atomic charge densities were calculated from the atomic configurations $\mathrm{Cr}\left(3 \mathrm{~d}^{5} 4 \mathrm{~s}^{1}\right)$ and $\mathrm{Fe}\left(3 \mathrm{~d}^{7} 4 \mathrm{~s}^{1}\right)$. The values of $\alpha$ were taken respectively as $\alpha_{\mathrm{Cr}}=0.725$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{Fe}}=1.0$. With these choices of potentials we have calculated the band structure, the DOS and the $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectrum, which are reported below.

### 3.1. Band structure, DOS and $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectrum of $\mathrm{Cr}$

The calculated band structure of paramagnetic $\mathrm{Cr}$ is shown in figure 1. The results are quite similar to those of Rath and Callaway (1973), Kulikov and Kulatov (1982) and Moruzzi et al (1978). The results are found to be quite sensitive to the Slater exchange parameter $\alpha$. There is no unique criterion governing the choice of $\alpha$ and hence each system should be treated individually. With this idea Yasui et al (1970) calculated the band structure for $\mathrm{V}$ and $\mathrm{Cr}$ for different choices of $\alpha$. They calculated the DOS of these elements at $E=E_{\mathrm{F}}$ and compared these with the values obtained from experimentally observed specific heat data and found that $\alpha=0.725$ agrees with the experiments better than all other choices of $\alpha$. We accepted this value for our $\mathrm{Cr}$ calculation. For $\mathrm{Fe}$, however, we ourselves studied with different values of $\alpha$ and found that the value $\alpha=1$ is most satisfactory for our calculation. Figure 2 shows the calculated DOS along with the EDC of Sugawara et al (1984) taken at the photon energy $\hbar \omega=35 \mathrm{eV}$. The experimental curve shows the broad valence band with a main peak located at $1.3 \mathrm{eV}(\mathrm{A})$, a shoulder at $3.4 \mathrm{eV}$ (B) and another peak around $6.0 \mathrm{eV}$ (C). The last peak is believed to be a satellite and is found to be strongly affected by the absorption of oxygen atoms on to the surface or the formation of oxidised layer (Sugawara et al 1984). ### 3.2. Band structure, DOS and $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectrum of $Fe$

The calculated band structure of paramagnetic Fe is shown in figure 5. The results are quite similar to the calculations of Moruzzi et al (1978). The value of the exchange parameter $\alpha$ was taken equal to 1.0. The calculated DOS curve is shown in figure 6 along with the EDC curve of Pessa et al (1976) obtained at $\hbar\omega=21.22$ eV. The EDC curve shows a prominent peak at 0.58 eV (A), a less pronounced maximum at $2.4\pm0.2$ eV and a broad maximum at 5.4 eV below $E_{F}$. Of these, the first two peaks A and B belong to the genuine iron band structure and the third peak might be a satellite structure originating from a double d-hole state and perhaps may be from the residual oxygen (Pessa et al 1976) which corresponds at most to a small fraction of an atomic monolayer and which has no effect on the iron band structure itself.

The calculated curve shows the main peak at the Fermi level and three other peaks are located around 1.63, 2.72 and 3.81 eV. It seems that the broad experimental band at B is a superposition of the peaks $B'$ and $C'$ whereas $D'$ has no experimental counterpart. This shift of the main peak by about 0.6 eV with respect to the experimental peaks seems to be a common feature in a number of band-structure calculations (Callaway and Wang 1977, Moruzzi et al 1978). However, the results of Callaway and Wang seem to explain the major peak (A) quite well, though the rest of the DOS curve is quite similar to our calculation.

## 4. Conclusions
In this paper we have presented the electronic structure, DOS and $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectra for Cr and Fe and the results explain quite satisfactorily the experimental EDC and optical absorption spectra. The agreement for Cr is quite good for both EDC and optical absorption spectra. For Fe, the calculation explains the broad features of the DOS and $\varepsilon_{2}$ spectra quite satisfactorily.
