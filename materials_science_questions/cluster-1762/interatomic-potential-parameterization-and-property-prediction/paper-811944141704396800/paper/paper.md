# Elastic Properties of Alpha Quartz and the Alkali Halides
Based on an Interatomic Force Model

Donald J. Weidner and Gene Simmons

Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02139

A two-body central-force atomic model can be used to describe accurately the elastic properties of $\alpha$ quartz if the nontetrahedral O:O forces are included. The strength of the Si:O interaction has little effect on the bulk modulus. The technique is sufficiently general to allow calculations of the elastic properties of a specified structure under arbitrary pressure from a complete description of the interatomic forces. The elastic constants for the NaCl structure and the CsCl structure are examined. Our model includes two-body, central, anion-anion, anion-cation, and electrostatic interactions. An anion-anion force term for the halogens is found as a function of separation from values of $c_{12}$ and $c_{44}$ for the B1 structure and $(c_{11}-c_{12})$ and $(c_{11}-c_{44})$ for the B2 structure. This anion-anion force term consistently describes these elastic constants for the alkali halides as the pressure, structure, and cation species are varied. Knowledge of this force term as a function of anion separation allows the calculation of these moduli as a function of pressure.

Equations of state can be used to relate high-pressure laboratory measurements to the inferred velocities of the earth's interior [Birch, 1952]. Most state equations are derived from an expansion of the strain energy [Birch, 1938, 1947] or the elastic properties [Murnaghan, 1951] as a power series of either strain or stress. The series is truncated after a few terms, and the coefficients of these terms are determined by fitting the resulting analytical expression to a finite set of observations (for a general review, see Knopoff [1963]). In general, such state equations should be confined to interpolation and not extrapolation, since the accuracy of the resulting expression can be evaluated only in terms of the fit of the expression to the data.

This restriction considerably limits the range of pressure for which the shear modulus can be described, since the shear modulus is virtually never measured above 10 kb. However, it may be possible to extend the range of prediction for the shear modulus by using an equation of state whose parameters are determined from several sources of observations. An interatomic force equation of state may be just such an expression. The shear modulus can be expressed in terms of the interatomic forces, which can be determined from the elastic properties of any particular material, as well as from the elastic properties of other materials that contain relevant pairs of atoms.

Most equations of state have a further limitation. Namely, they cannot predict, without measurement, the elastic properties of a given solid. Thus, the elastic properties of most high-pressure polymorphs are unknown. It may be possible to predict these properties with an interatomic equation of state. The elastic properties of a material depend only upon the atomic structure and the interatomic forces. The structure can be determined with X rays, and the interatomic forces can perhaps be determined from the elastic properties of other materials that contain relevant pairs of atoms.

To achieve these goals, two distinct steps are necessary. The first step is the calculation of the elastic properties of a solid from a description of the forces and atomic positions. The term 'forward problem' will be used for this step. The second step involves finding a description of the forces given the elastic properties and the crystal structure. This step will be called the 'inverse problem.' Both steps are discussed here.

The zero-pressure elastic properties of $\alpha$ quartz are investigated. This study serves as an example of the application of the forward problem to a complex atomic structure, and it

In general, we have refrained from assuming the actual form of the interatomic force. It allows us to see just how much information is contained in the elastic properties. After the complete analysis, a function can be fitted to the forces. However, if this fit is made before an analysis, we quickly lose sight of the actual control that the elastic constants are exerting on the forces.

### THE FORWARD PROBLEM

Several formalisms are available by which the forward problem can be solved. In one approach, all orders of elastic constants are expressed in terms of the interatomic forces [*Lincoln et al.*, 1966; *Ghate*, 1965]. This approach can be useful in comparing zero-pressure elastic constants and their pressure derivatives with theoretical values. However, *Birch* [1952] noted that this type of expansion converges slowly in pressure for the shear moduli.

Alternative approaches are those of long waves and of homogeneous deformation [*Born and Huang*, 1954]. *Liebfried and Ludwig* [1961] and *Wallace* [1965] extended Born and Huang's treatment of the method of homogeneous deformation to include the effects of an initial stress. *Wallace* [1965] showed that these two approaches are equivalent for an isotropic stress.

The formalism developed and used here differs slightly from the method of homogeneous deformation. Instead of equating the elastic constants with strain derivatives of the potential energy, stress has been equated with interatomic forces. While these approaches are equivalent, the interatomic force formalism has some conceptual advantages in terms of our goals. The interatomic forces are more directly related to the stress and elastic constants than is the crystal potential energy. Also, the notation developed here is more amenable to observing the effects of each interaction than is the notation of *Wallace* [1965].

Assumptions. Throughout this paper, two basic assumptions are imposed. One concerns the nature of the interatomic force; the other, the state of the lattice. All forces are considered to be two-body, central forces. This first assumption is reasonably accurate for the alkali halides. Although many-body, or noncentral, forces can be incorporated into the framework that will be developed, the form of these forces must be specified before inclusion, with the result that the actual form of these forces may be indeterminable. By ignoring these types of forces, we obtain effective two-body forces. As is exemplified by the alkali halides, these effective two-body forces are often sufficient to describe the elastic properties.

Under the second assumption, that of the state of the lattice, the atoms are taken to be situated at their respective lattice sites with no vibrations about these positions. (Temperature is not explicitly considered.) Though temperature is an important parameter in the earth, we are concerned here only with the effects of pressure.

Elastic constants. The basic approach here is to associate an applied stress with the interatomic forces that support the stress. Then, after imposing a specified strain on the crystal, the stress change is calculated in terms of properties of the interatomic forces. By requiring the system to meet different boundary conditions, different elastic constants are found.

The first step in constructing the model is to consider one unit cell of the crystal and all the surrounding atoms. The elastic constant of the unit cell is the same as the elastic constant of the crystal. A stress will then be given by the force density per unit area.

$$
\sigma_{\nu \eta}=-\sum_{\eta \text { face }} \frac{\mathbf{f}_{i j} \cdot \hat{e}_{\nu}}{a_{\eta}} \tag{1}
$$

where $\mathbf{f}_{i j}$ is the force vector between the $i$th and $j$th atoms; $\eta$ and $\nu$ are $x$, $y$, or $z$; $\hat{e}_{\nu}$ is the unit vector in the $\nu$ direction; and $a_{\eta}$ is the area being summed over. The area $a_{\eta}$ will normally be taken as one side of the unit cell. The remaining problem is to decide whether or not a particular $\mathbf{f}_{m n}$ will contribute to this sum. There are three requirements that must be fulfilled. First, the derived stress should be independent of the extent of area being summed over, as long as the dimensions of the area are multiples of the dimen-

sions of the unit cell. Second, all stress fields considered will be homogeneous. Thus the stress should be independent of the position of area being summed over. Third, all force interactions between atoms on opposite sides of an infinite area must be included in the sum of forces over this area. All these requirements are satisfied if we include in the sum all forces between atoms whose spatial-separation vector intersects the area.

Strain can be defined in terms of distortion of the unit cell, since the macroscopic strain is homogeneous. The strains take the form of displacements of the base vectors divided by the length of the base vectors. For orthogonal base vectors, we find
$$
\begin{aligned}
e_{(\alpha \beta)} & =d \alpha / \beta_{0}+d \beta / \alpha_{0} & & \alpha \neq \beta \\
& d \alpha / \alpha_{0} & & \alpha=\beta
\end{aligned} \quad(2)
$$
where zero subscript denotes the unit cell length in the $\beta$ direction, and $d \alpha$ represents the relative displacement in the $\alpha$ direction of the ends of the base vector, which is in the $\beta$ direction. The parentheses on the subscripts denote the use of the Voigt notation. If the base vectors are not orthogonal, they can be projected onto a Cartesian coordinate system.

We can define the strain state of the crystal once the displacement field is specified. In particular, if there is only one nonvanishing displacement corresponding to only one base vector, we obtain from equation 2
$$
\begin{aligned}
e_{(\alpha \beta)} & =d \alpha / \beta_{0} & & \\
e_{(m n)} & =0 & & (m n) \neq(\alpha \beta)
\end{aligned} \quad(3)
$$
Depending on whether $\beta$ and $\alpha$ are the same or not, the crystal is in a state of simple extension or simple shear [Love, 1944].

For the calculation of the elastic constants, we will allow only infinitesimal strains of a crystal that is in a state defined by a specified prestress. The infinitesimal strain must be supported by a small stress field denoted by $d \sigma_{(i j)}$. Hooke's law then reduces to an expression for an elastic constant given by
$$
c_{(i j)(\alpha \beta)}=\frac{d \sigma_{(i j)}}{e_{(\alpha \beta)}} \quad(4)
$$

Replacing the right-hand side with the expression for stress given in equation 1 and the definition of strain given in 3, after some algebra we find that
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{(\nu \eta)(\alpha \beta)} & =-\beta_{0} \sum_{\eta \text { face }}\left[\frac{1}{a_{\eta}} \frac{\nu_{i j}}{r_{i j}}\left(\frac{\partial f_{i j}}{\partial r_{i j}}-\frac{f_{i j}}{r_{i j}}\right)\right. \\
& \cdot\left(\frac{\partial r_{i j}}{\partial x_{i j}} \frac{d x_{i j}}{d \alpha}+\frac{\partial r_{i j}}{\partial y_{i j}} \frac{d y_{i j}}{d \alpha}+\frac{\partial r_{i j}}{\partial z_{i j}} \frac{d z_{i j}}{d \alpha}\right) \\
& \left.+\frac{f_{i j}}{r_{i j} a_{\eta}} \frac{d \nu_{i j}}{d \alpha}\right]-\frac{\beta_{0}}{a_{\eta}} \sigma_{(\nu \eta)} \frac{d a_{\eta}}{d \alpha}
\end{aligned}
$$
where $f_{i j}$ is the magnitude of the force and is positive if the force is repulsive and negative if it is attractive.

In solving the forward problem, we assume that $f_{i j}$ and $\partial f_{i j} / \partial r_{i j}$ are known and that such terms as $\partial r_{i j} / \partial x_{i j}$ can be determined from the lattice positions. The last term can be rewritten in terms of $d x / d \alpha$, $d y / d \alpha$, or $d z / d \alpha$, where the unsubscripted terms refer to the base vectors. The total set of unknowns in equation 5 is
$$
\left\{\frac{d x_{i j}}{d \alpha}, \frac{d y_{i j}}{d \alpha}, \frac{d z_{i j}}{d \alpha}\right\} \quad \text { for all } i, j \quad(i \neq j)
$$

$$
\frac{d x}{d \alpha}, \frac{d y}{d \alpha}, \frac{d z}{d \alpha}
$$

These unknowns can be determined with the use of boundary conditions and constitutive relations. The boundary conditions merely specify the applied strain.

There are three types of constitutive relations. They are derived from (1) symmetry of the lattice, (2) periodicity of the lattice, and (3) mechanical equilibrium of lattice points. The first two are used to define the independent unknowns. The last is a statement that the net force at each lattice point must vanish before and after deformation. Thus,
$$
\frac{d}{d \alpha} \sum_{i} f_{k i} \frac{x_{k i}}{r_{k i}}=0 \quad(6)
$$
at every atom $k$. Equation 6 becomes
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i}\left[\frac{x_{k i}}{r_{k i}}\left(\frac{\partial f_{k i}}{\partial r_{k i}}-\frac{f_{k i}}{r_{k i}}\right)\right. & \cdot\left(\frac{\partial r_{k i}}{\partial x_{k i}} \frac{d x_{k i}}{d \alpha}+\frac{\partial r_{k i}}{\partial y_{k i}} \frac{d y_{k i}}{d \alpha}\right. \\
& \left.\left.+\frac{\partial r_{k i}}{\partial z_{k i}} \frac{d z_{k i}}{d \alpha}\right)+\frac{f_{k i}}{r_{k i}} \frac{d x_{k i}}{d \alpha}\right]=0
\end{aligned}
$$

Two more relations of this form are found for all lattice positions by permutating $x, y$ and $z$. These relations are sufficient to determine all the unknowns for an arbitrary strain.

ELASTIC PROPERTIES OF $\alpha$ QUARTZ AND ALKALI HALIDES

The elastic constants $c_{i j}$ described here correspond to the $B_{i j}$ constants of *Wallace* [1967]. Equation 5 expresses the elastic constants in terms of the structure of the crystal and the interatomic forces. Any initial stress can be introduced by properly describing the resulting structure and forces in all equations. Under isotropic stress, *Wallace* [1967] shows that these elastic constants correspond to the elastic constants observed with both static and dynamic techniques.

## THE INVERSE PROBLEM

Cubic structures: General. For certain cubic structures, equation 5 reduces to a fairly simple form owing to the lattice symmetries. In particular, for cubic materials where every lattice point is a center of symmetry, we find

$$
K=\frac{2}{3} P-\frac{1}{3 a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} x_{i j} \frac{\partial f_{i j}}{\partial r_{i j}} \tag{8}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{11}=\frac{1}{a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} f_{i j} \frac{x_{i j}}{r_{i j}} & \left(\frac{x_{i j}{ }^{2}}{r_{i j}{ }^{2}}-1\right) \\
& -\frac{1}{a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} \frac{\partial f_{i j}}{\partial r_{i j}} \frac{x_{i j}{ }^{3}}{r_{i j}{ }^{2}} \tag{9}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{12}=P+\frac{1}{a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} f_{i j} & \frac{x_{i j} y_{i j}{ }^{2}}{r_{i j}{ }^{3}} \\
& -\frac{1}{a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} \frac{\partial f_{i j}}{\partial r_{i j}} \frac{x_{i j} y_{i j}{ }^{2}}{r_{i j}{ }^{2}} \tag{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{44}=-P+\frac{1}{a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} f_{i j} & \frac{x_{i j} y_{i j}{ }^{2}}{r_{i j}{ }^{3}} \\
& -\frac{1}{a_{x}} \sum_{x \text { face }} \frac{\partial f_{i j}}{\partial r_{i j}} \frac{x_{i j} y_{i j}{ }^{2}}{r_{i j}{ }^{2}} \tag{11}
\end{aligned}
$$

In all cases, $P$ represents hydrostatic pressure, and the forces and their derivatives are evaluated at $r=r(P)$. These elastic constants represent the response of the body to an additional infinitesimal stress. In all the above expressions, the elastic constant assumes the form

$$
c_{i j}=\alpha P+H(r) \tag{12}
$$

Hence

$$
\partial c_{i j} / \partial P=\alpha-\frac{r}{3 K} \frac{d H(r)}{d r} \tag{13}
$$

where $r$ represents a dimension of the unit cell. Thus, the knowledge of the pressure derivative of the elastic constants enhances the knowledge of the force function. On the other hand, independent determinations of the force laws allow a prediction of the pressure derivatives as well as the values of the elastic constants.

A final point to observe is the presence of the Cauchy relations in the above equations. Namely, at zero pressure, $c_{12}=c_{44}$. This equality depends only on the assumptions about the nature of the force and the symmetry of the structure. Any deviations from these assumptions (e.g., nonstatic lattice, many body forces, noncubic, each point not a center of symmetry) will destroy this equality.

The effect of pressure on the Cauchy relations can also be observed. This effect is given by

$$
\frac{\partial c_{12}}{\partial P}-\frac{\partial c_{44}}{\partial P}=2
$$

This form of the Cauchy relation can also be obtained from *Love* [1944]. Deviations from these Cauchy relations will reflect inadequacies of the basic assumptions.

Sodium chloride structure. The elastic properties of the alkali halides have been examined within the framework of this approach. Our reason for studying the alkali halides is threefold. First, there is an abundance of data on single-crystal elastic constants for materials of the same crystal structure [*Simmons and Wang*, 1971]. This fact is not true for the oxides. Of the oxides with the $B1$ structure, which is the predominant simple oxide structure, magnesium oxide is the only material for which a complete set of single-crystal elastic constants has been measured under pressure. On the other hand, the pressure dependence of the elastic constants for fifteen isostructural alkali halides has been measured. Secondly, the alkali halides constitute the simplest class of polyatomic materials. This simplicity results in large part from the success of the ionic approach, where each atom is assumed to be totally ionized. If an approach is to be useful in describing the elastic properties of oxides and silicates, it must be consistent in its description of the alkali halides. Thirdly, some of the alkali halides exhibit phase transitions ($B1$ to $B2$) at accessible pressures, whereas others are stable in the $B2$ structure at room conditions. We can examine the effect of structure on the interatomic forces, as well as look

In order to solve the forward problem for the sodium chloride (B1) and cesium chloride (B2) structure, we must formulate additional assumptions about the importance of the interactions between neighbors of varying distances. For the alkali halides, we assume that all neighbors contribute an electrostatic force to the interaction field of a given atom. In addition to this long-range force, the nearest neighbors and next nearest neighbors of the anion-anion type are assumed to contribute to the total force field. The justification for not considering cation-cation interactions follows from the observation that the radius of the anion is in most cases (all but RbF and CsF) larger than that of the cation. The inclusion of this term is important, even though it may be much smaller than the nearest neighbor term, since some of the elastic constants are independent of the nearest neighbor force.

With the above assumptions, the expressions for the elastic constants given in equations 8-11 reduce to the following for the sodium chloride structure:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K=\frac{2}{3} P+\frac{1}{3 r}\left\{f(r)+2 g\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]\right\} & \\
& +\alpha(K) / r^{4} \quad(14)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{11}=\frac{1}{r}\left\{f(r)+g\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]\right. & \\
& \left.-\frac{G\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]}{(2)^{1 / 2} r}\right\}+\alpha\left(c_{11}\right) / r^{4} \quad(15)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{12}=P+\frac{1}{2 r}\left\{g\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]\right. & \\
& \left.+\frac{G\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]}{(2)^{1 / 2} r}\right\}+\frac{\alpha\left(c_{44}\right)}{r^{4}} \quad(16)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{44}=-P+\frac{1}{2 r}\left\{g\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]\right. & \\
& \left.+\frac{G\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]}{(2)^{1 / 2} r}\right\}+\frac{\alpha\left(c_{44}\right)}{r^{4}} \quad(17)
\end{aligned}
$$

where

for anion-cation interactions
$$f(r)=-\left(\partial f_{i j} * / \partial r_{i j}\right)\left(r_{i j}\right)$$

for anion-anion interactions
$$g\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]=-\left(\partial f_{i j} * / \partial r_{i j}\right)\left(r_{i j}\right)$$

for anion-cation
$$F(r)=f_{i j} *\left(r_{i j}\right)$$

for anion-anion
$$G\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]=f_{i j} *\left(r_{i j}\right)$$
and $f_{i j} *$ is the nonelectrostatic part of the force between the stated pair of ions, and $r$ is the anion-cation separation. The effect of the ionic nature of the lattice is represented by $\alpha(K)$, $\alpha(c_{11})$, and $\alpha(c_{44})$. If the atoms are totally ionized, these will be constants resembling the Madelung constant. Their values are given in Table 1.

Although Anderson and Liebermann [1970] obtained similar expressions for these elastic constants for both the NaCl and the CsCl structures, their formulation was different. Specifically, they assumed the form of the nearest neighbor interaction and did not include the anion-anion interaction.

Equations 14-17 relate the measured elastic constants to the properties of the anion-anion and anion-cation forces, and thus constitute a solution to the inverse problem. The right-hand sides of these equations contain only three unknown terms, namely, $f(r)$, $g\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]$, and $G\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]$. However, only two of the equations are independent, because
$$c_{44}=c_{12}-2 P \quad K=\frac{1}{3}\left(c_{11}+2 c_{12}\right)$$

Furthermore, the number of unknowns has already been reduced by one with the relation
$$
\begin{aligned}
P=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left\{F(r)+(2)^{1 / 2} G\left[(2)^{1 / 2} r\right]\right\} & \\
& +\alpha(P) / r^{4} \quad(18)
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, the complete set of elastic constants for the materials considered will yield numbers corresponding to two independent combinations of these force terms.

A second and perhaps more powerful ap- proach exists for determining a combination of the anion-anion force and its derivative. The basis of this approach is derived from an obser- vation that the shear elastic constant $c_{44}$ is inde pendent of the anion-cation interaction. This fact, together with the neglect of the cation- cation interaction, allows us to evaluate the func- tion $(1 / 2 r)\{g[(2)^{1 / 2} r]+[G[(2)^{1 / 2} r] /(2)^{1 / 2}]\}$ as a function of $r$ by observing $c_{44}$ for several compounds with common anions. For example, LiCl, NaCl, KCl, and RbCl all exhibit the sodium chloride (or $B 1$ ) structure at room conditions. Furthermore, all these solids have different lattice cell dimensions (i.e., different $r$ 's). If we assume that the Cl-Cl interaction is independent of the cation type and that the over-all ionic nature of the solids is the same, we can determine the above-mentioned function of the Cl-Cl interaction for four different values of $r$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K=\frac{2}{3} P+\frac{1}{3 a}\left\{2 f\left[\frac{(3)^{1 / 2}}{2} a\right]+g(a)\right\} & \\
& +\alpha^{\prime}(K) / a^{4} \quad(19)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{11}=\frac{1}{a}\left\{\frac{2}{3} f\left[\frac{(3)^{1 / 2}}{2} a\right]+g(a)\right. & \\
& \left.-\frac{8}{3(3)^{1 / 2}} \frac{F\left[(3)^{1 / 2} a / 2\right]}{a}\right\}+\alpha^{\prime}\left(c_{11}\right) / a^{4} \quad(20)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{12}=P+\frac{1}{a}\left\{\frac{2}{3} f\left[\frac{(3)^{1 / 2}}{2} a\right]\right. & \\
& \left.+\frac{4}{3(3)^{1 / 2}} \frac{F\left[(3)^{1 / 2} a / 2\right]}{a}\right\}+\alpha^{\prime}\left(c_{44}\right) / a^{4} \quad(21)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{44}=-P+\frac{1}{a}\left\{\frac{2}{3} f\left[\frac{(3)^{1 / 2}}{2} a\right]\right. & \\
& \left.+\frac{4}{3(3)^{1 / 2}} \frac{F\left[(3)^{1 / 2} a / 2\right]}{a}\right\}+\alpha^{\prime}\left(c_{44}\right) / a^{4} \quad(22)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\left\{G(a)+\frac{4}{(3)^{1 / 2}} F\left[\frac{(3)^{1 / 2}}{2} a\right]\right\} & \\
& +\alpha^{\prime}(P) / a^{4} \quad(23)
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $a$ is the anion-anion separation, or the length of a unit cell. The $\alpha^{\prime}$ terms are given in Table 1. These expressions include the effect of the nearest anion-anion interactions.

Our general discussion of the NaCl structure applies to the CsCl structure. As with the form of $c_{44}$ discussed above, $c_{11}-c_{12}$ can be made to depend only on the anion-anion interaction if the proper pressure relation is added in favor of the anion-cation force.

Quartz. We now study the elastic properties of $\alpha$ quartz on the basis of various force models for the atomic interactions. By comparing the calculated values of the elastic constants with the measured ones, we obtain information about the interatomic forces.

Quartz is an especially interesting material to investigate with our technique for several reasons. First, it serves as a study of the feasibility of this approach for complicated structures. The unit cell of quartz contains six O and three Si atoms. The structure has one threefold axis and three twofold axes. Hydrostatic pressure does not affect this symmetry. However, the atoms in the unit cell can move with respect to each other in any manner as long as this symmetry is maintained. Quartz is a difficult subject for most atomic approaches and an interesting test of feasibility.

Second, a study of quartz is valuable geophysically. Compositionally $\text{SiO}_2$ is an important constituent in the material expected throughout the earth, excluding only the core. Information gained about the nature of the interatomic forces present in quartz is important for all other silicates.

Third, quartz appears to be anomalous with respect to all 'universal' equations of state. The elastic properties of quartz are generally lower than the values predicted by the various empirical relations [Simmons and England, 1969]. An understanding of the factors governing the properties of quartz may give insight into the factors governing these universal equations of state.

Fourth, quartz has six independent elastic constants that are well documented. Presumably, six constants contain more information than two as in the cubic case (with Cauchy relation satisfied).

In addition to the two basic assumptions discussed earlier we have constrained the forces more. We assume that there are no long-range forces like the electrostatic force. There are two reasons for this assumption. Because the degree of ionization is unknown, it is not clear how large the long-range forces should be. In addition, the electrostatic effects would be difficult to study. Since the relative motion of the atoms is constrained only by symmetry, varying degrees of ionicity will affect the relative motions. The ionic effect is not purely a constant that can be added to the effect of near neighbors, but rather it must appear in the constitutive relations. If the forces and their derivatives of the nontetrahedral oxygen interactions are set to zero, the bulk modulus becomes zero. This observation, along with Figures 9 and 10, indicates that these far neighbors are important in controlling the elastic properties of quartz. It is perhaps from this observation that we can understand the 'anomalous' behavior of quartz with respect to the universal equation of state. Namely, the elastic properties of quartz, to a large extent, depend on the interaction of these separated oxygens. The interaction forces and derivatives are small. This smallness is reflected in the elastic properties. As we have seen by the form of the equations for cubic materials, the bulk modulus is very sensitive to the 'strength' of the nearest neighbor interaction. We speculate that this sensitivity exists to a great extent in most silicates. Where this interaction is strong, the bulk modulus will be dominated by the cation-anion interaction. However, if the $\text{Si}:\text{O}$ interaction is strong in quartz, the bulk modulus still responds to the separated oxygens. We conclude that the universal equations of state are governed by the 'systematics' of the anion-cation interaction as suggested by *Anderson and Anderson* [1970].

The next step in the analysis of quartz is the prediction of the pressure derivatives. Such an analysis is accomplished by observing the relative motion of the atoms under hydrostatic pressure. Then the positions of the atoms are altered to correspond to the positions at, say, 1 kb. After adjusting the force terms to correspond to this new arrangement, the elastic properties can then be recalculated. The pressure derivatives will be given in terms of the change of the elastic constants. The pressure derivatives of the elastic constants of quartz will depend on both the change in the force terms and the change in atomic positions.

Saksena's [1944] model included Si:O and O:O interaction for oxygen bond lengths of 2.6 A. He also included angular or noncentral forces of the form Si:O:Si and O:Si:O. All interatomic forces were assumed to vanish. Saksena, however, did not correctly calculate the elastic constants. He required the displace- ment of every atom to be uniform.

Elcombe's [1967] model consisted of nearest Si:O and nearest O:O interactions. She allowed arbitrary interatomic forces, as well as 'spring' constants. She calculated the properties for two models, one of which also allowed an arbitrary electrostatic charge. The main calculations con- cerned finding the normal modes and matching these with the spectral analysis. The elastic con- stants were found from the slope of the disper- sion curves at low frequencies.

Elcombe did not require the forces on an atom to vanish. After altering the forces so that they do balance, we recalculated the elastic con- stants, and the bulk modulus did vanish as implied by the previous discussion for this type of model. The total forces do not contribute to the bulk modulus if mechanical equilibrium is assumed.

The force model of Julian and Lane [1968] was produced by Kleinman and Spitzer [1962]. It consisted of O:Si interactions and noncentral forces of the type O:Si:O and Si:O:Si. The bonds were considered to be force-free. The method of homogeneous deformation was used to find the elastic constants. A serious defect in their model is the failure to consider the far- distant O:O interactions which are important in controlling the elastic constants. We observe that the bulk modulus calculated from the elastic constants of Julian and Lane is zero!

The force model of Shiro [1968] is superior to the previous models. All tetrahedral forces, including noncentral forces, were considered. Also 'intramolecular' forces were included. This model included more parameters than our model where we tried to see whether the elastic prop- erties could be explained in terms of only two- body, central forces. It is difficult to interpret the 'intramolecular tension' of Shiro in terms of our next nearest O:O terms.

## CONCLUSIONS
The elastic properties of many materials can be described with a two-body, central force model. In particular, the elastic constants $c_{12}$ and $c_{44}$ ($c_{11}-c_{12}$ and $c_{11}-c_{44}$ for the $B2$ structure) of the alkali halides can be described by including anion-anion interactions with the usual model of anion-cation and electrostatic forces. The resultant model consistently describes these elastic constants as we vary pressure, structure, and cation species. Our conclusion is that we have obtained the anion-anion force term as a function of separation.

Once the force terms have been determined, several applications can be made. First, these elastic constants can be calculated as a function of pressure. To do this we need only the unit cell dimension as a function of pressure. The region of validity should no longer be limited by the region where the elastic constant is measured but by the region where the force terms are evaluated. As an example of this application, we have calculated $c_{44}$ for NaCl corresponding to the chlorine-chlorine separation of LiCl. In order to achieve this volume for NaCl ($V/V_{0}=0.752$), the pressure must be 140 kb [Drickamer et al., 1966]. Then, according to equation 17, $c_{44}(\text{NaCl}, 140\ \text{kb})=c_{44}(\text{LiCl}, 0\ \text{kb})-140\ \text{kb}$, which is 0.109 Mb. This value is slightly smaller than the zero-pressure value. The general philosophy of this approach closely resembles that of Anderson's [1966] law of corresponding states.

Secondly, we can use the force terms in the prediction of the elastic properties of other materials containing the same ions. We need only know the ion separation.

Finally, these force terms may be valuable in understanding solid-solid phase transitions. Many authors [Anderson, 1970; Thomsen, 1971; Anderson and Liebermann, 1970; Anderson and Demarest, 1971] have discussed the relationship of the negative pressure derivative of the shear modulus to phase transitions. Here, we observed that the negative pressure derivative of $c_{44}$ is a natural consequence of weak or attractive anion-anion forces. Perhaps we should not relate the shear modulus to the phase transition but rather relate the nature of the anion-anion force to the phase transition. While the nearest neighbor coordination changes from 6 to 8 for the $B1$ to $B2$ transition, the anion-anion coordination

changes from 12 to 6. Thus the bonding energy changes in the anion-anion interactions may be significant as compared with the anion-cation energy change.

α quartz was studied by means of the forward problem approach. A set of force terms was found that predicts values of the elastic con- stants which agree with the observations. The study of quartz serves several purposes. First, it demonstrates that one can solve the forward problem for a complicated structure even if several sets of interactions are considered. Sec- ond, it illustrates the type of information about the forces that can be extracted from the elastic properties. We found that nontetrahedral inter- actions greatly influence the elastic properties. Thirdly, it provides us with information that may be useful in describing the elastic proper- ties of other silicates.

An important point of this paper is that we need not always assume a functional form of the force. The pressure derivatives $c_{12}$ and $c_{44}$ for the alkali halides were described without assuming the halide-halide force law. We ob- served that the halide-halide force actually be- comes attractive for large separations. If we had assigned a functional form to this inter- action, we may not have allowed for this be- havior in the form of the force law assumed. We also gained more latitude in describing the oxygen force law in quartz by not assuming a priori the form of the force.
