# DISCUSSION OF SOME MECHANISMS OF $F$-CENTRE FORMATION IN ALKALI HALIDES

J. H. O. VARLEY

Metallurgy Division, A.E.R.E., Harwell, Berkshire

(Received 20 October 1961; revised 25 January 1962)

Abstract-Some mechanisms of the formation of $F$-centres in alkali halide crystals by X-irradiation are discussed with particular reference to the multiple ionisation mechanism. A model is presented for calculating the X-ray energy absorbed per $F$-centre formed and reasonable agreement with low temperature experimental values is obtained. Room temperature behaviour of X-irradiated alkali halides is also considered in some detail and reasonable agreement with experiment is again obtained using simple models to calculate the volume dilatation and rate of $F$-centre formation in typical cases.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

To CREATE colour centres, i.e. absorption bands on the long wavelength side of the fundamental absorption edge, in ionic solids, two conditions are necessary:

(i) appropriate electron or hole traps must be present initially or must be formed in the crystal, e.g. negative ion vacancies for $F$-centres (cf. Ref. 1)

(ii) electrons must be excited from the valence band states into these electron traps.

The excitation of electrons can be produced through

(a) the creation of excitons $^{(2,3,4,5)}$ by irradiation of a crystal with ultraviolet light which is absorbed in the long wave-length edge of the fundamental absorption band

(b) X-ray and $\gamma$-ray irradiation,

(c) high energy charged particle $(\alpha, \beta$, proton) irradiation,

(d) high energy neutrons, indirectly by producing displaced ions with high kinetic energy,

(e) high electric fields. $^{(6,7,8)}$

$F$-centres may also be produced by additive colour- ation with alkali metal; $^{(1)}$ similarly $V$-centres (positive hole absorption bands) may be created by introducing excess halogen $^{(9,10,11)}$ into the crystal.

This paper will deal with some of the mechanisms of creation of colour centres in alkali halide crystals, i.e. univalent ionic solids, such as NaCl and KCl.

## 2. THE FORMATION OF $F$-CENTRES BY X-RAYS AT LIQUID HELIUM TEMPERATURES

It has been found by RABIN and KLICK $^{(12)}$ that the rate of creation of $F$-centres by X-rays in alkali halides at very low temperatures, near absolute zero, is an intrinsic property of the perfect crystal lattice and is not structure sensitive. That is to say, $F$-centre formation at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ does not depend upon the presence of defects initially in the crystal prior to irradiation, e.g. vacancies introduced by cold work, dislocations, excess positive ion vacancies introduced by adding divalent ions such as $\mathrm{Ca}^{++}$, or the presence of $\mathrm{H}^{-}$ ions forming $U$-centres by the addition of say KH to KBr. Further, X-ray energy absorbed per $F$- centre created ranges from $\sim 10^{3} \mathrm{eV}$ in the case of $\mathrm{KCl}$ to $\sim 10^{5} \mathrm{eV}$ in the case of $\mathrm{NaBr}$, and Rabin and Klick have shown that this energy of creation is related to the ratio of halogen ion radius to interionic spacing in the lattice.

In addition to $F$-centres, $H$-centres are also formed at these very low temperatures (DUERIG

and MARKHAM) $^{(13)}$ and electron spin resonance studies of these $^{(14,15)} H$-centres in KBr, KCl and LiF by KANZIG and his colleagues have led them to the conclusion that the $H$-centre is associated with a chlorine molecule lying along a $\langle 110\rangle$ direction occupying a single chlorine ion lattice site.

From these facts one concludes that:

(i) isolated negative ion vacancies are not present in alkali halides at very low temperatures, otherwise $F$-centre creation would show structure sensitive characteristics,

(ii) halogen ions are displaced by relatively low energy ionizing radiation which in itself is incapable of producing atomic displacements directly, viz. by momentum transfer or recoil.

A mechanism whereby atomic displacements can be effected by low energy radiation was proposed by the author some years ago. $^{(16,17)}$ Objections to it have been raised $^{(18,19)}$ and modifications have recently been suggested by KLICK, $^{(20)}$ by SMOLUCHOWSKI and WIEGAND $^{(21)}$ and by HowARDVosko and Smoluchowski. $^{(22)}$

The mechanism is based upon the experimental observation that fast electrons with energies in the region of a few hundred electron volts produce multiple ionization of atoms in gases and vapours (cf. Ref. 23). It was originally suggested by McLENNAN $^{(24)}$ that a single ionization of a halogen ion in a monovalent ionic solid could result in the resultant neutral atom jumping away from its initial lattice site into an interstitial position, since it would no longer be bound electrostatically to its substitutional site. But such an event could not occur within the solid since the mutual repulsions between neighbouring ions and the singly ionized anion would require that the latter simultaneously received several electron volts kinetic energy (Refs.25 a,b) for it to move into an interstitial position. However, if a given anion were to have more than one electron simultaneously removed from it by a process of multiple ionization it would temporarily become a positive ion residing in a substitutional lattice site which is occupied by a negative ion in the normal ionic lattice. Since the ions are vibrating about their mean positions such a multiply-ionized anion may be ejected into an interstitial position which to a first approximation is an electrostatically neutral position with respect to the rest of the lattice.

Provided that

(a) sufficient electrons are removed temporarily from the anion and

(b) the time taken for the $n$-fold ionized anion to recover electrons is greater than a few lattice vibration periods,

the excited halogen ion will be displaced interstitially. It is possible alternatively that a nearest neighbour positive ion may be displaced interstitially by the temporarily positively charged anion. If condition (a) is not fulfilled the excited anion will not be able to overcome the lattice repulsions as it attempts to move through the shell of nearest neighbour ions, while if condition (b) is not satisfied the excited anion will not have reached a mechanically stable interstitial position before it has become electrically neutral. Such an interstitial site will be a few lattice spacings removed from the lattice vacancy created by the ejected anion. It is thus important to discuss these conditions in some detail.

### 2.1. The necessary degree of multiple ionization leading to displaced anions in typical alkali halides

As long as an excited anion remains positively charged it will be repelled from the negative ion vacancy which it has created when it is forced into an interstitial position. This follows since a unit charge in the vicinity of a negative ion vacancy is in an electrostatic potential approximately equivalent to the superposition of the potentials of the perfect lattice and of a unit positive charge situated at the negative ion vacancy. Beyond the first shell of nearest neighbours the excited anion will therefore be repelled with a force $ne^{2}/K(r)r^{2}$ where $K(r)$ is the effective dielectric constant at distance $r$ from the negative ion vacancy and $n$ is the positive charge of the multiply ionized anion.

A little consideration shows that after penetration of the first shell this interaction will transmit at least
$$\sim \{(2/3)^{1/2}-\frac{1}{2}\} ne^{2}/d$$
additional kinetic energy to the interstitial positively charged anion. The term $\frac{1}{2}$ makes approximate allowance for the variation of dielectric constant with separation distance. This is the

minimum kinetic energy received by an inter- stitial excited anion which has just succeeded in penetrating the first shell about its original sub- stitutional lattice site. In the general case (cf. below) the anion will have more kinetic energy, typically greater than $\sim 1.5 n$ eV. Therefore at least one and a half electron volts kinetic energy must be dissipated before the interstitial anion is finally brought to rest in the absence of thermal activation. An activation energy of $\sim 0.1 eV$ for the movement of the interstitial is reasonable (cf. Section 5) so that the final separation distance will be some fifteen interatomic spacings from the negative ion vacancy whence the interstitial originated. This separation will take $\sim 10^{-11}$ to $10^{-12} sec$ and electron capture by the excited anion is unlikely to occur within this time either on the grounds of energy conservation or transition probabilities (cf. Section 3). Further, the inter- stitial will receive almost all its additional kinetic energy in the first interstitial "jump" so that only electron capture processes with transition times smaller than $\sim 5 ×10^{13}$ secs will be relevant.

At the first shell the kinetic energy of the dis- placed anion will equal the decrease in potential energy of the anion in the absence of repulsive forces from neighbouring lattice ions. This de- crease in potential energy is of magnitude
$$\sim\left[M-(2 / 3)^{1 / 2}\right] \cdot \frac{n e^{2}}{d},$$
where $d$ is the smallest anion-cation distance in the crystal and $M$ is the Madelung constant.

Using the Born-Mayer formula (cf. Ref. 1) one may estimate the closed shell interaction energybetween the excited anion and the four ions (2 cations and 2 anions) at the corners of the square through which it passes into an interstitial position. In the case of complete closed shells the Born- Mayer expression for the exchange repulsion energy between two ions of radii $r_{1}$ and $r_{2}$ is given by
$$\epsilon_{R}=A \exp \left\{-\frac{\left(r-r_{1}-r_{2}\right)}{\rho}\right\}\qquad(1)$$
where $A$ and $\rho$ are constants. An incomplete closed shell of electrons will interact less strongly with neighbouring ions since the number of individual exchange interactions summed to give $\epsilon_{R}$ is fewer. Semi-empirically one may correct for this by intro- ducing the additional factor
$$\left[1-\frac{n+1}{z}\right]$$
in (1), where $z$ is the number of valence electrons in the closed shell and $(n+1)$ is the number of electrons removed in a multiple ionization event.

The repulsive energy will be offset by the difference in polarization energy of the surround- ing lattice ions when the excited positive halogen ion is at its original lattice site and interstitially at the centre of the square of nearest neighbour lattice ions. Lomer (private discussion) has pointed out that this change in polarization energy $\Delta \epsilon_{p}$ is appreciable and in addition is proportional to $n^{2}$ . An approximate estimate of $\Delta \epsilon_{p}$ for the sodium chloride type lattice is
$$\Delta \epsilon_{p}=-\frac{e^{2} n^{2}}{4 d^{4}}\left\{15 \alpha_{-}+7 \alpha_{+}\right\}\qquad(2)$$
where $d$ is the closest anion-cation distance in the normal lattice and $\alpha_{-}, \alpha_{+}$ are the polarizabilities of the normal lattice anion and cation respectively. The summation over positive and negative ions leading to expression (2) was carried out over the nearest eighteen neighbours for both configura- tions. The Lorentz-Lorenz forces were not in- cluded but the error involved is probably not large and will in any case be compensated by neglecting contributions from more distant neighbours. The number of electrons which must be removed from the anion if it is to be able to move into an inter- stitial position is $(n+1)$ where $n$ is the next greatest integer to the non-integral value of $n$ which satisfied the equation:
$$\begin{gathered}
\left\{M-(2 / 3)^{1 / 2}\right\} \frac{n e^{2}}{d}=\left[\left\{1-\frac{n+1}{Z}\right\} \sum_{i} A_{i}\right. \\
\left.\exp \left\{-\frac{\left(r-r_{1}-r_{i}\right)}{\rho}\right\}\right]-\frac{n^{2} e^{2}}{4 d^{4}}\left(15 \alpha_{-}+7 \alpha_{+}\right) \quad(3)
\end{gathered}$$

The summation is over the four lattice ions of radius $r_{i} ; r_{1}$ is the excited anion radius and $r$ is the closest distance of approach between one of the four lattice ions and the excited anion when the latter is in the plane of the four ions.

The values of $A_{i}, r_{i}$ and $\rho$ given by MoTT and

GURNEY(1) have been used together with values for $\alpha_{-}$ and $\alpha_{+}$ given by BORN $^{(27)}$ with $r$ set equal to the normal anion radius. The values of $\rho=(n+1)$ de duced from (3) for several alkali halides are given in Table 1. It will be seen that it is necessary to remove two or three electrons from a halogen ion if it is to be displaced; the importance of the polarization term $\propto n^{2}$ is clearly evident since without it one or more additional electrons would have to be removed.

For those compounds in which it is estimated that three electrons must be removed from the halogen ion it is possible that in fact only a two- fold ionization is necessary. In particular the closed shell interactions may be weaker than estimated, for the parameter $\rho$ will increase with decreasing interionic distance and the empirical values for $A_{i}, r_{i}$ and $\rho$ apply only to the interionic distances in the normal lattice.

The probability that a positive lattice ion (cation) will be displaced interstitially when a nearest neighbour anion is more than singly ionized is not negligible. The local potential energy will be reduced by the same amount when the excited anion moves onto the cation site, forcing the metal ion out, as when the excited anion moves directly into an interstitial position. For the same cation and excited anion radii the probabilities for each process will be of the same order of magnitude. When the cation is smaller than the anion as in NaCl it would seem that the ejection of $Na^{+}$ ions would dominate and similarly in the case of NaBr or LiF. This effect may be important in account- ing for the detailed differences between colour centre formation in the several alkali halides. In either event a negative ion vacancy will be created temporarily. The stability at low temperatures of an $F$-centre subsequently created by trapping an electron in the negative ion vacancy will pre- sumably be less when a neutral halogen ion is in an adjacent positive ion vacancy (cation ejection) than when the neutral halogen is in an interstitial position (anion ejection).

### 2.2. Estimation of the X-ray energy absorbed per F-centre formed in an alkali halide
In all but the lightest elements X-rays are absorbed almost entirely by producing photo- electrons whilst the production of low energy electrons by the Compton effect is relatively un- important. Thus each X-ray photon absorbed in a crystal produces one photoelectron with a kinetic energy which is the difference between the photon energy $E_{0}$ and the ionization energy, $I_{0}$, of the photoelectron. These electrons may be ejected from the $K L$ ... levels of the lattice ions with appropriate values of $I_{0}$ corresponding to the X-ray absorption edges. They will lose their kinetic energy principally through inelastic col- lisions with the ions giving rise to electron excita- tions producing excitons, single or multiple ionizations. Elastic collisions with the ions will result in an irregular path of the photoelectron through the crystal so that its true range will be greater than its apparent range. The number $n_{p}$ of $p$-fold ionizations of the anions produced by unit integrated flux ( 1 per $cm^{2}$ ) of photoelectrons is

$$
n_{p}=N_{-} \int_{0}^{R} \sigma_{p}(x) \mathrm{d} x \tag{4}
$$

where $\sigma(x)$ is the cross-section for $p$-fold ionization at a point $x$ within the true range $R$ of the photo electron. $N_{-}$ is the number of anions per unit volume in the crystal.

(4) may be written

$$
n_{p}=N_{-} \int_{\left(E_{0}-I_{0}\right)}^{\epsilon_{\min }} \sigma_{p}(E) / \frac{\mathrm{d} E}{\mathrm{~d} x} \mathrm{~d} E \tag{5}
$$

where $\epsilon_{\min }$ is the minimum energy for which the

photoelectron can produce $p$-fold ionization of an anion, $\mathrm{d} E / \mathrm{d} x$ is the range energy loss of the fast electron and is given by (cf. Ref. 28, p. 252).
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} E}{\mathrm{~d} x}=-\frac{2 \pi e^{4} \overline{N Z}}{E} \ln \left(\frac{2 E}{I}\right) \quad(6)
$$
$\overline{N Z}$ is the total number of all electrons per unit volume and $I$ is a mean excitation energy.

The cross-section for a $p$-fold ionization may be expressed in terms of the single-ionization cross- section using the following arguments. The total cross-section for primary (single) ionization of an anion valence electron is (cf. Ref. 27).
$$
\sigma_{1}=\frac{\pi e^{4}}{E} \frac{c_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}} Z \ln \left(\frac{4 E}{\epsilon_{1}}\right) \quad(7)
$$
where $Z$ is the number of valence electrons, $\epsilon_{1}$ is the first ionization energy and $\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}$ is of the order of the ionization energy. $c_{1}$ is a partial sum of oscillator strengths for transitions of an electron from the ground state into the continuum levels and is consequently less than unity. Consideration of the derivation of expression (7) shows that the cross-section $\sigma_{p}$ for a multiple ionization will have the same dependence upon the energy $E$ of the fast electron as does $\sigma_{1}$. Therefore the ratio $\sigma_{p} / \sigma_{1}$ will be approximately independent of energy for values of $E$ appreciably greater than $\epsilon_{p}$, the sum of the first $p$ ionization energies. This is observed experimentally in the case of helium ionizations by fast electrons. $^{(29)}$ One can thus use the experi mental ratios $\sigma_{1} / \sigma_{p}$ in conjunction with (7) to estimate $\sigma_{p}$. Interpolated values of these ratios, $s_{p}$, for halogen atoms have been deduced from data given in MASSEY and BURHOP $^{(22)}$ (p. 38) and it is assumed that the same values apply for halogen ions. The total X-ray energy absorbed by the crystal of thickness $x_{0}$ is:
$$
\int_{0}^{E_{M}} \int_{0}^{x_{0}} E_{0} n\left(E_{0}\right) e^{-\mu\left(E_{0}\right) x} \mu\left(E_{0}\right) \mathrm{d} x \mathrm{~d} E_{0}

$$
n\left(E_{0}\right)=\text { const. }\left\{E_{M}-E_{0}\right\} \quad(10)
$$
$\mu(E_{0})$ is the X-ray absorption coefficient for X-rays of energy $E_{0}$.

The number of $p$-fold ionizations within the crystal produced directly by the photoelectron is similarly, by (9) proportional to
$$
\int_{0}^{E} \int_{0}^{x}\left(E_{0}-I_{0}\right) n\left(E_{0}\right) e^{-\mu\left(E_{0}\right) x} \mu\left(E_{0}\right) \mathrm{d} x \mathrm{~d} E_{0}
$$

for alkali fluorides and chlorides of lithium, sodium and potassium the average X-ray energy expended per direct p-fold ionization is inde- pendent of the X-ray energy and is given by

$$
E_{p}^{\prime}=\frac{2\left(Z_{0 A}+Z_{0 C}\right) \epsilon_{1} s_{p}}{c_{1} Z} \quad(11)
$$

The following considerations show that a cor- rection is necessary to this result, arising from further ionizations produced by secondary elec- trons. $$
E_{1}=\frac{2\left(Z_{0 A}+Z_{0 C}\right) \epsilon_{1}}{c_{1} Z} \quad(12)
$$

Consequently the average energy lost per ionization by the inci- dent electron is somewhat greater than $\epsilon_{1}$.

The average photon energy expended per p-fold ionization is therefore:

Condition (ii) is most likely to be met when the anion-cation radius ratio is small. ### 3. RECOMBINATION MECHANISMS BETWEEN IONS AND ELECTRONS

It was emphasized at the beginning of Section 2 that a multiply-ionized lattice anion must remain so, long enough for it to be displaced into an interstitial position.

There are several mechanisms whereby the excited halogen can regain electrons, bearing in mind that a change in the local electron configuration, viz. the dispersion of positive holes, in general involves a decrease in total energy of the system which must be accommodated either by:

(i) the generation of kinetic energy amongst the dispersing positive holes,(19)
(ii) the ejection of an Auger electron from a neighbouring ion,
(iii) the creation of a photon,
(iv) the creation of phonons in a multiphonon process.

The multiply ionized halogen ion must remain ionized for at least a few multiples of its characteristic vibration time $(\sim 10^{-12}$ to $10^{-13} \mathrm{sec})$ if it is to escape into an interstitial position. It is therefore necessary that the lifetimes of the processes (i) to (iv), defined as the reciprocals of the transition probabilities, shall all be longer than say $10^{-12} \mathrm{sec}$.

#### Mechanism (i)

If two or more positive holes are localized at one anion site, it has been argued(19) that, on the collective electron band scheme, electrons will flow into this region to neutralize the effective positive charge on the excited anion in a time short compared with the lattice vibration time. The potential energy of the system will decrease by an amount
$$
\sim\left(e^{2} / r_{i}-e^{2} / d_{a}\right)
$$
when two holes on one anion site separate to one on each of two nearest neighbour sites distance $d_{a}$ apart; $r_{i}$ is the anion radius. This decrease in potential energy is converted into kinetic energy of the separating holes. Such a mechanism can only operate if the valence band width is greater than half the magnitude of the potential energy decrease. Thus for KCl the valence band width must be greater than $\sim 2 \cdot 2 \mathrm{eV}$. HowLAND's calculations for $\mathrm{KCl}^{(31)}$ suggest that the valence band width is about $1.5 \mathrm{eV}$ so that this mechanism of hole separation will not operate unless some of the kinetic energy is in turn converted into phonon energy. The latter possibility seems unlikely since for the case of KCl about $0.7 \mathrm{eV}$ would have to be converted into phonon energy within about one interatomic spacing. Normally the mean free path for collisions between the electron and phonon systems is several interatomic spacings and the average energy transferred to the phonon system at each collision is $\sim k T$.

For NaCl CASELLA(32) estimates a lower limit of 1.0 eV for the valence band width and suggests that the width of $4 \cdot 4 \mathrm{eV}$ obtained by SHOCKLEY $^{(33)}$ is an upper limit. Because of the smaller lattice spacing the decrease in potential energy will be less when two holes separate in NaCl compared with KCl. Possibly therefore this mechanism can

However, the correct description of the valence states necessitates a proper treatment of exchange and correlation effects between electrons. For instance, MoTT⁽³⁴⁾ has questioned the use of Bloch functions for describing valence states in NiO and PLASKETT⁽³⁵⁾ has shown that for a free electron gas the use of Hartree wave functions (a simple product of plane waves not antisymmetrized) does not minimise the energy of the system. In fact the energy can be lowered if some of the electrons are described by localized wave functions. For a sufficiently small electron density the energy is minimized if all electrons are localized.

It is possible therefore that the best variation function to describe the valence electrons in an alkali halide will be based on the Heitler-London approximation and not on Bloch functions (molecular orbitals). The discussion of the dispersion of positive holes based on this alternative is as follows.

Immediately after a multiple ionization event one halogen ion is deficient of p-electrons, i.e. there are p positive holes located at one halogen ion lattice site. Suppose p = 2 and consider the change in configuration energy when these two positive holes have redistributed themselves so that one hole is now located on each of two nearest neighbour halogen ions. This change in energy ΔE_c is

$$
\Delta E_{c}=I_{1}-I_{2}+\frac{e^{2}}{d_{1}} \tag{14}
$$

where I₁ is, in the case of an ion, numerically equal to the electron affinity of the halogen atom, I₂ is the first ionization potential of the neutral atom and d₁ is the closest distance between anion sites in the lattice. There will also be a small polarization term contributing to ΔE_c and this will approximately replace the term e²/kd in magnitude and sign as the positive holes move apart. For typical alkali halides ΔE_c ~ -5 eV.

Following the treatment used by FRENKEL⁽³⁶⁾ for excitons it can be shown (cf. Ref. 1, p. 92) that, within the Heitler-London approximation, there are to a zero approximation a set of N degenerate eigenfunctions describing the excited state of a lattice with anions in normal ground states, E, at all but one of the N lattice sites. At this site there is an excited anion—singly or multiply ionized with energy E_x. This degeneracy is removed in part by the perturbing potential which is the difference between the potential of an electron in the lattice and in the free ion. The perturbed wave functions now describe a positive hole moving through the lattice with a wave vector k and an energy within a continuum of energy levels of the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(k) & =E_{0}+12 V_{0}+8 V_{1}\left\{\cos k_{x} d \cos k_{y} d\right. \\
& \left.+\cos k_{y} d \cos k_{z} d+\cos k_{z} d \cos k_{x} d\right\}
\end{aligned}
$$

where only nearest neighbour wave-function overlaps are taken into account. V₀ and V₁ are the matrix elements

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{0}= \iint & \left|\psi\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{g}\right)\right|^{2} U\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{g}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h}\right) \\
& \times\left|\psi_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h}\right)\right|^{2} \mathrm{~d} \tau \mathrm{d} \tau^{\prime}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{1}= \iint & \psi^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{g}\right) \psi_{x}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h}\right) \\
& \times U\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{g}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h}\right) \psi\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h}\right) \psi_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}_{g}\right) \mathrm{d} \tau \mathrm{d} \tau^{\prime}
\end{aligned}
$$

and $N E_{0}=(N-1) E+E_{x}$.

$\psi$ and $\psi_x$ are normal and excited anion atomic wave functions respectively. $\boldsymbol{r}_g$ and $\boldsymbol{r}_h$ nearest neighbour lattice site co-ordinates and U the perturbing potential, a symmetrical function in $(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{g})$ and $(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h})$.

d is the smallest anion-cation distance. In terms of d the Brillouin zone boundary planes are given by

$$
\left(k_{x} \pm k_{y} \pm k_{z}\right)= \pm \frac{3 \pi}{2 d} ; k_{x}=k_{y}=k_{z}= \pm \pi / d
$$

The lowest energy state is at the centre of the zone

$$
k_{x}=k_{y}=k_{z}=0
$$

and the highest at a corner of the Brilloiun zone, e.g.

$$
k_{x}=k \pi / d, k_{y}=\pi / 2 d, k_{z}=0
$$

It follows that the band width is 32V₁.

The perturbing potential U is principally due to the positive ion separating two cations. A rough

estimate of $V_1$ may be made by taking the average potential, $\bar{V}$, of the positive ion assuming the extranuclear electrons to be uniformly distributed over a sphere of radius $r_0$. This gives

$$
\bar{V}=-0 \cdot 3 Z_{c} e^{2} / r_{0}
$$

where $Z_{c}$ is the cation atomic number. Taking this averaged potential outside the integral for and assuming the hydrogenic form $\exp (-r / a)$ for that part of $\Psi$ which penetrates the region of the cation it is evident that the product of wave functions is everywhere less than $\exp (-2 d / a)$, i.e. the value along the line joining the centres of two anions. The integral over the wave functions will thus be less than

$$
\frac{Z \Omega}{\pi a^{3}} e^{-2 d / a}
$$

where $Z$ is the number of valence electrons on the anion and $\Omega$ is a volume of order $4 \pi r_{0}^{3} / 3$.

The rough estimate of $V_1$ is thus

$$
V_{1} \sim-\frac{0.4 Z_{c} Z e^{2} r_{0}^{2}}{a^{3}} e^{-2 d / a}
$$

Setting $Z_{c}=20, r_{0} \sim 1 \AA, Z=8, a \sim 0.7 \AA$ (cf. Section 3) gives $V_{1} \sim-3 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{eV}$ and it is difficult to see how this estimate, although crude, could be an order of magnitude larger. The positive hole band width is thus estimated to lie between $0 \cdot 1$ and $1 \mathrm{eV}$ for single holes and to be somewhat less for multiple-hole states representing multiply ionized anions.

The total energy difference between the doublehole ground state and the sum of two single-hole ground states equals the configuration energy change $(\sim-5 \mathrm{eV})$. If the total energy is to be conserved, each single hole must gain $\sim 2.5 \mathrm{eV}$ additional energy (kinetic), being excited to states of higher $k$ within the single hole bands as the positive holes move away from each other. In fact they can gain only $\sim 1 \mathrm{eV}$ each so that this mechanism is not possible. The single positive holes accelerate away from each other initially but their effective mass (or charge) changes sign as they move to states of high $k$ near the top of the band; with further increasing energy they are then retarded until their group velocity is again zero at the top of the band. Under these conditions the positive holes can thus never move far away from each other and mechanism (i) is not possible for large changes in configuration energy and narrow positive hole bands.

## Mechanism (ii)
The excited anion may recover one electron from a nearest neighbour normal anion with a decrease of several $\mathrm{eV}$ in the energy of the system and this energy may be used to excite another electron on a neighbouring ion to an unoccupied higher energy state. This is the Auger effect (cf. Refs. 28, 37, 38) and the transition time for this process may be short compared with the vibration time of the ion.

Again, as for mechanism (i), one must consider whether such a process is possible on energetic grounds. If a doubly ionized anion, before it is ejected from its lattice site, captures an electron from a nearest neighbour normal anion, the decrease in energy is $\left\{I_{2}-I_{1}-e^{2} / d_{1}\right\}$ (eq. (14)). In an insulator the ejected Auger electron must receive sufficient energy to excite it either (a) into the conduction band from its valence band level or (b) into an exciton level. The latter requires the lesser energy, $\epsilon_{g}$, which is equal to the first absorption peak in the fundamental ultraviolet absorption band. $^{(1)}$ Table 5 indicates that $\left(I_{2}-I_{1}-e^{2} / d_{1}\right)$ is less than $\epsilon_{g}$ so that the Auger effect cannot operate in the case of a doubly ionized anion.

Similarly a triply ionized anion may capture one electron with a configuration energy decrease of $\left(I_{3}-I_{1}-2 e^{2} / d_{1}\right)$ and it will be seen from Table 5 that this quantity is greater than $\epsilon_{g}$. The Auger effect may possibly operate in this case and an electron may be ejected into the conduction band. The uncertainty in this conclusion arises for the following reason. With three electrons removed from one halogen ion, the energy required to eject an electron on a neighbouring cation into the conduction band is raised by an amount $3 e^{2} / d_{1}$, for the valence state of such an electron is depressed by this amount, the conduction level remaining relatively unperturbed. This effect is offset by the local increase in lattice polarization when the configuration of three positive holes located on one halogen ion changes to two holes on the halogen ion with a single hole on each of two nearest neighbour halogens. If polarization effects and the depression of the valence levels are neglected, the energy required to raise an electron from a valence level to

the conduction band is the same as in the case of a perfect crystal, again neglecting the polarization term. This energy is ~ 11 eV (cf. Ref. 1, p. 97) i.e. the energy corresponding to the second maximum in the ultraviolet absorption spectrum plus the magnitude of the polarization term (~ 1·5 eV). Without detailed calculation of the change in polarization energy in the case of the triply ionized ion regaining one electron it is difficult to say whether this will effectively cancel out the de- pression of the valence band level, although it will certainly offset the latter. It is possible, then, that the Auger effect may operate for cases where the halogen ion is more than doubly ionized and one must therefore discuss the transition time for such an event.

The transition probability, $\omega$, per unit time for this process is given by:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega= & \frac{2 \pi N\left(\epsilon_{c}\right)}{\hbar}\left[\iint \mathrm{d} \tau_{1} \mathrm{~d} \tau_{2}\left\{\phi_{1}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{x}\right)\right.\right. \\
& \times u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{c}}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right) \exp \left(-i \boldsymbol{k}_{c} \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right) \\
& \left.\left.\times \frac{e^{2}}{\left|\boldsymbol{r}_{1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right|} \times \phi_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{A}\right) \phi_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{2}-\boldsymbol{r}_{B}\right)\right\}\right]^{2}
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
$$
where $\boldsymbol{r}_{1}$ and $\boldsymbol{r}_{2}$ are the co-ordinates of the two electrons involved. Electron 1 makes a transition from a localized state $\phi_{0 A}$ on anion $A$ to a localized state $\phi_{1 x}$ on the excited anion $X$, whilst electron 2 is excited from a localized state $\phi_{0 B}$ on another neighbouring anion $B$ into the conduction band state
$$u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{c}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right) \exp \left(i \boldsymbol{k}_{c} \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right)$$
$\epsilon_{c}$ is the energy relative to the bottom of the conduction band, $N(\epsilon_{c})$ the number of energy states per unit energy range about $\epsilon_{c}$ and $\boldsymbol{k}_{c}$ the corresponding wave vector of the conduction state into which electron 2 is ejected.
$$
\epsilon_{c} \text { is given by } \epsilon_{c}+\epsilon_{1}=\left|\Delta E_{c}\right| \tag{16}
$$
where $|\Delta E_{c}|$ is the magnitude of the configuration energy change.

An approximate evaluation of the matrix element leads to the following expression for $w$,
$$
w \sim \frac{64 \pi m e^{4} a k_{c}}{\hbar^{3}\left(1+a^{2} k_{c}^{2}\right)^{4}} \cdot \exp (-2 R / a) \tag{17}
$$
where an exponential form $\exp (-r / a)$ has been assumed for the localized wave functions $\phi_{0}$ and $\phi_{1}$ and $u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{c}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{2})$ has been set equal to unity (free electron approximation). $R$ is the closest distance between the excited anion and a nearest neighbour lattice anion, and $\boldsymbol{k}_{c}$ is given by
$$
\epsilon_{c}=\frac{\hbar^{2} k_{c}^{2}}{2 m} \tag{18}
$$

A consideration of the exchange interaction leading to the Born-Mayer closed shell repulsion formula suggests that $a / 2$ should be set equal to $\rho$ in this expression. Since $\rho=0.345 \AA$ is the empirical value for the alkali halides, $a$ in (17) has been set equal to $0.69 \AA$. The value of $a$ found by HARTREE and HARTREE $^{(39)}$ for the exponential factor in the calculated outer shell wave functions for $Cl$ is $a \sim 1 \AA$.

Numerical evaluation of (17) gives for lifetimes $\tau(=1 / w)$ of excited anion values lying between $\sim 10^{-12}$ and $10^{-14}$ sec. for KI to $NaCl$; $\tau$ decreases with lattice spacing and these values apply to electron capture by the Auger effect when the excited anion is still at its original lattice site. As the anion attempts to move into an interstitial position it comes much closer to two lattice anions and the lifetime for electron capture will be shorter than the values given above.

The following general conclusions may thus be drawn:
1. A doubly ionized halogen ion in an alkali halide cannot recapture an electron by the Auger effect since there is no accessible energy level into which the excited electron can be ejected.
2. A triply ionized halogen ion probably cannot escape into an interstitial position before it has recaptured one electron.
3. A 4-fold ionized halogen ion will likewise regain one electron before it reaches an interstitial position but then the configuration will be one triply ionized halogen ion between two singly ionized halogen ions. There is no further decrease in the configuration energy by transferring another electron from one of the neutral halogens to the 3-fold ionized halogen ion large enough to allow the Auger mechanism to operate again.
4. It seems necessary for a halogen ion to be either doubly or quadruply ionized if it is to

be displaced interstitially. This may be the reason why the mean X-ray photon energy per $F$-centre created is an order of magnitude larger, experimentally, than the calculated value for the removal of three electrons from $Br^{-}$or $I^{-}$. If four electrons must in reality be removed for halogen ion displacement in these cases the energy $E_{F}$ will be about a factor of ten greater since the multiple ionization cross-section decreases by the same factor for each additional electron re- moved.

### Mechanism (iii)
The transition probability, $A$, for spontaneous emission of a photon when the excited anion re- captures an electron is given by:
$$
A=\frac{2 e^{2} f \epsilon^{2}}{3 \hbar^{2} m c^{3}} \quad(19)
$$
where $f$ is the oscillator strength for the transition $(<1)$ and $\epsilon$ is the energy of the photon emitted, equal to the change in configuration energy, $|\Delta E_{c}|$. If $\epsilon$ is measured in eV, the transition time $A^{-1}$ for this process is $6.9 \times 10^{-8}\left(f \epsilon^{2}\right)^{-1} \sec$ which is of the order of $10^{-8} \sec$ if $|\Delta E_{c}|$ is a few eV. Photon emission will thus not occur until the excited anion has been displaced interstitially.

### Mechanism (iv)
Multiphonon processes (cf. Frenkel $^{(36)}$ and MARKHAM $^{(40)}$ ), in which the change in configura tion energy is used to create many phonons each with mean energy $k T$, also give transition times much longer than $10^{-12} sec$. for values of $\Delta E_{c}$ of the order of a few volts.

## 4. FORMATION OF F-CENTRES BY ELECTRON TRAPPING AT ROOM TEMPERATURE
Experimentally at room temperature, colour centre formation is found to be composed of two processes, one a fast mechanism saturating in a time of the order of a few minutes (cf. RABIN and KLICK $^{(12)}$ ) under typical X-irradiation conditions, the other a slow mechanism which forms colour centres over long times, up to 50 hr and more. This slow mechanism gives a non-linear rate of formation of colour centres, possibly in the early stages when the fast mechanism is still opera- tive $^{(41)}$ and also at longer times when the rate increases. $^{(42,30)}$ RABIN and KLICK $^{(12)}$ have shown that the X-ray energy absorbed per $F$-centre formed is essentially the same for the slow mechan- ism at room temperature as it is for initial $F$-centre formation at liquid helium temperatures. In addition the X-ray energy to form an $F$-centre by the rapid mechanism in the initial stages of coloura- tion at room temperature is the same ( $\sim 28 eV$ in KCl) as for retrapping electrons in negative ion vacancies at $4^{\circ} K.^{(12)}$ It is two or three orders of magnitude less than for the slow mechanism. NowICK $^{(41)}$ has found the same ratio in the case of colour centre formation by $\gamma$-irradiation, although the $\gamma$-ray energy absorbed per $F$-centre created is roughly a factor ten larger, for both the slow and fast mechanisms, than in the X-ray case. The reason for the differences between $X$ and y-irradiation in the energy absorbed per F-centre created is not clear.

Consider the fast colouration process. The absorption of high energy electro-magnetic radi- ation ionizes electrons from the valence states of the ions in an alkali halide. When these electrons have lost their excess kinetic energy through in- elastic collisions they will occupy conduction band levels in the crystal. The electrons can combinewith the positive holes (empty valence levels) complementary to them and this will result in the emission of a photon equal in energy to the second maximum of the fundamental ultra-violet absorp- tion band ( $\sim 10 eV)$ . This photon will in turn be reabsorbed by the crystal in a mean free path equal to the reciprocal of the absorption co- efficient, $\mu$ , of the fundamental ultraviolet band. Since $\mu \sim 10^{5} ~cm^{-1}$ this means that only electronhole recombinations in the surface layers $\sim 10^{-5}$  cm. thick will result in the energy escaping from the crystal. Therefore $\sim 10^{5}$ photons must be created on average* before the electron has fallen permanently to its ground state by combining with the positive hole. If the life-time of each electron hole pair regenerated is say $10^{-8} sec$ (cf. later dis cussion) the whole process will take $\sim 10^{-3} sec$ . and this is the lifetime of a dissociated electron hole pair in the crystal. A similar argument holds for internally generated exciton levels.

\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=n_{e}\sigma_{F}v_{e}(N_{0F}-N_{F}) \tag{21b}
$$

$n_{e}$ is the number of free electrons per unit volume, in conduction band levels of the crystal, generated by X-ray absorption and $\beta$ is their rate of creation. The second term on the R.H.S. of (21a) is the rate of capture of free electrons with velocity $v_{e}$ by the density $(N_{0F}-N_{F})$ of empty negative ion vacancies; $\sigma_{F}$ is the vacancy capture cross-section
$$
(\mu l/n_{p}\sigma_{p}v_{e})
$$
is the lifetime of a free electron before it combines finally with one of the $n_{p}$ free positive holes, with capture cross-section $\sigma_{p}$, the resultant photon escaping from the crystal; $\lambda$ is the thickness of the crystal and $\mu$ the absorption coefficient for the emitted photon. $N_{F}$ is the density of $F$-centres at time $t$ and $N_{0F}$ is the final density. It is assumed in these rate equations that the removal of conduction electrons by combination with trapped positive holes is small and may be neglected in (21a) and that the elimination of $F$-centres by free positive holes is negligible in (21b). Evidence to support these assumptions comes from the experiments of ALEXANDER and SCHNEIDER(43) who showed that in aged crystals, in which optical bleaching experiments were carried out several hr after X-irradiation ceased,

(i) free electrons optically excited from $F$-centres combine with trapped positive holes very slowly,

(ii) positive holes freed from traps do not combine with electrons trapped in $F$-centres,

(iii) freed holes and freed electrons recombine rapidly, resulting in complete optical bleaching.

In crystals which have not aged there is an initial rapid recombination of electrons, optically freed from $F$-centres, with positive holes. In addition in aged crystals if a small number of positive holes are freed by ultraviolet irradiation, subsequent $F$-light irradiation produces rapid recombination of the freed electrons with the freed holes (iii) until all have been eliminated; thereafter $F$-light illumination produces only slow bleaching of the remaining trapped holes (i). Therefore, to a first approximation $F$-centres created during X-irradi- ation will be stable as also will be trapped positive holes, $V_{2,3}$ centres. A similar pair of equations to (21a, b) will hold for the positive holes comple- mentary to the free electrons. Even if additional terms for the annihilation of trapped positive holes by free electrons are included in equations (21) the following results of this section will not be modi- fied by orders of magnitude.

If the initial density of isolated negative ion vacancies, $N_{0F}$, is large enough then
$$\sigma_{F} N_{0 F} \gg \sigma_{p} n_{p} / \mu l$$

The last term in (2a) may then be neglected and the solution of equations (21) for values of $N_{F}$ such that $(N_{0F}-N_{F})$ is not too small is:
$$1-f=\frac{\exp \left[-(1-\alpha t)^{2} / 2 \alpha \tau_{F}\right]}{\exp \left(-\frac{1}{2} \alpha \tau_{F}\right)+\alpha^{2} \tau_{F} \int_{-1}^{(1-\alpha t}: \exp \left[-\left(1-\alpha t^{\prime}\right)^{2} / 2 \alpha \tau_{F}\right] \mathrm{d} t^{\prime}}$$
where
$$\alpha=\beta / N_{0 F}, f=N_{F} / N_{0 F} \text { and } \tau_{F}=\left(\sigma_{F} v_{e} N_{0 F}\right)^{-1}$$

Saturation is approached for values of $t \gg 1 / \alpha$, when $f \to 1$. Now $\beta$ is the rate at which free elec trons are produced in the crystal and this is of the order $R E_{p} / \epsilon$, where $R$ is the number of X-ray photons absorbed in unit volume per unit time, $E_{p}$ is the photon energy and $\epsilon$ is somewhat greater than but of the order of the forbidden gap width in the crystal, i.e. the energy required to excite an electron from the valence band into the conduc- tion band. $R E_{p}$ is thus the X-ray energy absorbed in unit volume per unit time. Typically $R E_{p} \sim 3 \times 10^{16} \mathrm{eV} \mathrm{cm}^{-3} \mathrm{sec}^{-1}$ and $\epsilon \sim 20 \mathrm{eV}$ so that $\beta \sim 1.5 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3} \mathrm{sec}^{-1}$. The saturation time for an initial concentration $N_{0F} \sim 10^{17} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ would thus be approximately one minute. Rapid colouration of this nature is found on re-irradiating specimens at liquid helium temperature $^{(12)}$ as discussed above, but the saturation time at room temperature for the fast stage is longer.

The reason for this difference lies in the fact that isolated negative ion vacancies are not present in an unirradiated crystal at room temperature,(cf. Ref. 44). The binding energy of a positive- negative ion vacancy pair has been calculated to be $\sim 0.6 eV^{(45,46)}$ and in addition the jump fre quency of positive ion vacancies is $\sim 1 sec^{-1}$ at room temperature. $^{(47,48)}$ The much more slowly moving negative ion vacancies will thus become asssociated into pairs by combining with the more rapidly moving positive ion vacancies. A concen- tration of $10^{17} ~cm^{-3}$ isolated negative ion vacancieswill combine with positive ion vacancies in $\sim 10^{5}$  sec and a very small fraction will be dissociated at room temperature. Furthermore, the work of LAURENCE $^{(49)}$ suggests that the activation energy for movement of these pairs is $\sim 1.2 eV$ so that many will be trapped in the lattice, not necessarily at impurities, as a result of supersaturation when the fresh crystal is cooled from the melt.

At room temperature, therefore, the fast $F$- centre colouration mechanism will involve the capture of an electron in the dipole field of a vacancy pair with the simultaneous or subsequent movement of a positive ion into the positive ion vacancy. $^{(47,50,51)}$ In this way an $F$-centre will form with a positive ion vacancy among the next nearest positive ion sites. The process is essentially a multiphonon process for which it is necessary that the energy difference between the initial and final states is only a few tenths of an eV at room temperature, producing intense local lattice vibra- tions rather than being released as a photon. The saturation time for this mechanism will differ from that for the capture of electrons by isolated negative ion vacancies since the relevant matrix elements determining the transition probabilities

will not be the same. There are two possibilities,
(a) the transition probability for electron capture by the vacancy pair may be smaller than for capture by an isolated negative ion vacancy, thus leading to a longer saturation time,
(b) the transition probability for the dissociation of the electron-vacancy pair complex into an $F$-centre and an isolated positive ion vacancy may be the rate controlling step. At low enough temperatures this will certainly be so, since multiphonon processes are strongly temperature dependent, and possibly it will hold at room temperature.

If either of these alternatives is operative the saturation time for the fast $F$-centre formation mechanism will be longer than for $F$-centre formation by the capture of free electrons at isolated negative ion vacancies. It will be noted that multiphonon processes will be very unlikely at low enough temperatures so that the fast $F$-colouration mechanism will not occur at low temperatures as is in fact observed in annealed crystals. Detailed calculations will be necessary to discuss these points further.

For a very small concentration of $F$-centres there is the alternative case that
$$\sigma_{F} N_{0 F} \ll n_{p} \sigma_{p} / \mu l$$
in equation (2a). Neglecting now the second term on the R.H.S. of (21a) the solution of equations (2), assuming that $n_{e}=n_{p}$ is
$$1-f=\exp \left\{-\left(\beta \mu l v_{e} \sigma_{F}^{2} / \sigma_{p}\right)^{1 / 2} F(t)\right\}$$
where

and
$$\tau_{e}=\left(\frac{\mu l}{4 \beta \sigma_{p} v_{e}}\right)^{1 / 2} \quad(23)$$
$F(t) \to t$ for values of $t \gg \tau_{e}$ while for short times
$$F(t) \sim \frac{t^{2}}{2} \tau_{e}$$

Using the same values for the parameters as before and setting $\sigma_{F}=\sigma_{p}=10^{-15} \mathrm{~cm}^{2}$ one obtains $\tau_{e} \sim 0.04$ sec. The factor multiplying $F(t)$ in the exponential is of order $10^{6} \mathrm{sec}^{-1}$ so that one must take the approximation $F(t) \sim t^{2} / 2 \tau_{e}$ to discuss the saturation time for $f$. One then finds a saturation time for the formation of $F$-centres of $\sim$ $(\beta v_{e} \sigma_{F})^{-1 / 2}$ which is $\sim 2.5 × 10^{-4}$ sec. Therefore all those isolated negative ion vacancies which are present in the crystal will quickly be converted to $F$-centres both when $N_{0 F}$ is large and when it is small. The second approximation to the solution of equations (2) holds if
$$N_{0 F} \ll \frac{n_{e} \sigma_{p}}{\sigma_{F} \mu l}$$
and if the saturation value of $n_{e}$ is taken, $N_{0 F}$ must certainly be appreciably less than
$$n_{e_{\text {sat }}} / \mu l \text { with } \sigma_{p} \sim \sigma_{F}.$$

The saturation value of $n_{e}$ in the absence of $F$-centre formation would be
$$\left\{\beta \mu l / \sigma_{p} v_{e}\right\}^{1 / 2}$$
so that $N_{0 F}$ must be less than
$$\left\{\beta \sigma_{p} / \sigma_{F}^{2} \mu l v_{e}\right\}^{1 / 2}$$
which is of the order of $10^{9} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$.

## 5. THE CREATION OF F-CENTRES BY ANION DISPLACEMENT AT ROOM TEMPERATURE
At very low temperatures halogen ions in alkali halides are displaced under X-irradiation by multiple ionization events and the displaced halogen atoms form $H$-centres as KÄNZIG and WoodRUFF $^{(14,15)}$ have shown experimentally using electron spin resonance techniques. They also found that the $H$-band disappears rapidly on warming to $40^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ in agreement with observations of MARKHAM and KONITZER (cf. Ref. 15). This implies that the activation energy for movement of the interstitial halogen is $\sim 0.1 \mathrm{eV}$ so that at room temperature a halogen ion displaced interstitially by e.g. multiple ionization will make $\sim 2 × 10^{11}$ jumps per sec. It will therefore remain an interstitial for a very short time before it is trapped. Possible traps are
(i) Lattice vacancies of either kind, positive ion or negative ion, with or without trapped holes or electrons,
(ii) Dislocations.

Interstitial halogen atoms will take up substi- tutional positions by occupying positive ion vacancies since this will remove the considerable strain energy accompanying an interstitial atom in the lattice. Dislocations will exert a force on an interstitial atom at a large number of lattice spacings from the dislocation core through the interaction of the strain fields round the dislocation and the interstitial.(52) Depending on the sign of the dislocation strain field at a given position in the lattice, interstitials will either be attracted or repelled from dislocations. Once in the core of the dislocations the displaced halogens will migrate along the core and will be trapped finally at dis- location jogs. Alternatively they may migrate along dislocations near the surface to the four faces of the crystal forming crystallites on the surface with the dislocation cores as nuclei. The experimental observations of HIBI and IsHIKAWA $^{(53)}$ (cf. also LEIDER $^{(54)}$ ) might be interpreted this way. Further, Yoshida and IKEDA $^{(55)}$ have observed in tense colour centre formation, also using electron microscope techniques, around small internal voids in NaCl. CRAWFORD and YOUNG $^{(56)}$ did not observe dislocation climb from surface observa- tions whereas they calculated that in NaCl an F-centre density of $4 ×10^{17} cm^{-3}$ should give3 x 10-4 cm climb per dislocation at a dislocation density of $5 ×10^{5} cm^{-2}$ . However, if interstitial atoms formed near the surface migrate along dis- locations to form fresh layers on the surface as HIBI and IsHIKAWA $^{(54)}$ have found, no dislocation climb will be observed by surface observations whereas it can still take place within the crystal. The different experimental observations are then not inconsistent.

In its migration through the lattice the inter- stitial atom will execute a random walk. The probability that it will make N jumps before it is trapped at a vacant lattice site such as a positive ornegative ion vacancy or a vacancy pair is $\exp (-N c)$  where c is the concentration of such sinks. The probability that it will travel a linear distance r from its initial position in making a random walk of N jumps, each of distance d, is $\exp (-r^{2} / 2 N d^{2})$ . If now one considers a single crystallite in a single crystal, this will be misoriented from its neighbour by a few minutes of arc and the boundary between two crystallites will be made up of an array of dis- locations (cf. Ref. 57, p. 409). Interstitials created within a crystallite of radius a, assuming for con- venience that it is a sphere, therefore have a probability

$$p=\frac{\omega}{4 \pi} \exp \left(-r^{2} / 2 N d^{2}\right) \exp (-N c) \quad (24)$$

of reaching the crystallite boundary in N jumps if they are created at a linear distance (r/d) from the boundary. $\omega$ is the solid angle subtended at the point of creation by the surface of intersection of a sphere of radius r about this point with the crystallite boundary.

It follows that if an interstitial is to reach theboundary with a sensible probability:

$$r \leqslant 2 N d^{2} k_{1}$$

and

$$N \leqslant k_{2} / c$$

i.e.

$$\frac{r \leqslant\left(2 k_{1} k_{2}\right)^{1 / 2}}{c^{1 / 2}}$$

where $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ are small integers. If the rate of creation of interstitials per unit volume is $\beta_{i}$ , then the number reaching the boundary per unit time from a small volume element $d \nabla$ about a point of creation a distance x from the centre of the crystal- lite is, upon evaluating $\omega / 4 \pi$ , found to be

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2} N_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} t} \sim \frac{\beta_{i} \mathrm{~d} v}{4 r x}\left\{(r+x)^{2}-a^{2}\right\} \quad (25)$$

where

$$r=q d c^{-1 / 2} \geqslant a-x$$

and q is a small number of order unity. The total number of interstitials reaching the crystallite boundary per unit time is then, integrating over the volume of the shell lying between (a-r) and a, given approximately by

$$N_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} t} \sim \beta_{i} \pi a^{2} q d c^{-1 / 2} \quad (26)$$

if $r \ll a$ . Typically $c 5 ×10^{-6}, a 10^{-4} cm$ and d ~ 4x10-8 cm giving

$$r / a=\frac{q d}{a c^{1 / 2}} \sim 0 \cdot 3,$$

so that the expression for $N_{i}$ is approximate.

Some of the interstitial halogen ions created in a shell of thickness r near the boundary of the crystallite will reach the dislocation forming the boundary at a rate $N_{i}'$ given by equation (26) above. Those interstitials which reach the boundary will be re- sponsible for the volume expansion which is found experimentally in X-irradiated alkali halides. It is reasonable that halogen atoms trapped in the dis- location cores (at jogs) will trap electrons and revert to halogen ions. If there is to be no charge build-up at the dislocations, which is a necessary condition for the process to continue, positive ions must be pulled into the dislocation core. In this way, even if halogen ions alone are displaced by irradiation, dislocation climb can occur with the simultaneous creation of further isolated negative and positive ion vacancies in a diffuse shell of thickness somewhat greater than r at the crystallite boundary. The positive ion vacancies have a jump frequency of $\sim 1 sec^{-1}$ at room temperature so that they will migrate a distance $10^{-5} cm$ from the crystallite boundary in ~ 18 hr-a time longer than the total irradiation time in many experi- ments. The positive ion vacancies will thus be confined within a shell of thickness r for several hours.

The negative vacancies are converted to F- centres in a very short time (cf. end of section 4) and the concentration of point defects within the shell of volume $4 \pi a^{2} r$ will increase at a rate
$$\frac{\mathrm{d} c}{\mathrm{~d} t}=\frac{2 N_{i}^{\prime}}{4 \pi a^{2} r}=\frac{\beta_{i}}{2 N_{0}}\quad(27)$$
the factor 2 accounting for both positive and negative ion vacancy production; $N_{0}$ is the number of ion pair sites per unit volume of crystal. Whether these vacancies have trapped holes/electrons or not they can still act as sinks to relieve the strain energy of interstitial halogen atoms. The solution of (27) with the initial condition $c=c_{0}$ , t = 0, is
$$c=c_{0}+\beta_{i} t / 2 N_{0}\quad(28)$$

Therefore, the total number of lattice ion pairs, $N_{p}$ , which have reached the crystallite boundary after time t is, combining (28) and (26),
$$N_{p}=\int N_{i}^{\prime} \mathrm{d} t=4 \pi a^{2} q N_{0} d\left\{\left(c_{0}+\frac{\beta_{i} t}{2 N_{0}}\right)^{1 / 2}-c_{0}^{1 / 2}\right\}$$

The volume of the crystallite is $4 \pi a^{3} / 3$ and the volume of an ion pair in the NaCl type lattice is $2 a_{0}^{3}$ where $a_{0}$ is the closest anion-cation distance and d, the jump distance in the above equations equals $a_{0}$ . $$\frac{\Delta l}{l}=\frac{1}{3} \frac{\Delta V}{V}=\frac{a_{0} q}{a}\left[\left(c_{0}+\frac{\beta_{i} t}{2 N_{0}}\right)^{1 / 2}-c_{0}^{1 / 2}\right] \quad(30)$$

For small times this becomes
$$\frac{\Delta l}{l} \sim \frac{a_{0} q \beta_{i} t}{4 a N_{0} c_{0}^{1 / 2}} ; \quad t \ll \frac{2 N_{0} c_{0}}{\beta_{1}}\quad(31)$$

For a rate $\beta_{i}=2 ×10^{16} cm^{-3} t^{-1}, N_{0} 2 ×10^{22}$ cm-3, ao ~ 3x10-8 cm, a ~ 10-4 cm, co~5x10-6 and arbitrarily setting g = 4 one obtains from (31) for the linear dilatation in one hour
$$\frac{\Delta l}{l} \sim 1.5 × 10^{-7}$$

This is somewhat smaller than RABIN's results $^{(58)}$  which lie in the region $10^{-6}$ after one hr. One implication is that $\beta_{i}$ is larger at room temperature than it is at liquid helium temperature-the value chosen above. This will be discussed later. It will be seen that the dilatation is more sensitive to variations in dislocation density through plastic deformation and hence to the value of a than to changes in impurity concentration $c_{0}$ .

The number of negative ion vacancies generated per unit volume after time t is similarly
$$N_{v}=\frac{3 N_{p}}{4 \pi a^{3}}=\frac{3 q a_{0} N_{0}}{a}\left[\left(c_{0}+\frac{\beta_{i} t}{2 N_{0}}\right)^{1 / 2}-c_{0}^{1 / 2}\right] \quad(32)$$
and this will equal the number of F-centres generated by virtue of those halogen ions which reach the crystallite boundaries. The ratio
$$\frac{\Delta l}{N_{v} l}=\frac{1}{3 N_{0}}$$

and this is found to hold experimentally⁽⁵⁸, ⁵⁹⁾ after an initial period when

$$
\frac{\Delta l}{N_{v} l}>\frac{1}{3 N_{0}}
$$

Thus in the initial stages more $F$-centres form than are commensurate with the measured dilata- tion, which latter is smaller than expected if one vacancy pair were created for each $F$-centre formed.

The behaviour in the early stages of irradiation is sensitive to the degree of purity and depends, as many authors have discussed, on the conversion of negative ion vacancies already in the crystal (isolated or as neutral vacancy pairs) to $F$-centres. This has already been considered above. Apart from this electron trapping by negative ion vacan- cies there is another mechanism operative whereby further $F$-centres will be formed during the early stages of irradiation. This is through the capture of interstitial halogen atoms formed in the body of the crystallite by positive ion vacancies initially present in the crystal.

Let $n_{+}$and $n_{-}$be the number of positive and negative ion vacancies per unit volume at time $t$ and let $n_{i}$ be the number of interstitial halogens. The following relations hold:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} n_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} t} & =\beta_{i}-\frac{\nu Z_{1}}{N_{0}} n_{i}+\left(n_{+}+n_{-}\right) \\
\frac{\mathrm{d} n_{+}}{\mathrm{d} t} & =-\nu Z_{1} \frac{n_{i} n_{+}}{N_{0}} \\
\frac{\mathrm{d} n_{-}}{\mathrm{d} t} & =\beta_{i}-\nu Z_{1} \frac{n_{i} n_{-}}{N_{0}} \\
n_{+}+n_{-} & =n_{0+}+n_{0-}+n_{i}
\end{aligned} \quad(33)
$$

where the last relation expresses the fact that no interstitials escape from the body of the crystallite. Equations (33) thus apply to all the crystallite volume save the shell of thickness $r$. $Z_{1}$ is an effective co-ordination number for the trapping of an interstitial by a vacant lattice site and $\nu$ is the interstitial jump frequency. $n_{0+}$ and $n_{0-}$ are initial densities of positive and negative ion vacancies.

The jump frequency of the interstitials is so large $(\sim 10^{11} \mathrm{sec}^{-1})$ at room temperature that the quasi steady state approximation is valid viz.

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} n_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} t} \sim 0,
$$

$n_{i} \ll n_{0+}+n_{0-}$ save at negligibly small initial times. Under these conditions the solution of equations (33) gives

$$
n_{+}=n_{0+} \exp \left[-\beta_{i} t /\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right)\right] \quad(34 \mathrm{a})
$$

$$
n_{-}=n_{0-}+n_{0+}\left(1-\exp \left\{-\beta_{i} t /\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right)\right\}\right\} \quad(34 \mathrm{~b})
$$

The half-life for the conversion of positive to negative ion vacancies is thus

$$
\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right) / \beta_{i} \text {. }
$$

For a typical rate of creation $\beta_{i}$ of interstitials of $10^{16} / \mathrm{hr} \mathrm{cm}^{3}$ in $\mathrm{NaCl}$ and with $\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right) \sim 10^{17} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ saturation of this effect will occur in $\sim 10 \mathrm{hr}$. The X-ray energy absorbed per $F$-centre created, once the initial fast stage has saturated, at room tem- perature is essentially the same as the energy per $F$-centre formed in unirradiated crystals at liquid helium temperature.⁽¹²⁾ For $\mathrm{KCl}$ this energy is a factor ten less than for $\mathrm{NaCl}$ so that for the same rate of absorption of X-ray energy $\beta_{i}$ will be a factor $\sim 10$ greater for $\mathrm{KCl}$ than for $\mathrm{NaCl}$. Thus for $\mathrm{KCl}$ saturation would occur in $\sim 1 \mathrm{hr}$ in the above example.

It was mentioned above that $\beta_{i}$ at room tem- perature is greater than at very low temperatures near absolute zero. This might be expected when account is taken of thermal activation which will help to move multiply ionized anions away from their lattice sites. Equation 34(b) gives

$$
n_{-}=\frac{n_{0+}}{n_{0+}+n_{0-}} \beta_{i} t

for short times. Therefore after the initial con- version of negative ion vacancies initially in the crystal to $F$-centres in a short time (a few minutes) the creation of further $F$-centres resulting from the formation of fresh negative ion vacancies will pro- ceed at a rate

which could be as small as $\beta_{i} / 2$. The room tem- perature value of the energy absorbed per $F$-centre formed would then be twice the very low tem- perature value. Thermal activation would tend to

make these values more nearly equal by increasing the room temperature value of $\beta_i$. Experimentally the ratio of the energies absorbed per $F$-centre formed is near unity in support of these arguments. Therefore $\beta_i$ for room temperature is probably nearer twice the low temperature value and hence the calculated value of $\Delta l / l$ will be $\sim 3 \times 10^{-7}$ in one hr. The discrepancy between theory and experiment is then reduced and may possibly lie in the uncertainty of the value of $a$.

Finally one must consider the effect upon $F$-centre creation of introducing a number of isolated positive ion vacancies into the sub-grains equal to the number of halogen atoms trapped at dislocations. The average concentration, $c$, of isolated vacancies of both sign in the annular region is given by equation (28). $$

$$
n_{+}=n_{0+}\left\{1+\beta_{i} t / 4\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right)\right\}^{-2} \quad (36)
$$

Note that (35) differs from (33) in that one must allow for the increasing concentration of isolated point defects within the annulus, whereas it is constant within the body of the crystallite; hence the differing relations (34a) and (36) between $n_{+}$and $t$ for the two regions.

The direct rate of creation of negative ion vacancies and hence of $F$-centres per unit volume is $\beta_{i} / 4$, which follows from (26) above. Differentiating (36), the indirect rate of $F$-centre production through the capture of halogen atoms by continuously generated positive ion vacancies in the peripheral region of a sub-grain is
$$
-\frac{\mathrm{d} n_{+}}{\mathrm{d} t}=\frac{n_{0+} \beta_{i}}{\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right)}\left\{1+\beta_{i} t / 2\left(n_{0+}+n_{0-}\right)\right\}^{-3} \quad (37)
$$

It follows that for times appreciably greater than
$$2(n_{0+}+n_{0-})/\beta_i$$
the ratio of the indirect to the direct $F$-centre production rate is proportional to $t^{-3}$. Thus for long times those positive ion vacancies initially present have all captured neutral anions so that essentially only negative ion vacancies are present apart from the small number of positive ion vacancies which are continuously generated. Hence the rate of further capture of neutral anions by positive ion vacancies varies as $t^{-3}$ while the rate of fresh negative ion vacancy formation remains constant. Therefore after an initial period one $F$-centre will be formed for each vacancy pair created, as is found experimentally.

## 6. SUMMARY
$F$-centre formation at room temperature can occur by three mechanisms.

(i) Electrons are trapped in negative ion vacancies initially in the crystal either isolated or combined with positive ion vacancies. The experimental saturation time for this mechanism is of the order of a few minutes.

(ii) Negative ion vacancies are created by multiple ionization events causing the ejection of halogen ions interstitially with the subsequent trapping of the displaced ions in positive ion vacancies. The centre composed of a halogen atom in a positive ion vacancy is possibly the $V_{3}$ centre $^{(17)}$ and has the qualitative properties of thermal stability and resistance to optical bleaching in heavily irradiated crystals as observed experimentally. The calculated saturation time for this process is $\sim 1 \mathrm{hr}$ in $\mathrm{KCl}$ and $\sim 10 \mathrm{hr}$ in $\mathrm{NaCl}$ for typical cases. Complementary "hard" $F$-centres are produced by this mechanism.

(iii) Mechanism (ii) applies within the body of crystallites and therefore throughout the major part of the crystal volume. In a shell of $\sim 10^{-5} \mathrm{~cm}$ thickness near the boundaries of the crystallites some of the halogen ions displaced by multiple ionization can reach the sub-grain boundaries. This process continues over long times and for large $t$ is proportional to $t^{1 / 2}$. It can account quantitatively reasonably well with experiment for the continued formation of $F$-centres over long times and for the volume dilatation which is observed. $F$-centres both

stable and unstable to optical bleaching (hard and soft) will be produced by this mechanism, the latter predominating at long times.

At very low temperatures interstitial halogen atoms are found experimentally with complementary $F$-centres. Multiple ionization theory predicts reasonably well the rate of formation of these centres, i.e. the X-ray energy absorbed per $F$-centre created.

The models presented above all have the defect of predicting only $F$-centre/time curves with a negative curvature, whereas the experimental results of MITCHELL, WIEGARD and SMOLUCHOW$\operatorname{SKI}^{(37)}$ show a positive curvature, particularly at low dose rates, in the region between the saturation of the fast forming $F$-centre mechanisms (Stage 1) and the slow forming mechanism (Stage 2). It is suggested that the reason for this may lie in an incubation period during which the internal stresses are increasing in such a way as to increase the density of traps for interstitial halogens reaching dislocation networks at the sub-grain boundaries.
