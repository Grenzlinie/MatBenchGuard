# Stability of $AB'_{1/2}B''_{1/2}O_3$-Type Mixed Perovskite Proton Conductors

Sanjeevani V. Bhide* and Anil V. Virkar**

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, Utah 84112-0560, USA

Several mixed perovskites of the type $A_2B'B''O_6$ ($AB'_{1/2}B''_{1/2}O_3$), where A is $Ba^{+2}$ and B' and B'' are trivalent and pentavalent ions, respectively, were synthesized by calcining requisite mixtures of $BaCO_3$, $B'_2O_3$, and $B''_2O_3$ in air. X-ray diffraction confirmed the formation of the perovskite phase in each of the samples synthesized. The as-calcined powders were boiled in water to examine their stabilities in water. All mixed perovskites were found to be stable. By contrast, $BaCeO_3$ and $BaPrO_3$ decomposed when boiled in water while $BaZrO_3$ was found to be stable. Madelung energies of simple and mixed perovskites were computed. The higher stability of mixed perovskites compared to simple perovskites of the type $ABO_3$, such as $BaCeO_3$ and $BaPrO_3$, where B is a tetravalent ion, was rationalized on the basis of lower Madelung energies of mixed perovskites.

Perovskites such as $BaZrO_3$, $SrZrO_3$, $BaCeO_3$, and $SrCeO_3$ when doped with a rare-earth oxide are known to be proton conductors at elevated temperatures ($\geq600^\circ$C). $^{1-15}$ There is considerable interest in these perovskite proton conductors for high-temperature applications which include fuel cells, hydrogen sensors, steam electrolyzers for hydrogen production, hydrogen sulfide electrolyzers for desulfuration, HCl electrolyzers for $Cl_2$ recovery, hydrogenation or dehydrogenation of organic compounds, hydrogen extractors, hydrogen pumps, and hydrogen gas controllers. $^{15}$

The perovskites by themselves are not proton conductors. For these perovskites to exhibit proton conduction, they must be doped with aliovalent dopants to create oxygen vacancies. In addition, the presence of water is essential for the incorporation of protons and their conduction. An aliovalent dopant can create oxygen vacancies, as given by

$$2\text{BaO} + \text{Gd}_2\text{O}_3 \to 2\text{Ba}_{\text{Ba}}^\times + 2\text{Gd}_{\text{Ce}}' + 5\ \text{O}_{\text{O}}^\times + \text{V}_{\text{O}}^{\bullet\bullet} \tag{1}$$

When such a perovskite with oxygen vacancies present is heated in a water-containing environment, nearly free protons are formed by the absorption of water given by

$$\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{V}_{\text{O}}^{\bullet\bullet} + \text{O}_{\text{O}}^\times \to 2\ \text{OH}_{\text{O}}^\bullet \tag{2}$$

This is the origin of proton conduction in these perovskites. However, widespread application of these proton conductors requires that two important factors be satisfactorily addressed. These are (i) protonic conductivity and (ii) stability in the application environment. In a high-temperature fuel cell, the gaseous species present in the environment are $CO$, $CO_2$, $O_2$, $H_2$, and $H_2O$. Thus, the electrolyte material used in this application should be a good proton conductor and should be stable in the environment. Considerable research effort has been devoted toward identifying materials exhibiting a high proton conductivity. However, the second requirement concerning material stability in the application environment has received limited scrutiny. Thermodynamic stability, or at least long-term kinetic stability, of the material in the application environment is essential to ensure reliable performance and long service life of the device.

Of the perovskite-based materials investigated to date, 20 mol % Gd-doped $BaCeO_3$ is known to exhibit the highest protonic conductivity. $^{4,7-9}$ Many researchers have examined protonic conductivity of $BaCeO_3$ containing several dopants at various concentrations, but very few studies of stability have been reported. Thermodynamic measurements have shown that $BaCeO_3$ is unstable with respect to the constituent oxides, $BaO$ and $CeO_2$, below about $630^\circ$C. $^{16}$ That is, below $\sim630^\circ$C, the $\Delta G^\circ$ for the reaction

$$\text{BaCeO}_3 \to \text{BaO} + \text{CeO}_2 \tag{3}$$

is negative. In addition, in the presence of water, the reaction

$$\text{BaCeO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{Ba(OH)}_2 + \text{CeO}_2 \tag{4}$$

at $\sim$430 Torr water pressure is favored below $\sim$1000°C. $^{17}$ The equilibrium temperature is a function of composition and $p_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$. For example, $BaCeO_3$ doped with 20 mol % Gd has been shown to be stable at temperatures greater than $600^\circ$C in a moist environment at a $p_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \approx 0.5$ atm. However, when boiled in liquid water, it rapidly decomposes. $^{18}$ Recent work has also shown that 20 mol % Gd-doped $BaCeO_3$ is unstable in water vapor ($p_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = 0.0964$ atm) at $200^\circ$C. $^{19}$ The report of decomposition of $BaCeO_3$ when heated in liquid water$^{18,19}$ or water vapor$^{19}$ is a concern if it is to find practical applications. This is because devices such as fuel cells are expected to undergo several heating and cooling cycles during their useful life. Thus, despite its stability at elevated temperature ($600^\circ$C), degradation is expected at lower temperatures. The damage caused by the decomposition of $BaCeO_3$ at lower temperatures is cumulative, because the reverse reaction (the formation of dense $BaCeO_3$) would require resintering, a process that cannot be accomplished at temperatures typically lower than $\sim$1500°C, which is well above the operating temperature range of all the devices of interest. Recent work has also shown that once water is absorbed into the structure at elevated temperatures, the kinetics of subsequent degradation at lower temperatures may become accelerated. $^{20}$ The preceding suggests that there is a need for stable, high-temperature proton conductors for numerous potential applications.

The perovskite structure is also known to occur in materials wherein either the A or B site (or both) from the $ABO_3$ lattice is (are) replaced by two or more ions of different valences while still maintaining the average valence consistent with the site. For example, several materials of the type $AB'_{1/2}B''_{1/2}O_3$ or $AB'_{1/3}B''_{2/3}O_3$, where B' is a trivalent ion, B'' a pentavalent ion, B* a divalent ion, and A a divalent ion, are known to crystallize in the perovskite structure. Oxygen vacancies can be introduced by an appropriate deviation in stoichiometry on the B sublattice. Nowick and co-workers$^{21-25}$ examined proton conduction in a number of perovskites. For example, Liang *et al.*$^{25}$ have examined protonic conduction in mixed perovskites of type $A_2B'_{(1+x)}B''_{(1-x)}O_{(6-\delta)}$ with A being Ba or Sr, B' a trivalent ion, and B'' a pentavalent ion, and in compounds of the type $A_3B'_{(1+x)}B''_{(2-x)}O_{(9-\delta)}$ with B* a divalent ion, and B'' a pentavalent ion. Nowick and Du$^{23}$ reported that off-stoichiometric $Ba_3CaNb_2O_9$ [$BaCa_{1/3}Nb_{2/3}O_3$] exhibits higher protonic conductivity as compared to Nd-doped $BaCeO_3$. However, with the exception of $Ba_3CaNb_2O_9$, protonic conductivity of the mixed perovskites was lower than that of doped $BaCeO_3$ despite a wide range in lattice parameters. Nowick and Liang$^{22}$ also reported similar results. However, the observation that off-stoichiometric $Ba_3(CaNb_2)O_9$ exhibits good protonic conductivity is encouraging in that there may be other mixed perovskites which are also good proton conductors.

Similar to $BaCeO_3$, an important question concerns the stability of mixed perovskites with respect to the constituent oxides and in the application environments. It would be of interest to know if the mixed perovskites exhibit higher or lower stability with respect to the constituent oxides and in water-containing atmospheres compared to $BaCeO_3$. The stability of the perovskite structure has often been rationalized in terms of radii of the ions comprising the unit cell using a parameter known as the Goldschmidt tolerance factor, $t$, which is defined as

$$
t = \frac{r_{\text{A}} + r_{\text{O}}}{\sqrt{2}(r_{\text{B}} + r_{\text{O}})} \tag{5}
$$

where $r_{\text{A}}$, $r_{\text{B}}$, and $r_{\text{O}}$ are the ionic radii of A, B, and O ions, respectively. For example, Navrotskii, $^{26}$ Gramsch and Morss, $^{27}$ Morss and Mensi, $^{28}$ and Kreuer $^{29}$ have explained the stability of many perovskites in terms of the tolerance factor. The tolerance factor, which is a measure of ionic packing, is 1.0 for the ideal perovskite structure. Most perovskites deviate from this ideal value. However, it is known that the closer the $t$ is to unity, the greater is the expected stability of the perovskite structure. For example, $BaTiO_3$ has a tolerance factor above 0.97 and is stable over a wide temperature range, from room temperature to its melting point.$^{\text{a}}$ Its stability at low temperatures can be confirmed using low-temperature molten-salt synthesis techniques. For example, it can be shown that $BaTiO_3$ can be readily formed starting with $BaO$ (or $BaCO_3$) and $TiO_2$ in a molten NaOH-KOH bath at temperatures on the order of 350 to $400^\circ\text{C}$. $^{30}$ $BaCeO_3$, on the other hand, has a tolerance factor of ~0.89. Experimental work has shown that $BaCeO_3$ is stable above about $630^\circ\text{C}$ with respect to the constituent oxides but not below. Instability at low temperatures has been demonstrated using molten-salt experiments which showed that $BaCeO_3$ cannot be formed from $BaO$ (or $BaCO_3$) and $CeO_2$ in a molten salt bath at temperatures on the order of $400^\circ\text{C}$. On the contrary, $BaCeO_3$ powder made by a high-temperature $(1250^\circ\text{C})$ calcination of precursors readily decomposes when placed into a molten-salt bath, thus confirming its instability at low temperatures. $^{16}$ Galvanic cell measurements with alkaline earth fluoride ion conductors have also shown that $BaCeO_3$ is unstable at low temperatures. $^{16}$

An important factor which determines the stability of a structure is its cohesive energy. In compounds with predominantly ionic bonding, the cohesive energy consists of two terms: $(i)$ Madelung energy arising due to electrostatic interactions and $(ii)$ repulsive, short-range interactions. The repulsive contribution is typically ~10% of the Madelung energy (in magnitude). As a result, the cohesive energy is ~90% of the Madelung energy. For the $\text{A}^{+2}\text{B}^{+4}\text{O}_3^{-2}$ perovskite, the Madelung energy (a negative quantity) is inversely proportional to the lattice parameter. Thus, the smaller the lattice parameter, the greater should be the stability. $BaTiO_3$ with a lattice parameter of $4.031\ \text{\AA}$ is expected to be more stable than $BaCeO_3$ with a lattice parameter of $4.377\ \text{\AA}$. Nakamura $^{31}$ and Kimizuka et al. $^{32}$ have examined the stability of a number of perovskites on the basis of their Madelung energies.

In the mixed perovskites under consideration here, the B-site is occupied by two different ions. A tolerance factor may then be defined in terms of the average radius of ions occupying the B-site. Also, the B-site is occupied by ions of different valences. Thus, it would appear that this should also lead to differences in the electrostatic interaction energy and may influence the stability apart from factors related to ionic sizes, i.e., apart from the tolerance factor. In order to investigate the stability of these perovskites, some simple Madelung energy calculations were performed. The rationale for the calculations is first described using a simple, one-dimensional lattice.

### Madelung Energy Calculations

The Madelung energy of a crystal is the electrostatic interaction energy between the constituent ions when they are brought from infinity to the equilibrium separation to form a crystal. The electrostatic interaction potential energy between two charged species is given by

$$
U_{\text{ij}} = \frac{z_{\text{i}}z_{\text{j}}e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_{\text{o}}|r_{\text{ij}}|} \tag{6}
$$

where $z_{\text{i}}$ and $z_{\text{j}}$ (sign included in $z_{\text{i}}$ and $z_{\text{j}}$) are the valences of species i and j, respectively, $e$ is the electronic charge, $\epsilon_{\text{o}}$ is the permittivity of free space, and $|r_{\text{ij}}|$ is the magnitude of the distance between the two ions. The Madelung energy is simply the sum $\Sigma U_{\text{ij}}$ and is a finite, negative number consistent with an electrically neutral crystal. The nonelectrostatic repulsive interaction decays much more rapidly with separation and can usually be given in terms of the nearest-neighbor distance and the so-called hardness parameter. $^{33}$ As mentioned earlier, this contribution is typically ~10% of the Madelung energy (in magnitude) and thus, the cohesive energy is ~90% of the Madelung energy. Thus, it is expected that the Madelung energy should provide a basis to compare relative stabilities of different materials belonging to a given structure type.

Simple Madelung energy calculations were performed for a hypothetical linear crystal to determine its dependence on the spatial distribution of charges. For a linear crystal shown in Fig. 1a, which consists of ions with charge $+ze$ and $-ze$ ($z$ is a positive integer) placed on alternate sites at an interionic distance of $r_{\text{o}}$, the Madelung energy per ion is readily shown to be given by$^{34}$

$$
E_{\text{Madelung}} = -\alpha_1 \frac{z^2e^2}{r_{\text{o}}} \tag{7}
$$

where $\alpha_1$ is the Madelung constant given by

$$
\alpha_1 = 2\ \text{ln}\ 2 \tag{8}
$$

Alternatively, the Madelung constant may also be defined including the valence $z$ as

$$
E_{\text{Madelung}} = -\alpha_1^{\text{M}} \frac{e^2}{r_{\text{o}}} \tag{9}
$$

where

$$
\alpha_1^{\text{M}} = z^2\ (2\ \text{ln}\ 2) \tag{10}
$$

Consider now a linear crystal wherein there are two cations of valence $z+\delta$ and $z-\delta$ (with $\delta \le z$) on alternate cation sites, and there are two anions of valence $-z+\lambda$ and $-z-\lambda$ (with $\lambda \le z$) on alternate anion sites as shown in Fig. 1b. In this case, it can be easily shown that the Madelung energy, defined per ion, is given by$^{20}$

$$E_{\text{Madelung}} = -\alpha_2 \frac{z^2 e^2}{r_{\text{o}}} = -\left\{1 + \frac{(\delta^2 + \lambda^2)}{4z^2}\right\}(2 \ln 2)\frac{z^2 e^2}{r_{\text{o}}} \quad [11]$$

That is, the Madelung constant is given by
$$\alpha_2 = \left\{1 + \frac{(\delta^2 + \lambda^2)}{4z^2}\right\}(2 \ln 2) \tag{12}$$
or
$$E_{\text{Madelung}} = -\alpha_2^{\text{M}} \frac{z^2 e^2}{r_{\text{o}}} = -\left\{z^2 + \frac{(\delta^2 + \lambda^2)}{4}\right\}(2 \ln 2)\frac{e^2}{r_{\text{o}}} \quad [13]$$
where
$$\alpha_2^{\text{M}} = \left\{z^2 + \frac{(\delta^2 + \lambda^2)}{4}\right\}(2 \ln 2) \tag{14}$$
which is clearly greater than $\alpha_1^{\text{M}} = z^2 (2 \ln 2)$ for a linear crystal with cations of valence $z$ and anions of valence $-z$. If the anions are of a fixed valence (i.e., $-z$) but cations are of valence $z + \delta$ and $z - \delta$, the Madelung constant would be
$$\alpha_2^{\text{M}} = \left\{z^2 + \frac{\delta^2}{4}\right\}(2 \ln 2) \tag{15}$$
again greater than $\alpha_1^{\text{M}} = z^2$ $(2 \ln 2)$. A larger Madelung constant where cations and/or anions of two different valences occupy the respective sites indicates a greater stability of such a structure. Clearly, the larger the $\delta$, the greater should be the stability.

The reason for this enhanced stability can be traced to the fact that the electrostatic repulsive interaction between ions of valence $z + \delta$ and $z - \delta$ is proportional to $(z^2 - \delta^2)$ and that between ions of valence $(-z + \lambda)$ and $(-z - \lambda)$ is proportional to $(z^2 - \lambda^2)$. Thus, the electrostatic repulsive interaction is lower than in the case where all cations have the same valence, viz. $z$, and all anions have the same valence, i.e., $-z$, in which case it is proportional to $z^2$. However, the attractive interaction between the ions of opposite signs remains unaltered; in the former case it is proportional to $(z + \delta)\{-z + \lambda - z - \lambda\} + (z - \delta)\{-z + \lambda - z - \lambda\}$ which is $-2z^2$, the same as in the latter case. The net effect is that the Madelung energy is lower (|Madelung energy| is higher), i.e., a larger Madelung constant when the cation of valence $z$ is replaced by cations of valence $z + \delta$ and $z - \delta$ on alternate cation sites and/or the anion of valence $-z$ is replaced by anions of valence a d $(-z + \lambda)$ and $(-z - \lambda)$ on alternate anion sites.

This simple logic can now easily be extended to a three-dimensional lattice. Consider a perovskite of the type $\text{AB}'_{1/2}\text{B}''_{1/2}\text{O}_3$ where the valences of $\text{B}'$ and $\text{B}''$ are +3 and +5, respectively. The valence on the B site, however, averages out to +4 as in the usual $\text{ABO}_3$-type perovskite. Similar results are also expected for a perovskite structure of the type $\text{AB}^*_{1/3}\text{B}''_{2/3}\text{O}_3$ with the valences of $\text{B}^*$ and $\text{B}''$ being +2 and +5, respectively. In order to verify this, a calculation was also performed for $\text{BaCa}_{1/3}\text{Nb}_{2/3}\text{O}_3$. In both cases, the Madelung energy is lower (|Madelung energy| is higher) than their counterparts of the type $\text{ABO}_3$ having the same lattice parameters. Here, it is expected that the attractive interaction between $\text{B}'$ & $\text{O}$ and $\text{B}''$ & $\text{O}$, or $\text{B}^*$ & $\text{O}$ and $\text{B}''$ & $\text{O}$ would average out to be the same as between $\text{B}$ and $\text{O}$. But, at the same time, the electrostatic repulsive interaction between $\text{B}'$ and $\text{B}''$ or $\text{B}^*$ and $\text{B}''$ would be lower than that between $\text{B}$ and $\text{B}$. The electrostatic repulsive interaction between $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}'$ & $\text{B}''$, or between $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}^*$ & $\text{B}''$, however, would be the same as that between $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}$. That is, a reduction in the Madelung energy is the result of ions of differing valences on the same sublattice. The net effect still would be lower Madelung energy (higher |Madelung energy|), i.e., higher Madelung constants for the mixed perovskites (due to a lower repulsive interaction between $\text{B}'$ and $\text{B}''$ or between $\text{B}^*$ and $\text{B}''$ as compared to that between $\text{B}$ and $\text{B}$) resulting in a more stable compound.

In order to estimate the Madelung energies of mixed perovskites, a program in C language was developed. The following steps were used in calculating the Madelung energy

1. The crystal was simulated using an equivalent cubic lattice parameter.
2. The two B-site elements were dispersed randomly or in an ordered manner, as desired, for the mixed perovskites.
3. In the case of doped perovskites with an average B-site valence less than four, the dopant was randomly dispersed in appropriate proportions and oxygen vacancies were added to maintain charge neutrality and site balance.
4. Finally, the electrostatic interactions were calculated by summing over a crystal large enough so that convergence of the Madelung energy to the second decimal (in electron volts) was obtained.

The difference in the energies is on the order of $-124\ \text{kJ/mol}$, which is large enough to influence the stability of the material.

These calculations show that the Madelung constant in the mixed perovskite of the type $\text{AB}'_{1/2}\text{B}''_{1/2}\text{O}_3$ where $\text{B}' = +3$ and $\text{B}'' = +5$, is $-166.94/-165.64 = 1.008$ times higher than for the simple $\text{ABO}_3$ perovskite. For a linear crystal with $z = 4$ and $\delta = 1$, the expected ratio of the Madelung constants for the mixed and simple compounds is $(1 + 1/64) = 1.016$. The observation that the calculated ratio of the Madelung constants for the perovskites is smaller than that for the linear crystal can be attributed to the following two reasons

1. The perovskite lattice contains two cations and one anion. Of the three sublattices, ions with multiple valences were assumed to be on only one sublattice (B). As a result, only the repulsive electrostatic interaction among ions on the B-site is different (lower) in the mixed perovskites compared to $\text{ABO}_3$. However, the repulsive electrostatic interaction between ions on the $\text{A}$ and $\text{B}$ sublattices remains unaltered. For this reason, the decrease in Madelung energy is lower than in the case of a simple, linear crystal with one cation and one anion.
2. The real perovskite structure is three-dimensional and its (relative) Madelung constant cannot be expected to be in a quantitative agreement with that for a simple, linear crystal.

## Experimental
Sample preparation.—Doped $BaCeO_3$ powders of stoichiometry: $BaCe_{1-x}RE_xO_{3-\delta}$ were made, where RE is the rare earth aliovalent dopant added and $x$ is the site fraction occupied by the dopant. Gd and La were used as the aliovalent dopants with $x$ varying between 0.05 and 0.3. $BaCO_3$, $CeO_2$, and the dopants in their respective oxide forms were used as the starting materials. These materials were mixed in requisite proportions and ballmilled for 24 h. The powder mixtures were then calcined in air at $1450^\circ$C for 6 h.

Doped $BaPrO_3$ powders were prepared using the same doping strategy as was used for $BaCeO_3$. The dopant used in this case was Gd. $BaCO_3$, $Pr_6O_{11}$, and $Gd_2O_3$ were used as the starting materials. The ballmilled powder mixture was calcined at $1350^\circ$C for 1 h.

For the mixed-perovskite type of compounds, the mixing strategy used was $AB'_{1/2}B''_{1/2}O_3$. Ba was the divalent element A, with Er, Gd, Yb, or La as the trivalent element (B'), and Ta or Nb as the pentavalent element (B''). The materials from this group were made by mixing $BaCO_3$ and the respective oxides ($Gd_2O_3$ and $Nb_2O_5$ in the case of $BaGd_{1/2}Nb_{1/2}O_3$) in proper proportions and calcining them at $1450^\circ$C for 6 h.

Stability in a water-containing atmosphere.—All synthesized powders were boiled in water for a period between a few hours to a few days to study their stabilities.

Materials characterization.—All the powders used in this study were first examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu $K\alpha$ radiation, to ensure the formation of a single-phase perovskite. Also, the stability of perovskites was investigated by examining the powders using XRD after exposure to water and then analyzing the patterns.

## Results and Discussion
All the doped $BaCeO_3$ powders used in the present study when boiled in water for a period of few hours, typically 3-6 h, decomposed into $Ba(OH)_2$ and $CeO_2$ (doped $CeO_2$ in the case of doped $BaCeO_3$), as reported previously.$^{19}$ An XRD pattern of 10 mol % Gd-doped powder boiled in water is shown in Fig. 3. Also, 15 mol % Gd-doped $BaPrO_3$ was found to be unstable in boiling water as shown in Fig. 4. However, all the mixed perovskites synthesized were found to be stable when boiled in water for a period of 5 days. Figure 5 provides XRD traces of $BaGd_{1/2}Nb_{1/2}O_3$ in the as-calcined state as well as after boiling in water for 15 days. It is readily seen that the material is indeed stable.

As mentioned earlier, several researchers have used the Goldschmidt tolerance factor to explain the stability of perovskites. The tolerance factors for the simple and mixed perovskites are listed in Table II. For the calculation of tolerance factors for mixed perovskites, the radius for the B-site ion was taken as the arithmetic average of radii of B' and B'' ions. $BaZrO_3$ and $BaTiO_3$ with tolerance factors of 0.92 and 0.97, respectively, are stable with respect to the constituent oxides over a wide temperature range and in boiling water. By contrast, $BaCeO_3$ and $BaPrO_3$ both with a tolerance factor of about 0.89 are observed to be unstable with respect to constituent oxides (as evidenced by their decomposition in a molten salt bath) and in boiling water. It is to be noted that $BaCeO_3$ and $BaPrO_3$ also have substantially higher Madelung energies (lower |Madelung energies|) compared to $BaTiO_3$ and $BaZrO_3$. Thermodynamic measurements have shown that $BaCeO_3$ is unstable with respect to the constituent oxides below about $630^\circ$C. $^{16}$ An examination of Table II shows that all mixed perovskites are stable in boiling water. Several of the mixed perovskites have tolerance factors about the same as $BaCeO_3$ and $BaPrO_3$. For example, $BaGd_{1/2}Nb_{1/2}O_3$ has about the same tolerance factor as $BaPrO_3$; the former is stable in water while the latter is not. In addition, recent work has shown that $BaGd_{1/2}Nb_{1/2}O_3$ can be synthesized in a molten salt at $350^\circ$C from precursors but $BaPrO_3$ cannot.$^{36}$ That is, at $350^\circ$C, the $\Delta G^\circ$ from the reaction

$$

As mentioned earlier, off-stoichiometric $\mathrm{Ba}_3\mathrm{CaNb}_2\mathrm{O}_9$ $[\mathrm{Ba}_3\mathrm{Ca}_{(1+x)}\mathrm{Nb}_{(2-x)}\mathrm{O}_{(9-\delta)}]$ exhibits excellent proton conductivity.$^{22}$ The present calculations show that its Madelung energy is lower than a simple $\mathrm{ABO}_3$ perovskite of the same lattice parameter. Recent work has shown that it can be readily synthesized using the molten salt method, thus confirming its low-temperature stability.$^{36}$

Table III gives the calculated Madelung energies of Gd-doped and La-doped $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ as a function of composition. It is seen that the Madelung energy increases (|Madelung energy| decreases) with an increasing dopant concentration, consistent with expectations based on electrostatic interactions. Thus, based on the Madelung energy considerations alone, it is expected that the addition of dopants may not enhance the stability of $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$. There are other factors, however, which may influence the stability. These include the following: (i) nearest-neighbor interactions. The nearest-neighbor, short-range interactions may be different in the doped samples and may affect stability. That is, the hardness factor in general may be different for doped materials. (ii) Configurational entropy. In the doped samples, the configurational entropy arises due to a random mixing of Ce and the dopant (Gd or La) on the B-sublattice, and due to a random mixing of oxygen ions and oxygen vacancies on the O-sublattice. This configurational entropy thus should have a stabilizing influence on the structure, and especially at elevated temperatures. Notwithstanding these contributions, based on the Madelung energy alone, it is expected that the addition of the dopants need not necessarily increase the stability of $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$. It was indeed observed that all doped powders of $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ when boiled in water readily decomposed, in agreement with expectations based on the Madelung energy considerations. The observation that doped $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ powders are unstable in boiling water can also be readily rationalized in terms of simple solution-theory arguments. When a dopant (solute) is dissolved in a solvent ($\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$), at small values of the solute concentration, the solvent behaves Raoultian. Thus, its activity is about the same as its mole fraction. Because pure $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ (activity = 1) is unstable in boiling water, for small $x$ values there is no reason to expect that doped $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ (with $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ activity $\approx 1-x$) would be stable.

It may, in principle, be possible to enhance the stability of $\mathrm{BaCeO}_3$ by the incorporation of a dopant provided (i) the cation radius of the dopant is small enough that the lattice parameter decreases with an increase in the dopant concentration and (ii) the dopant concentration is high enough such that dilute-solution approximation is invalid. The preceding discussion has attempted to establish a basis for assessing the thermodynamic stability of mixed perovskites in terms of the Madelung energy. A complete description, however, must account for the change in free energy that occurs when the following reaction occurs

$$
\mathrm{BaO} + \frac{1}{4} \mathrm{B}_2^\prime \mathrm{O}_3 + \frac{1}{4} \mathrm{B}_2^{\prime\prime} \mathrm{O}_5 \rightarrow \mathrm{BaB}_{1/2}^\prime \mathrm{B}_{1/2}^{\prime\prime} \mathrm{O}_3 \tag{21}
$$

which entails first the estimation of the enthalpy of this reaction, the first step being the estimation of the difference in Madelung energies between the product and the reactants, and second, the differences in the nearest-neighbor repulsive interactions. $^{31,32}$ This, however, requires an accurate knowledge of the nearest-neighbor interactions or the hardness factor. This information is not readily available. Also, the present calculations assume the bonding to be purely ionic and does not account for any covalency. Finally, it is also necessary to estimate the differences in entropy. In the case of mixed perovskites with the B-site disordered, it is also necessary to include the entropy of random mixing.

## Summary

Powder samples of simple perovskites such as $BaCeO_3$, $BaPrO_3$, and a number of mixed perovskites of the type $BaB_{1/2}^\prime B_{1/2}^{\prime\prime}O_3$ where $\mathrm{B}^\prime = \mathrm{Er}, \mathrm{Gd}, \mathrm{La}, \mathrm{or}\ \mathrm{Yb}$, and $\mathrm{B}^{\prime\prime} = \mathrm{Nb}\ \mathrm{or}\ \mathrm{Ta}$, were synthesized by calcining mixtures containing $BaCO_3$ and requisite oxides in air. The perovskite structure formed in each case. Subsequently, the calcined powders were boiled in water for periods of time from a couple of hours to several days. Both $BaCeO_3$ and $BaPrO_3$ readily decomposed to form $Ba(OH)_2$ and the respective oxide. However, all the mixed perovskites were observed to be stable. Madelung energies for the simple perovskites as well as for the mixed perovskites were computed. It was observed that the Madelung energy was lower for the mixed perovskites as compared to $ABO_3$ of the same lattice parameter. Lower Madelung energy of mixed perovskites is the result of a lower repulsive electrostatic interaction between ions of different valence, that is $(z+\delta)$ and $(z-\delta)$, as compared to ions of valence $z$. The higher stability of mixed perovskites was attributed in part to their lower Madelung-energies. Madelung energy calculations also suggest that a partial substitution of Ce by a trivalent ion need not necessarily enhance the stability of $BaCeO_3$. Indeed, it was observed that all doped $BaCeO_3$ readily decomposed when boiled in water. The present work thus suggests that the mixed perovskites in general may be more stable compared to simple perovskites due to their lower Madelung energies. The present work also suggests that mixed perovskites as proton conductors may be potentially superior to $BaCeO_3$ from the standpoint of stability, provided they exhibit a reasonable proton conductivity. Based on the published report of excellent proton conductivity $^{22}$ and the observed stability in the present work, off-stoichiometric $Ba_3Ca_{(1+x)}Nb_{(2-x)}O_{(9-\delta)}$ appears to be a good candidate as a proton conductor.
