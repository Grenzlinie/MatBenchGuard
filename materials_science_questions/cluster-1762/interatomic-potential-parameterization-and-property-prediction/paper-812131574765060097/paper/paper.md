JOURNAL OF THE PHYSICAL SOCIETY OF JAPAN Vol. 10, No. 11, NOVEMBER, 1955

# Theory of Spontaneous Deformation of Barium Titanate

By Wataru KINASE and Hidetoshi TAKAHASHI

Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, University of Tokyo, Tokyo

(Read November 2, 1954 and April 10, 1955; Received August 8, 1955)

The spontaneous ferroelectric deformation of the $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ crystal to tetragonal form is calculated, taking account of the electrostatic energy and the short-range interaction of the type $\lambda r^{-9}-\mu r^{-6}$, but neglecting the effect of thermal motion. It can be deduced that ionic shift causes the lattice spacing to extend a few percent to the same direction (i.e. the direction of spontaneous polarization). The result is sensitive to the choice of the "effective charge" of the Ti ion. However, for a certain range of this parameter, the dependence of the lattice constant on it shows a striking resemblance to the observed dependence of the lattice constant on temperature. Contraction perpendicular to the direction of polarization is also calculated here considering the interaction among ions. We can show that the extension and contraction suddenly occur when the dipole interaction becomes stronger than some critical value. By taking a proper value for the dipole interaction energy the result can be made to agree with the experimental data.

## § 1. Introduction

The lattice of ferroelectric crystal such as $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ is said to be pseudocubic in the temperature region of its ferroelectric phases, that is, its atomic arrangement is the same as in a cubic crystal (the form of the same crystal above Curie temperature), but the lattice constants are a little different in the three directions of coordinates and/or the coordinate angle is a little different from $90^{\circ}$. This is usually interpreted as electrostriction caused be spontaneous polarization. Although various works have appeared that deal with the spotaneous polarization of $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$, there seems to be no attempt to deal with the spontaneous deformation by a molecular theory. In this paper, the spontaneous deformation of $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ in tetragonal form will be treated theoretically using known interaction energies between the ions.

It is known that every forroelectric crystal of perovskite type has a positive electrostriction, that is, elongates in the direction of its polarization, while it contracts in the direction perpendicular to it $^{1)2)3)}$. This seems at first sight paradoxical, because if we assume that the spontaneously polarized crystal is composed of rigid electric dipoles aligned parallel to each other, the attraction of dipoles would cause contraction in the direction of polarization, and elongation perpendicular to it. This paradox may be cleared just by noting the fact that the actual polarization in $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ is caused by shift of ions from their symmetrical positions, and that these shifts depend

The dipole interaction energy is defined by the strength of the internal field and the magnitude of the shift of the ions. If the lattice had somewhat extended spacing in the direction of spontaneous polarization, repulsion between the ions would become weaker and the shift would become greater than in the case of shorter spacing. This mechanism explains qualitatively the electrostriction of $BaTiO_{3}$ crystal. Fig. 1 shows schematically the relation between the lattice spacing and the energy. The dipole interaction energy is equal to zero as long as the lattice spacing is less than a critical value $l_{c}$. When the lattice spacing exceeds $l_{c}$, there occurs a spontaneous shift of ions due to the dipole interaction, which gives the additional negative energy $U_{2}$. Comparing the curves for $U_{1}$ and $U_{1}+U_{2}$, it will be readily found that the minimum point is shifted rightwards by the addition of the term $U_{2}$ in accordance with the fact that $BaTiO_{3}$ crystal extends its lattice spacing in the direction of spontaneous polarization.

The aim of this article is to obtain a quantitative theory of the spontaneous electrostriction of $BaTiO_{3}$ crystal in the tetragonal phase. As a first step we shall neglect the effect of temperature altogether in this paper for the sake of simplicity. This would seem rather inadequate, because the real lattice is deformed in rhombohedral form at the lowest temperature region $^{3)}$. However, it will be seen later that even such a simplified theory is adequate to explain certain aspects of the ferroelectric transition.

## § 2. The Energy Expression for the Spontaneously polarized Crystal

The energy of $BaTiO_{3}$ crystal will be written simply as
$$
U=U_{1}+U_{2}, \quad(1)
$$

Where $U$ is total energy, and $U_{1}$ is that of a virtual unpolarized crystal (which, however, has the same lattice spacing as the real, polarized crystal), and $U_{2}$ is the additional energy term caused by the dipole interaction.

Now, $U_{1}$ may be supposed to consist of three types of interatomic interaction, that is, overlap energy, van der Waals' energy and Coulomb energy, namely,
$$
U_{1}=U_{\text {overlap }}+U_{\text {Waals }}+U_{\text {Coulomb }}. \quad (2)
$$

The term $U_{2}$ is caused by the shift of the ions. For the sake of simplicity, we shall consider hereafter the shift of only one sort of the ions, namely, the titanium ions. If we denote the magnitude of the shift of a Ti ion from the centre of the tetragonal unit lattice by $x$, the additional term caused by the shift (in other words, the potential for the Ti ion in the lattice composed of other ions) will be written as
$$
U_{2}=\left(a x^{2}+b x^{4}\right)+a^{\prime} x^{2}, \quad (3)
$$
where the terms in the bracket mean the potential for a Ti ion owing to overlap and van der Waals' interaction, and $a^{\prime} x^{2}$ is the term produced by the (electrostatic) Coulomb interaction. We have neglected here the higher power terms than $x^{4}$. It will be shown later by numerical calculation that both $a$ and $b$ take positive values, while $a^{\prime}$ is generally negative.

From the three equations (1), (2) and (3) we can write the energy as
$$
U=U_{\text {overlap }}+U_{\text {Waals }}+U_{\text {Coulomb }}+U_{2}. \quad (4)
$$

In the expression (4) the first three terms do not contain $x$, so we can readily get the value of $x$ by minimizing $U$ with respect to $x$. Namely
$$
0=\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}=\left(2 a x+4 b x^{3}\right)+2 a^{\prime} x. \quad (5)
$$

Solving (5), we get
$$
x=0 \quad \text { or } \quad \pm \sqrt{\frac{-a^{\prime}-a}{2 b}}. \quad (6)
$$

If the absolute value of $a^{\prime}$ is greater than $a$, that is $a^{\prime}+a<0, x=0$ and $x= \pm \sqrt{\frac{-a^{\prime}-a}{2 b}}$ correspond respectively to a maximum and double minimum of $U$. The minimum value of $U$ with respect to $x$ is thus obtained if we put $x= \pm \sqrt{\frac{-a^{\prime}-a}{2 b}}$ into equ. (4), namely
$$
U=U_{\text {overlap }}+U_{\text {Waals }}+U_{\text {Coulomb }}-\frac{\left(a^{\prime}+a\right)^{2}}{4 b}.
$$

We shall be concerned from now on with the energy expression (7) that does not contain $x$ explicitly.

If the absolute value of $a^{\prime}$ is not greater

than $a$, that is, $a+a'\geqq0$, equ. (5) has only one real solution $x=0$ corresponding to a single minimum of $U$. Apparently this cor- responds to the paraelectric state. In this case the energy expression is simply

$$
U=U_{\text{overlap}}+U_{\text{Waals}}+U_{\text{Coulomb}}. \qquad (7)
$$

## § 3. Overlap and Van der Walls' energies of the Tetragonal Lattice

The overlap and Van der Waals' energies $^{4)}$ of $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ crystal has been worked out in detail by A.F. Devonshire $^{5)}$, and explicit formulas for them have heen given for the case of cubic crystal. We shall follow his method and apply it to the case of tetragonal lattice.

The dimension of a unit call of $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ crystal lattice is nearly equal to a cube of which edges are $4 \mathring{\mathrm{A}}$. So it is convenient to express the three edges of a unit cell in the tetragonal phase as $2l(1+\Delta_{a}), 2l(1+\Delta_{b1})$ and $2l(1+\Delta_{b2})$, where $l=2\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$. $\Delta_{a}, \Delta_{b1}$ and $\Delta_{b2}$ show the ratios of the magnitude of extension of the cube edges to those of the standard cube $4\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$ in the direction parallel to the polarization and to the two directions perpendicular to it respectively. These three directions correspond to the lines connecting a titanium and three neighbouring oxygens. If we consider only the interaction among the nearest neighbour ions, the overlap and Van der Waals' energies will be expressed as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_{\text{overlap}}= & 2 \lambda_{O T} l^{-s}\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)^{-s}+\left(1+\Delta_{b1}\right)^{-s}+\left(1+\Delta_{b2}\right)^{-s}\right\} \\
& +\left(4 \lambda_{O O}+4 \lambda_{B O}\right) l^{-s}\left[\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{b1}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\Delta_{b2}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-s / 2}+\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{b2}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-s / 2}\right. \\
& \left.+\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\Delta_{b1}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-s / 2}\right] \\
& +8 \lambda_{B T} l^{-s}\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\Delta_{b1}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\Delta_{b2}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-s / 2} \\
& +\left(3 \lambda_{O O}+\lambda_{B B}+\lambda_{T T}\right)(2 l)^{-s}\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)^{-s}+\left(1+\Delta_{b1}\right)^{-s}+\left(1+\Delta_{b2}\right)^{-s}\right\},
\end{aligned} \qquad(8)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_{\text{Waals}}= & -2 \mu_{O T} l^{-t}\left\{\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)^{-t}+\left(1+\Delta_{b1}\right)^{-t}+\left(1+\Delta_{b2}\right)^{-t}\right\} \\

## §4. Coulomb energy for the Tetragonal Lattice

Here we shall again use the expression for the Coulomb energy for $\mathrm{BaTiO}_{3}$ calculated by A.F. Devonshire $^{5)}$, namely $-49.1 e^{2} / R$ c.g.s. unit, where $e$ is the electron charge and $R$ is the lattice constant. To obtain the Coulomb energy for the tetragonal lattice we adopt the following approximation. Namely, we take the arithmetic mean of Coulomb energies for three cubic lattices whose edge lengths are respectively equal to those of the three edges of the tetragonal unit cell. Thus, the Coulomb energy will be expressed approximately as

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_{\text {Coulomb }}= & -49.1 e^{2} \\
& \times \frac{1}{3}\left\{\frac{1}{2 l\left(1+\Delta_{a}\right)}+\frac{1}{2 l\left(1+\Delta_{b 1}\right)}+\frac{1}{2 l\left(1+\Delta_{b 2}\right)}\right\} .
\end{aligned}
$$

If we differentiate $U_{\text {Coulomb }}$, we readily obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{\partial}{\partial \Delta_{a}} U_{\text {Coulomb }}=\frac{49.1 e^{2}}{6 l}\left(1-2 \Delta_{a}\right), \\
& \frac{\partial}{\partial \Delta_{b 1}} U_{\text {Coulomb }}=\frac{49.1 e^{2}}{6 l}\left(1-2 \Delta_{b 1}\right), \\
& \frac{\partial}{\partial \Delta_{b 2}} U_{\text {Coulomb }}=\frac{49.1 e^{2}}{6 l}\left(1-2 \Delta_{b 2}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

where we have neglected the higher power terms $\Delta^{2}, \Delta^{3}$ etc. in the expansion.

Using (11) and (13) we can determine the lattice constants of the unpolarized crystal by solving the equations

$$
\begin{aligned}
& 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial \Delta_{a}}\left(U_{\text {overlap }}+U_{\text {Waals }}+U_{\text {Coulomb }}\right), \\
& 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial \Delta_{b 1}}\left(U_{\text {overlap }}+U_{\text {Waals }}+U_{\text {Coulomb }}\right), \\
& 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial \Delta_{b 2}}\left(U_{\text {overlap }}+U_{\text {Waals }}+U_{\text {Coulomb }}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

with the result

$$
\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b 1}=\Delta_{b 2}=0.00811 .
$$

Thus the lattice shape of the unpolarized crystal is cubic with lattice constant

$$
4(1+0.00811) \AA \doteqdot 4.0324 \AA \text {. }
$$

Here we have neglected the long range con- tribution of the overlap and van der Waals' energy. The result is in good agreement with Devonshire's calculation $^{5)}$ and also with the experimental data ${ }^{122}$.

## §5. Dipole Interaction Energy in the Tetragonal System

Since it seemed to be too laborious to evalu- ate precisely the dipole energies for the tetragonal lattice of perovskite type, an approx- imate method has been adopted to obtain them by using the known values for the cubic lattices as starting values and modifying them for the tatragonal distortion, taking into account the nearest lattice points only. If we admit this approximation, the field of an ar-

ray of unit dipoles directed to $z$-axis will be given by $S_{z}(\mathrm{l}, \mathrm{m}, \mathrm{n})$ in the following equations (17), (l, m, n) being the coordinates of the points in question with respect to the unit lattice.

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{z}(0,0,0 ; \Delta_{a})= & -12 \Delta_{a}, \\
S_{z}(1 / 2,1 / 2,1 / 2 ; \Delta_{a})= & (512 / 9 \sqrt{3}) \Delta_{a}, \\
S_{z}(1 / 2,1 / 2,0 ; \Delta_{a})= & -p, \\
S_{z}(1 / 2,0,1 / 2 ; \Delta_{a})= & p / 2+12 \sqrt{2} \Delta_{a}, \\
S_{z}(0,1 / 2,1 / 2 ; \Delta_{a})= & p / 2+12 \sqrt{2} \Delta_{a}, \\
S_{z}(0,0,1 / 2 ; \Delta_{a})= & q-96 \Delta_{a}, \\
S_{z}(0,1 / 2,0 ; \Delta_{a})= & -q / 2, \\
S_{z}(1 / 2,0,0 ; \Delta_{a})= & -q / 2,
\end{aligned} \quad(17)
$$

where $\Delta_{a}$ corresponds to the relative elongation $z$-axis of the lattice, where $p=8.668^{8)}, q=30.081^{9)}$. We assume here that $S_{z}(1 / 2,1 / 2,0), S_{z}(0,1 / 2,0)$ and $S_{z}(1 / 2,0,0)$ are unaltered because the nearest lattice points are not shifted by the extension of the $z$-axis of the lattice.

In the same manner we can modify $S_{z}$'s for the relative extension $\Delta_{b 1}$ and $\Delta_{b 2}$, but the crystalline field at Ti ion may be little affected by $\Delta_{b 1}$ and $\Delta_{b 2}$ because the dipole interaction between $\mathrm{Ti}$ and $\mathrm{Oa}$ ions would be considerably greater than that between $\mathrm{Ti}$ and $\mathrm{Ob}_{1}$ or $\mathrm{Ob}_{2}$ ions $^{10) 11)}$. For this reason we calculate here the dipole interaction only for the extension ratio $\Delta_{a}$, neglecting $\Delta_{b 1}$ and $\Delta_{b 2}$ altogether.

By using the modified $S_{z}$'s in (17), we can evaluate the change of the dipole interaction caused by the extension of the lattice in $z$-direction. To do this, however, we have to know the local field acting on $\mathrm{Ti}$ ions caused by the shift of the Ti ions themselves $^{10) 11) 12)}$. Thus, we have to solve a system of simultaneous equations as was done by J.C. Slater $^{12)}$ : (Dipole moment of one ion)=(Polarizability of the ion) multiplied by (Local field at its location).

By expressing the local field as a function of dipole moments of all ions, we can write down the simultaneous equations. Here we must pay attention to the fact that coefficient for the local field also needs to be modified in tetragonal lattice. By taking account of only the nearest neighbours, the local field acting on $\mathrm{Ti}$ ion directly produced by the shift of Ti itself will be shown to be expressed approximately as

$$
\left\{\frac{4 \pi}{3}+\left(\frac{-256}{9 \sqrt{3}}-48\right) \Delta_{a}\right\} u_{0} \quad(18)
$$

instead of simply $4 \pi / 3 \cdot u_{0}$ as in the cubic lattice, where $u_{0}$ means the product of the shift of $\mathrm{Ti}$ ion and its charge.

## §6. The potential acting on Ti Ion
