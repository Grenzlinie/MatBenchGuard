Values of $C=|\partial^2 \mathrm{A}/\partial \mathrm{k}_\mathrm{H}^2|^{-1/2}$, where $\mathrm{A}$ is the area of cross section of the Fermi surface normal to $\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{H}$ (the derivative taken for the extremal cross section), have been computed over a wide range of orientations, for several different descriptions of the Fermi surfaces of $\mathrm{Cu}$, $\mathrm{Ag}$, and $\mathrm{Au}$. These values, which are needed for the interpretation of de Haas-van Alphen oscillation amplitudes (see preceding paper), are presented in tabular form together with approximate values of $\partial^4 \mathrm{A}/\partial \mathrm{k}_\mathrm{H}^4$.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

One of the factors that enters into the theoretical formula for the amplitude of the de Haas-van Alphen (dHvA) oscillations in a metal is the curvature factor $C$, defined by

$$
C=\left|\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}\right|^{-1 / 2} \tag{1}
$$

where $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface (FS) normal to the direction of the magnetic field $\mathbf{H}$, $k_{H}$ is the component parallel to $\mathbf{H}$ of the wave vector $\boldsymbol{k}$, and the derivative is taken at the extremum of $A$ as a function of $k_{H}$. From measurements of the absolute amplitude of the oscillations it is possible to extract the quantity $CG$, where $G$ is given by

$$
G=\cos \left(\frac{1}{2} \pi g m / m_{0}\right) \tag{2}
$$

$g$ is the spin-splitting factor and $m/m_0$ is the ratio of the cyclotron mass to the free electron mass. Thus if $C$ is known, $G$ and hence $g$ can be

In the course of recent experiments¹ to determine $G$ and $g$ in this way for the noble metals, it became apparent that there were serious inconsistencies in the published values²⁻⁴ of $C$ based on different mathematical descriptions of the FS and new computations of $C$ were accordingly undertaken. The immediate aim was to interpret the experimental results of Ref. 1 on $CG$ at particular orientations, but since reliable values of $C$ may be required at other orientations, the computations were extended to cover the whole range of orientations in the (110) and (100) planes for Cu, Ag, and Au. The present paper collects together in tabular form the values of $C$ for the various descriptions of the FS and discusses the degree of consistency that has been achieved. Since values of $\partial^{4} A / \partial k_{H}^{4}$ are automatically produced by the computations, these, too, are presented. Rough values of $\partial^{4} A / \partial k_{H}^{4}$ are useful in indicating the limits of validity of the conventional calculation of dHvA amplitude, in which only the quadratic term in an expansion of $A$ in even powers of $k_{H}$ ($k_{H}$ measured from the extremal cross section) is retained.

## 2. MATHEMATICAL DESCRIPTIONS OF THE FERMI SURFACES OF THE NOBLE METALS

Two kinds of description have been found to give very good fits to the experimental data on the extremal areas as a function of orientation (in both kinds of description the radius vectors of the surface are probably reliably known to something like one in $10^{3}$). These are:

(a) A Fourier series description, which automatically reproduces the cubic symmetry of the metal and contains a number of adjustable coefficients (typically five or seven) whose values are chosen to give the best fit to the corresponding number of experimentally determined areas. This representation, originally introduced by Roaf⁵ and since refined²,⁶,⁷ as more accurate experimental data become available, has the merit that the formulation is relatively simple, but it is really no more than an interpolation formula and the coefficients have no physical significance in themselves.

Computations have been made based on Halse's⁶ five- and seven-coefficient formulas, which we shall denote by HCu5, HCu7, HAg5, HAg7, and HAu5, as well as Bosacchi, Ketterson, and Windmiller's (BKW)² formula* R8 for Au and Coleridge and Templeton's⁷ Halse-like formulas CTCu5VI, CTAg5IV, and CTAu5VI, which were obtained by fitting to rather more accurate data than were available to Halse.

(b) A description based on a Korringa-Kohn-Rostoker (KKR) band structure calculation in which a number of phase shift parameters $\eta_{l}$ (usually three, i.e., $l=0,1,2$) are chosen to give the correct FS areas at certain
*Note that this formula contains only seven fitted coefficients, so it is analogous to HCu7 or HAg7; however, R8 is fitted to slightly different experimental data from those used in HAu5 and CTAu5VI.

orientations (more precisely, to give a minimum RMS deviation of the computed areas from the experimental ones at, say, five or more orien-tations). One other parameter that has to be fixed is the Fermi energy; although the quality of the best fit is not very sensitive to the Fermi energy, different assumptions do lead to just significant differences, as we shall see later. This approach was pioneered by Lee $^{8,9}$ for Cu and has been applied to all three noble metals by Shaw et al. $^{10}$ (SKW) and more recently by Holzwarth. $^{11}$ It has the advantages that there are fewer fitting parameters, that these parameters have some physical meaning, and that the resulting FS might perhaps be expected to be a sounder interpolation than the Fourier one for orientations not used in the fitting procedure. On the other hand, the relation between the parameters and the geometry of the surface is a much more complicated one than for the Fourier representation and more computing is inevitably involved in calculating a radius vector of the FS.

Sets of phase shift parameters for $l=0,1,2$ and Fermi energies that fit the experimentally found areas for Cu, Ag, and Au have been evaluated by SKW and by Holzwarth. Preliminary computations made at a few selected orientations showed that the $C$ values given by the SKW and the Holzwarth schemes do not differ by more than the uncertainties of computation, and that the $C$ values are also insensitive to the choice of Fermi energy. For Cu and Ag the Holzwarth parameters (see Appendix), which give an excellent fit to the experimental areas, were used for detailed computations over a wider range of orientations. In Au all the fits were rather poorer and a search for a better fit was made by one of us (PTC), by varying the Fermi energy parameter. The resulting parameters, which gave an appreciably better fit, were used for the computations of $C$ for Au over a wider range of orientations and are listed in the Appendix. It should be noted that all the KKR calculations are nonrelativistic and although this simplification is probably adequate for Cu and Ag, it may lead to appreciable errors in Au and perhaps explains the difficulty in obtaining a good fit to the area data.

### 3. METHODS OF COMPUTATION

The straightforward method of computing $\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}$ is to compute $A$ to sufficient accuracy for a number of sufficiently small values of $k_{H}$ (the origin of $k_{H}$ being such that $k_{H}=0$ for the extremal area) and then to make a least squares fit to an equation of the form

$$
A=A_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}\right) k_{H}^{2}+\frac{1}{24}\left(\partial^{4} A / \partial k_{H}^{4}\right) k_{H}^{4} \tag{3}
$$

thus determining $A_{0}, \partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}$, and $\partial^{4} A / \partial k_{H}^{4}$.

If $A_{0}$ can be regarded as accurately known, a somewhat simpler procedure is to fit a linear regression to $(A-A_{0}) / k_{H}^{2}$ as a function of $k_{H}^{2}$. The

latter procedure was used for most of the computing with the Fourier formulas, but the former procedure was used for the computing with the KKR formulation, for which the areas were computed somewhat less accurately.

A quite different method of computing $\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}$, which was proposed and used by Bosacchi et al. $^{2}$ in their work on $\mathrm{Au}$, is possible when the FS is described by an analytic formula such as in the Fourier description. If the analytic formula is expressed as $F(\mathbf{k})=0, \partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}$ (and any other derivative of $A$) can be expressed as an integral around the boundary of a cross section of the FS of a rather complicated expression involving derivatives of $F(\mathbf{k})$ [Eq. (10) of BKW]. These derivatives can be explicitly written down, so that the final result is an integral of an explicit, but complicated function of $\mathbf{k}$. In principle, for a given precision of computing a radius vector of the FS, this method should be more accurate than the straightforward method of computing areas for varying $k_{H}$, but in practice the procedure is complicated and it is doubtful if it has so far been correctly carried through. Thus the BKW values of $C$ computed by their alternative method with their formula R8 (for $\mathrm{Au}$ ) show some very implausible features and are drastically different from those computed (by the straightforward method) with a KKR formulation. Crabtree et al. ${ }^{3,4}$ (CWK), who discovered this discrepancy (see Fig. 2 of Ref. 3) drew the conclusion that it arose from the inability of Fourier formulas (such as R8) to interpolate sufficiently correctly between the fitted areas. However, our own computations show that if the straightforward method is used with R8, the implausible features disappear and only relatively small quantitative differences remain between the R8 and KKR values of $C$. The values of $C$ given by Franzosi $^{12}$ for $\mathrm{Cu}$ and $\mathrm{Ag}$ based on Fourier formulas also show some qualitative discrepancies with our cal- culations by the straightforward method and it is likely that he, too, has used the same method as BKW (though he does not explicitly say so). We conclude therefore that there is nothing drastically wrong with the Fourier formulas, but that something has gone wrong in the practical application by BKW and perhaps by Franzosi of their ingenious method of computing $\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}$.

Returning to the detailed application of the straightforward method, the procedures used for the Fourier formulas (computed in Cambridge) and for KKR (computed in Ottawa) were somewhat different. In both pro- cedures each radius vector in a particular direction in the desired plane was determined by the method of false position. The precision achieved in the Ottawa computing was of order 5 in $10^{5}$ of the radius or better, and in the Cambridge computing, 1 in $10^{7}$ or better. The area is given by

$$
A=\frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} k^{2}(\psi) d \psi \tag{4}
$$

where $k(\psi)$ is the radius for an angle $\psi$ in the plane of the section and the integration was carried out in Ottawa by Simpson's rule with a Romberg technique (using 40 or more points) and in Cambridge by a Gaussian-type quadrature technique to produce accuracy in area of $10^{-7}$ or better. The areas were determined for several values of $k_{H}$ small enough for higher order terms in (3) to be unimportant [in Ottawa the values of $k_{H}$ (in units of $2\pi/a$) were from 0 to 0.01 or 0.02 in steps of 0.002 or 0.004 and in Cambridge from 0 to 0.01 in steps of 0.0025]. The accuracy achieved in computing $\partial^{2}A/\partial k_{H}^{2}$ was probably considerably higher than might be expected on the basis of the accuracy of the individual area determinations because the same directions $\psi$ were used for determining radius vectors at different values of $k_{H}$, i.e., in effect eliminating systematic errors in the area determination associated with the number of steps around the orbit. This consideration is probably less important in the Cambridge computations since an appreciably higher accuracy was maintained at all stages of the computation. Rough estimates of the accuracy of the computed $C$ values are indicated in the tables below.

Finally it should be mentioned that the computation of extremal areas for the rosette areas normal to directions in the (100) plane away from $\langle 100\rangle$ is less straightforward than in the (110) plane because the center of the extremal orbit shifts as the normal tilts away from the symmetry direction. Equation (3) is then no longer correct if $k_{H}$ is still measured from the fixed origin appropriate to the $\langle 100\rangle$ direction, rather than from the position of the extremal orbit. The difficulty was dealt with by computing the areas for both positive and negative values of $k_{H}$ (from the fixed origin) and determining the value of $C$ by an appropriate analysis.

It can be seen that all the different formulations agree quite well for the belly orbits around <100> and for the rosette and dogsbone orbits.* However, there are considerable differences around <111> and at orientations approaching those for which $\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}$ vanishes and also approaching those for which a closed orbit ceases to exist. The last two discrepancies are not surprising, since the different formulations predict slightly different angles for these critical orientations and the discrepancies amount to little more than slight shifts in the angular scale. The discrepancies at <111> are, however, more serious and it is difficult to decide which formulation is likely to be closest to the truth. It is perhaps significant that the seven-coefficient formulas for Cu and Ag give $C$ values in excellent agreement with the KKR values even at <111> and this might suggest that the seven-coefficient formulas are as good a description of the FS as we have. However, for Au the seven-coefficient formula R8 gives $C$ values around <111> which are 20% lower than the Coleridge KKR values. This may be because R8 is a less adequate interpolation formula than HCu7 and HAg7,† but it is also possible that the KKR interpolation is appreciably in error because of the neglect of relativistic effects. As argued in Ref. 1, perhaps the truth lies somewhere between R8 and KKR.

For completeness, $C$ values were also computed for the neck orbits (Tables I and V). Once again there is excellent agreement between the H7 and the KKR values for Cu and Ag, but somewhat poorer agreement between the Fourier and KKR values for Au. In the derivation of the Lifshitz-Kosevich (LK) formula for the ampli- tude of the dHvA oscillations it is necessary to integrate the oscillatory term over its phase (proportional to $A/H$) and it is usually assumed that in the expansion of Eq. (3) only the quadratic term need be retained. If, however, $\partial^4 A/\partial k_H^4$ is large enough, the quartic term in (3) may become comparable to the quadratic one for a value of $k_H$ that produces a phase change of order $2\pi$ and the LK formula is no longer reliable. As shown in Ref. 13, the condition for the LK formula to be a reliable approximation is that*

$$
\alpha^{-1 / 2} \equiv\left(\frac{24\left(\partial^{2} A / \partial k_{H}^{2}\right)^{2}}{A_{s} \partial^{4} A / \partial k_{H}^{4}} \frac{F_{s}}{H}\right)^{1 / 2} \gg 1
\tag{5}
$$

where $F_s$ is the dHvA frequency for the spherical FS of equal volume (which is proportional to $A_s$, the corresponding area) and $H$ is the magnetic field. As can be seen from (3), $\partial^4 A/\partial k_H^4$ is given by the slope of the plot of $(A-A_0)/k_H^2$ against $k_H^2$ if higher order terms can be neglected, and values of $\alpha^{-1/2}$ can readily be obtained from the same computations as are required to obtain values of $C$. Not surprisingly, the $\alpha^{-1/2}$ values agree only roughly as among the various Fourier formulas, partly because the fourth derivative is a more sensitive feature of the surface and partly because the accuracy of computation of the areas was insufficient to give $\alpha^{-1/2}$ to better than one or two significant figures (particularly when $\partial^4 A/\partial k_H^4$ was small and $\alpha^{-1/2}$ large). The KKR computations were not sufficiently accurate to give more than very rough estimates of $\alpha^{-1/2}$, which were, however, reasonably consistent with the Fourier estimates. Since only fairly rough estimates of $\partial^4 A/\partial k_H^2$ are required to test the validity of (5), we present in Tables II-IV the values of $\alpha^{-1/2}$ for only the seven-term Fourier formulas. These values are given for $F_s/H = 10^4$, which corresponds to $H$ of order 5 T; exact values of $F_s$ are given in Ref. 6 and it is easy to calculate $\alpha^{-1/2}$ for other values of $H$ if required. It can be seen that the condition (5) is amply satisfied except within a degree or so of the critical orientations where $\partial^2 A/\partial k_H^2$ passes through zero as it changes sign.
