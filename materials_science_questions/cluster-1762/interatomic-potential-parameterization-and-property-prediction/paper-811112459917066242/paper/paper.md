# Oscillatory Magnetostriction and the Stress Dependence of the Fermi Surface of Aluminum
R. Griessen and R. S. Sorbello
Laboratorium für Festkörperphysik, Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule, Zürich, Switzerland
(Received 30 March 1972)

We have measured the absolute amplitude of the torque and the oscillatory magnetostriction of single crystals of aluminum between 1.2 and 4.2 K and in fields up to 22 kOe. From these results we determined the uniaxial stress dependence of six different orbits. The values for $(1/A)(\partial A/\partial\sigma)$, where $A$ is the orbit area and $\sigma$ is a tension in the positive [001] direction were found to range from $-2.2\times 10^{-5}\ \text{bar}^{-1}$ for the $\gamma_2$ orbits to $+15.0\times 10^{-5}\ \text{bar}^{-1}$ for the $\alpha_1$ orbits. The experimental values are in close agreement with the results of a four-OPW (orthogonalized-plane-wave) calculation in which we use the pseudopotential matrix elements and the Fermi energy determined by Ashcroft and take the stress dependence of the pseudopotential to be given by the Heine-Animalu form factor with a local correction for volume changes. By fitting our stress-dependence data to the theoretical calculation we were also able to deduce the slopes of the actual form factor at the two smallest reciprocal-lattice vectors. The values we obtained for the slopes are not very different from those of the Heine-Animalu form factor. Our experimentally determined values for the slopes are $\partial V/\partial q=0.15\pm 0.07$ a.u. at $\vec{q}=(2\pi/a)(1,1,1)$ and $\partial V/\partial q=0.05\pm 0.01$ a.u. at $\vec{q}=(2\pi/a)(0,0,2)$.

## I. INTRODUCTION
Experimental investigation of the stress dependence of the Fermi surface may provide a rather critical test of the theoretical models used in describing a metal. $^{1}$ Previous work has been concentrated in directly observing the change in the frequencies of the de Haas-van Alphen oscillations as a metal is subjected to hydrostatic pressure. $^{2}$ From such observations the pressure dependence of extremal orbits of the Fermi surface can be deduced, and the results compared to theoretical predictions based on specific models.

This procedure has been only partially successful because a sufficient body of experimental data has not yet been collected, and in most cases an adequate systematic framework of interpretation has been missing. With these points in mind, it would appear valuable to present and analyze additional data for a simple system. This provides the motivation for our studies of the stress dependence of the Fermi surface of aluminum by means of the oscillatory-magnetostriction technique.

We have chosen to study aluminum because extensive theoretical and experimental Fermi-surface data exist for the zero-pressure case, thus affording us greater scope and reliability in interpreting our results. Furthermore, Ashcroft's four-OPW (orthogonalized-plane-wave) description $^{3}$ gives very good agreement with the de Haas-van Alphen experiments for the unstressed metal, so it seems reasonable to extend his calculation to the stressed metal. But there is in fact no guarantee that the stress dependence is adequately described by such an extension of the theory, and any disagreement between the resulting theory and experiment would cast serious doubt on the physical meaning of the theoretical model used in the first place for the zero-pressure case. In this connection, Melz $^{4}$ has measured the hydrostatic pressure dependence of the Fermi surface of aluminum and found agreement between experiment and the predictions of a simple pseudopotential model. The model was not strong enough, however, to allow a critical quantitative comparison. A recent calculation by Hepfer and Rayne $^{5}$ is in quantitative agreement with Melz's measurements.

While experimental investigation of the Fermi surface under hydrostatic pressure might be expected to give a good test of the theory, a more sensitive test might be expected by subjecting the metal to a uniaxial stress. For example, in a one-OPW model there is no change in the shape or topology of Fermi-surface sections of a cubic crystal under hydrostatic pressure, while under uniaxial stress there is a change of shape, and possibly, even of topology. It might therefore be expected that uniaxial stress would be a more sensitive probe for investigating band-structure parameters. Moreover, a uniaxial stress generally destroys a considerable amount of the crystal symmetry, allowing us to investigate a greater number of distinct orbits than can be investigated in the hydrostatic-pressure experiments.

Direct observation of the de Haas-van Alphen effect oscillations under uniaxial stress is, however, a tricky affair. $^{6,7}$ The problem is that there is the danger of applying too high a stress and thereby causing irreversible deformation that destroys the ordered crystalline structure. One should therefore apply relatively small stresses, and this leads to loss of sensitivity. In view of

## II. EXPERIMENT

### A. Experimental Equipment and Procedure

The crystals of 99.9999% pure aluminum used in this work were obtained from Metals Research. The samples were cut by a low-damage spark- erosion technique to a length of 35 mm and a diam- eter of 6 mm. The [001] direction was chosen to be parallel to the axis of the cylinder (within 2°).

The length changes were measured using a mod- ification of the capacitor apparatus described by Brändli.¹⁰ Relative length changes of nearly 10⁻¹⁰ could be detected with our dilatometric device. Although the reproducibility is excellent, it is dif- ficult to make an exact calibration for length changes of the order of a few angstroms. Never- theless, it seems probable that the systematic error is not greater than 10%.

The torque acting on the specimen in a magnetic field was also measured by a capacitor technique to be described elsewhere. The sensitivity of this torquemeter was about 0.1 dyn/cm² for the torque per unit volume and its compliance was nearly 10⁻⁷ rad/dyn cm. Thus the small changes in the orien- tation of the sample relative to the magnetic field strength remained smaller than 10⁻⁴ rad. The calibration was done by replacing the specimen by a small coil of known dimensions and number of turns. The torque acting on this coil was measured as a function of the external magnetic field and the current in the coil.

The orientation of the crystal was done "in situ" by means of the field-rotation method. The length changes and the torque were measured as a func- tion of the magnetic field direction. With the mag- netic field rotated in a plane perpendicular to the [001] direction, the recorder tracing of the length changes shows axes of reflection spaced at 45° in- tervals, each axis corresponding to a symmetry plane of the crystal. The torque recorder tracing shows an inversion point for each symmetry plane. With this method the orientation of the crystal could be determined to within 1°. The directions where phase cancellation occurs could be determined ac- curately by observing the beat pattern in the torque or the oscillatory magnetostriction as a function of the magnetic field strength. For example, we could use the beat pattern of the $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ orbits (see Fig. 1) for an exact determination of the (100) plane.

We measured the oscillatory magnetostriction and the torque at temperatures between 1.1 and 4.2 K in fields up to 22 kOe provided by a conven- tional 40-cm-type Bruker Physik electromagnet. The homogeneity over the specimen was better than 5×10⁻⁵. The magnet could be rotated to all field orientations perpendicular to the [001] axis of the specimen. Both the length changes and the torque were measured as a function of the magnetic field and continuously traced on an $X$-$Y$ recorder. Be- cause of the relatively large samples and the low magnetoresistance of pure aluminum, the magnetic field had to be changed at a rate slow enough to minimize the effect of eddy currents. The sweep times were typically of the order of 200 Oe/min.

The angle $\theta$ is measured in a plane perpendicular to the $j$ direction. In our experiment the $i$ and $j$ directions are identical and correspond to the [001] direction of the crystal, which is also the rotation axis of the magnet.

The relation (1) is valid only if $\epsilon_{i}^{(\mu)}$ and $\tau_{j}^{(\mu)}$ are measured with the specimen in the same condition. As it was not possible to determine both quantities simultaneously, we made separate measurements of them on the same crystal, and this required two different sample holders. In order to avoid any irreversible deformations the crystals were maintained in place by two springs. In that way, the only force acting on the sample is due to the springs, and the corresponding stresses were smaller than $10^{-1}$ bar. After performing the experimental runs, we repeated several measurements and verified that within the experimental uncertainty the new amplitudes were the same as the previous ones.

The stress dependence could also be determined from oscillatory-magnetostriction data alone as was done by Aron and Chandrasekhar $^{13}$ for Bi. For this however, the effective mass, the Dingle temperature, and some geometric properties of the Fermi surface have to be known. The effect of the spin splitting of the Landau level on the amplitude must also be taken into account. Furthermore, the validity of the Lifshitz-Kosevich theoretical expression for the absolute amplitude of the de Haas-van Alphen oscillations has been checked only for a few metals. $^{14}$ The great advantage of the relation (1) is that an explicit expression for the amplitude factor is not needed and that only the measured amplitudes of the strain and torque per unit volume play a role. It must be pointed out, however, that an accurate determination of the angular dependence $(1/A)(\partial A/\partial\theta)$ of extremal areas may be difficult for certain orbits.

In order to use the relation (1), we Fourier analyzed the recorder tracings of the length changes and of the torque to determine the amplitudes and the frequencies corresponding to various extremal areas $A^{(\mu)}(\theta_{0})$. Recorder tracings of oscillatory magnetostriction and torque are shown in Ref. 9. In Fig. 2 we show a typical example of the high-frequency portion of the Fourier spectrum of a magnetostriction curve ($\theta=15^{\circ}$ from [100]). The resolution around the peaks is large enough to give reliable values for the amplitudes. Extensive testing of our Fourier analysis computer program has shown that the error in the determination of the absolute amplitudes is less than $\pm4\%$.

### D. Discussion of Sources of Experimental Error

a. Systematic errors. The length changes and the torque are measured with a capacitor technique using the same electronics. The torquemeter could be accurately calibrated while for the dilatometer we had to use a formula for a capacitor to convert capacitive changes into length changes. Our experience with dilatometric devices indicates that this systematic error is about $\pm 10 \%$. As mentioned before, we have checked that remounting and cooling down of the crystal did not affect the Dingle temperature.

b. Statistical errors. Extensive testing of our Fourier analysis computer program has shown that for peaks which may be easily separated, the error is within $4 \%$. For the $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ orbits, however, where the periods are close to each other, the error is certainly larger. Another source of error is the determination of the angular dependence of the periods, especially when $\partial P / d \theta \cong 0$. Nevertheless, it is seen from the curves describing the stress dependence of the various orbits that the statistical error is certainly smaller than $15 \%$ except for the $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ orbits.

c. Total error for stress dependence. Figures 3-6 show that the stress dependence of the $\gamma_{1}, \gamma_{2}$, $\alpha_{1}$, and $\alpha_{2}$ orbits is (within the experimental uncertainties) independent of the magnetic field direction. This is also strongly supported by the results of the four-OPW calculation described in Sec. III. It seems reasonable, therefore, to fit a constant to the measured stress dependence. In taking the mean value of the measured $(1 / A)(\partial A / \partial \sigma)$ for different orientations we may obtain values with a very small statistical error. ### III. THEORETICAL CALCULATION

Our calculation for the stress dependence of the Fermi surface is based on a four-OPW pseudopotential model. The calculation is essentially the same as that of Ashcroft⁸ for the zero-pressure case except that the reciprocal-lattice vectors, the Fermi energy, and the pseudopotential matrix elements all depend on the applied stress. In addition, the form of the 4×4 secular determinant is less symmetric. Assuming a uniaxial stress along the positive [001] direction it is convenient to perform our calculations in terms of the quantity $\rho$ which is the axial ratio defined as $a/c$, where $a$ and $c$ are the respective lattice spacings in the transverse (001) plane and in the [001] direction. The four-OPW secular equation for the Fermi surface is then of the following form (atomic units assumed throughout):

$$
\begin{vmatrix}
T_0 & V_1 & V_1 & V_3 \\
V_1 & T_1 & V_2 & V_1 \\
V_1 & V_2 & T_2 & V_1 \\
V_3 & V_1 & V_1 & T_3
\end{vmatrix} = 0 \, , \tag{2}
$$

where
$$
\begin{align}
T_0 &= \frac{1}{2}\vec{k}^2 - E_F \, , \\
T_1 &= \frac{1}{2}[\vec{k} - (2\pi/a)(1, 1, \rho)]^2 - E_F \, , \\
T_2 &= \frac{1}{2}[\vec{k} - (2\pi/a)(1, -1, \rho)]^2 - E_F \, , \\
T_3 &= \frac{1}{2}[\vec{k} - (2\pi/a)(0, 0, 2\rho)]^2 - E_F \, ,
\end{align} \tag{3}
$$

and where $V_1$, $V_2$, and $V_3$ are pseudopotential matrix elements obtained from the value of the form factor $V(q, k_F)$ at the appropriate reciprocal-lattice vectors $\vec{q}_i$ according to the following prescription:
$$V_i = V(q_i, k_F) \, ,$$
where
$$
\begin{align}
\vec{q}_1 &= (2\pi/a)(1, 1, \rho) \, , \\
\vec{q}_2 &= (2\pi/a)(2, 0, 0) \, , \\
\vec{q}_3 &= (2\pi/a)(0, 0, 2\rho) \, .
\end{align}
$$

It is important to realize throughout that the Fermi energy $E_F$, the Fermi wave vector $k_F$, and the lattice spacing $a$ are implicit functions of $\rho$. In the neighborhood of $\rho=1$ the $\rho$ dependence of $a$ is described by the relation $(1/a)(da/d\rho)=0.268$, which follows from the low-temperature elastic constants given by Kamm and Alers¹⁷ and Sutton.¹⁸

The secular equation (2) is valid in only a limited region of $\vec{k}$ space, specifically in the region for which the nearest four reciprocal-lattice points are $(0,0,0)$, $(2\pi/a)(1,1,\rho)$, $(2\pi/a)(1,-1,\rho)$, and $(2\pi/a)(0,0,2\rho)$. For the case $\rho=1$ this region corresponds to two $\frac{1}{48}$th symmetry sections of the Brillouin zone shown in Fig. 1. For the stressed crystal we generally require the Fermi surface in the full $\frac{1}{16}$th symmetry section of the Brillouin zone, and therefore certain modifications of (2) and (3) may be necessary. These modifications involve only changes in the reciprocal-lattice vectors appearing in (3) and either an interchange of $V_2$ and $V_3$ or a replacement of $V_3$ by $V_2$ in (2).

For purposes of computer calculation it is convenient to expand the determinant in (2) and obtain the polynomial expression:
$$
\begin{align}
&(T_0T_3 - V_3^2)(T_1T_2 - V_2^2) \\
&\quad - V_1^2(T_0 + T_3 - 2V_3)(T_1 + T_2 - 2V_2) = 0 \, . \tag{4}
\end{align}
$$

From numerical solution of (4) we obtain cross sections of the Fermi surface for any orbit of interest as a function of $\rho$. In order to obtain the stress dependence of the orbit we use the following conversion factor determined from the low-temperature elastic constants:
$$\frac{d\rho}{d\sigma} = -1.927 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{bar}^{-1} \, . \tag{5}$$

Before we can use (4) it is necessary to know $E_F$ as a function of $\rho$. Since a sufficiently accurate computation of $E_F(\rho)$ from first principles requires a very laborious volume integration, we adopt the simpler procedure of using Ashcroft's empirically determined values for $\rho=1$, and we approximate the additional contributions by assuming a free-electron dependence corrected for the presence of each Bragg plane. That is, we write
$$
\begin{align}
E_F(\rho) = E_F(\rho=1) &+ [\epsilon_F(\rho) - \epsilon_F(\rho=1)] \\
&+ [\Delta\epsilon_F(\rho) - \Delta\epsilon_F(\rho=1)] \, , \tag{6}
\end{align}
$$
where $E_F(\rho=1)=0.4280$ is Ashcroft's value, $\epsilon_F(\rho) = 0.4303\rho^{2/3}$ is the $\rho$-dependent free-electron Fermi energy, and $\Delta\epsilon_F$ is the perturbation theory correction arising from the Bragg planes associated with each of the reciprocal-lattice vectors appearing in $T_1$, $T_2$, and $T_3$. In the Brillouin-zone section where (2) is valid, for example, the correction $\Delta\epsilon_F$ is given by
$$
\begin{align}
\Delta\epsilon_F = -\frac{V_1^2}{2q_1k_F} \ln\left| \frac{1+2k_F/q_1}{1-2k_F/q_1} \right| & \\
- \frac{V_3^2}{4q_3k_F} \ln\left| \frac{1+2k_F/q_3}{1-2k_F/q_3} \right| &\quad . \tag{7}
\end{align}
$$

For the smaller orbits which are very sensitive to changes in the Fermi energy, it is important that the correction (7) be included in $E_F$.

In order to make contact with experiment we need the quantity $(1/A)(dA/d\rho)$. Any extremal cross section $A$ determined from the secular Eq. (2) clearly has the form $A=A(\rho, E_F, V_i)$, where $E_F=E_F(\rho, V_i)$ and $V_i=V(q_i(\rho), k_F(\rho))$. It then follows that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{A} \frac{d A}{d \rho} & \left(\rho, E_{F}, V_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{A} \frac{\partial A}{\partial \rho}+\frac{1}{A} \frac{\partial A}{\partial E_{F}} \frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial \rho} \\
& +\sum_{i=1,2,3}\left(\frac{1}{A} \frac{\partial A}{\partial E_{F}} \frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial V_{i}}+\frac{1}{A} \frac{\partial A}{\partial V_{i}}\right) \frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial \rho}.
\end{aligned} \quad (8)
$$

For convenience we split up $\partial V_i/\partial \rho$ as follows:

$$
\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial \rho}=\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}\right|_{q_{i}} \frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial \rho}+\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial k_{F}} \frac{\partial k_{F}}{\partial \rho}. \quad (9)
$$

It is understood that all the quantities appearing in (8) and (9) are to be evaluated at $\rho=1$, since only the immediate neighborhood of $\rho=1$ is of physical interest in our problem.

Now, on the right-hand side of Eq. (8) the only unknowns are $\partial V_i/\partial \rho$, since all derivatives of $A$ may be numerically computed from the secular equation, while the derivatives of $E_F$ may be computed from Eq. (6). The values obtained for these quantities are shown in Table II and Table III. Using these calculated values and any choice of $\partial V_i/$ $\partial \rho$, one can immediately compute $(1/A)(dA/d\rho)$ and then compare the results with experiment by using the conversion factor (5). Conversely, from the experimental values for $(1/A)(\partial A/\partial \sigma)$ one can determine $\partial V_i/\partial \rho$ provided that three or more consistent equations for these quantities can be obtained.

### A. Calculation of Stress Dependence of Orbits from Model Pseudopotential

For the purposes of calculating $(1/A)(dA/d\rho)$ we assume that the stress dependence of the form factor is local in nature. That is, we assume that $V(q, k_F)$ may be written as follows:

$$
V(q, k_{F})=f(q) /\left[\Omega_{0} \epsilon(q, k_{F})\right], \quad (10)
$$

where the $k_F$ dependence enters only in the atomic volume $\Omega_0$, which is proportional to $k_{F}^{-1/3}$, and in the Hartree dielectric function $\epsilon(q, k_F)$. It turns out that the resulting contribution to $\partial V_i/\partial \rho$ arising from the $\rho$ dependence of $k_F$ is much smaller than that arising from $\partial V/\partial q$ so that the precise form of the $k_F$ dependence of the form factor is probably not important. Our faith in (10) is further reinforced by the work of Harrison, $^{19}$ who shows that the form (10) is in good approximate agreement with the result of a calculation based on the full nonlocal theory for the pressure dependence of the pseudopotential for aluminum.

By assuming values for the slope of the form factor at $q_1$ and $q_2$ it is now possible to compute the quantity $(1/A)(\partial A/\partial \sigma)$. The results of these calculations are shown in Figs. 3-6 and Table I. We explicitly display some cross sections obtained from this calculation in Fig. 7. ### B. Calculation of the Stress Dependence of the Pseudopotential from Experimental Data

We now address the problem of determining in- formation about $V(q, k_{F})$ directly from the experi mental data. Since we have an equation of the form (8) for each of the six orbits for which we have experimental values of $(1 / A)(d A / d \rho)$ , it would appear possible to determine $\partial V_{i} / \partial \rho$ directly. This is not an easy task because of the experimen- tal error which is always present. Furthermore, this error is in effect magnified when solving for $\partial V_{i} / \partial \rho$ through the set of Eqs. (8). The basic difficulty is that the first two terms on the right- hand side of (8) already account for a substantial amount of the experimental values of $(1 / A)(d A / d \rho)$ , with the remaining contributions from the $\partial V_{i} / \partial \rho$ terms being relatively small. [Compare the sec- ond columns of Tables II and IV using the conver- sion factor (5).] It is then difficult to find a good single statistical fit to our six equations for the three unknowns $\partial V_{i} / \partial \rho$ .

We then may express the $\partial V_{i} / \partial \rho$ in terms of only $\partial V / \partial q$ at $q_{1}$ or $q_{2}$ , since all other quantities in (9) are calculated num- bers appearing in Table III. Armed with six equations in two unknowns we may attempt to find a good fit that falls within the experimental error of $(1 / A)(d A / d \rho)$ . To do this we cast theequations in the following very convenient form:
$$y_{\mu}=B x_{\mu}+C, \quad(11)$$
 where $B=(\partial V / \partial q)_{q=q_{2}}$ and $C=(\partial V / \partial q)_{q=q_{1}}$ are to be determined. The quantities $y_{\mu}$ and $x_{\mu}$ are calcu lated numbers appropriate to orbit $\mu$ , and may be obtained by inspection of (8) and (9).

Explicitly, we have
$$y_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\phi_{1}}\left[\left.\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{d A^{(\mu)}}{d \rho}\right|_{\text {expt }}-\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{\partial A^{(\mu)}}{\partial \rho}-\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{\partial A^{(\mu)}}{\partial E_{F}} \frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial \rho}-\sum_{i=1,2,3}\left(\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{\partial A^{(\mu)}}{\partial E_{F}} \frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial V_{i}}+\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{\partial A^{(\mu)}}{\partial V_{i}}\right) \frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial k_{F}} \frac{\partial k_{F}}{\partial \rho}\right] \quad(12)$$
 and
$$x_{\mu}=\left(\phi_{2}+\phi_{3}\right) / \phi_{1}, \quad(13)$$
 where
$$\phi_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{\partial A^{(\mu)}}{\partial V_{i}}+\frac{1}{A^{(\mu)}} \frac{\partial A^{(\mu)}}{\partial E_{F}} \frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial V_{i}}\right) \frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial \rho}. \quad(14)$$

The experimental value of $(1 / A)(d A / d \rho)$ appears only in $y_{\mu}$ . Numerical values for all other quanti ties appearing in $y_{\mu}$ and $x_{\mu}$ may be found in Tables II and III.
Since in an x-y coordinate system (11) is an equation for all points falling on a straight line of slope B and intercept C, we can determine these quantities by fitting a straight line through the six coordinate points $(x_{\mu}, y_{\mu})$ at our disposal. The systematic experimental error may be treated by assuming a value for the error and subsequently scaling all the experimental values by the assumed error. As the value of the assumed error is var- ied, the $y_{\mu}$ coordinates of each of the six points will then change according to (12). The statistical uncertainty in determining the mean value of(1/A)(dA/dp) from experiment may be directly taken into account by means of the appropriate error brackets in the $y_{\mu}$ values. We have taken this uncertainty to be $\pm 5 \%$ for the $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ orbits and $\pm 20 \%$ for the $\beta$ orbits. We show the resulting picture for our assumed values in Fig. 8, in which we have taken the systematic experimental error to be $-2.5 \%$ , this choice being made so as to ob tain a straight-line fit through our six bracketed points $(x_{\mu}, y_{\mu})$ . Our results are thus consistent with a systematic error considerably smaller than the maximum $10 \%$ error we had allowed possible in Sec. II. By reading off the slope and interceptof the straight line, we obtain the following values:
$$\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}\right|_{q=q_{1}}=0.15 \pm 0.07 \text { a.u. },$$

$$\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}\right|_{q=q_{2}}=0.05 \pm 0.01 \text { a.u. }. \quad(15)$$

IV. DISCUSSION
The basic results of our investigation are pre- sented in Figs 3-6 and in Table IV, and they re- veal good over-all agreement between theory and experiment. Perhaps the most striking feature of our results is the observed and calculated angu- lar constancy of the stress dependence of the or- bits. This constancy exists despite the fact that the extremal orbit areas themselves change quite markedly with orientation. The source of this be- havior can be traced to the locally cylindrical, or two dimensional, nature of the Fermi-surface sec- tions where the orbits are located. In such regions the stress-induced perturbations felt in a trans- verse plane are more or less independent of the location of the plane along the cylinder axis. It

Another noteworthy result of both our experiments and our theoretical calculations is that the stress dependence $(1 / A)(\partial A / \partial \sigma)$ is greater for the $\beta$ orbits than for the $\gamma$ orbits and greater for the $\alpha$ orbits than for the $\beta$ orbits. This result is consistent with the view that smaller orbits lie closer to Bragg planes and therefore are generally more sensitive to perturbations. This situation is illustrated explicitly in Fig. 7, which displays the results of calculations of various extremal orbits for different values of $\rho$. These pictures are meant to be more illustrative than physical, since from (5) we see that a change of 0.01 in $\rho$ would require a uniaxial stress of about 5 kbar.

Our calculations have also revealed the important sources of the stress dependence of the Fermi surface. For all the orbits which we have investigated, the main source of the stress dependence comes not from changes in the pseudopotential but rather from direct changes in the reciprocal-lattice vectors and in the Fermi energy. Because of this, one must have quite accurate experimental data in order to deduce the stress dependence of the pseudopotential directly from experiment. We have shown, however, that it is possible to derive values for the slope of the form factor by means of a systematic analysis combining our measurements with the results of a four-OPW calculation. The relevant calculated quantities appear in Tables II and III. Because of their general validity, these numbers would be of direct utility to future workers in fitting or interpreting new data for the indicated orbits.

With the aid of these numbers and the simple graphical procedure shown in Fig. 8, we were able to determine from our experiments the values of the slope of the form factor at the first two reciprocal-lattice vectors. We obtained the values $0.15 \pm 0.07$ a. u. at $q_{1}$ and $0.05 \pm 0.01$ a. u. at $q_{2}$. This compares with the values 0.13 a. u. at $q_{1}$ and 0.07 a. u. at $q_{2}$, which are the slopes of the Heine- Animalu form factor, and which we used in computing the theoretical stress dependence shown in Figs. 3-6 and in Table I. Since the values we have determined from our graphical procedure for the slopes of the form factor were derived by optimizing over-all agreement with experiment, it is clear that a calculation for the stress dependence using these fitted slopes will result in even better agreement with experiment than appears in Figs. 3-6 and Table I. In Table IV we show the resulting stress dependence derived from both our fitted form factor slopes and from the Heine-Animalu slopes. We note that agreement with experiment has been substantially improved for the $\alpha_{1}$ and $\beta_{1}$ orbits.

Ultimately, the most persuasive test of the consistency of experiment and theory comes from the fact that our graphical procedure works so well, meaning that it is possible to draw a straight line through the six brackets in Fig. 8. It therefore follows that within the systematic experimental error and within the experimental uncertainty in the mean value of $(1 / A)(\partial A / \partial \sigma)$ for each of the six orbits, the experimental results are in perfect accord with the results of a four-OPW calculation in which the values (15) are taken for the form factor slopes.

It will be of interest to see how well future experimental work on these and other orbits fit into our graphical analysis scheme of Fig. 8. Since additional data will generally give us new points in the figure, it is expected that a more accurate determination of the slopes of the form factor will

then prove possible. $^{21}$

## V. CONCLUSION
The oscillatory-magnetostriction technique has proven to be a very useful method for investigating the stress dependence of the Fermi surface of aluminum. The experimental results were shown to be in very good agreement with the results of a four-OPW pseudopotential calculation. Because of this consistency of theory and experiment it was possible to provide a systematic procedure for experimentally determining the slope of the pseudo- potential form factor at the two smallest recipro- cal-lattice vectors.
