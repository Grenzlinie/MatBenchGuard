# Many-Body Effects on the Third-Order Elastic Constants and Pressure Derivatives of the Second-Order Elastic Constants of CsCl Structure Solids

By
D. S. PURI, V. K. GARG, and M. P. VERMA

Lundqvist's potential for ionic solids is used to derive expressions for the second- order elastic (SOE) constants and the third-order elastic (TOE) constants. It is observed that the Cauchy relations among these constants do not hold even at 0 K and the dis- crepancies in this relation are directly expressible as function of the many-body term in the crystal potential. It is demonstrated that all parameters except one appearing in the expressions for the TOE constants and pressure derivatives of the SOE constants can be calculated from the values of the SOE constants, the equilibrium condition, and an approximate assumption concerning the overlap repulsion. Determining the remaining parameter $(a^{2} \partial^{2} f / \partial r^{2})_{0}$ from the expression of the pressure derivative of the bulk modulus all the six TOE constants and the remaining pressure derivatives of the SOE constants can be calculated. The values so obtained approach quite close to the measured values as against those of other theoretical workers in all the cases in which experimental results are available.

Es wird gezeigt, daß alle Parameter mit Ausnahme eines Parameters in den Ausdrücken für die elastischen Konstanten 3. Ordnung und für die Druckkoeffizienten der elastischen Konstanten 2. ## 1. Introduction

A quantum mechanical analysis of the cohesive energy of ionic solids was first made by Löwdin [1] and later in a somewhat simplified manner by Lund-qvist [2]. The analysis resulted in a many-body potential constituting 5 to $10 \%$  of the total potential and was able to provide an adequate explanation for the Cauchy discrepancy $C_{12}-C_{44}$ in alkali halides. The Lundqvist potential was later used in lattice dynamical studies on ionic solids by Verma and co-workers and the three-body force shell model initially developed by Verma and Singh [3] has successfully explained the dispersion curves in most ionic [4 to 6] and parti- ally ionic solids [7, 8] (other references given therein), clearly demonstrating

the effectiveness of the many-body potential term. These studies are however, limited to the harmonic approximation.

With a view to investigate the effect of the Lundqvist potential on the an- harmonic behaviour of ionic solids, Puri and Verma [9] obtained general expres- sions for the second-order elastic (SOE) and the third-order elastic (TOE) con- stants of ionic solids using the finite deformation theory and applied their analysis to solids crystallizing in the NaCl structure with excellent results. The present communication deals with an extension of this analysis to the case of ionic solids having the CsCl structure. It has been observed in the case of these solids also that the many-body term in the crystal potential leads to deviations from the Cauchy relations ($C_{12}=C_{44}$, $C_{112}=C_{166}$, $C_{123}=C_{144}=C_{456}$) among the SOE and the TOE constants while an identity $C_{123}+2C_{456}-3C_{144}=0$, connecting the TOE constants, still exists.

The only theoretical studies on the TOE constants of this system of solids available so far are those due to Nranyan [10] and Ghate [11] who have used the Born central force model for which Cauchy relations are satisfied at 0 K. They deduced the discrepancies in the Cauchy relations at room temperature by taking into consideration the thermal vibrations of the crystals. In their deduc- tion they assumed that the TOE constants vary linearly with temperature which cannot be considered to be a valid assumption as it is known that even the SOE constants do not vary linearly with temperature. Our analysis explains the Cauchy discrepancies among the TOE constants in a more satisfactory way and clearly demonstrates the breakdown of the Cauchy relations even at 0 K.

We have used our analysis to evaluate the TOE constants and pressure deri- vatives of the SOE constants by taking the contribution of the overlap repulsive potential up to the nearest and the next-nearest neighbours separately. The derivation of the expressions for the SOE and the TOE constants is given in Section 2. Section 3 describes the pressure derivatives of the SOE constants in parametric form. The calculations and results are given in Section 4 and are followed by a short discussion in Section 5.

## 2. Expressions for the SOE and TOE Constants

Based upon Born's method of homogeneous deformation, as outlined in our earlier paper [9], the potential energy can be expressed in a series of powers of the displacement gradients $U_{\alpha\beta}$ as

$$
\Phi=\Phi_{0}+V A_{\alpha \beta} U_{\alpha \beta}+\frac{V}{2} A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta} U_{\alpha \beta} U_{\gamma \delta}+\frac{V}{6} A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta \lambda \mu} U_{\alpha \beta} U_{\gamma \delta} U_{\lambda \mu}+\cdots, \quad(1)
$$

where summation is implied for repeated Greek suffixes. Various coefficients $A$ can be defined in terms of the derivatives of the potential energy with respect to the relative coordinates. So

$$
A_{\alpha \beta}=V^{-1} \sum_{k} \sum_{l^{\prime}, k^{\prime}}\left[\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_{\alpha}\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right)}\right] x_{\beta}\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right), \quad(2)
$$

$$
A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta}=V^{-1} \sum_{k} \sum_{l^{\prime}, k^{\prime}} \sum_{l^{\prime \prime}, k^{\prime \prime}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2} \Phi}{\partial x_{\alpha}\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right) \partial x_{\gamma}\left(l^{\prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime}\right)}\right] x_{\beta}\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right) x_{\delta}\left(l^{\prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime}\right), \quad(3)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta \lambda \mu}=V^{-1} \sum_{k} \sum_{l^{\prime}, k^{\prime}} \sum_{l^{\prime \prime}, k^{\prime \prime}} \sum_{l^{\prime \prime \prime}, k^{\prime \prime \prime}} & {\left[\frac{\partial^{3} \Phi}{\partial x_{\alpha}\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right) \partial x_{\gamma}\left(l^{\prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime}\right) \partial x_{\lambda}\left(l^{\prime \prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime \prime}\right)}\right] \times } \\
& \times x_{\beta}\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right) x_{\delta}\left(l^{\prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime}\right) x_{\mu}\left(l^{\prime \prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime \prime}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $(l = l_1, l_2, l_3)$ represents the cell to which the ion in the lattice belongs and $k = 1$ and $2$ for the positive and the negative ions, respectively. Index $l$ for the origin atom has been dropped. $\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(l', kk')$ gives the position vector joining the $(0, k)$-th ion to the $(l', k')$-th ion and $V$ is the elementary unit cell volume, equal to $8a^3$, for the CsCl-type ionic solids.

The $A$-coefficients can now be expressed in terms of corresponding elastic constant coefficients in the following form as given by Wallace [12] under equilibrium conditions:

$$
A_{\alpha \beta}=C_{\alpha \beta}=0, \tag{5}
$$

$$
A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta}=C_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta}, \tag{6}
$$

$$
A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta \lambda \mu}=C_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta \lambda \mu}+C_{\alpha \beta \delta \mu} \delta_{\gamma \lambda}+C_{\gamma \delta \beta \mu} \delta_{\alpha \lambda}+C_{\beta \delta \lambda \mu} \delta_{\alpha \gamma}, \tag{7}
$$

where $C_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta}$ and $C_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta \lambda \mu}$ are those defined by Brugger[13]. Changing to Voigt notation, we obtain from (6) and (7)

$$
C_{11}=A_{11}, \quad C_{12}=A_{12}, \quad C_{44}=A_{44}, \tag{8}
$$

$$
\left. \begin{array}{l}
C_{111}=A_{111}-3 C_{11}, \ C_{112}=A_{112}-C_{12}, \ C_{166}=A_{166}-C_{12}-2 C_{44}, \\
C_{123}=A_{123}, \quad \quad \quad C_{144}=A_{144}-C_{12}, \ C_{456}=A_{456}.
\end{array} \right\} \tag{9}
$$

The potential energy of the ionic crystal as derived by Lundqvist [2] is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi= & \frac{e^{2}}{2} \sum_{k} \sum_{l^{\prime}, k^{\prime}} \frac{\varepsilon(k) \varepsilon\left(k^{\prime}\right)}{r\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right)}+e^{2} \sum_{k} \sum_{l^{\prime}, k^{\prime}} V\left[r\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right)\right]+ \\
& +e^{2} \sum_{k} \sum_{l^{\prime}, k^{\prime}} \sum_{l^{\prime \prime \prime}, k^{\prime \prime \prime}} f_{k}\left[r\left(l^{\prime}, k k^{\prime}\right)\right] \frac{\varepsilon\left(k^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}\right)}{r\left(l^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}, k k^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}\right)},
\end{aligned} \tag{10}
$$

where $\varepsilon(k)$ is the valence of the $k$-type ion. The various terms in the potential energy expressions are the Coulomb energy, the overlap repulsive energy, extended up to next-nearest neighbours, and the three-body potential term, respectively. The function $f$ is related to the overlap integrals of free-ion one-electron wave functions and is assumed significant only for nearest neighbours. However, in the present study, we have chosen this function $f$ and its derivatives as disposable parameters.

The $A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta}$ and $A_{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta \lambda \mu}$ can be evaluated from (3) and (4) using various lattice sums, already determined by Verma and Lal [14], which leads to the following expressions for the SOE and TOE constants for CsCl-type crystals:

$$
C_{11}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{0.7010 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{A_{1}+2 B_{1}}{6}+\frac{A_{2}}{2}+3.1336 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\}, \tag{11}
$$

$$
C_{12}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{-0.5201 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{A_{1}-B_{1}}{6}+3.1336 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\}, \tag{12}
$$

$$
C_{44}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{-0.3505 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{A_{1}+2 B_{1}}{6}+\frac{B_{2}}{2}\right\}, \tag{13}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{111}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}} & \left\{-4.5656 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}-24 B_{1}}{18}+\frac{C_{2}-3 A_{2}}{2}+\right. \\
& \left.+2.7138 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}+13.1567 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned} \tag{14}
$$

\begin{aligned}
C_{112}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}} & \left\{0.7001 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}+3 B_{1}}{18}+\right. \\
& \left.+2.7138 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-6.2677 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{166}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}} & \left\{0.3609 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}+3 B_{1}}{18}-\left(B_{1}+B_{2}\right)+\right. \\
& \left.+0.9046 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-5.3266 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{123}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}} & \left\{1.2000 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}+3 B_{1}}{18}+\right. \\
& \left.+2.7138 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-15.9799 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{144}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}} & \left\{1.2000 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}+3 B_{1}}{18}+\right. \\
& \left.+0.9046 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-5.3266 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
C_{456}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{1.2000 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}+3 B_{1}}{18}\right\}.
$$

The expressions for $[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0},[a \partial f / \partial r]_{0}$, and $\left[a^{2} \partial^{2} f / \partial r^{2}\right]_{0}$ are evaluated for the equilibrium configuration with $r=a \sqrt{3} ; a$ being the lattice parameter. The parameters $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}, A_{2}, B_{2}$, and $C_{2}$ are defined as

$$
\left.\begin{array}{l}
2\left[\frac{\partial^{2} V_{1}^{\prime}(r)}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{r=a \sqrt{3}}=\frac{A_{1}}{8 a^{3}}, \quad 2\left[\frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial V_{1}^{\prime}(r)}{\partial r}\right]_{r=a \sqrt{3}}=\frac{B_{1}}{8 a^{3}}, \quad 2\left[r \frac{\partial^{3} V_{1}^{\prime}(r)}{\partial r^{3}}\right]_{r=a \sqrt{3}}=\frac{C_{1}}{8 a^{3}}, \\
{\left[\frac{\partial^{2} V_{2}^{\prime}(r)}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{r=2 a}=\frac{A_{2}}{8 a^{3}}, \quad\left[\frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial V_{2}^{\prime}(r)}{\partial r}\right]_{r=2 a}=\frac{B_{2}}{8 a^{3}}, \quad\left[r \frac{\partial^{3} V_{2}^{\prime}(r)}{\partial r^{3}}\right]_{r=2 a}=\frac{C_{2}}{8 a^{3}},}
\end{array}\right\}
$$

where $V_{1}^{\prime}(r)$ and $V_{2}^{\prime}(r)$ correspond to the overlapping repulsive potential of the nearest and the next-nearest neighbours, respectively. They are related to the Lundqvist repulsive potential $V(r)$ in the following manner:

$$
V^{\prime}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{M}} \varepsilon}{a} f(r),
$$

where $\alpha_{\mathrm{M}}$ is the Madelung constant of $\mathrm{CsCl}$ structure solids and $\varepsilon$ the valence of the ions. Now, under equilibrium condition, we have

$$
\left[\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial r}\right]_{r=a \sqrt{3}}=0,
$$

where $\Phi$ is the Lundqvist potential energy function given by (10). Then

$$
B_{1}+B_{2}=-0.3392 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}.
$$

The Cauchy discrepancies among the SOE and the TOE constants of CsCl-type ionic solids are therefore given by

$$
C_{12}-C_{44}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{3.1336 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\tag{24}
$$

$$
C_{112}-C_{166}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{1.8092 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-0.9411 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\tag{25}
$$

$$
C_{123}-C_{456}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{2.7138 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-15.9799 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\tag{26}
$$

$$
C_{144}-C_{456}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 a^{4}}\left\{0.9046 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-5.3266 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\}.
\tag{27}
$$

Obviously the Cauchy relations break down both for the SOE and for the TOE constants in the presence of three-body interactions. However, if we put the function $f$ and its derivatives equal to zero, the Cauchy relations are satisfied leaving only two independent SOE constants $(C_{11}$ and $C_{12})$ and three independent TOE constants $(C_{111}, C_{112}$, and $C_{123})$, a consequence of the central force model.

It is also obvious from (26) and (27) that even in the presence of the three- body forces, the TOE constants satisfy the following identity

$$
C_{123}+2 C_{456}-3 C_{144}=0
\tag{28}
$$

which comes out to be exactly identical with that obtained in the case of NaCl- type crystals [9].

### 3. Pressure Derivatives of the SOE Constants

Birch [15] has derived a set of effective SOE constants $C_{i j}^{\prime}$ from which the values of the pressure derivatives of these constants are obtained in the form

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} C_{44}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} p}=-\frac{C_{11}+2 C_{12}+C_{44}+C_{144}+2 C_{166}}{C_{11}+2 C_{12}},
\tag{29}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} S^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} p}=-\frac{3 C_{11}+3 C_{12}+C_{111}-C_{123}}{2\left(C_{11}+2 C_{12}\right)},
\tag{30}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} K^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} p}=-\frac{C_{111}+6 C_{112}+2 C_{123}}{3\left(C_{11}+2 C_{12}\right)},
\tag{31}
$$

where $K^{\prime}=\left(C_{11}^{\prime}+2 C_{12}^{\prime}\right) / 3, S^{\prime}=\left(C_{11}^{\prime}-C_{12}^{\prime}\right) / 2$, and $C_{i j k}$ are those defined by Brugger [13]. Hence pressure derivatives of the SOE constants, derived from the Lundqvist potential, take the parametric form

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} C_{44}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} p}=-(\Omega)^{-1} & \left\{1.2321 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{A_{1}-7 B_{1}+C_{1}+3 A_{2}-9 B_{2}}{6}+\right. \\
& \left.+2.7138 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-6.5791 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
\tag{32}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} S^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} p}=-(2 \Omega)^{-1} & \left\{-5.2229 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+A_{1}-B_{1}+\mathrm{C}_{2} / 2+\right. \\
& \left.+47.9383 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
\tag{33}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} K^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} p}=-(3 \Omega)^{-1} & \left\{2.0350 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{C_{1}-3 A_{1}+C_{2}-3 A_{2}}{2}+\right. \\
& \left.+24.4242 \varepsilon\left[a^{2} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial r^{2}}\right]_{0}-56.4091 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}\right\},
\end{aligned}
\tag{34}
$$

where

$$
\Omega=-0.3392 \varepsilon[\varepsilon+16 f(r)]_{0}+\frac{A_{1}+A_{2}}{2}+9.4008 \varepsilon\left[a \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right]_{0}.
\tag{35}
$$

## 4. Results

The six overlap repulsive potential parameters $A_{1}, B_{1}, C_{1}, A_{2}, B_{2}$, and $C_{2}$ can be transformed into two independent parameters by approximating the overlap repulsive potentials $V_{1}^{\prime}(r)$ and $V_{2}^{\prime}(r)$ as two parameter potentials of the form, given by Born and Mayer [17] (see also [16]),

$$
\sum_{\mathrm{NN}} V_{1}^{\prime}(r)=b N\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{n_{1}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{n_{2}}\right) \mathrm{e}^{\left(r_{1}+r_{2}-r\right) / \varrho},
\tag{36}
$$

$$
\sum_{\mathrm{NNN}} V_{2}^{\prime}(r)=\frac{b N^{\prime}}{2}\left[\left(1+2 \frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{n_{1}}\right) \mathrm{e}^{2 r_{1} / \varrho}+\left(1+2 \frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{n_{2}}\right) \mathrm{e}^{2 r_{2} / \varrho}\right] \mathrm{e}^{-r / \varrho},
\tag{37}
$$

where $b$ and $\varrho$ are two unknown constants. $N$ and $N^{\prime}$ correspond to the number of nearest and second-nearest neighbours which in the case of the CsCl structure are 8 and 6, respectively. $r_{1}, \varepsilon_{1}, n_{1}$ are the ionic radii, valence, and number of valence electrons in the outermost shell of the positive ion. Similarly $r_{2}, \varepsilon_{2}, n_{2}$ refer to the negative ion. In view of (36) and (37), we obtain

$$
A_{1}=-\frac{\sqrt{3} a B_{1}}{\varrho},
\tag{38}
$$

$$
A_{2}=-\frac{2 a B_{2}}{\varrho},
\tag{39}
$$

$$
\frac{B_{2}}{B_{1}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\left\{\frac{3}{4} \mathrm{e}^{\left[r_{1}-r_{2}-(2-\sqrt{3}) a\right] / \varrho}+\frac{5}{4} \mathrm{e}^{\left[r_{2}-r_{1}-(2-\sqrt{3}) a\right] / \varrho}\right\},
\tag{40}
$$

$$
C_{1}=\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{B_{1}},
\tag{41}
$$

$$
C_{2}=\frac{A_{2}^{2}}{B_{2}}.
\tag{42}
$$

The SOE constants (11) to (13), the equilibrium condition (23), and (38) to (40) give us seven equations in the seven parameters $A_{1}, B_{1}, A_{2}, B_{2},[f(r)]_{0},[a \partial f / \partial r]_{0}$, and $\varrho$. A method of successive approximations with a suitably chosen initial value of $\varrho$ quickly leads to a consistent set of these parameters. Now the parameters $C_{1}$ and $C_{2}$ can be evaluated from (41) and (42). The remaining parameter $[a^{2} \partial^{2} f / \partial r^{2}]_{0}$ may be evaluated from the experimental value of the pressure derivative of the bulk modulus viz. $\mathrm{d} K^{\prime} / \mathrm{d} p$ (or from the pressure derivative of $C_{44}^{\prime}$ ) and all the six TOE constants and the remaining two pressure derivatives of the SOE constants can then be easily calculated.

### 5. Discussion

Equations (11) to (19) have been derived ignoring the thermal vibrations and are therefore expected to be best satisfied at 0 K. In absence of measurements at low temperatures the numerical applications of our analysis had to be done assuming these relations to hold at room temperature, too. Hence our results can be considered only approximately correct. An important finding of our analysis, however, is the breakdown of the Cauchy relations for the TOE constants even at 0 K. Ghate [11] and Nranyan [10] deduced expressions for the TOE constants in the framework of the central force Born model for which the Cauchy relations ($C_{112}=C_{166}$, $C_{123}=C_{144}=C_{456}$) are satisfied at 0 K. The failure of the Cauchy relations at room temperature was assumed by these authors to be entirely due to the vibrational part of the crystal energy. Our analysis clearly shows that a substantial part of the discrepancy at room temperature also can be ascribed to the presence of many-body interactions which have played an important role in describing the vibrational properties of these ionic solids in general.

It is interesting to note that, even in the presence of many-body interactions, the identity $C_{123}+2C_{456}-3C_{144}=0$ is satisfied both in the case of NaCl structure [9] and the CsCl structure solids. Low-temperature measurements of the TOE constants of these solids can in view of this identity provide a direct check on the validity of the Lundqvist potential for this system.

Measurements on the TOE constants of cesium halide crystals have not been made so far. The only available experimental data on this system are the room temperature pressure derivatives of the SOE constants as defined by (29) to (31). We have, therefore, compared our theoretical results with these measurements at room temperature. Our calculated values with the repulsive potential extended up to second-nearest neighbours, agree far better with the experimental values than those of Ghate [11] and Nranyan [10].
