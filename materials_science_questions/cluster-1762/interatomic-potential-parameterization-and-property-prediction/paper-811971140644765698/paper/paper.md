# EQUATION OF STATE OF NaCl: ULTRASONIC MEASUREMENTS TO 8 KBAR AND 800°C AND STATIC LATTICE THEORY*

H. SPETZLER
Sandia Laboratories, Livermore, Calif., U.S.A.

and

C. G. SAMMIS† and R. J. O'CONNELL‡
Seismological Laboratory, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, Calif. 91109, U.S.A.

(Received 9 August 1971; in revised form 7 February 1972)

Abstract--The elastic properties of NaCI have been measured ultrasonically as a function of pressure and temperature, from 0 to 8 kbar and from 300 to 800°K. Together with zero pressure data on thermal expansivity and heat capacity, these data permit the specification of the equation of state ($PVT$) throughout the $P$-$T$ range measured. This permits extrapolation of high temperature ($T > \theta_D$) data to zero temperature for comparison with properties predicted for a static lattice. A Born lattice model calculation shows the importance of second nearest neighbor interactions, ionicity and the functional form of the potential. The measured equation of state permits detailed comparison with theory, and the separation of volume and temperature effects. Grüneisen's parameter is shown to be primarily dependent on volume, with small temperature dependence; it is not a linear function however.

## 1. INTRODUCTION
THE MOST precise techniques for determining elastic constants involve ultrasonic measurements. The resonant frequencies of carefully cut specimens give precise elastic constant data. By carefully selecting specimen shape and orientation all elastic constants of a crystal may be determined. This resonant technique may be used for measurements as a function of temperature, but it is difficult if not impossible to obtain measurements as a func- tion of pressure. An alternate technique which has been pioneered at Bell Labs by McSkimin [1] involves an ultrasonic interferometer. This technique has been modified by various in- vestigators and adopted to measurements under hydrostatic pressure. The simultaneous application of high temperature and high pressure has remained a considerable problem.

If the entire pressure and temperature range over which precise elastic constant data exist were to be shaded on a $P$-$T$ diagram (with $STP$ as the origin), very little shading could be done off the axis. That is, all pressure data exist over a very limited temperature range, and all high temperature data have been taken at atmospheric pressure or under vacuum. If one wished to plot lines of constant volume, constant entropy, shock Hugoniots or maybe a pressure vs. temperature curve for the earth, one would find a great lack of data upon leaving the axes along any of these paths. In lattice dynamics, the various parameters are usually expressed as functions of pressure and temperature along lines of constant volume. In the reduction of shock wave data, rather stringent assumptions are

The desirability to extend experimental measurements of the elastic constants over large ranges of simultaneously high pressure and temperature is evident from above. Anderson *et al.*[2] in a recent paper expressed the need for higher order pressure derivatives and mixed temperature and pressure derivatives in the following way:

'In summary, the experimental situation is such that we cannot make meaningful comments on the magnitude of

For the reasons outlined above, a technique was developed by which ultrasonic interferometry could be done under simultaneously high pressure and high temperature. Because of geophysical interests in oxides and the abundance of data along the temperature and pressure axis, polycrystalline and single crystal MgO were first studied by this technique (Spetzler[3]). Because of the high bulk modulus (1624 Kb) and the high Debye temperature ($\sim 940^\circ$K), only small effects of pressure upon temperature derivatives and vice-versa were obtainable.

NaCl was chosen for the present study for the following reasons: its wide use as an X-ray pressure standard, the availability of shock data, its relative amiability towards lattice calculations, its simple crystal structure, its low bulk modulus, its low Debye temperature and finally its availability and ease of handling.

The experimental results are interpreted in terms of a Born-type electrostatic lattice model. Although such calculations have been made in the past (e.g. Lincoln *et al.*[4]), the unique simultaneous temperature and pressure range covered by the data presented in this paper allows the pressure derivatives to be extrapolated to their zero-temperature values appropriate to the static lattice model. The effects of ionicity, van der Waals interaction, and second neighbor anion-anion interactions are investigated.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
A detailed description of the experimental technique employed in these measurements has been given earlier (Spetzler[3]). Only a short outline will be given here. A simplified schematic of the ultrasonic interferometer is given in Fig. 1. Bursts of r.f. (radio frequency) energy are applied to a quartz transducer at the end of a fused silica buffer rod. Acoustic signals so generated travel down the buffer

![](./images/811971140644765698_1.jpg)

Fig. 1. A simplified schematic of the ultrasonic interferometer. A frequency synthesizer provides the frequency for the coherent r.f. bursts which are needed for interferometry.

rod, are partially reflected from the buffer rod-sample interface and also subsequently reverberate within the sample and buffer rod. If the travel time through the sample is less than one-half that through the buffer rod, at least two sample echoes can be observed before they are obscured by buffer rod echoes. When two such r.f. bursts are applied and their spacing is approximately twice the travel time through the sample, the sample echoes will at least partially overlap. At the portion where the sample echoes overlap the acoustic waves will interfere. Depending upon the phase relationship between the echoes, this interference can be constructive or destructive. When the two applied r.f. pulses are phase coherent, i.e., originate from the same continuous r.f. source, constructive or destructive interference can be obtained by adjusting the r.f. frequency. Such constructive or destructive interference occurs whenever the frequency is such that there are a half-integer number of wavelengths in the sample. The velocity, $v$, in the sample is then determined by

$$
v=\frac{2 l}{\tau}=\frac{2 l F_{n}}{n}
$$

where $\tau$ is the travel time through twice the sample length $l$ and is given by $\tau=n / F_{n}, n$ being the integer or half integer number mentioned above and $F_{n}$ is the corresponding frequency at which constructive or destructive interference occurred. When such a condition occurs, the amplitude of the interferring echoes is either a maximum or minimum.

The two r.f. bursts are generated by gating and amplifying the output of a crystal controlled frequency synthesizer. The gating pulses for the gated amplifier are provided by a dual pulse generator. The nonlinear attenuation network allows the r.f. power bursts to get to the transducer, at the same time protecting the input of the signal amplifier. The signals from the echoes pass with little attenuation to the signal amplifier and detector, and are displayed on an oscilloscope.

The transducer is bonded to the buffer rod with an epoxy resin. Acoustic coupling between buffer rod and the sample is provided through a lapped bond. Both the sample and the buffer rod are lapped flat to a small fraction of a wavelength of Na light and then brought into contact after careful cleaning of the surfaces. With perserverance and some luck a good acoustic coupling can be achieved.

The lengths of the buffer rod and sample are approximately 2 and $\frac{1}{2}$ in. respectively. Their diameters are $\frac{1}{2}$ in. The diameter of the contact area between buffer rod and sample is $\sim \frac{1}{4}$ in. To perform the actual measurements under high temperature and high pressure, the buffer rod and sample are placed in a furnace, which in turn is inserted into the cavity of a high pressure vessel. Two Chromel-Alumel thermocouples are in contact at the middle and the end of the sample. The buffer rod extends out of the hot zone to allow the transducer to remain at a lower temperature. The outside furnace dimensions are 2 in. in dia. by 6 in. long. The pressure generating system is a conventional 2 stage gas system. Argon is used as the pressure medium. The pressure is measured with a manganin wire coil.

The data were fit initially by a multiple linear regression routine, which retained fitting parameters only if they significantly improved the fit, as determined by a $F$-test at the 5 per cent level. After the significant parameters were thus selected, the data were refit again using a different least squares routine that was computationally more accurate. The values of the parameters obtained from either routine agreed.

### 4. THEORETICAL INTERPRETATION

The elastic constants are calculated as the second derivatives of the internal energy density with respect to some tensor measure of the strains. This is usually done in one of two ways. The energy density is either expanded as a Taylor's series in the strains or expressed as the sum of two-body interatomic potentials. We call the former the finite strain approach and the latter the interatomic potential approach.

![](./images/811971140644765698_5.jpg)

Fig. 5. $F_2$ vs. pressure at 3 temperatures. + represent one standard error obtained by fitting a surface in $P$-$T$ space to the experimental points. $X$ represents one standard error when this surface is calculated from the other three surfaces according to the relations given in Table 2 and the data in Table 3.

The best and most complete formulation of the finite strain approach is given by Leibfried and Ludwig[8]. It has recently been applied to NaCl by Thomsen[9]; the reader is referred to these works for the details. By assuming a functional form for the two-body potentials, the free energy density may be written as an analytical func- tion of the volume at any compression. The convergence question facing the finite strain approach is related to two new questions: (1) can the free energy be adequately de- scribed in terms of two-body interactions and (2) is the assumed functional form of the two- body potential a good approximation to the complex bonding forces? Unlike the conver- gence question facing the finite strain approach, these two may be answered using the ultrasonic data. In the following develop- ment, a physically motivated two-body poten- tial will be formulated using only two empirical parameters and thus requiring only two pieces of input data, the bulk modulus $\tilde{K}$ and density $\tilde{\rho}$ of the static lattice. All the other elastic constants and their pressure derivatives will then be computed and com- pared to the data. It should be pointed out that the following computations are for a static lattice and thus give the elastic constants and their pressure derivatives at $T=0$ and do not contain contributions from the zero point vibrations. However, the measurement of $\mathrm{d} C_{i j} / \mathrm{d} T$ and the unique determination of $\mathrm{d}^2 C_{i j} / \mathrm{d} T \mathrm{~d} P$ given in this work allow us to make an approximate extrapolation of the elastic constants and their pressure deriva- tives to $T=0$ for comparison with the static lattice predictions. Tosi[12] investigated the cohesive energy of the alkali-halides in the Born approximation by comparing the cohesive energy calculated from the lattice model with the experimental value calculated from the Born-Haber cycle. He concluded that the experimental energies were best fit by a Born-model with an exponential dependence of the repulsive energy on the ionic separation and which explicitly included the van Waals interactions and the effects of the vibrational internal pressure. He found no large or systematic discrepancy between the energies calculated from this model and the experimental values and concluded that estimates of the amount of covalent character per molecule based on Pauling's scale for the electronegativity of atoms are too large.

It is the objective of the following discussion to further test the Born-model by comparing the predicted elastic constants and their first and second pressure derivatives with the new experimental data given in this paper. Specifically, the effects on the elastic constants of the functional form of the repulsive energy, the inclusion of van der Waals forces, and the explicit consideration of second neighbors will be investigated. While the effects of the two latter modifications on the internal enegy are small due to a readjustment of the repulsive parameters[11], it will be shown that their effect on the elastic constants can be quite large, especially for the shear constant $c_{44}$.

## 5. THE BORN MODEL

Expressions for the volume dependence
of the elastic constants of cubic crystals are
given by Blackman [13].

$$
C_{11}=\frac{1}{2 \Delta} \sum_{k k^{\prime} l}\left\{P_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\left(X_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}+Q_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\left(X_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{4}\right\}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{12}= & \frac{1}{2 \Delta} \sum_{k k^{\prime} l}\left\{-P_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\left(X_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}+Q_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\left(X_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}\right. \\
& \left.\times\left(Y_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}\right\}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{44}^{*}= & \frac{1}{2 \Delta} \sum_{k k^{\prime} l}\left\{P_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\left(Y_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}+Q_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\left(X_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}\right. \\
& \left.\times\left(Y_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}\right)^{2}\right\}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
C=\frac{1}{\Delta} \sum_{l} Q_{12}^{l} X_{12}^{l} Y_{12}^{l} Z_{12}^{l}
$$

$$
D=\frac{1}{\Delta} \sum_{l}\left\{P_{12}^{l}+Q_{12}^{l}\left(X_{12}^{l}\right)^{2}\right\}
$$

$$
C_{44}=C_{44}^{*}-\frac{C^{2}}{D}
$$

where
$$
P_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}=\left\{\frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial \phi_{k k^{\prime}}}{\partial r}\right\} \quad Q_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}=\left[\frac{1}{r} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} r}\left\{\frac{1}{r} \frac{\mathrm{d} \phi_{k k^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d} r}\right\}\right]_{\mathbf{r}_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}}
$$

$\phi_{k k^{\prime}}(r)=$ two body potential
$$
\mathbf{r}_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}=\mathbf{r}_{k}-\mathbf{r}_{k^{\prime}}+l_{1} R \hat{i}+l_{2} R \hat{j}+l_{3} R \hat{k}
$$

$l\left(l_{1}, l_{2}, l_{3}\right)=$ lattice cell index
$X_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}, Y_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}, Z_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}=$ cartesion component of $\mathbf{r}_{k k^{\prime}}^{l}$
$R=$ nearest neighbor distance
$k=1, S$ indexes the primitive
lattices
$\Delta=$ volume/ion.

Since $\sum_{kk'l}P'_{kk'}=0$ at equilibrium $(r=\tilde{r})$, we have for centrosymmetric crystals $(C=0)$ the Cauchy relation $\tilde{C}_{12}=\tilde{C}_{44}$. Experimentally this is not exactly obeyed for NaCl where $\tilde{C}_{12}=127$ kb and $\tilde{C}_{44}=140$ kb. Thus the assumption that the internal energy can be represented as a sum of two body potentials breaks down. Löwdin[14] has shown that this discrepancy can be explained in terms of three body interactions which arise in the quantum-mechanical treatment, and that the deviation from Cauchy's relation increase as the ratio of anion to cation radius increase. Since this deviation is not too severe in NaCl (as compared with MgO for example) we will proceed with the two-body treatment.

### 6. NEAREST NEIGHBOR REPULSION ONLY
Anderson and Liebermann[15] have evaluated equations (1) for the NaCl, CsCl, and ZnS lattices with nearest neighbor repulsion only. For the NaCl lattice they give:

$$
\begin{align}
C_{11}(R) &= \frac{-2\cdot55604\ \varphi e^2}{2R^4} + \frac{V_{AB}''}{R} \\
C_{12}(R) &= \frac{0\cdot11298\ \varphi e^2}{2R^4} - \frac{V_{AB}'}{R^2} \\
C_{44}(R) &= C_{44}^*(R) = \frac{1\cdot27802\ \varphi e^2}{2R^4} + \frac{V_{AB}'}{R^2}.
\end{align}
\tag{2}
$$

In these expressions the prime denotes differentiation with respect to $R$ and $V_{AB}(RO)$ is the repulsive part of the cation-anion potential. The ionicity factor $0<\varphi<1$ has been introduced to test the effect of a lowered ionicity on the elastic constants. $V_{AB}$ is usually assumed to be of the form

$$
V(R)=\frac{B}{R^n} \quad \text{or} \quad V(R)=\lambda e^{-R/\rho}.
$$

Computations have been carried out for both forms of the potential. As will be shown the exponential form is in better agreement with the data.

The first and second pressure derivatives of the elastic constants were computed according to the relations

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}P} = \frac{\mathrm{d}C_{ij}/\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}R}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}P^2} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}R}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}P}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}P}.
$$

The identity $\mathrm{d}R/\mathrm{d}P=-R/3K$ allows these expressions to be more simply written. At $P=0$ they are,

$$
\begin{align}
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}P}\right)_0 &= -\frac{\tilde{R}}{3\tilde{K}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}R}\right)_{\tilde{R}} \\
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}P^2}\right)_0 &= \frac{\tilde{R}}{3K}\Bigg\{ \frac{\tilde{R}}{3\tilde{K}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}R^2}\right)_{\tilde{R}} + \left[\frac{1}{\tilde{K}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}K}{\mathrm{d}P}\right)_0\right. \\
&\quad\left. + \frac{1}{3\tilde{K}}\right]\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}C_{ij}}{\mathrm{d}R}\right)_{\tilde{R}} \Bigg\}.
\end{align}
\tag{3}
$$

Direct differentiation of equations (2) allows these expression to be evaluated directly for the case of nearest neighbor interactions only and no explicit van der Waals forces.

### 7. INCLUDING VAN DER WAALS AND SECOND NEIGHBOR ANION-ANION INTERACTIONS
Based on the relative ionic radii $(R_{\mathrm{Na}+}=0.95\mathring{A},\ R_{\mathrm{Cl}-}=1.81\ \mathring{A})$ one would expect the larger $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ ions to have a larger overlap and thus contribute more to the second neighbor interactions than the smaller $\mathrm{Na}^+$ ions. The second neighbor interaction is usually assumed to have the same functional form as the nearest neighbor repulsion, thus introducing two new empirical constants which must be evaluated from the data; i.e. two elastic constants. However, if one notes that $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ is isoelectronic with argon, it seems reasonable to assume that two $\mathrm{Cl}^-$ second neighbors will have the same two-body

potential function as solid Argon. Lennard-

a number of oxides in the rutile structure.

\tag{4}

### 8. EVALUATING THE EMPIRICAL CATION-ANION REPULSIVE PARAMETERS

The only two input parameters are the
nearest neighbor distance and the bulk
modulus of the static lattice which in the
notation of Leibfried and Ludwig[8] are
$\tilde{K}$ and $\tilde{R}=(\tilde{V} / \mathrm{Na})^{1 / 3}=(M / 2 \mathrm{Na} \tilde{\rho})^{1 / 3}$. In terms
of these quantities the equilibrium equation
$(\partial \tilde{W}_{L} / \partial V)=0$ and the bulk modulus $\tilde{K}=$
$\tilde{V}(\partial^{2} \tilde{W}_{L} / \partial V^{2})$ yield

$$
\tilde{R} / \rho=n+1=\tau / \sigma
\tag{9}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau= & 18 \tilde{K}+\frac{2 \alpha \varphi e^{2}}{\tilde{R}^{4}}+\frac{42 Z_{A B} C_{A B}}{R^{3}}+\frac{Z_{B B}}{2}\left[\frac{42 C_{B B}}{8 \tilde{R}^{9}}\right. \\
& \left.-\frac{156 D_{B B}}{64 \tilde{R}^{15}}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\sigma=\frac{\alpha \varphi e^{2}}{\tilde{R}^{4}}+\frac{6 Z_{A B} C_{A B}}{\tilde{R}^{9}}+\frac{Z_{B B}}{2}\left[\frac{6 C_{B B}}{8 \tilde{R}^{9}}-\frac{12 D_{B B}}{64 \tilde{R}^{15}}\right].
$$

Note that for the simpler case of nearest

neighbor repulsion only and no van der Waals forces, setting $C_{AB}=C_{BB}=D_{BB}=0$ in the above expression gives the familiar relation (Kittel[17])

$$
\bar{R} / \rho=n+1=\frac{18 \tilde{K} \bar{R}^{4}}{\alpha \varphi e^{2}}+2. \tag{10}
$$

The second empirical constant is given by

$$
\lambda=\frac{\bar{R} e^{R / \rho}}{(\bar{R} / \rho) Z_{A B}} \delta \quad B=\frac{\bar{R}^{n+1}}{n Z_{A B}} \delta
$$

$$
\delta=\frac{\alpha \varphi e^{2}}{\bar{R}^{2}}+\frac{6 Z_{A B} C_{A B}}{R^{7}}+\frac{Z_{B B}}{2}\left[\frac{6}{8} \frac{C_{B B}}{\bar{R}^{7}}-\frac{12}{64} \frac{D_{B B}}{\bar{R}^{13}}\right].
$$

Because of the zero point vibrations, $\tilde{V}$ and $\tilde{K}$ can not be directly measured. Their values may be obtained by a linear extrapolation from the high temperature regime as will now be demonstrated. Assume the equation of state of NaCl is adequately represented in the high temperature regime $(T>\Theta_{D})$ by the Mie-Gruneisen equation

$$
P=-\frac{\partial F(V, T)}{\partial V}=-\frac{\mathrm{d} W_{L}}{\mathrm{~d} V}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{V}\right) E_{\mathrm{vib}} \quad(12)
$$

$$
E_{\mathrm{vib}}=\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} h \nu_{i}+k T \sum_{i} \frac{h \nu_{i} / k T}{e^{h \nu_{i} / k T}-1}. \tag{13}
$$

Assume further that the Grüneisen constant $\gamma$ is independent of temperature and linear in volume.

Following Born and Huang[11], expand $W_{L}$ in a Taylor's series in $V$ and $\tilde{V}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{L}(V)= & W_{0}+\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^{2} W_{L}}{\partial V^{2}}(V-\tilde{V})^{2}+\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^{3} W_{L}}{V^{3}} \\
& \times(V-\tilde{V})^{3}+\ldots
\end{aligned}
$$

Retaining only linear terms one has

$$
\frac{\partial W_{L}}{\partial V}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2} W_{L}}{\partial V^{2}}\right)_{\tilde{V}}(V-\tilde{V})+\ldots
$$

$$
\frac{\partial^{2} W_{L}}{\partial V^{2}}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2} W_{L}}{\partial V^{2}}\right)_{\tilde{V}}+\left(\frac{\partial^{3} W_{L}}{\partial V^{3}}\right)_{\tilde{V}}(V-\tilde{V})+\ldots
$$

At $P=0$, equation (12) may be written

$$
\left(\frac{\partial^{2} W_{L}}{\partial V^{2}}\right)_{\tilde{V}}(V-\tilde{V})=\frac{\gamma E_{\mathrm{vib}}}{V}
$$

so

$$
V=\tilde{V}+\frac{\tilde{V}}{\tilde{k}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{V}\right) E_{\mathrm{vib}}. \tag{14}
$$

Since the high temperature limit of equation (13) is $E_{\mathrm{vib}}=3 N k T$ (expanding the exponential term to second order gets rid of the zero point term), assuming $\gamma / V$ is constant at high $T$, equation (14) has the form

$$
V=\tilde{V}+(\text { constant }) T. \tag{15}
$$

Thus $\tilde{V}$ may be found by a linear extrapolation of $\left.V(T)\right|_{P=0}$ from the high temperature region

## 10. COMPARISON WITH STATIC COMPRESSION AND SHOCK WAVE RESULTS

The static compression of NaCl (and 26 other halides) has most recently been reported by Vaidya and Kennedy [20]. These measurements were performed in a piston-cylinder apparatus, and extend to 45 kbar pressure. They report for NaCl, with $P$ in kbar:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{V}{V_{0}}= & 1-4 \cdot 273 \times 10^{-3} P+4 \cdot 658 \times 10^{-5} P^{2} \\
& -3 \cdot 75 \times 10^{-7} P^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

as the best polynomial fit to their data. This yields upon differentiation $K_{T}=234 \cdot 03$ kbar, $K_{T}^{\prime}=4 \cdot 10$ and $K_{T}^{\prime \prime}=0 \cdot 116(\mathrm{kbar})^{-1}$. A Murnaghan equation $(K_{T}(P)=K_{T}(0)+K_{T}^{\prime}(0)P)$ fit to the same data yields $K_{T}=232 \cdot 3$ kbar and $K_{T}^{\prime}=4 \cdot 92$. The different values of $K_{T}$ and $K_{T}^{\prime}$ resulting from fitting different functions to the data is an illustration of the effect of the functional form of the assumed equation of state on the derived parameters. (This has been discussed at length by MacDonald[21].) Obviously this should be borne in mind whenever comparing parameters measured by different techniques over different pressure ranges.

A more direct comparison of the volumes at 5 and 10 kbars gives $(V/V_{0})(5)=0.98034$, $(V/V_{0})(10)=0.96267$ and $(V/V_{0})(5)=0.9798$, $(V/V_{0})(10)=0.9616$ for the ultrasonic and static compression results respectively. The static compression data indicate a greater compressibility, as implied by the values for $K_{T}$ and $K_{T}^{\prime}$. The relative precision and pressure ranges of the two techniques should be borne in mind in assessing the importance of this comparison. At lower pressures, friction and uncertainty of the initial piston displacement corresponding to $V_{0}$ limit the precision of the static compression measurements. In addition the derived equation of state parameters represent some average of the actual values over the pressure range of the measurements. Thus the average ultrasonic value over 10 kbar of $K_{T}^{\prime}=5 \cdot 35-0 \cdot 40=4 \cdot 95$ compares well with the static compression value of $4 \cdot 92$ which represents some average of $\partial K_{T}(P)/\partial P$ from 0 to 45 kbar. At pressures greater than 10 kbar the value of $K^{\prime}(P)$ extrapolated from the ultrasonic results decreases more rapidly than implied by the compression data. Nevertheless the two methods give the same value for the compressibility at $P \sim 20$ kbar.

It is not obvious to what extent these differences and convergences are due to lack of precision or errors in the measurements or to the inadequacy of the assumed equation of state to represent the actual behavior of the material. In view of these possibilities, we conclude that the measurements are consistent over their common pressure range (0-10 kbar) and that there is an indication that the second order expansion of $K(P)$ that fits the ultrasonic data to 10 kbar may not be safely extrapolated to much greater pressures.

The shock wave compression of NaCl and the reduction of the Hugoniot to an isotherm has been reported by Fritz et al.[22]. These measurements extend throughout the range of stability of NaCl in the NaCl structure, up to $\sim 230$ kbar. Owing to the nature of the experiment, the direct data corresponding to

pressures less than $\sim 50$ kbar are few and possibly unreliable, so that a direct comparison of the ultrasonic, static compression and shock wave results over a common pressure range is difficult. Nevertheless, the isotherm computed by Fritz et al.[23] gives $(V / V_{0})$ $(5\ \text{kbar})=0.9803,\ (V/V_{0})(10)=0.9629$ and $(V/V_{0})(45)=0.8753$ while static compression gives $(V/V_{0})(45)=0.8724$. Thus the static data implies a greater compressibility to 45 kbar than the shock wave results, as do the ultrasonic values to 10 kbar.

A parameteric comparison may be made by relating the parameters in the fit to the data relating shock velocity $u_{s}$ to particle velocity $u_{p}$

$$
u_{s}=c+su_{p}+s'u_{p}^{2}+...
$$

to the bulk modulus along an isentrope:

$$
K_{s}(P)=K_{s}^{\circ}+K_{s}'(P)+\frac{1}{2}K_{s}''P^{2}.
$$

This yields[23]

$$
c=(K_{s}^{\circ}/\rho)^{1/2}
$$

$$
s=(K_{s}'+1)/4
$$

$$
s'=[s(7-K_{s}'+4\gamma_{0})+2K_{s}^{\circ}K_{s}'']/24c
$$

where $\gamma_{0}$ is the value of the Grüneisen parameter at zero pressure. Fritz et al.[22] obtained $c=3.403,\ s=1.5422$ and $s'=$ $-0.07345$ as the best fit to their data when $c$ was constrained to fit Haussühl's[24] ultrasonic value of $K_{s}^{\circ}=250.5$ kbar. By omitting eight questionable data points at low pressures, Weaver[25] obtained $1.5141$ and $-0.05738$ for $s$ and $s'$ for the same data. Our ultrasonic data $K_{s}=252.09,\ (\partial K_{s}/\partial P)_{s}^{\circ}=$ $5.040$ and $(\partial^{2}K_{s}/\partial P^{2})_{s}^{\circ}=-0.068$ yields $c=3.4131,\ s=1.510$ and $s'=-0.262$. The agreement of $s$ with Weaver's value is excellent; the $s$ values correspond to values of $(\partial K_{T}/\partial P)_{T}$ of $5.33$ and $5.35$ for the shock wave and ultrasonic values respectively. The ultrasonic value of $s'$ is much greater than the shock wave value, and in fact the implied $u_{s}-u_{p}$ curve falls far below the data. A value of $(\partial^{2}K_{s}/\partial P^{2})_{s}^{\circ}=-0.034$ yields $s'=-0.0531$ which agrees with Weaver's value. It therefore appears that a second order expansion of $K_{s}(P)$ with $K_{s}''=-0.068$ will not fit the shock wave data, whereas it will fit with $K_{s}''=$ $-0.034$. The errors we have quoted on our measured value of $(\partial^{2}K_{s}/\partial P^{2})_{T}$ are roughly $\pm 25$ per cent, which come from the fit of the raw frequency vs. pressure data and the redundancy check. The stated error for the isentropic derivative would be slighly greater owing to uncertainties of $(\partial^{2}K_{s}/\partial P\partial T)_{s,P}$, but this would not account for an error of 50 per cent.

Thus an isentropic equation of state

$$
K_{s}(P)=K_{s}^{\circ}+K_{s}'P+\frac{1}{2}K_{s}'P^{2}
$$

with the ultrasonic values of the parameters does not accurately fit the shock wave data, although it would if the ultrasonic value of $K_{s}''$ were reduced by 50 per cent. It is not obvious whether the discrepancy is due to an error in $K_{s}''$ or the inadequacy of the equation of state. A value of $\partial^{3}K_{s}/\partial P^{3}=0.0003$ would reduce the average magnitude of $\partial^{2}K_{s}/\partial P^{2}$ to an acceptable value over the range of the shock pressures, yet would be beyond the resolution of ultrasonic measurement. In view of the absence of any compelling reason to believe that the second order expansion of $K(P)$ is adequate to such high pressures, and that the higher derivatives of $K(P)$ are negligible, there is no compelling reason to suppose that the measured value of $K''$ is in serious error.

### 11. ANHARMONIC EFFECTS

The specification of the $PVT$ equation of state of NaCl over the range of our measurements permits us to examine the functional dependence of the Grüneisen function $\gamma(V,T)$ $\equiv \alpha K_{s}/\rho C_{p}=\alpha K_{T}/\rho C_{v}$. This is of considerable interest since it permits the separation

of the volume and temperature dependence of $\gamma$, and thus is a direct test of the assumption that $\gamma$ is only a function of volume at high temperature.

Figure 19 is a contour plot of $\gamma(V, T)$ over the range of our ultrasonic measurements. The contours are nearly vertical, especially at high temperature, and it is evident that $\gamma$ is very nearly independent of temperature. In fact the temperature dependence indicated in Fig. 19 must be near the limit of resolvability, owing to the possible errors in measuring $\alpha$ and $C_{p}$ at high temperature.

The zero pressure isobar shows that the change of $\gamma$ along such a path is almost entirely due to the change of volume from thermal expansion.

Figure 19 shows $\gamma$ decreasing slightly with rising temperature at a constant volume. If this is a real effect, it is not as expected from extrapolation of the behavior of $\gamma(V_{0}, T)$ from temperatures less than $\sim 200^{\circ} K$, where $\gamma$ increases with temperature[27]. Thus for temperatures lower than those indicated, the $\gamma$ contour lines must curve around towards the lower left.

The functional dependence of $\gamma$ on the volume is of considerable interest, especially in the reduction of shock wave data. Figure 20 shows $\gamma(V, T)$ as a function of volume along adiabats starting at $300^{\circ}, 400^{\circ}$ etc. If $\gamma$ were independent of $T$, these lines would coincide. The fact that they nearly do coincide indicates that assuming $\gamma$ is a function of volume only is justified, especially at higher temperatures.

The fact that $\gamma$ is not a linear function of volume is apparent from the curvature of the lines in Fig. 20. If we represent $\gamma / \gamma_{0}=(V / V_{0})^{A}$, we obtain $A \sim 1 \cdot 1$ for the high temperature adiabats and $A \sim 1 \cdot 4$ for the $300^{\circ}$ adiabat. Thus $\gamma / V$ seems to approach a constant at higher temperatures.

Both the trend of $\gamma$ along individual adiabats and the whole family of curves in Fig. 20 indicate that at greater compressions $\gamma$ varies more strongly with volume. This may be of some importance in the reduction of shock wave data where the compressions are up to 10 times greater than we report here.
