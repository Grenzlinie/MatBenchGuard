## ABSTRACT

Motivated by a large number of applications, the dispersion ($C_3$) coefficients for the interaction of a Cs atom with different material media such as Au (metal), Si (semiconductor) and various dielectric surfaces like vitreous SiO₂, SiNₓ, sapphire and YAG are determined using accurate values of the dynamic polarizabilities of the Cs atom obtained employing the relativistic coupled-cluster approach and the dynamic dielectric constants of the walls. Moreover, we also give the retardation function in the graphical representation as functions of separation distances to describe the interaction potentials between the Cs atom with the above considered material media. For the easy access to the interaction potentials at a given distance of separation, we give a simple working functional fitting form for the retardation functions in terms of four fitting parameters that are quoted for the respective medium.

## 1. Introduction

Atom-surface interactions are important for understanding numerous physical, chemical and biological processes. Owing to this, research works of Lennard-Jones [1], Bardeen [2], Casimir and Polder [3] and Lifshitz [4] have drawn a lot of attention over the last few decades [5-9]. In the non-retarded regime (at small distances), the forces between the fluctuating atomic dipole and its immediate image associated with the polarization charges induced in the surface brings about the atom-surface van der Waals interactions [1,10]. Thus, for the short separation distance '$a$' between the atom and surface, the interaction energy scales as $1/a^3$ (at the intermediate distances, the retardation effects are being accounted by damping functions), while for large atom-surface distances as compared to a typical atomic wavelength, the interaction energy scales as $1/a^4$ [3,11-14].

Investigations of the van der Waals dispersion forces between atoms and surfaces are of immense interest to the physicists working in various domains of physical sciences. Assessing these forces accurately can result in new pathways towards engineering, technology and research. Finding out well behaved interactions of atoms or molecules with atomically well defined surfaces are beneficial for advocating future device applications at the nanometer dimensions [15]. These atom-surface interactions can cause shifts in the oscillation frequencies of the trap that can change the trapping conditions. Therefore, when the trapping potentials are determined, particularly at the magic wavelengths, these effects need to be accounted for [16]. A comprehensive cognizance of the dispersion coefficients is necessary for the experimental studies of photoassociation, realization of Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs), interpreting fluorescence spectroscopy, determination of scattering lengths, and analysis of Feshbach resonances [17-23]. Knowing their behaviors is significantly important in generating novel atom optical devices, also known as the atom chips [24].

The atom-wall interaction potentials have significant dependences on the dielectric properties of the materials [25-28]. Majority of the earlier theoretical works involve the interactions of the ground states of atoms with metallic or dielectric surfaces, considering both the non-retarded and retarded van der Waals forces [1-4,11,14]. Since precise measurement of the interaction potentials or in that sense the dispersion coefficients is extremely difficult, very few experimental investigations of the van der Waals interactions between the atoms in their ground states and solid state surfaces have been accomplished [29]. However, very precise measurements of ratios of the dispersion coefficients of some of the alkali atoms with SiNₓ have been reported [30,31]. This gives an opportunity to test the validity of a method to reproduce the experimental results. ## 2. Theory
The formula given by E.M. Lifshitz and collaborators in Moscow in 1955 can efficiently be applied to describe the van der Waals and Casimir-Polder interactions between an atom and a semispace, a material plate, or a layered structure [4,32]. For the intermediate separations, the atom-wall force of attraction is almost negligible, the atom-wall interactions can be computed by considering a polarizable particle interacting with a surface or a wall as a continuous medium having a frequency dependent permittivity $\epsilon(i\omega)$. The working formula for the interaction potential in terms of the dielectric constants is given by [4,23,26,32]

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(z)= & -\frac{\alpha_{f s}^{3}}{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} d \omega \omega^{3} \alpha(i \omega) \\
& \times \int_{1}^{\infty} d \xi e^{-2 \alpha_{f s} \xi \omega z} H(\xi, \epsilon(i \omega)),
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

where $\alpha_{fs}$ is the fine structure constant, $\alpha(i\omega)$ is the dynamic E1 polarizability of the atom, $z$ is the distance of separation between the Cs atom and a surface or a wall and $\epsilon(i\omega)$ is the frequency dependent dielectric constant of the wall. The expression for $H(\xi, \epsilon(i\omega))$, which is a function of the dielectric permittivity of the material wall, is given by

$$
H(\xi, \epsilon(i \omega))=\left(1-2 \xi^{2}\right) \frac{\xi^{\prime}-\epsilon \xi}{\xi^{\prime}+\epsilon \xi}+\frac{\xi^{\prime}-\xi}{\xi^{\prime}+\xi},
\tag{2}
$$

where $\xi' = \sqrt{\xi^2 + \epsilon - 1}$. Evaluation procedure of this functional form is described in Refs. [23,26]. The general expression for the potential described in Eq. (1), for both the retarded and short distances, is conveniently expressed by

$$
V(z)=-\frac{C_{3}}{z^{3}} f_{3}(z),
\tag{3}
$$

where $f_3(z)$ is canonically called the retardation function and the expression for the dispersion coefficient $C_3$ is given by

$$
C_{3}=-\frac{1}{4 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} d \omega \alpha(i \omega) S(i \omega),
\tag{4}
$$

for

$$
S(i \omega)=\frac{\epsilon(i \omega)-1}{\epsilon(i \omega)+1}.
\tag{5}
$$

At the short distance, this interaction potential can be approximated to a simple form as [4,34]

$$
V(z)=-\frac{C_{3}}{z^{3}}.
\tag{6}
$$

For a perfect conductor, $\epsilon(\omega) \to \infty$. Thus, it yields

$$
C_{3}=-\frac{1}{4 \pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} d \omega \alpha(i \omega)
\tag{7}
$$

and

$$
f_{3}(z)=\frac{1}{4 \pi C_{3}} \int_{0}^{\infty} d \omega \alpha(i \omega) e^{-2 \alpha_{f s} \omega z} P^{(\infty)}\left(\alpha_{f s} \omega z\right)
\tag{8}
$$

with $P^{(\infty)}(x)=1+2 x+2 x^{2}$.

Adopting a similar approach described in Ref. [23], we intend to evaluate the $C_3$ coefficients and $f_3(z)$. We calculate the $C_3$ coefficients using Eq. (4) and interaction potentials using Eq. (1). By combining these two quantities, we determine the retarded function $f_3(z)$ using Eq. (3). To calculate the $C_3$ and retarded functions for a perfect conductor, we use Eqs. (7) and (8) directly. As can be noticed, accurate evaluation of these quantities entirely depend on the accuracies in the values of the dynamic polarizabilities of the atom and dynamic dielectric constants of the material or a wall. In the present work, we attempt to determine these dynamic properties rigorously of the Cs atom and the materials under consideration. In the next section, we describe approaches adopted to evaluate the dynamic polarizabilities of the Cs atom and dynamic dielectric constants of Au, Si, and SiO₂, SiNₓ, sapphire and YAG having conducting, semiconducting and dielectric surfaces, respectively.

## 3. Method of calculations
The dynamic E1 polarizability expression for the ground state of the Cs atom with an imaginary frequency $i\omega$ can be expressed as

$$
\alpha(i \omega)=-\sum_{l \neq v} \frac{\left(E_{v}-E_{l}\right)\left|\left\langle\Psi_{v}|D| \Psi_{l}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left(E_{v}-E_{l}\right)^{2}+\omega^{2}},
\tag{9}
$$

where $v$ represents the 6s valence orbital, $l$ represents all possible allowed intermediate states and $D$ is the dipole operator. For convenience, sometime single oscillator model (SOM) approximation is used to estimate polarizability over a wide range of $\omega$s. In the SOM method, $\alpha(i\omega)$ is given by

$$
\alpha(i \omega)=\frac{\alpha(0)}{1+\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}},
\tag{10}
$$

where $\alpha(0)$ is the static dipole polarizability and $\omega_0$ is the characteristic frequency used in the Lorentz oscillator model at the infrared absorption of the atom. However, due its semi-empirical nature, this approximation cannot account for the dynamic behavior of the core correlation effects which are discussed below. For the *ab initio* evaluation of polarizabilities, one can express them as

$$
\alpha(i \omega)=\left\langle\Psi_{v}|D| \Psi_{v}^{-}\right\rangle+\left\langle\Psi_{v}^{+}|D| \Psi_{v}\right\rangle
\tag{11}
$$

with $|\Psi_{v}^{ \pm}\rangle=\sum_{l \neq v}|\Psi_{l}\rangle \frac{\langle\Psi_{l}|D| \Psi_{v}\rangle}{(E_{l}-E_{v}) \pm i \omega}$ which can be treated analogous to the first order wave function with respect to the ground state

wave function $|\Psi_{n}\rangle$ due to the operator $D$. It is not straightforward to obtain these wave functions using sophisticated many-body methods like the RCC theory owing to the presence of the imaginary factor in the denominator. To estimate the above quantities, we adopt an alternative procedure that is computationally cost effective and can determine these quantities more precisely than $ab$ initio calculations. For this purpose, we divide the ground state of the Cs atom into two parts: the closed core $[5p^{6}]$ and the valence orbital 6s. First we calculate the Dirac-Fock (DF) wave function $(|\Phi_{0}\rangle)$ for the closed core and then define the DF wave function of the entire state as $|\Phi_{v}\rangle=a_{v}^{\dagger}|\Phi_{0}\rangle$ for the valence orbital $v=6s$. The exact atomic wave function of the ground state of Cs can now be evaluated by considering the correlations among the electrons within $|\Phi_{0}\rangle$ which is referred to as core correlation, correlations seen by the valence and core electrons of $|\Phi_{v}\rangle$ separately due to their interactions termed as valence correlation and the correlations between the core electrons with the valence electron $v$ named as the core-valence contributions. Using the wave operator formalism, we can write these wave functions accounting the above correlations independently as [39]

$$
\left|\Psi_{v}\right\rangle=a_{v}^{\dagger} \Omega_{c}\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle+\Omega_{c v}\left|\Phi_{v}\right\rangle+\Omega_{v}\left|\Phi_{v}\right\rangle,\qquad(12)
$$

where $\Omega_{c}$, $\Omega_{cv}$ and $\Omega_{v}$ are known as the wave operators for the core (c), core-valence (cv) and valence (v) correlations, respectively. In this framework, the total E1 polarizability is expressed as

$$
\alpha=\alpha^{c}+\alpha^{v}+\alpha^{c v},\qquad(13)
$$

for the notations $\alpha^{c}$, $\alpha^{v}$ and $\alpha^{cv}$ corresponding to the above mentioned correlation contributions, respectively.

It is possible to evaluate dominant contributions to $\alpha^{v}$ by calculating E1 matrix elements between many low-lying singly excited states $|\Psi_{I}\rangle$ by expressing as

$$
\alpha^{v}(i \omega)=\frac{2}{3\left(2 J_{v}+1\right)} \sum_{I \neq v} \frac{\left(E_{v}-E_{I}\right)\left|\left\langle\Psi_{v}\|\mathbf{D}\| \Psi_{I}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left(E_{v}-E_{I}\right)^{2}+\omega^{2}},\qquad(14)
$$

where $\langle\Psi_{v}\|D\| \Psi_{I}\rangle$ is the reduced E1 matrix element calculated using the Wigner Eckart theorem and $J_{v}=1/2$ is the angular momentum of the ground state. In order to determine the E1 matrix elements between the ground state wave function $|\Psi_{v}\rangle$ and the excited state wave function $|\Psi_{I}\rangle$, we express them in a general form as $|\Psi_{n}\rangle$ with a common core and replacing valence orbital $n$ by $v$ or $I$ appropriately for different excited states. In the Fock-space RCC theory formalism, it yields

$$
\left|\Psi_{n}\right\rangle=e^{T}\left\{1+S_{n}\right\}\left|\Phi_{n}\right\rangle.\qquad(15)
$$

Here the operator $T$ and $S_{n}$ excite core electrons and the respective valence electron $n$ along with the core electrons due to the electron correlations. We consider all possible single and double excitations with the important valence triple excitations in our calculations (referred to as CCSD(T) method in the literature) within a sufficiently large configuration space. From the practical limitation, we calculate as many as $|\Psi_{I}\rangle$ states possible for the estimation of their contributions to $\alpha^{v}$ and refer as main contribution $(\alpha_{v})$. To improve the accuracies in our calculations, we use the experimental energies. Further, we use very accurately known E1 matrix elements of the transitions involving the ground to the first few low-lying excited states from the precisely measured lifetimes of the $6p\ ^{2}P_{1/2}$ and $6p\ ^{2}P_{3/2}$ states [35]. Contributions from the highly excited states and continuum, defined here as tail contribution $(\alpha_{t})$, are usually very small. So we have estimated them using the following equation using the DF wave functions as

$$
\alpha_{t}(i \omega)=\frac{2}{3\left(2 J_{v}+1\right)} \sum_{K>I} \frac{\left(\epsilon_{v}-\epsilon_{K}\right)\left|\left\langle\Phi_{v}\|\mathbf{D}\| \Phi_{K}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left(\epsilon_{v}-\epsilon_{K}\right)^{2}+\omega^{2}},\qquad(16)
$$

where $|\Phi\rangle$ are the DF wave functions, $K>I$ corresponds to the excited states including continuum whose matrix elements are not accounted in the $\alpha_{v}$ contribution, and $\epsilon s$ are the DF energies.

The $\alpha^{c}$ contribution could be significant, but cannot be estimated using the sum-over-states approach. Hence, we employ the relativistic random phase approximation (RRPA) to estimate its contribution using the expression

$$
\alpha^{c}(i \omega)=\frac{2}{3}\left\langle\Phi_{0}|D| \Psi_{0}^{(1)}\right\rangle,\qquad(17)
$$

by defining [40]

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\Psi_{0}^{(1)}\right\rangle= & \sum_{\beta}^{\infty} \sum_{p, a} \Omega_{a \rightarrow p}^{(\beta, 1)}\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle \\
= & \sum_{\beta=1}^{\infty} \sum_{p q, a b}\left\{\frac{\left[\langle p b\left|\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right| a q\right\rangle-\langle p b\left|\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right| q a\rangle\right] \Omega_{b \rightarrow q}^{(\beta-1,1)}}{\left(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{a}\right)^{2}+\omega^{2}}\right. \\
& \left.+\frac{\Omega_{b \rightarrow q}^{(\beta-1,1)}\left[\langle p q\left|\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right| a b\right\rangle-\langle p q\left|\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right| b a\rangle\right]}{\left(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{a}\right)^{2}+\omega^{2}}\right\} \\
& \times\left(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{a}\right)\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the subscript 0 means it does not contain the valence orbital $v$, $\Omega_{a \to p}^{(\beta,1)}$ is the wave operator that excites an occupied orbital $a$ of $|\Phi_{0}\rangle$ to a virtual orbital $p$ which alternatively refers to a singly excited state with respect to $|\Phi_{0}\rangle$ with $\Omega_{a \to p}^{(0,1)}=\frac{\langle p|(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{a})D|a\rangle}{(\epsilon_{p}-\epsilon_{a})^{2}+\omega^{2}}$ and the superscripts $\beta$ and 1 representing the number of the Coulomb $(\frac{1}{r_{12}})$ and $D$ operators, respectively.

Similarly, it is also extremely strenuous to determine the dynamic electric permittivity of any real materials. One of the most convenient ways of determining these constants for simple metals, such as gold, is to use the Drude-Lorentz model as was done in Ref. [26]. In pursuance of obtaining more realistic values of these constants for different materials, we prefer to use the known real and imaginary parts of the refractive indices of a material at few real values of frequency $\omega$. The imaginary parts of the dielectric permittivities of the materials can then be obtained using the relation

$$
\operatorname{Im}[\epsilon(\omega)]=2 n(\omega) \kappa(\omega),\qquad(19)
$$

where $n(\omega)$ and $\kappa(\omega)$ are the respective real and imaginary parts of the refractive index of a material at frequency $\omega$. We use the optical data from the handbook by Palik [41] for the frequencies ranging from 0.1 eV to 10000 eV for Au metal to calculate $\operatorname{Im}[\epsilon(\omega)]$. Thereafter, the required real values of the dielectric constants at the imaginary frequencies $(\epsilon(i \omega))$ are obtained by using the Kramers-Kronig formula. The available data, however, does not cover the whole frequency range to carry out the integration of Eq. (4). Thus, we extrapolate these values for the lower frequencies to increase the domain over which the integrations are to be performed [42,43]. For the frequencies below 0.1 eV, the classified values from [41] are extrapolated using the free electron Drude model, in which, the dielectric permittivity along the imaginary axis is represented by

$$
\epsilon(i \omega)=1-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i \gamma)},\qquad(20)
$$

where $\omega_{p}=(2 \pi c / \lambda_{p})$ is the plasma frequency and $\gamma$ is the relaxation frequency. The optical data values for $\omega_{p}$ and $\gamma$ available from various sources differ slightly, but we use these values as $\omega_{p}=9.0$ eV and $\gamma=0.035$ eV as outlined in [26,42-44]. ### 4. Results and discussion

The evaluation of $C_3$ coefficients requires the precise estimation of dynamic polarizabilities of the Cs atom. Table 1 presents the scalar polarizabilities of the Cs atom in its ground state along with the E1 matrix elements for different transitions that are used to estimate $\alpha_{v}$ and other contributions to the polarizabilities. The E1 matrix elements for the $6 S_{1 / 2}-6 P_{1 / 2,3 / 2}$ transitions are taken from the experimentally measured data given in [35]. Our calculated value of the static scalar polarizability $(\alpha(0))$ for the $6 S$ state is 398.8(8) a.u.. This is in very good agreement with the value (399.0 a.u.) obtained by Borschevsky et al. [36] and experimentally measured value 401.0(6) a.u. of Amini and Gould [38]. As seen, contributions from $\alpha_{c v}$ and $\alpha_{t}$ are quite small and justify the use of a lower order method for their evaluation. Our $\alpha_{c}$ from RRPA also matches with the value of Derevianko et al. [48]. Good agreement between the calculated and experimental results for $\alpha(0)$ indicates that our approach can also give similar accuracies for the estimated dynamic polarizabilities.

In order to generate our results for the $f_3(z)$ functions at a given distance of separation for future theoretical and experimental verifications or for various applications, it is advantageous to provide a certain logistic functional form for the retardation function as a function of separation distance. Analogous to the fitting procedure adopted in [26], we represent the retardation function in the functional form as

$$
f_{3}(z)=\frac{1+A_{1} \alpha_{f S} z+A_{2}\left(\alpha_{f S} z\right)^{2}}{1+A_{1} \alpha_{f S} z+B_{2}\left(\alpha_{f S} z\right)^{2}+B_{3}\left(\alpha_{f S} z\right)^{3}}. \tag{22}
$$

## 5. Conclusion

In the present work, we have analyzed the atom-surface interactions between the cesium atom in its ground state with a perfect conductor, Au (metal), Si (semiconductor) and various dielectric surfaces (vitreous $SiO_2$, $SiN_x$, sapphire and YAG). We have calculated the dispersion $C_3$ coefficients for the above interactions and then studied the retardation effects of the above interactions by plotting the damping function $f_3$ values as function of separation distances. More accurate values of dynamic polarizability of the Cs atom are used in determining these $f_3(z)$ values. Furthermore, readily usable functional form of the retardation function for the above atom- -surface interactions is used and the fitting parameters are given in order to extract out the potential at a given distance for the practical applications.
