X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic core level shifts of phosphorus in phosphates and native oxide layers on InP(100). Applications of the Auger parameter concept

T. Chassé*, R. Franke, C. Urban, R. Franzheld, P. Streubel, A. Meisel

Fachbereich Chemie, Universität Leipzig, Linnéstr. 2, O-7010 Leipzig, Germany

(Received 3 February 1992; in final form 5 October 1992)

## Abstract
Solid phosphorus compounds have been investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray Auger electron spectroscopy. The core level shifts have been separated into initial and final state shifts by employing the Auger parameter concept. Although strong chemical trends in general dictated the shifts, the significant differences between compounds with fourfold oxygen coordination could essentially be attributed to environmental effects. We have been able to demonstrate that the extra-atomic or environmental contributions to the shifts of relaxation and initial state terms may be semiquantitatively estimated by dielectric screening and the Madelung potential models, respectively. The investigations were extended to native oxide layers on InP(100). The chemical state plots have been found to provide information on the nature of the layers beyond a conventional XPS analysis. In a similar way to that for bulk samples, the initial state-related shift contributions indicate the condensation of phosphates in the layers, and the Auger parameter shifts are sensitive to the composition of the layers and to substrate screening.

## 1. Introduction
The phosphates form a large group among the phosphorus compounds. The well known key feature of these compounds is the fourfold coordina- tion of phosphorus by oxygen atoms. The structure of orthophosphates is composed of single $PO_{4}$ units connected by the cations. In condensed phosphates some of these phosphate groups are linked together by sharing oxygen atoms to form larger structural units. Moreover, there are numerous substituted phosphates in which particular oxygen atoms are replaced by other atoms. The variety of phosphate compounds implies a wide range of physical and chemical properties [1]. Thus, an understanding of the structure, bonding and electronic structure of phosphates is both of

basic interest and of great importance for many applications. For example, native oxide layers on InP are candidates for insulating layers in MIS (metal insulator semiconductor) structures based on this semiconductor [2] because good dielectric properties have been reported, especially for anodi- cally prepared layers [3,4]. These oxide layers were shown to be composed mainly of phosphates of varying composition [5-9].

Results of numerous investigations on phosphates by X-ray photoelec- tron spectroscopy (XPS) have been reported in the past [10-13]. Even for specific studies of oxide layers on InP, photoelectron spectroscopy has been used extensively. Most of these studies have used the core level shifts for characterization of the phosphorus compounds. Although these are of course powerful tools for the identification of chemical species, it is well known that the interpretation of these spectra may suffer from overlap of the shifts related to the initial and final states and from band bending- induced shifts in semiconductors; additionally, sample charging may occur in insulating samples. Hollinger [3,6] suggested the use of the distinct differences in the valence band (VB) spectra of the different phosphorus oxides, and especially the phosphates, for the identification of the chemical species in native oxide layers, and that procedure has indeed been applied successfully by several groups [4,14]. On the other hand, VB spectra may be distorted to some extent by the overlap with substrate spectra in the case of thin layers or by valence emission from contaminating layers, and they are less informative in the case of non-monochromatic XPS excitation because of the In4d satellites.

It is the aim of this paper to investigate the influence of the solid state environment on phosphorus core level shifts in phosphates and phosphorus oxides. This knowledge will be applied to XPS/XAES (X-ray Auger electronspectroscopy) core level studies of phosphorus in oxide layers on InP(100) which have been prepared by different methods.

First we will outline the preparative and experimental procedures. Phos- phorus core level shift data will then be discussed in terms of the initial and final state contributions. Special attention will be paid to the influence of dielectric core hole screening. The solid state potential will be calculated approximately for some P oxides and orthophosphates. The discussion will be extended also to condensed phosphates. Then the core level shift data for some specially prepared oxide systems on InP(100) will be presented and used for the characterization of thermal oxides, anodic oxides, chemical oxides, and air-oxidized samples. In a later section we will present chemical state plots derived from core level and Auger energies of the phosphorus in the layers. The data will be discussed by reference to the general results obtained for the phosphates and interpreted in terms of condensation of the phosphates and inhomogeneity in the oxide layers on InP(110) which modifies the core hole screening in the final XPS state.

## 2. Experimental

Indium phosphate reference samples were prepared using standard techniques [15]. The amorphous $InPO_{4x}\cdot2H_2O$(a) obtained was recrystallized to $InPO_4$ by extensive annealing at $800^\circ$C. The crystallinity of the powdered reference samples $InPO_4$(c) and $In(PO_3)_3$(c) and the sodium phosphates was confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis [16-18].

Tin-doped $(\approx1 \times 10^{18}\text{cm}^{-3})$ n-type InP wafers of (100) orientation were employed in this work. The wafers were chemically treated according to a procedure proposed by Kurth et al. [19], which includes several rinsing and etching steps; the most important step is based on bromine (1%) in methanol. This procedure has been shown to remove damage from abrasive polishing and to produce nearly oxide-free surfaces.

Thermal oxidation was performed in a quartz tube in air for 1.5 h at $450^\circ$C. AGW electrolyte (3% tartaric acid-glycol = 1:1) was used to prepare thick anodic oxide layers $(\approx$240 nm) by operating the cell at $500\mu\text{A cm}^{-2}$ and a final voltage of 170 V in the galvanostatic region. Oxidation in dry air was performed for 100 days at room temperature. The chemical oxidation of the InP wafers was effected in a $40\%$ $HNO_3$ solution under UV illumination (1000 W) for 1-10 minutes. Thin oxide layers were further produced by brief etching with either $HNO_3$ or $H_2O_2$.

The XPS data for the solid phosphorus compounds were taken from ref. 20. Al $K\alpha$ and Ag $L\alpha$ radiation were used to excite P1s, P2p, P2s and $\text{PKL}_{23}\text{L}_{23}(^1D_2)$ photoelectron and Auger spectra in that work. Here we used Al $K\alpha$ and bremsstrahlung excitation only to obtain P2p and $\text{PKL}_{23}\text{L}_{23}(^1D_2)$ spectra in a VG ESCA 3 photoelectron spectrometer. We omitted recording of the P1s core levels because of intensity problems and to circumvent difficulties by separating bulk and layer contributions in the comparatively broad 1s spectra excited by non-monochromatized Ag $L\alpha$ radiation.

Spectra of InP and its native oxide layers were recorded at $(1-5) \times 10^{-7}$ Pa under irradiation of X-rays excited by 12 kV and 20 mA. The energy scales were aligned as described in [20] and by adopting the binding energy (BE) value of carbon contamination as 285.0 eV. In the case of the oxide layers, charging of about 5 eV was observed only for the anodically and chemically prepared native oxides on InP(100).

The shift of the P $\text{KLL}(^1S_0)$ Auger line to lower energy and its relative intensity compared with the $^1D_2$ main line have been determined as being 7.7 eV and 0.09, respectively, in good agreement with the values of 7.7 eV and 0.11 given by Schärli and Brunner [21]. These data were kept fixed in a nonlinear least squares procedure, which has been used to separate energy and intensity parameters of bulk phosphide and oxidized phosphorus.

## 3. Core level shifts in the phosphates

### 3.1. Relaxation energy and dielectric screening

It is well known that photoelectron binding energy (BE) shifts are affected by differences $\Delta V$ of the initial state (IS) contributions due to the potentials generated by the ground state charge distribution, often called the potential contribution, and a relaxation contribution $\Delta R_{\mathrm{D}}$ due to the screening of the photohole in the final state (FS) of photoemission [22-24]

$$\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(2 p)=\Delta V(2 p)-\Delta R_{\mathrm{D}}(2 p)\tag{1}$$

Wagner first proposed that the shifts of a prominent core level and of Auger lines be combined to derive an Auger parameter $\alpha^{\prime}$, which offers a way to estimate relaxation energy shifts [25].

$$\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}} \approx \mathrm{E} \Delta \alpha^{\prime}=\Delta\left[E\left(\mathrm{KL}_{23} \mathrm{~L}_{23} ;{ }^{1} D_{2}\right)+E_{\mathrm{B}}(2 p)\right]\tag{2}$$

More accurately, shifts of relaxation energies are determined by using the Auger parameters $\xi$ or $\beta$ [23,24,26,27], and thus

$$\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}}(2 p 2 p)=\Delta E\left(\mathrm{KL}_{23} \mathrm{~L}_{23} ;{ }^{1} D_{2}\right)+2 \Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(2 p)-\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(1 s)\tag{3}$$

The static relaxation energy shift $\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}}$ is related approximately to the dynamic or one hole relaxation energy by [22,23]

$$\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}}(2 p 2 p) \approx 2 \Delta R_{\mathrm{D}}(2 p)\tag{4}$$

Relaxation energies in phosphorus compounds have recently been inves- tigated in detail for both volatile [28,29] and solid compounds [20,30-32]. Dielectric models have been found adequate to explain the trends of the relaxation energies in solid phosphorus compounds, and chemical trends have been related to dielectric and bonding properties [32].

In Fig. 1 we present the static relaxation energies $\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}}$ and experimental differences of BE shifts $[\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(1 s)-\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(2 p)]=\Delta \Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}$ of solid phosphorus compounds. Both experimental quantities are referred to the red phos- phorus modification $P_{r}$ . Note further that both $\Delta R_{S}$ and $\Delta \Delta E_{B}$ are indepen dent of the energy reference level because they have been derived from energy differences.

The core level shifts $\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}$ are related to the $\mathrm{K} \alpha$ shifts in X-ray emission spectra according to

$$\Delta E(\mathrm{~K} \alpha)=\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(1 s)-\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}(2 p)\tag{5}$$

### 3.2. Madelung potential effects in orthophosphates

In solids we have to recognize a substantial problem beyond the possible accumulation of experimental errors. Whereas the vacuum level is an unique reference level for BE in atoms or molecules, the Fermi energy is the common energy reference in solids. There are thus systematic uncertainties present in binding energies and IS shifts of solids due to the inaccessibility of electrochemical potentials or work functions $\varphi$ of samples to correct for these reference level shifts in the experimental BE, $\Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $\Delta V$. The subject has been extensively reviewed by Egelhoff [39]. Indeed, the $\Delta V$ values in this work have also been determined by neglecting the frequently small $\Delta \varphi$ terms. We have to be aware of that fact during the discussion, and we will focus our attention primarily on significant trends.

In Fig. 3 the initial state shifts $\Delta V$, calculated from experimental shift data [20] by eqns. (1), (3) and (4), are compared with the 1s-2p BE shift

A clear tendency to increasing $\Delta V$ is shown in Fig. 3 with growing $\Delta \Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}$ and thus effective charge on the phosphorus after replacing the nearest neighbour atoms by more electronegative ones in the sequence P-S-N-O-F. This chemical sensitivity is of course a well known feature of core level shifts in XPS. It is quite evident from looking at a simple ground state charge and potential model for the IS shift with respect to the elemental solid. $\Delta V$ may be written as the sum of an atomic shift contribution due to the change of the effective valence charge $q(\mathrm{P})$ and an extra-atomic contribution $\Delta V_{\mathrm{M}}$ due to the difference between the potentials in the solids, the latter often being described approximately by lattice sums of Madelung type

$$\Delta V=\Delta[k q(\mathrm{P})]+\Delta V_{\mathrm{M}} \tag{7}$$

The quantities here have the usual meanings [31,39,40]. Chemical trends due to nearest neighbour interactions for the phosphorus compounds of interest have been discussed in more detail elsewhere [31].

In striking contrast to such a simple chemical trend, the IS shifts deter- mined for the O-coordinated P compounds cover a wide range of $\approx 3.2 \mathrm{eV}$, although the experimental effective charges of P are very close to each other, as may be judged from the $\Delta \Delta E_{\mathrm{B}}$ in Fig. 3. The range in $\Delta V$ is obviously larger than that observed for the relaxation energies in Fig. 1. Evidently the IS shifts among these phosphorus compounds are strongly affected by differences in the potentials introduced by the charge distribu- tion in the higher coordination spheres of the phosphorus.

In the special case of $Na_{3}PO_{4}$ the total potential or initial state contribution to $E_{B}(P 2 p)$ is close to that obtained for red phosphorus $P_{r}$, although $\Delta \Delta E_{B}=0.75 eV$ indicates an appreciable effective charge of +1.28 on the phosphorus in that phosphate. The BE shift $\Delta E_{B}(2 p)=2.1 eV$ with respect to the elemental solid $P_{r}$ is thus due essentially to the reduced core hole screening for this phosphate (see also Table 2). The result is somewhat surprising at first view. However, it was indicated by the measured Auger and BE shifts [20]. The ratio $|\Delta E(KL_{23} ~L_{23} ;^{1} D_{2}) / \Delta E_{B}(2 p)| \approx 2.7$ is close to the value 3.0 which has to be expected for pure relaxation-induced shifts in thesimplest models. On the other hand, the rather covalent double oxide $BPO_{4}$  falls close to the main chemical trend of the BEs in Fig. 3. All the phos- phates range between these two extrema. In this manner, a further chemical trend is suggested by the data that is related to the electro- negativities of the second neighbours rather than the next neighbours.

To shed more light on this subject, we have investigated the effect of the solid state potential by employing an approximate calculation. In the framework of an ionic approach, the potential acting on an ion due to the arrangement of all the other ions in the solid can be calculated from lattice sums of the Madelung type. The analysis of core level shifts using Madelung potentials has been shown to give good agreement to experimen- tal core level shifts for, e.g., alkali halides [41] or oxides [42]. However, the method has been demonstrated to fail in the case of ions of high formal charge, and it may be somewhat questionable for compounds with signifi- cant covalent character [42]. Both problems have to be taken into account in the present compounds of interest, because the $P$ ions would appear with an unrealistic formal charge of +5 in the lattice sums, and the $P-O$ bonding is known to be polar covalent and far from being ionic.

In the following analysis, we will neglect the minor differences of 0.1 eV in $\Delta \Delta E_{B}$ from the average value of $0.75 eV$ , which corresponds to $q=1.28$ . It is hard to judge whether these differences are significant, because the expected error limit in our data is just 0.1 eV. A narrow range of effective charges for phosphates was also suggested from the literature data [34].

To obtain an estimate of the solid state potential contribution to the BE of the $P 2 p$ core level we proceed as follows. The ternary phosphoruscompounds are approximated by binary compounds like $A_{l} B_{m}$ with $B=$  $PO_{4}$ . The electrostatic potential felt by the $P$ atoms may then be separated into a term due to the electronic charge at the oxygen neighbours within the $PO_{4}$ group and a term due to the charge distribution outside. The nearest neighbour contribution to the potential at the phosphorus positions may be estimated from the charge transfer of $1.28 e$ due to the ionicity of the $P-O$ bonds and a term due to the anionic charge. We have performed MO-LCAO (molecular orbital-linear combination of atomic orbitals) calculations of Fenske-Hall type [43] on isolated $PO_{4}$ and hyd

rogen-saturated clusters to simulate the effects of the solid state environment on the charge distribution in $PO_{4}$ clusters. The hydrogen termination introduced a huge charge redistribution on the oxygen, whereas the charge on the phosphorus remained nearly unaffected by this gross change of coordination. Thus we have assumed the anionic charge to be located in the oxygen coordination sphere at an average P-O distance of $r_{PO} \approx 0.154 \mathrm{nm}$. In the extreme case of an ideal $\mathrm{PO}_{4}^{3-}$, two terms of $-12.0 \mathrm{eV}(q(\mathrm{O})=1.28 e)$ and $-28.0 \mathrm{eV}\left(q\left(\mathrm{PO}_{4}\right)=3 e\right)$ add up to a total potential of $-40.0 \mathrm{eV}$ due to the negative charge in the first coordination of the $\mathrm{P}$.

The second potential contribution $V_{\mathrm{M}}\left(\mathrm{PO}_{4}\right)$ is due to the potential acting on $\mathrm{PO}_{4}$, which is determined by the arrangement and distances of the $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{PO}_{4}$ ions in the crystal. In this manner

$$
V_{\mathrm{M}}(P)=\frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0}} \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{PO}}}\left[q(\mathrm{O})+\mathrm{q}\left(\mathrm{PO}_{4}\right)\right]+V_{\mathrm{M}}\left(\mathrm{PO}_{4}\right)
$$

Based on earlier work by Templeton on Madelung constants [44], Broughton and Bagus [42] demonstrated that the result of lattice sum calculations for individual potential contributions of the ions in binary $\mathrm{A}_{l} \mathrm{~B}_{m}$ crystals may be well reproduced by the semiempirical potential

$$
V_{\mathrm{M}}(B)=\frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0}} l\left(1.89-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{A}}}\right) \frac{z}{r_{\mathrm{AB}}}
$$

with $N_{\mathrm{A}}=$ coordination number of the ion $\mathrm{A}, z=$ largest common factor of the individual ion charges $z_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $z_{\mathrm{B}}$, and $r_{\mathrm{AB}}=$ distance between ions $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$. pretation. Looking at the phosphorus core level shifts again, we correct our potentials by introducing coordination charges instead of ideal ionic charges $Z$. The concept of coordination charge has been applied successfully in the interpretation of near edge structures of X-ray absorption spectra (XANES) [46]. The coordination charge $\eta$ is defined by

$$
\eta=Z-C N \tag{11}
$$

with coordination number $N$, ionic charge $Z$, and covalency $C=1-i$. The multiple bond ionicity $i$ is defined according to

$$
i=1-\frac{Z}{N} \exp \left[-\frac{1}{4}\left(\chi_{\mathrm{A}}-\chi_{\mathrm{B}}\right)^{2}\right]=1-\frac{Z}{N}(1-I) \tag{12}
$$

where $I$ is the single bond ionicity and $\chi_{\mathrm{A}}, \chi_{\mathrm{B}}$ are the electronegativities according to the Pauling scale [47]. Thus, $\eta$ may be simply written as

$$
\eta=Z I \tag{13}
$$

Ionicities derived for $\text{A-PO}_4$ bonds are given in Table 1. The electronegativity of $\text{PO}_4$ has been approximated by a geometric mean of the elemental values. The corrected potential contributions obtained from eq. (8) with $q(\text{PO}_4)=3eI$ are collected in the column $V_{\text{Mc}}$ of the table. These values are smaller in magnitude by 2–4 eV than the original $V_{\text{M}}(\text{P})$. This rather small reduction compared to the significant differences of the single bond ionicities is attributable to the fact that the order of magnitude is mainly determined by the $\text{P-O}$ charge transfer term of $-12.0$ eV. The correction is

applied only to the other two terms, which cancel to a large extent. In this manner an explanation for the success of Madelung approximations, even for the less ionic compounds, may be given. We recognize an improved overall correlation of experimental $\Delta V$ values after introduction of the ionicity correction in the theoretical estimation of the Madelung contri- butions, although the slope of 1.3 of the solid line matching the data in Fig.4 is slightly larger than unity.

We may estimate the Madelung potential contribution independently by using experimental data and theoretical binding energies. Similarly to Nefedov et al. [29], we have calculated the solid state potential contributionto the core level BEs from the difference of the experimental $E_{B}(P 2 p)$ [20] and relativistic Hartree-Fock calculations of the BEs for free ions ofcharge q [29] after correcting for the extra-atomic relaxation energies $R_{D}$ [20]. The results obtained for q=1.28 are compiled in column $V_{Msp}$ of Table 1. There is good agreement of the spectroscopic estimate $V_{Msp}$ and the corrected structural approach $V_{Mc}$ with a scatter of only ≈1.0-1.5 eV. However, the strikingly good accordance of these two estimates might be due in part to some fortuitous cancellation effects. Nefedov, for example, has reported a potential contribution of -15.9 eV for $Na_{3} PO_{4}$ in contrast to our value of 16.75 eV. The difference is due both to another effective charge of phosphorus and to the use of another set of calculations for the derivation of absolute extra-atomic relaxation energies [29,20]. Moreover, other initial state effects like reference level shifts or charge compression are incorporated completely differently in the spectroscopic and the struc- tural approaches. The comparison of relative shifts is thus believed to be much more reliable than that of absolute values. The satisfactory agree- ment of these absolute values nevertheless supports the validity of the approximations used in the calculations of the structural estimate of the environmental potential contribution.

### 3.3. Condensed phosphates
The $PO_{4}$ units in the condensed phosphates are linked to chains, rings or networks [1]. This condensation of the phosphates is a second coordination influence, too. In condensed phosphates, terminal (P-O) and bridging (P-O- P) oxygen atoms are incorporated in building up the structure, resulting in a distortion of the tetrahedra owing to the difference in the P-O bond lengths (≈0.01 nm) between terminal and bridging O. Two phosphate tetra- hedra are linked together by one bridging oxygen per formula unit in the diphosphate $Na_{4} P_{2} O_{7}$ . Phosphate chains have formed in $NaPO_{3}$ by means of P-O-P bonds. The structure of the pentoxide $P_{4} O_{10}$ , too, may be described as a three-dimensional network connected by P-O-P bridge bonds.

Core level shift data of these sodium phosphates have been summarized

in Table 2 together with literature data. The shifts have been given with respect to $P_{4} O_{10}$. The data for red phosphorus have also been included for comparison. There are clear shifts to lower BEs with decreasing degree of condensation. The relaxation energies and the atomic initial state contri- butions do not vary significantly between these oxygen coordinated phos- phorus compounds, as is reflected by values of $\Delta R_{S}$ and $\Delta \Delta E_{B}$ in Table 2. The shifts in the BE can thus be definitely assigned to an initial state effect attributable to the change of the environmental potential by our data. There is a striking correlation of $Na / P$ atomic ratios and the steps of $\approx 1 eV$ in $BE$ and $\Delta V$ from compound to compound, suggesting that the amount of charge transfer from $Na$ to $O$ and the related change of the solid state potential acting on $P$ might be the origin of the shifts. The latter process becomes less effective owing to the reduction of the number of sodium valence electrons available for charge transfer. A decreasing charge transfer from $Na$ to $O$ should result in a reduced valence level occupationon the oxygen. This is indeed suggested by the evident increase of $E_{B}(O 1 s)$  in Table 2 with decreasing $Na / P$ or $Na / O$ atomic ratios. The two O1s energies reported for the condensed phosphates are due to bridging and terminal oxygen atoms. As with the orthophosphates, the change of the environmental potential will be of some importance for the oxygen core level shifts also. However, further qualitative support for the above charge transfer explanation of the IS shifts comes from looking at the data for $Na_{2} PO_{3} ~F$ in Table 2, which are similar to those for $Na_{4} P_{2} O_{7}$ with respect to $Na / P, \Delta \Delta E_{B}$ and $\Delta V$ . Note also that the $P-F$ distance of $0.159 ~nm$ [1] is very near the typical $P-O$ bond length in phosphates.

In strong contrast to the BEs of the P2p and O1s levels, the $E_{B}(Na 1 s)$  values are known to vary by not more than $\approx 0.5 eV$ [12]. Thus, we can rule out reference level shifts as significant influential factors on the core level shifts in these compounds.

LeBeuze et al. [14] calculated the valence electronic structures of $Na$ and In phosphates in the framework of a tight-binding approximation. That work had been stimulated by previous XPS investigations of the valence band spectra of indium phosphates by Hollinger and co-workers [3,6]. LeBeuze et al. showed that the metal-oxygen bonds are less ionic in the condensed phosphates than in the orthophosphates. This was demonstrated by an increase of orbital overlap populations calculated for the In-O bonds in $In(PO_{3})_{3}$ compared with those in $InPO_{4}$ .

Summarizing this qualitative discussion, we have presented arguments that emphasize the importance of ionicity and the amount of charge transfer to the oxygen in the covalently bonded units of more or less condensed phosphates for the initial state shifts of the $P 2 p$ BE. ## 4. Oxide layers on InP(100)

### 4.1. Characterization by core level spectroscopy

The common procedure for interface characterization involves measure- ments of reference spectra from well defined samples which are close in composition or structure to the interface species. There have been several papers reporting XPS data recorded from native oxide layers on InP, and including standard samples. In Table 3 we have summarized the BEs given in some of the references together with our own values. The energies are in reasonable agreement for phosphorus, oxygen, and indium spectra. This overall consistency gives us further confidence in the data, even regarding the reference level problem.

The native oxide layers on InP differ considerably in composition, depending on the method of preparation. There have indeed been numerous studies concentrating on modification of the oxide layer composition to improve the electrical characteristics. Recently, some success has been reported in controlling the oxidation process by preparation parameters [4,8].

X-ray photoelectron spectra of the P$2p$ core levels recorded from the reference compounds and oxidized InP(100) samples are shown in Fig. 5.

The bottom spectrum corresponds to the crystalline $InPO_4$ reference sample, and the topmost spectrum has been obtained from crystalline $In(PO_3)_3$. The spectra between are representative for thick and thin thermal oxides, air-oxidized InP, and a chemical oxide. The energies of P2p in InP, $InPO_4$ and $In(PO_3)_3$ have been marked on the figure. The peak at lower BE observed in the case of thin oxide layers is due to the bulk phosphide signal penetrating the oxides. The higher energy phosphorus peak corresponds to oxidized P species. The BE of P2p in the condensed phosphate is again larger than in the orthophosphate. There is no evidence in the form of shoulders for additional phosphorus components in high oxidation states within the limits of resolution.

Results obtained for the investigated oxide layers are also given in Table 3. From conventional analysis of the XPS core level data, we conclude that the investigated chemical oxide layers of thickness $\approx$3 nm consisted pri- marily of condensed phosphates with atomic ratios P/In $\simeq$ 2.7-3. The prolonged oxidations in air have generated thin orthophosphate-like oxide layers of thickness $\approx$0.3 nm on top of the semiconductor. Thermal anneal- ing to $450^{\circ}$C has produced films of 30 nm consisting primarily of In oxide with a content of 20-40% of orthophosphate-like phosphorus compounds included. Only the data recorded from the front face of an anodic oxide layer of total thickness 240 nm will be reported. This region of the film, too, is mainly composed of orthophosphate-like compounds. These results are generally in line with data published on related subjects by other groups [5,8,50,51]. More data on the oxide layers investigated by our group, includ- ing O1s and In spectra, sputter profiles and a more extended discussion, will be given in detail elsewhere [49]. However, some comments have to be added regarding the reference level problem. As mentioned above, there is overall agreement between our data and BEs from other groups to within a few tenths of an eV. Although we do not have detailed information on electronic defect states arising from the preparation of the layers, this observation indicates that reference level shifts due to elec- tronic defect states may contribute only a small amount to the core level shifts to be discussed below.

### 4.2. Chemical state plots

To look at these features of the data in more detail, we again take advantage of the Auger parameter approach. Because of the lack of P1s energies for the layers, we approximate the relaxation energy shift by $\alpha'$ as given in eq. (2). This approach is reasonable despite the fact that only phosphates will be compared. The energies of the P2$p$ and the PKL$_{23}$L$_{23}(^1D_2)$ Auger transition of the investigated systems are summarized in a chemical state plot according to Wagner [25] in Fig. 6. As usual, the Auger parameters $\alpha'$ are indicated by a series of diagonal lines in steps of 0.5 eV. It is interesting that the data points representing oxidized phosphorus species in different layers are widely spread in the two-dimensional plot.

The reference compounds $\text{InPO}_4(\text{c})$ and $\text{In(PO}_3\text{)}_3(\text{c})$ differ in respect of both binding and Auger energies but exhibit about the same value of the Auger parameter. This result is quite analogous to the similar relaxation energies $\Delta R_{\text{S}}$ of the sodium phosphates presented in Table 2.

The data recorded from P within the chemically oxidized surfaces layers fall very close to those for $\text{In(PO}_3\text{)}_3(\text{c})$. This striking coincidence in both BE and AE strongly supports our previous conclusion that the layer is composed of condensed phosphates. Similarly, the anodic oxide (front face only) is located near $\text{InPO}_4(\text{c})$ in the chemical state plot, thus giving

evidence for orthophosphate being the main constituent in that region of the anodic oxide layer.

In contrast, the data points derived for the thermal oxide and the air-oxidized InP are shifted towards lower BE and higher $\alpha'$, significantly. The data for these layer systems and ${\text{InPO}}_{4}(\text{c})$ fall close to the broken line of slope 3 in the plot. This line connects states in the plot which are characterized by equal IS contributions to the BEs. From the former analysis, all the systems near this line include single phosphate units, which evidently exhibit very similar experimental $\Delta V$ values. With respect to $\Delta V$, these systems are well separated from the condensed phosphates. The difference is by no means as large as observed for the sodium phosphates, but it is nevertheless significant. It is difficult to assign that larger potential contribution to some physical origin in detail, but from the experience of the investigations on the bulk-like systems we may attribute it to a potential effect of the environment of P, which is related to the condensation of the phosphates. In this manner, that contribution to the core level shift may be indicative of single or condensed phosphates in the layers.

The $\Delta \alpha'$ or relaxation energy shift may be interpreted in a more straightforward fashion because there are no reference level problems. In line with the former analysis of the bulk samples, we again employ the dielectric model to estimate these shifts. For the thermally prepared oxide layer we estimate the polarization charge $(1-1/\epsilon )e$ by averaging according to the composition of the layer of about 70% indium oxide and 30% phosphate. Whereas ${\text{InPO}}_{4}$ has an energy gap of 4.5 eV and is an insulator, the semiconductor ${\text{In}}_{2}{\text{O}}_{3}$ has a band gap of 2.6 eV. Adopting approximate dielectric constants of $\approx 4.3$ for the semiconducting indium oxide and $\approx 2.6$ for the insulator indium orthophosphate, and approximating the cutoff radius again by about 0.154 nm, we obtain an increase in relaxation energy $R_{\text{s}}$ of $\approx 1.0$ eV from eq. (6). There is only qualitative agreement with the measured increase in $\alpha'$ of 0.6 eV. The air-oxidized InP exists on the surface as a rather thin film of thickness $\approx 0.3$ nm. In this case we attribute the increased $\alpha'$ and thus relaxation energy to the screening contributions from the InP substrate. Such screening effects have been predicted by Bechstedt et al. [52] from model calculations on metal/Si and ${\text{SiO}}_{2}/{\text{Si}}$ interfaces. Experimental evidence on significant substrate screening on adsorbed layers has been obtained by Chiang et al. [53] by adsorbing noble gas multilayers on metal surfaces. We may crudely estimate such bulk contributions by considering the change of $\epsilon$ at an interface by classical image charge models [52,53]

$$
\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}}=2 \frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0}} \frac{\left(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}\right)}{\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}\right)} \frac{1}{4 \epsilon_{2} r} \tag{14}
$$

Using $\epsilon_{1}=9.6$ (InP), $\epsilon_{2}=2.6$ (${\text{InPO}}_{4}$), and now $r \simeq 0.2$ nm for the distance

of the P atom inside the phosphate from the interface of the two dielectric materials, we obtain the estimated additional screening $\Delta R_{\mathrm{S}} \simeq 0.8$ eV. This estimate is close to the experimental $\Delta \alpha^{\prime}$ of 1.0 eV measured for this thin native oxide layer.

Thus, the order of magnitude of the increase of experimental Auger parameters in the oxide layers with respect to the crystalline reference sample may be reasonably estimated in the framework of dielectric models. This is encouraging in spite of the simplicity of the models which neglect variations in oxide layer composition, the presence of the oxide-vacuum interface, and the uncertainties in both theoretical input data and ex- perimental values.

Another point which might be of importance for the low BE values measured for P2p in these thin layers are possible suboxides, as have been reported by Hollinger, Hughes and co-workers [51]. On the other hand, the intensities from such suboxide species have been found to be generally low on InP, even in high resolution synchrotron radiation studies [51].

## 5. Concluding remarks
We have investigated core level shifts in phosphate systems in terms of the Auger parameter approach, the dielectric screening model and an approximate Madelung potential. These approaches themselves are not new in photoelectron spectroscopy. However, by combining these methods and applying them to phosphorus compounds with fourfold oxygen coor- dination, we have been able to distinguish local and environmental contri- butions to both initial and final state effects on the BEs of P2p. Shifts of the relaxation energies reflect differences in the chemical environment beyond the $\mathrm{PO}_{4}$ structural units by probing changes in the dielectric properties, as has been achieved for other systems. This is especially interesting for layer systems of mixed composition or spatial inhomogeneity.

The contribution of the solid state potential to the BEs in orthophos- phates has been calculated by structural and spectroscopic approaches with reasonable agreement to experimental shifts. This explains the short- comings of early interpretations of phosphorus core level shifts based on charge models only [11]. The consideration of ionicity in the structural approach has been found to improve the agreement with experiment, although the absolute correction remained small. Unfortunately, there is a lack of experimental evidence on the striking correspondence of P2p and O1s core level shifts in the compounds of interest. The importance of additional core level and Auger data is thus again emphasized.

The key assumption in our interpretation of the initial state effects has been the neglect of differences in the nearest neighbour P-O interactions within $\mathrm{PO}_{4}$ structural units, irrespective of the particular compound under

consideration. However, the occupation of $d_{\pi}-p_{\pi}$ orbitals of phosphorus in $PO_{4}$ tetrahedra is known to vary in dependence on minor structural distor tions [1,10,14], which have been reported for the more ionic phosphates [18] or condensed phosphates [14]. Moreover, the nearest neighbour and environmental effects will not be completely independent, and their arti- ficial separation requires simplifying assumptions, which may introduce significant uncertainties. In particular, an error of 0.1 eV in $\Delta \Delta E_{B}$ will result in an uncertainty of about 1 eV in the Madelung potential contribu- tions [29]. Thus, the reliability of our approach for the IS shifts is limited.

The reference level problem and its effect on our conclusions regarding binding energy and initial state contributions call for further comment. In the preceding sections we have provided evidence that this kind of effect is probably of less importance for condensed phosphates and the layered systems compared with the large experimental shifts in the IS contribu- tions. The large initial state effects observed in the case of the orthophos- phates let us similarly believe that reference level variations might be of minor importance, although no final evidence has been provided. In contrast, the potential contribution of the phosphorus environment has been demonstrated to reflect the experimental shifts satisfactorily.

We have summarized arguments on the need for further calculations of binding energies in these compounds. A closed theoretical description, which takes into account both structural and electronic effects appro- priately, should clearly be preferred. Nevertheless, the procedures outlined above, which have been based on the Auger parameter approach and approximate separation of environmental contributions to the BE in photo- emission from core lines, have been able to assign the experimental trends satisfactorily. They should be especially useful for further investigations of compounds containing similar complex anions.
