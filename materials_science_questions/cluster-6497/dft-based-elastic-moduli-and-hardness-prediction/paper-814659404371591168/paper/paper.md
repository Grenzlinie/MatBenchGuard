# Elastic and mechanical properties of hexagonal diamond under pressure

E. Güler · M. Güler

Received: 20 December 2014/Accepted: 27 January 2015
© Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2015

**Abstract** Hexagonal diamond is the harder and stiffer alternative of traditional cubic diamond for today's technology. Although several theoretical attempts have been performed to understand the ground-state elastic properties of hexagonal diamond, little is known about the high-pressure elastic properties of this key material. Unlike previous theoretical methods, we report the application of second-generation reactive bond order potential for the first time to elaborate the *pressure-dependent* properties of hexagonal diamond in conjunction with geometry optimization calculations up to 500 GPa. Pressure dependency of density, five independent elastic constants, bulk, shear and Young moduli, Poisson ratio, elastic wave velocities, anisotropy parameter, Kleinman parameter, and stability conditions of hexagonal diamond were evaluated. Overall, considered properties of hexagonal diamond display evident increments under pressure, and their ground-state values are in reasonable agreement with available theoretical data.

## 1 Introduction

The computational modeling of materials has been an efficient and quick tool to address the unclear issues of physical interest.

The results of these computations help us to predict new materials and can replace experiments that are quite expensive and even impossible in the laboratory. Further, predicting good elastic and mechanical properties of materials is an attractive subject for current solid-state science and industry. In particular, these properties at high pressure and temperature are most significant in many modern technologies [1, 2].

Despite the remarkable physical features of cubic diamond such as high thermal conductivity, ultra-hardness, and good insulating capacity, many theoretical and experimental efforts have been devoted to finding materials that are harder and thermally more stable than cubic diamond [3]. Accordingly, the situation of cubic diamond has been challenged after the discovery of harder and stiffer hexagonal diamond (also referred as 2H diamond or lonsdaleite) [4]. As well, because of its crucial fabrication conditions, available size of the hexagonal diamond is only at the nanometer scale in general. Thus, it is difficult to accomplish direct experiments on many mechanical properties of hexagonal diamond [5].

Apart from the difficult high-pressure experiments, several previous theoretical works usually report the ground-state ($T = 0$ K, $P = 0$ GPa) physical properties of hexagonal diamond. In 1990, Phelps calculated the five independent elastic constants of hexagonal diamond from the typical elastic constants of cubic diamond [6]. Later in 2003, Wang and Ye [7] reported the elastic constants of hexagonal diamond by using plane wave pseudo potential (PWPP) in the scheme of density functional theory (DFT). Wen et al. [8] surveyed structural, energetic, and electronic properties of various diamond polytypes including hexagonal diamond with PWPP through DFT computations in 2008. More recently, Shao et al. [9] investigated the temperature-dependent elastic constants of hexagonal diamond by combining continuum elasticity theory and first principles calculations. Nonetheless, pressure-dependent elastic and in particular mechanical properties of hexagonal diamond remain unclear. Therefore, in this work, unlike above theoretical calculations, we report the application of second-generation reactive bond

## 2 Computational details

Geometry optimization is a significant method for both molecular dynamics (MD) and DFT computations to search out a stable configuration for a molecule or periodic system through rapid and inexpensive energy computations. An optimization procedure involves the repeated sampling of the potential energy surface until the potential energy reaches a minimum where all forces on all atoms are zero. All herein done geometry optimization calculations were performed with General Utility Lattice Program (GULP) code [10, 11]. This MD code allows numerous property calculations for 3D periodic solids, 2D surfaces, and gas- phase clusters by employing two-body, three-body, four- body, six-body, and many body (EAM) potentials [12, 13] depending on the demands of the related research. Since REBO potentials provide satisfactory results for solid carbon and hydrocarbon molecules, we employed second- generation REBO-type potential [14] to this study for hexagonal diamond. The lattice constants for hexagonal diamond were set as $a=2.53$ Å and $c=4.12$ Å accord- ing to Wurtzite hexagonal symmetry (space group $P6_{3}/$ mmc), which are in very good agreement with the results of previous DFT works [4, 5]. Details of applied second- generation REBO-type potential and its bond order for- malism can be also found in Ref. [14].

It is possible to optimize related structures at constant pressures (includes all internal and cell variables) and constant volume (unit cell remains frozen) with GULP code [10, 11]. To achieve a successful geometry optimi- zation without constraints [15], constant pressure optimi- zation was applied for hexagonal diamond in the present work. The geometry of cells was optimized by the New- ton–Raphson method based on the Hessian matrix calcu- lated from the second derivatives. Hessian matrix was also recursively updated during optimization by using the BFGS [1, 2, 10–13] algorithm. After setting the prerequisites for the crystal structure of hexagonal diamond, we adopted multiple runs at zero Kelvin temperature and checked the pressure ranges starting from 0 GPa up to 500 GPa in the steps of 100 GPa.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the behavior of density in hexagonal dia- mond under pressure. As in many materials, density of hexagonal diamond shows a smooth increment with the increasing pressure. On the other hand, Fig. 2 outlines five independent elastic constants ($C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, $C_{13}$, $C_{33}$ and $C_{44}$) of hexagonal diamond under pressure. These constants are also responsible for mechanical hardness and necessary for specifying the stability of the material. Elastic constants usually derived from the total energy calculations represent the single-crystal elastic properties. On the other hand, Voigt–Reuss–Hill approach is a confident scheme for elastic constants of polycrystalline materials [1, 2]. To capture the correct values of elastic constants and relevant parameters of hexagonal diamond, we considered the Voigt–Reuss–Hill values during calculations. In Fig. 2, all elastic constants exhibit a straight increment with pressure for the entire pressure range. Besides, the magnitudes of the obtained elastic constants are in the order of $C_{33}>C_{11}>$ $C_{44}>C_{12}>C_{13}$. This result for elastic constants agrees well with the former findings of Shao et al. [9]. Beyond Fig. 2, Table 1 lists the numerical results of all considered parameters during this research and available theoretical data for the sake of comparison. A closer inspection of Table 1 reveals that the present results for elastic constants are in harmony with former DFT data.

From the stability outlook, the traditional Born mechanical stability condition for a hexagonal structure must satisfy the below expression [16]:

$$C_{44}>0,\ C_{11}>C_{12}\ \text{and}\ (C_{11}+2C_{12})C_{33}-2C_{13}^2>0.$$

Bulk modulus $(B)$ is a measure of resistance to external deformation and yields much information about the bonding strength of materials [17, 18]. From the common physical equation of bulk modulus, $(B=\Delta P/\Delta V)$, a linear increment can be expected for $B$ because of its direct proportion to the applied pressure. For this reason, bulk modulus of hexagonal diamond dramatically increases in

Fig. 3. The obtained value of $B$ is 442 GPa and of about previous DFT data [4]. This value of $B$ is also very close to the experimental value (443 GPa) of the cubic diamond [5]. By the way, shear modulus $(G)$ portrays the resistance to shape change caused by a shearing force [19–21]. Our obtained value for $G$ is 612.9 GPa. Moreover, Young modulus $(E)$ is the resistance to uniaxial tensions and gives the stiffness degree, i.e., the higher value of $E$, the stiffer is the material [22, 23]. The present value of $E$ for hexagonal diamond is calculated as 1,400.7 GPa, and this result is consistent with 1,324.6 GPa of Ref. [4] and 1,234 GPa of Ref. [8]. In addition, our result for $E$ (1,400.7 GPa) is larger than by about 40 % for the Young modulus of cubic diamond (1,048.5 GPa) calculated with the same REBO potential by authors [24]. This result means that hexagonal diamond is a stiffer material than cubic diamond and compares well with the findings and of Ref. [4]. As another outcome, like bulk modulus $(B)$, both $G$ and $E$ moduli of hexagonal diamond indicate an apparent increment under pressure as in Fig. 3.

Since ductility and brittleness play a key role during materials production, we also evaluated the ductile (brittle) nature of hexagonal diamond under pressure. The adjectives brittle and ductile stand for the two distinct mechanical behaviors of solids when they subjected to stress. In general, brittle materials are not deformable or less deformable before fracture. In contrast to brittle materials, ductile materials are deformable a lot before fracture. At this point, the Pugh $B/G$ ratio is a determinative limit for ductile (brittle) behavior and has a common use in the literature. If $B/G$ ratio of a material is about 1.75 and higher, the material is ductile; otherwise, the material becomes brittle. The plot in Fig. 4 gives $B/G$ values of diamond up to 500 GPa. Poisson ratio $(v)$ is the ratio between the transverse strain $(e_{\mathrm{t}})$ and longitudinal strain $(e_{1})$ in the elastic loading direction [25]. It provides detailed knowledge about the bonding character of solids. In general, Poisson ratio values are of about 0.1 for covalent materials, 0.25 for ionic materials and change between 0.28 and 0.42 for metals [2, 19, 26-28]. As in Fig. 5, Poisson ratio of hexagonal diamond is determined to be -0.02 at 0 GPa. This relatively low value of hexagonal diamond reflects the strict covalent bonding nature [25]. So as a combined result, the relatively low values of $B/G$ hence $v$ for diamond impose the high degree (tetrahedral) of covalent bonding for hexagonal diamond. The maximum value of $v$ with 0.16 at 500 GPa is also suggesting that the hexagonal diamond sustains its covalent bonding character even under high pressures.

In solids, low-temperature acoustic modes may initiate the vibrational excitations. Being dependent on this case, two typical elastic waves, namely the longitudinal wave and shear wave, arise [1, 2, 12, 13]. $V_{\mathrm{P}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{S}}$ refer the velocities of these waves, respectively. Figure 6 illustrates the elastic wave velocities $V_{\mathrm{P}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{S}}$ under pressure. Both $V_{\mathrm{P}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{S}}$ velocities of hexagonal diamond have an increment trend with the increasing pressure in Fig. 6, and this is the case for most of the materials.

## 4 Conclusions

It should be noted that this work reports the use of a different and effective method by applying a proper potential and addresses the pressure dependency of density, bulk modulus, shear modulus, Young modulus, Poisson ratio, elastic wave velocities, Kleinman parameter, and anisotropy of hexagonal diamond up to 500 GPa. Application of second-generation REBO potential (originally created for cubic diamond) through geometry optimization calculations reproduces reasonable results for the hexagonal diamond under pressure and compares well with previous DFT data. The negligible deviations between our results and earlier theoretical data seem to originate from the difference between the methodologies used in the calculations.

Finally, our all results are credible, and they will be especially shedding further light on the future experimental and theoretical works regarding the high-pressure elastic and relevant properties of hexagonal diamond.
