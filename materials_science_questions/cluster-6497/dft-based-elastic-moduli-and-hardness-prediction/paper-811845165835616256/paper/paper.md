## ABSTRACT

We investigated the effects of both bias voltage and annealing on the structure and mechanical properties of $\mathrm{WC_{0.75}N_{0.25}}$ thin films, deposited on $\mathrm{Si\left(100\right)}$ substrates by a direct current reactive magnetron sputtering system, in which the negative substrate bias voltage $(V_{\mathrm{b}})$ was varied from floating $(-1.6\,\text{V})$ to $-200\,\text{V}$, and the deposited films were annealed at $800\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ for $2\,\text{h}$. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and selected area electron diffraction analyses, along with the density-functional theory (DFT) calculations on the electronic structure, showed that $\mathrm{WC_{0.75}N_{0.25}}$ films were a single-phase of carbonitrides. After annealing, a significant decrease in hardness for the films was observed, being a result of point-defect annihilation as $V_{\mathrm{b}}$ was in the range of floating to $-120\,\text{V}$. However, when $V_{\mathrm{b}}$ was in the range of $-160$ to $-200\,\text{V}$, the hardness increased from $\sim37\,\text{GPa}$ for the as-deposited film to a maximum of $\sim43\,\text{GPa}$ for the annealed one. This increase in hardness after annealing might be attributed to age-hardening.

## 1. Introduction

During the last decades, ternary $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films are widely used in industrial applications because of their good adhesion to $\mathrm{Cu}$, low resistivity, high thermal and chemical stability [1,2]. In particular, tungsten carbonitride is known to be a superior barrier to copper diffusion in microcircuitry because of its low resistivity $(\sim350\,\mu\Omega\mathrm{cm})$, densely packed microstructure, favorable $\mathrm{N/W}$ ratio and ability to block $\mathrm{Cu}$ diffusion [3]. In some of these applications, $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films should require a higher durability, which is determined by their mechanical properties. Thus the mechanical property for $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films is an important issue for their reliable long-term performance. It looks very likely that $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ may exhibit high hardness similar to $\mathrm{TiCN}$, $\mathrm{CrCN}$, and $\mathrm{TiSiN}$ systems [4–8]. Up to the present, some optical, electrical, and structural properties have already been reported for the $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films, using different techniques such as physical vapor deposition (PVD) [9], chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [10,11] or atomic layer deposition (ALD) [3,12]. However, so far, there have been very few reports on the mechanical properties for $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films.

On the other hand, annealing processes have been widely employed to improve the properties of film through reducing the residual stress and defects [13–18]. In some of industrial applications, $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films will subject to localized heating caused by friction, power dissipation or endure an elevated temperature. Hence, it is necessary to explore the effects of annealing on the mechanical properties of $\mathrm{WC_{x}N_{y}}$ films. The hardness of films subject to annealing has been reported with quite different results [14,15]. Mader et al. [14] reported a significant drop of the film hardness and proposed a softening mechanism, which rationalized that the massive point defects would form voids at grain boundary. However, Chou et al. [15] found that the annealed specimens had 10–30% higher hardness than the as-deposited specimens without a significant change of microstructure or packing factor. Therefore, the softening mechanisms involved in annealing, which may influence the film's microstructures and properties, are not entirely understood.

In this work, $\mathrm{WC_{0.75}N_{0.25}}$ films have been prepared by direct current (DC) reactive sputtering of pure $\mathrm{W}$ target in a $\mathrm{CH_{4}/Ar/N_{2}}$ mixture discharge. The effects of the bias voltage applied on the substrate and annealing on the structure and mechanical properties of $\mathrm{WC_{0.75}N_{0.25}}$ films have been explored.

gas discharge. The distance between the target and substrate holder was fixed at 100 mm, and the chamber was evacuated by a turbomolecular pump to $1 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Pa}$ prior to film deposition. The substrates used in this work were commercially available single crystal Si (1 0 0) wafers with a root-mean-square (rms) roughness lower than 0.2 nm and a resistivity of $5\ \Omega$ cm. Before being introduced into the vacuum chamber, the Si (1 0 0) substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone, alcohol and distilled water. During the deposition, the mixing gas pressure was kept at 1.0 Pa in which the flow rates of Ar, $CH_4$, and $N_2$ are fixed at 20, 3 and 20 sccm, respectively. Keeping substrate temperature at $500\,^\circ\text{C}$, the applied current on the W target was 0.25 A, and various DC substrate bias ($V_b$) voltages ranging from floating ($-1.6\,\text{V}$) to $-200\,\text{V}$ were used. After deposition for 120 min, the films with a thickness of about $1.5\,\mu\text{m}$ were obtained. After deposition, the as-deposited films were annealed at $800\,^\circ\text{C}$ for 2 h in a tube furnace in vacuum. Prior to the annealing, the tube furnace was evacuated to $2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Pa}$ to protect the film surface from oxidation during annealing treatment.

The concentration and bonding states of the obtained films were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Perkin-Elmer PHI-5702), in which the analyzer was operated in the fixed analyzer transmission mode at constant pass energy of 11.75 eV. The binding energy scale was calibrated against the Cu 3p3/2 and Cu 2p3/2, respectively. The instrumental energy resolution, measured using the Cu 3p3/2 peak, was 0.1 eV. The accuracy of XPS measurements of the elemental concentration in films is about 7%. The film surface was firstly cleaned using ethanol in an ultrasonic bath, and subsequently etched down about 30 nm to remove the surface oxide contamination. The phase structure and preferred orientation of films were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bragg-Brentano diffractometer (D8 tools) in $\theta-2\theta$ configuration with Cu K$\alpha$ line at 0.15418 nm as a source. The phase structure of the films was also analyzed by X-ray diffraction (Bruker 8) in grazing incidence and selective area electron diffraction (SAED) (Hitachi H-8100IV), operating at 120 kV. The mechanical properties were evaluated by MTS Nanoindenter XP with continuous stiffness measurements (CSM) mode. A Berkovitch-type pyramidal diamond tip indented the films to a maximum depth of 500 nm. Constant stiffness data measurements were obtained by oscillating the tip during indentation with a frequency of 45 Hz and amplitude of few nanometers, and hardness values were taken at approximately 50-100 nm depth to avoid influence of the surface roughness and of the substrate. At least six indentations at different places on the film surface of each sample were made, and results here represented their average value.

The curvatures of the Si wafers before and after deposition were measured by an AMBIOS XP-2 surface profiler, and the residual stress was calculated using the Stoney's equation [19]. Assuming that the film was at a biaxial stress state, the thermal contribution to the total measured stress was estimated as

$$
\delta_{\text{th}}=\Delta\alpha\Delta T\frac{E_{\text{s}}}{\left(1-\nu_{\text{s}}\right)} \tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta\alpha$ was the difference in thermal expansion coefficients between the film and substrate, $\Delta T$ the temperature change after deposition. $E_{\text{s}}$ and $\nu_{\text{s}}$ are the Young's modulus (125 GPa) and Poisson's ratio (0.28) of the Si (1 0 0) substrate [19], respectively. In this work, the intrinsic stress was obtained through the total measured stress by subtracting the thermal stress.

The elastic constants for $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ were obtained by density-functional theory (DTF) calculation. Our DFT calculations were done using CASTEP code [20], in which the plane-wave pseudopotential total-energy calculation was performed. Vanderbilt-type ultrasoft pseudopotential [21] was employed to represent the interactions of electrons with ion cores for W, C and N atoms. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew and Wang parameterization [22] was used to describe the exchange and correlation energy in our calculations. The plane-wave basis set cutoff was set as 450 eV for all cases. The special points sampling integration over the Brillouin zone was based on the Monkhorst-Pack [23] method. The optimization of the lattice parameters was based on the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) minimization scheme [24]. The tolerances for the geometry optimization were selected as the change in total energy below $5.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{eV/atom}$, the maximum ionic force less than $0.01\ \text{eV/\AA}$, the maximum ionic displacement less than $5.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, and the maximum stress within 0.02 GPa. These parameters were sufficient to lead to well converged total energy and geometrical configurations. The theoretical elastic constants were determined by applying a set of small strain with a finite value and calculating the resulting stress, as implemented by Milman and Warren [25]. The elastic stiffness was calculated from a linear fit of the calculated stress and the applied strain. The compliance matrix was calculated as the inverse of the stiffness matrix, $S=C^{-1}$.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Film composition and structure

Fig. 1 shows the W, C and N concentrations of the as-deposited films obtained by XPS analysis. Upon increasing $V_b$ up to $-200\,\text{V}$, the carbon ($x$) and nitrogen ($y$) concentrations in the $WC_xN_y$ film keep almost constant with the value of 0.75 and 0.25, respectively, in which the ratio of [W]/([C]+[N]) is equal to 1:1, indicating that $V_b$ almost has no influence on the content in the $WC_xN_y$ films. The similar results have been reported in the sputtered Ti-Si-N films [26].

Fig. 2 exhibits the W 4f, C 1s, and N 1s XPS spectra for as-deposited $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ films as a function of $V_b$, in which there are no obvious changes in the line shape and position for the W 4f, C 1s or N 1s peak with increasing $V_b$ up to $-200\,\text{V}$, indicating that $V_b$ has little effect on the bonding state of the films. In order to further analyze the bonding structure of the obtained $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ film, the XPS spectra for pure WC and WN films deposited at $V_b=-80\,\text{V}$ are also shown in Fig. 2. In the C 1s XPS spectra (Fig. 2(a)), $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ films exhibit an approximately symmetric peak at binding energy of 283.1 eV, which is similar to that for pure WC film, indicating W-C bonds are formed in $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ films [27,28]. However, there is a shift of about 0.5 eV to higher binding energy for W-C bonds comparing to the pure WC which can be ascribed to the effect of nitrogen incorporation, indicating that the chemical environment surrounding C atoms is significantly influenced by the incorporated N. It is worth noting that neither $\text{C-N/C=N/C}\equiv\text{N}$ peaks, generally occur in the range of 284.5-289.9 eV [29,30], nor C-C ones at about 284.4 eV [29,30] appear in the C 1s XPS spectra for $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ samples, meaning that no $\text{C-N/C=N/C}\equiv\text{N}$ or C-C bonds are formed in the $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ films. This can be further verified by their N 1s XPS spectra (Fig. 2(b)), in which only an approximately symmetric W-N peak at about 397.0 eV appears [31,32], and no $\text{C-N/C=N/C}\equiv\text{N}$ ones, generally occurring in the range of 398.3-400.3 eV in N 1s XPS spectra [29,30], can be observed. In the W 4f XPS spectra (Fig. 2(c)), the pure WC film shows two peaks at binding energy of 31.8 and 33.9 eV, corresponding to the W 4f5/2 and W 4f7/2 state in W-C bonds [27,28], respectively. The first W $4\text{f}_{7/2}$ peak at 31.8 eV lies between the peaks of pure WN (32.7 eV) and WC (31.5 eV) phases, indicating that the W atoms are bonded to both C and N. The above XPS results of these films indicate that our obtained $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ films are single-phase of tungsten carbonitrides.

Generally, $\text{MeC}_x\text{N}_y$ (Me stands for transition metal) is a single-phase of metal carbonitride [4,33]. On the other hand, some researchers synthesized nanocomposite $\text{MeC}_x\text{N}_y$ thin films of nanocrystalline MeC or Me(C, N) embedded into an amorphous matrix [5,34]. In order to further prove that our obtained films are single-phase of tungsten carbonitrides, we have calculated the electronic structure of a cubic ($\beta-$) or hexagonal ($\alpha-$) $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ using CASTEP code based on DFT. It is worth noting that the preferred orientation of film is also significantly influenced by $V_{\text{b}}$. From Fig. 6, the intensity of the $\beta$-(1 1 1) peak increases whereas $\beta$-(2 0 0) peak decrease as $V_{\text{b}}$ increases from floating to $- 40$V. [38] found that the grains competitive growth might be the reason for selection of orientation. In this work, a growth competition occurs among neighboring crystals with different orientations, and the selected orientation is the one corresponding to the fastest growth rate due to anisotropies in surface atom diffusivities. Peleg et al. [39] and Johansson and Sundgren [40] proposed that the preferred orientation was determined by a competition between surface free energy and strain energy. With an increase of substrate bias, the strain energy in the film increases linearly if the substrate bias is in a proper range. Thus, at sufficiently low bias, the film exhibits an orientation in which the lowest surface free energy is realized. For the NaCl-structured $\beta$-WC$_{0.75}$N$_{0.25}$ films, this preferred orientation is (2 0 0). At high substrate bias, however, the strain energy will eventually exceed the surface energy. In this case, the preferred orientation of film is predicted to evolve into (1 1 1). For the hexagonal $\alpha$-WC$_{x}$N$_{y}$ films, only (1 0 0) appears under a high compressive-stress condition ($\geq 3.5$ GPa), and hence, it can be expected that the texture of $\alpha$-WC$_{0.75}$N$_{0.25}$ may be controlled by strain energy. In our previous work [36,37], we have investigated the preferred orientation of hexagonal NbN and N-doped WC films, and found that the c-axis of hexagonal phase will lie in the plane of the film with the lowest strain energy if the biaxial stress is high enough.

Fig. 6(c) displays the X-ray diffraction patterns for the annealed films. Comparing Fig. 6(a) with (c), the diffraction patterns of the as-deposited films and the annealed films exhibit a similar trend, i.e., the diffraction intensity of $\beta$-WC$_{0.75}$N$_{0.25}$ (1 1 1) preferred orientation increases with increasing $V_{\text{b}}$ and the phase transition occurs at $V_{\text{b}}$ in the range of $- 40$ to $- 120$V. However, after annealing, a decrease of full width at half maximum (FWHM) values is noticed. This decrease is caused by the process of recrystallization during annealing, which leads to an increase in grain sizes of film.

### 3.2. Mechanical properties

The evolution of the intrinsic stresses for the as-deposited and annealed $WC_{0.75}$N$_{0.25}$ films as a function of $V_{\text{b}}$ is shown in Fig. 8, wherein all films are under the compressive stress. The intrinsic stress for as-deposited film is significantly influenced by $V_{\text{b}}$. For the film grown at floating, a relatively low compressive stress of $\sim 1.2$ GPa is found. The compressive stress increases dramatically with increasing $V_{\text{b}}$ and reaches a maximum of $\sim 5.8$ GPa at $V_{\text{b}}$ of $- 200$V. An increase in stress with increasing $V_{\text{b}}$ is a well reported phenomenon [41], which is due to the increased energy of the bombarding ions with increased substrate bias voltage. This ion bombardment has been likened to a shot peening process which generates higher defect concentrations [42] with increasing shot intensity and thus leads to an increase in residual stress. For the annealed films, the compressive intrinsic stresses are significantly relieved to about 1.5 GPa and the variation of the stresses do not vary considerably with increasing $V_{\text{b}}$. Since the intrinsic residual stress has to be explained by lattice defects in atomic dimension, thermal energy provided by annealing can assist in re-arranging the atoms and annihilating the point defects through diffusion leading to the decrease in defect density, thereby decrease the stress.

The structure of the film is another factor to influence its hardness. It is generally accepted that two conditions must be met in order for a material to be hard: The material must support the volume decrease created by the applied pressure, therefore it must have a high bulk modulus B; the material must not deform in a direction different from the applied load, it must have a high shear modulus G. For cubic β-WC₀.₇₅N₀.₂₅, B and G can be obtained from elastic constants [45],

$$
B = \frac{(c_{11} + 2c_{12})}{3} \tag{2}
$$

$$
G = \frac{(c_{11} - c_{12} + 3c_{44})}{5} \tag{3}
$$

whereas for hexagonal α-WC₀.₇₅N₀.₂₅ phase, B and G are given by [46]

$$
B = \left( \frac{1}{9} \right) [2(c_{11} + c_{12}) + 4c_{13} + c_{33}] \tag{4}
$$

$$
G = \left( \frac{1}{30} \right) [c_{11} + c_{12} + 2c_{33} - 4c_{13} + 12c_{44} + 12c_{66}] \tag{5}
$$

This means that α-WC₀.₇₅N₀.₂₅ should have a higher intrinsic hardness than β-WC₀.₇₅N₀.₂₅, which accords with what we have observed in Fig. 9, in which the hardness of α-WC₀.₇₅N₀.₂₅ films grown at Vᵦ of −120 V is significantly higher than β-WC₀.₇₅N₀.₂₅ grown at Vᵦ in the range of floating to −40 V.

Additionally, the densification of film due to the ion bombardment also affects the hardness as Vᵦ changes from floating to −120 V. It has been reported that the enhancement of the hardness in thin films is affected by a high compressive stress [47,48]. Therefore a proper stress may be needed to achieve a higher hardness. Otherwise, the material may undergo a plastic flow, and a reduction in hardness will occur if the energy of incident ions is too high [49]. Excessive increasing the energy of the incident ions by increase negative bias voltages not only increase the mobility of the condensing atoms but also result in repeated nuclei. The re-sputtering of the depositing atoms might also occur. Therefore excessive energy of the depositing atoms may cause a damaging effect to the coating structure [50,51]. The impaired crystalline quality of the films is probably the reason for the decrease in hardness with increase bias voltages from −120 to −200 V. The similar trend has been observed in the indium tin oxide films [52]. Although many significant investigations have been presented to explain the effects of bias voltage on the hardness, a comprehensive picture of the relationship between bias voltage and mechanical properties is still lacking, and further work is needed.

The Vᵦ-dependent hardness for annealed films (Fig. 9) shows that the hardness increases linearly with increasing Vᵦ, and reaches a maximum value of 43.1 GPa at Vᵦ = −200 V. Compared to the as-deposited film, there is a significant 9–20% decrease in hardness for the annealed film when Vᵦ varies from floating to −120 V. An important controlling factor that reduces the film hardness may be that there is a lower defect density in annealed film than that in as-deposited one, which is confirmed from the fact that a pronounced relief of intrinsic residual stress occurs after annealing (Fig. 8). It turns out that the defects have a remarkable influence on nano-scale film hardness. This also implies that for the as-deposited films, a large amount of defects may be responsible for the observed higher hardness, compared to the annealed films. Therefore, a decrease in hardness can be attributed to the reduction in defect densities when Vᵦ is in the range of floating to −120 V.

It is worth noting that the hardness of our obtained $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ film increases from $\sim$37 GPa for the as-deposited film to a maximum of $\sim$43 GPa for the film after annealing when $V_{\text{b}}$ is in the range of $- 160$ to $- 200$ V. This behavior is in sharp contrast to binary nitride and carbide films, in which the hardness typically decreases after annealing due to relief of compressive stress [53–58]. The increase in hardness by annealing may be caused by age-hardening. In order to detect the microstructure of the annealed films, the typical XPS spectra of annealed film with $V_{\text{b}}$ of $- 200$ V are shown in Fig. 3. From the N 1s spectra for annealed films, it is found that there is no marked change in line shape and peak position after annealing except for some decrease in intensity. However, with annealing at $800^{\circ}\text{C}$ the pronounced changes for C 1s spectra occur, which implies that the chemical environment surrounding C atoms is significantly changed during annealing. After annealing at $800^{\circ}\text{C}$, the C 1s spectra are obviously asymmetric and the lines can be well decomposed into two peaks at 282.6 and 283.1 eV, respectively, as shown in Fig. 3. The peaks at 282.6 and 283.1 eV are close to that of pure WC and $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$, respectively. This indicates that the films are composed of two different phases: $\text{WC}_{x}\text{N}_{y}$ and $\text{WC}_{x}$ accompanied by the N loss during annealing at $800^{\circ}\text{C}$. Although the annealing leads to defect annihilation, atomic rearrangement, and recrystallization, resulting in a decrease in hardness of film, a decrease in growth-defects during annealing can be compensated by the onset of age-hardening, and hence, a significant increase in the hardness of film is observed in comparison to the as-deposited film. The similar phenomenon has been reported in the sputtered TiBN and TiAlN film [55,59].

## 4. Conclusions

The $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ films can be deposited on Si (1 0 0) substrates at different $V_{\text{b}}$ using direct current (DC) reactive magnetron sputtering in a mixture of $\text{CH}_{4}/\text{N}_{2}/\text{Ar}$ discharge. The substrate voltage bias and annealing have an influence on the structure and mechanical properties of the obtained films. The intensities of the $\beta$-(1 1 1) peak increase whereas $\beta$-(1 1 1)(2 0 0) peak decrease as $V_{\text{b}}$ increase from floating to $- 40$ V. With further increasing $V_{\text{b}}$ from $- 80$ to $- 120$ V, a phase transition from $\beta$- to $\alpha\text{-WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ occurs. The hardness of as-deposited film increases from $\sim$32 to $\sim$47 GPa as $V_{\text{b}}$ changes from floating to $- 120$ V, and then decreases with further increasing $V_{\text{b}}$. The effect of $V_{\text{b}}$ on hardness can be ascribed to the mixed roles of defect density, preferred orientation, and phase structure. Annealing causes a significant change in the hardness of film. When $V_{\text{b}}$ is below or equal to $- 120$ V, a stress relief due to a decrease in defect density during annealing leads to a significant loss in hardness, compared to the as-deposited films. However, when $V_{\text{b}}$ is in the range of $- 160$ to $- 200$ V, the annealed film exhibits a substantial increase in hardness with a maximum hardness of 43 GPa, which is much higher than 37 GPa for the as-deposited film. The age-hardening may be responsible for this hardness enhancement for the annealed $\text{WC}_{0.75}\text{N}_{0.25}$ film.
