# Equilibrium structure of multilayer van der Waals films and nanotubes

M. I. Mendelev and D. J. Srolovitz*
Princeton Materials Institute and Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Princeton University,
Princeton, New Jersey 08544

S. A. Safran and R. Tenne
Department of Materials and Interfaces, Weizmann Institute of Science, Rehovot, Israel

(Received 3 August 2001; published 7 January 2002)

Recent experimental results demonstrate that it is possible to grow a variety of different multiphase, nested nanotube structures. This paper predicts the structure and energetics of such multiphase nanotubes. There are several distinct contributions to the energetics: the internal and external surface energies, the energy of the interface between the different phases, the long-range (van der Waals) interactions between interfaces, and the elastic bending energy. We perform energy minimizations to compare the energies of two- and three-layer films and nanotubes. We present physical guidelines, quantitative theory, and structure maps that show how materials and geometric parameters influence the stability of competing structures.

## I. INTRODUCTION

A wide variety of nanotube and nanotubelike structures have been investigated in the past ten years. Nanotubes have been synthesized from a wide variety of materials, including $C,^{1}$ C-BN, $^{2-4}$ WS $_{2},^{5}$ SiO $_{2}, Al_{2}O_{3}, V_{2}O_{5}$ , and $MoO_{3}^{6}$ The widespread attention paid to nanotubes can be traced not only to the interesting structure, but also to a wide range of electrical, chemical, and mechanical properties associated with these structures. Multilayer nanotubes consisting of many layers that are only weakly (van der Waals) bound (the bonding within the layers is generally covalent) have been observed in a variety of systems, e.g., $C,^{7} MoS_{2},^{8} WS_{2},^{9}$ BN, $^{10}$ and $V_{2}O_{5}^{11}$ Recent experiments showed the existence of phase-separated, multilayer nanotubes in the CBN system. $^{12}$ Some of these nanotubes consisted of a solid core of one material, surrounded by one or two types of other layers. In other cases, the core was hollow and surrounded by two, three, or five concentric layers of different phases (C and BN). Such complex structures may be a result of the local environments in which these materials were synthe- sized, or they may be equilibrium structures. Structures pro- duced by gas phase synthesis are more likely to be equilib- rium than those produced by diffusional transformations. In principle, the energetics of such structures could be deter- mined from first-principles calculations. $^{13}$ In order to deduce the dependence of the equilibrium structure on layer thick- ness, tube radius, elastic constants, and surface tensions, it is instructive to take a more macroscopic approach. This allows an analytic determination of how the materials properties of the individual phases and the structure of the nanotubes deli- cately balance to determine the thermodynamically stable forms of bimaterial, multilayer nanotubes. In the present pa- per, we perform such an analysis to determine the equilib- rium structures of such nanotubes.

Rather than focus specifically on the CBN case, we take a more general approach in this paper and consider the case of the distribution of two, arbitrary layered (graphitelike) phases A and B (of fixed composition), within a multilayer nanotube. We discuss on the four nanotube structures that can form around a large, hollow core, with either two or three layers, namely, AB, BA, ABA, or BAB, as indicated in Fig. 1 (the layers are denoted from the inside to the outside). Our goal is to determine which of these structures has the lowest energy as a function of materials parameters, the rela- tive volumes of the two phases, and the internal nanotube radius. There are several contributions to the energy of such a system: the interfacial energies, the interactions between the interfaces, and the bending energies. In these graphitelike systems, atomic planes parallel to the axis of the nanotube are covalently bonded with relatively weak van der Waals interactions between the planes. Therefore, the interfacial en- ergies and interactions between interfaces are determined by van der Waals interactions. While the interfacial energies do not depend on the thickness of the A and B layers, the van der Waals energies result in relatively long-range interactions between the interfaces that yield a contribution to the energy that depends on the separations between interfaces. In addi- tion, for tubes, there is a curvature elastic energetic penalty for bending the atomic planes around the axis of the nano-

[From this point on, we use the term elastic energy to repre- sent the curvature energy (rather than any misfit or disloca- tion energy).] There are two interesting elastic limits for the bending energy. If the interactions between the atomic planes are very weak, dislocations will be generated upon bending and the atomic planes will be decoupled from one another. $^{14}$  This is the incoherent limit, where the bending energy scales linearly with the number of planes. If, on the other hand, the interactions between atomic planes are strong, dislocations will not form upon bending and the system will remain co- herent; the elastic energy in this limit scales as the cube of the number of planes. The real situation probably lies some- where between these two limits, but since van der Waals energies are typically much smaller than those associated with covalent bonds, the incoherent limit is expected to be more appropriate in the present case. It is the competition between the interfacial energies, the interactions between in- terfaces and the elastic energies that determine the distribu- tion of the phases within the nanotubes.

In the present paper, we determine the optimal distribu- tion of the phases for the four nanotube structures shown in Fig. 1. We begin by calculating the energies of two- and three-layer flat films of infinite extent in order to determine the effects of interfacial energetics in a situation where bend- ing elasticity plays no role. Next, we examine the energies of the four optimized nanotube structures to determine which is thermodynamically preferred. These results are then com- piled in the form of structure maps which identify the stable structures as a function of the properties and quantities of the individual phases. Finally, we compare the predictions of the present analysis to some published experimental results on CBN-based nanotubes.

## II. MULTILAYER FILMS
We begin the analysis by considering the relatively simple case of a flat, multilayer film. Unlike the case of a layered cylinder discussed above, there is no elastic contribution to the energy (i.e., no bending) here and, hence, we need only consider the van der Waals energy. The van der Waals self- energy of the system is
$$U=-\frac{1}{2} W n^{2} \int \frac{d \vec{r} d \vec{r}^{\prime}}{\left[\left(\vec{r}-\vec{r}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+a^{2}\right]^{3}},\qquad(1)$$
where $W$ is related to the Hamaker constant, $n$ is the number of atoms per unit volume, and $a$ is an atomic-scale cutoff necessary to avoid the singularity associated with an atominteracting with itself. $^{15,16}$

For a thin, homogeneous, flat film of thickness of $D \gg a$ ,integration of Eq. (1) yields the self-energy per unit area: $^{15}$ 
$$\frac{U}{A} \approx \frac{W n^{2}}{a^{2}}\left[-\frac{\pi}{8}\left(\frac{D}{a}\right)+\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{12}\left(\frac{a}{D}\right)^{2}\right]=-\beta D+2 \sigma-\frac{\varepsilon}{D^{2}},$$
where we neglect terms of order $(a / D)^{3}$ and higher. The first term in this expression is proportional to the thickness and represents the bulk self-energy $(\beta=\pi W n^{2} / 8 a^{3})$ . The second term does not depend on thickness and is the surface energy(σ=πWn²/8a²). The last term is a correction to this simple picture associated with the finite film thickness and can be thought of as an effective, attractive interaction between the two surfaces $(\varepsilon=\pi W n^{2} / 12)$ .

We now examine a multilayer system consisting of $n$ flat layers. The thickness of each layer can be much larger than that of an atomic plane. The van der Waals energy of this system has two contributions: (i) the self-energy of a layer which is described by Eq. (2) and (ii) the interaction between the layers. Two types of interaction can be distinguished de- pending on whether the two layers are adjacent or not. For two arbitrary adjacent $A$ and $B$ layers $i$ and $i+1$ , the interaction energy (per unit area) can be determined from Eq. (1):
$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{E_{i, i+1}}{A}= & -\frac{\pi}{4} \frac{W_{A B}}{a_{A B}^{2}} n_{A} n_{B}+\frac{\pi}{12} W_{A B} n_{A} n_{B} \\
& \times\left[\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{1}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(z_{i}-z_{i+1}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{i+1}\right)^{2}}\right], \\
& (3)
\end{aligned}$$
where $z_{i}$ is the position of the interface between layers $i$ -1 and $i$ such that the thickness of layer $i$ is $z_{i}-z_{i-1}$ . The first term accounts for the energy of the interface. The second term (i.e., in square brackets) is associated with interactions between interfaces and will be discussed below. If layers $i$ and $j$ are not adjacent, the interaction energy (per unit area)can also be determined from Eq. (1):
$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{E_{i, j}}{A}= & -\frac{\pi}{12} W_{t_{i} t_{j}} n_{i} n_{j}\left[\frac{1}{\left(z_{i}-z_{j-1}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{j-1}\right)^{2}}\right. \\
& \left.-\frac{1}{\left(z_{i}-z_{j}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{j}\right)^{2}}\right], \quad|i-j|>1,
\end{aligned}\qquad(4)$$
where $t_{i}$ labels the type of material in layer $i(t_{i}$ is either $A$ or B).

Combining Eqs. (2)-(4) yields an expression for the totalenergy of the multilayer film system:
$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{U}{A}= & -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_{i}\left(z_{i}-z_{i-1}\right)+\sigma_{t_{1}}+\sigma_{t_{n}}+(n-1) \sigma_{A B} \\
& -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\varepsilon_{t_{i} t_{i}}}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{i}\right)^{2}}+\varepsilon_{A B} \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\{\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{i}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(z_{i}-z_{i+1}\right)^{2}}\right. \\
& \left.-\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{i+1}\right)^{2}}\right\}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-2} \sum_{j=i+2}^{n} \varepsilon_{t_{i} t_{j}}\left\{\frac{1}{\left(z_{i}-z_{j-1}\right)^{2}}\right. \\
& \left.-\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{j-1}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\left(z_{i}-z_{j}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(z_{i-1}-z_{j}\right)^{2}}\right\},
\end{aligned}$$
where $\sigma_{A B}$ is the $A / B$ interface energy and $\varepsilon_{t_{i} t_{j}}$ =(π/12)W_ntnt_j. In this paper we assume that any elastic

misfit between the individual layers is relaxed by slipping between the layers (e.g., as from interfacial dislocations) and the interfacial energy is that appropriate for incoherent interfaces. In this analysis, we do not explicitly consider the contribution of the dislocation array energy that accounts for the slipping between layers (i.e., difference between coherent and incoherent interfacial energies). This energetic contribution, though small, is localized at the interface, and therefore could be included as an additional contribution to the interface energy. For layers that interact primarily through van der Waals interactions, the interfacial energy is

$$
\sigma_{A B}=\frac{\pi}{8} \frac{W_{A} n_{A}^{2}}{a_{A}^{2}}+\frac{\pi}{8} \frac{W_{B} n_{B}^{2}}{a_{B}^{2}}-\frac{\pi}{4} \frac{W_{A B} n_{A} n_{B}}{a_{A B}^{2}}.
$$

Rearranging the last two sums in the previous general equation yields the following more transparent result:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U}{A}= & -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_{t_{i}}\left(z_{i}-z_{i-1}\right)+\sigma_{t_{1}}+\sigma_{t_{n}}+(n-1) \sigma_{A B} \\
& -\frac{\varepsilon_{t_{1} t_{n}}}{\left(z_{n}-z_{0}\right)^{2}}-\left(\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{t_{1} t_{1}}\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{i}}{\left(z_{i}-z_{0}\right)^{2}} \\
& -\left(\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{t_{n} t_{n}}\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{i+n}}{\left(z_{i}-z_{n}\right)^{2}}-\left(2 \varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}-\varepsilon_{B B}\right) \\
& \times \sum_{i=1}^{n-2} \sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{\left(z_{i}-z_{j}\right)^{2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The arguments of the first summation in Eq. (5) represent the bulk energies of each layer (they only depend on layer type) and the entire sum in the first term is the total bulk energy of the materials that comprise the multilayer film. The next three terms correspond to the surface energies of the bottom and top surfaces of the film and all of the internal interface energies, respectively. The fifth term describes the van der Waals interactions between the two free surfaces. The sixth and seventh terms represent the interactions between the internal interfaces and the free surfaces. Finally, the double sum describes all of the interactions between the internal interfaces. If we convert all of the $B$ layers into $A$ layers, Eq. (5) reduces to Eq. (2).

The interactions between surfaces are always inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Equation (5) also shows that the free surfaces attract each other, independent of the types of materials on the surfaces. An internal interface is either attracted to or repelled from the free surface depending on the order of the materials at the interface (i.e., $A / B$ or $B / A$ ) and the relative strength of the interactions ( $\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{t_{s} t_{s}}$, where $s$ is either 0 or $n$ ). For example, if the first layer is of type $A$, then the $A / B$ interface (with the $A$ closer to the bottom surface) is attracted to the bottom surface if the van der Waals interactions between $A$ atoms is stronger than the interaction between $A$ and $B$ atoms (i.e., $\varepsilon_{A B}<\varepsilon_{A A}$ ). Finally, the sign of the interaction between internal interfaces depends on whether the $A B$ interactions are greater or smaller than the average of the $A A$ and $B B$ interactions and the order of the materials meeting at the interface. For example, if $\varepsilon_{A B}<\left(\varepsilon_{A A}+\varepsilon_{B B}\right) / 2$, an $A / B$ interface will repel another $A / B$ interface, but attract a $B / A$ interface.

We now restrict our attention to two- and three-layer flat films. Figure 2(a) shows a stability diagram for the multilayer film that indicates whether the equilibrium film has two or three layers as a function of the quantity of material $A$ and $B$ contained within the film. This diagram was obtained by comparing the energies of the two-layer $(A B)$

and three-layer (ABA and BAB) films from Eq. (5). We explicitly assume that the two outer layers of the three-layer film are of the same thickness, as shown below. The material parameters employed in the calculation of the stability diagram are presented in the figure caption. Figure 2(a) shows that if the volumes of A and B per unit area of the film are large (i.e., large $D_A$ and $D_B$), the ABA film is more stable than the AB film, but if $D_A$ and/or $D_B$ is small, the two-layer structure is preferable. The stability diagram is not symmetric about $D_A$=$D_B$; i.e., more B is needed to stabilize the ABA film relative to the AB film at large $D_A$ than the analogous amount of material A needed at large $D_B$. This is because the stability diagram represents a case in which $\sigma_A$$\neq\sigma_B$ and $\varepsilon_{AA}\neq\varepsilon_{BB}$.

In order to understand the stability diagram presented in Fig. 2(a), we consider the two- and three-layer films in more detail. For the two-layer flat film, Eq. (5) reduces to

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U_{A B}}{A}= & -\left[\beta_{A} D_{A}+\beta_{B} D_{B}\right]+\sigma_{A}+\sigma_{B}+\sigma_{A B}-\frac{\varepsilon_{A B}}{\left(D_{A}+D_{B}\right)^{2}} \\
& +\frac{\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}}{D_{A}^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{B B}}{D_{B}^{2}},
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
$$

where there is no interface-interface interaction term because there is only a single internal interface. For the three layer system (assuming that the top and bottom surfaces are of type A), the total energy of the system obtained from Eq. (5) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U_{A B A}}{A}= & -\left[\beta_{A} D_{A 1}+\beta_{B} D_{B}+\beta_{A} D_{A 2}\right]+2 \sigma_{A}+2 \sigma_{A B} \\
& -\frac{\varepsilon_{A A}}{\left(D_{A 1}+D_{A 2}+D_{B}\right)^{2}}-\left(\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}\right) \\
& \times\left[-\frac{1}{D_{A 1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{D_{A 2}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(D_{A 1}+D_{B}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(D_{A 2}+D_{B}\right)^{2}}\right] \\
& +\left(2 \varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}-\varepsilon_{B B}\right) \frac{1}{D_{B}^{2}},
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

where the terms associated with the interaction between the interfaces and the surfaces have been combined (both surface layers are of the same type, A).

We now determine the equilibrium distribution of A and B between the different layers. First we consider the relative thickness of the two A layers in the three-layer film (we label whichever phase is adjacent to the surfaces as material A). Minimization of the total energy [Eq. (7)] with respect to the thickness of the lower A layer, $D_{A1}$, at fixed quantities of materials A and B shows that $D_{A1}$=$D_{A2}$, provided that $\varepsilon_{AB}>\varepsilon_{AA}$. If $\varepsilon_{AB}<\varepsilon_{AA}$, then the minimum energy is obtained when either $D_{A1}$→0 or $D_{A2}$→0. This suggests that the three-layer film will transform to a two-layer film. This suggestion cannot be rigorously proven from Eq. (7), since it was derived under the assumption that the film has three layers. However, this observation implies that if a three-layer film exists for $\varepsilon_{AB}<\varepsilon_{AA}$, the thickness of the thin A layer must be no larger than a few interatomic spacings; such extremely thin layers cannot be described on the basis of continuum theory and hence are omitted from further consideration. The tendency for either $D_{A1}$→0 or $D_{A2}$→0 can be understood by reference to Eq. (5), and the subsequent discussion which shows that when $\varepsilon_{AB}<\varepsilon_{AA}$, the interfaces are attracted to the closest free surface. Interestingly, the total energy $U_{ABA}$ is completely independent of the relative thicknesses of the two A layers if we do not consider the interface/surface interaction terms.

The relative stability of the two- and three-layer film can be determined from the relative energies of the two films [i.e., Eqs. (6) and (7)] for the case $\varepsilon_{AB}>\varepsilon_{AA}$. In order to ensure that the comparison is made for the same quantities of materials A and B, we write $D_{A1}$+$D_{A2}$=$D_A$ for the three-layer film:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U_{A B A}}{A} & -\frac{U_{A B}}{A} \\
= & \sigma_{A}-\sigma_{B}+\sigma_{A B}+\left(\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}\right) \\
& \quad \times\left(\frac{7}{D_{A}^{2}}+\frac{1}{D_{B}^{2}}-\frac{2}{\left(D_{A} / 2+D_{B}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(D_{A}+D_{B}\right)^{2}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

Since we compare the energies of the two- and three-layer films with the same volumes of materials A and B, the bulk energy terms from Eqs. (6) and (7) cancel each other in Eq. (8). We can now look at two important limits: small and large layer thicknesses. For large layer thicknesses ($D_A$→∞ and $D_B$→∞), the terms that describe interactions between interfaces or between interfaces and surfaces are negligible and the equilibrium structure is determined solely from the balance of the interface and surface energies. If $\sigma_A-\sigma_B+\sigma_{AB}$>0, then the two-layer film is stable, while in the opposite limit the three-layer film is table. This can be understood by considering the energy required to change the two-layer film to a three-layer film is simply the energy required to replace a B surface with an AB interface and an A surface.

If the layers are thin, the interactions between interfaces [the term in Eq. (8) containing the large brackets] must be considered. Since this term is always positive for $\varepsilon_{AB}$>$\varepsilon_{AA}$, the two-layer film is stable with respect to the three-layer ABA film provided that the interaction term is larger than the surface/interfacial terms, e.g., in the limit where the A layers are very thin. In this case, the interaction between the AB interface and the free A surface is repulsive. For the ABA films, the AB interface is closer to the A surface than is the AB interface in the AB film (because in the ABA film each A layer is half as thick as the A layer in the AB film). Thus the AB-interface-A-surface interaction energy is higher in the ABA film compared with the AB film. Equation (8) also shows that if $\sigma_B$>$\sigma_A$+$\sigma_{AB}$, there must be a transition from a two-layer film to a three-layer film as long as the interaction terms do not prevent this. The interaction terms will be least important for thick films. If the B layer is very thick, we

find a transition from $AB$ to $ABA$ when $D_A^2\gt 7(\varepsilon_{AB}
-\varepsilon_{AA})/(\sigma_B-\sigma_A-\sigma_{AB})$—that is, if the $A$ layer is thick enough so its interaction energy is negligible and the three- layer film can be stabilized by the sum of the surface and interfacial energies alone. Similarly, if the $A$ layer is thick, we find a transition from $AB$ to $ABA$ when $D_B^2\gt (\varepsilon_{AB}
-\varepsilon_{AA})/(\sigma_B-\sigma_A-\sigma_{AB})$. Finally, if $\sigma_B\lt\sigma_A+\sigma_{AB}$, then the $AB$ film is always lower energy than the $ABA$ film and no transition occurs.

In order to apply these results to any physical system, we must know the materials parameters $W_A$, $W_B$, $W_{AB}$, $a_A$, $a_B$, and $a_{AB}$. Unfortunately, all six are rarely available and, hence, we usually must make some approximations. While $W_A$ and $W_B$ may be obtained from the Hamaker constants of the individual materials, $W_{AB}$ is usually not available. Fol- lowing Israelachvili, $^{16}$ we estimate $W_{AB}$ as $W_{AB}=\sqrt{W_A W_B}$. If we assume that $n_A=n_B$ and $a_A=a_B$, this approximation ensures that the $AB$ interface energy $\sigma_{AB}$ is non-negative. $a_A$ and $a_B$ can be obtained from experimental surface tension data. A reasonable approximation for the constant $a_{AB}$ is to take the arithmetic average of those from the pure materials $a_{AB}=(a_A+a_B)/2$. The conditions $\varepsilon_{AB}\gt\varepsilon_{AA}$ discussed at length above, can be reexpressed in terms of the approxima- tion for $W_{AB}$ as $\sqrt{W_B/W_A}\gt n_A/n_B$. From this inequality, we see that if $\varepsilon_{AB}\gt\varepsilon_{AA}$, then $\varepsilon_{AB}\lt\varepsilon_{BB}$. Therefore, if the $ABA$ structure is stable, then the $BAB$ structure is unstable and vice versa.

We can use the results derived above to understand some of the features of the stability diagram shown in Fig. 2(a). First, note that this stability diagram was obtained for the special case of $\varepsilon_{AB}\gt\varepsilon_{AA}$. This implies that the interface interaction energies favor relatively thin $A$ layers so that there is a tendency to make two, separate $A$ layers as found in the $ABA$ structure. Thus the $BAB$ multilayer film is inher- ently unstable and, hence, there is only competition between the $AB$ and $ABA$ structures. Figure 2(a) shows that the $ABA$ film is more stable than the $AB$ film when both $D_A$ and $D_B$ are large. This is simply a result of the fact that for the present set of physical parameters $\sigma_B\gt\sigma_A+\sigma_{AB}$; i.e., the energy of the system is lowered by replacing a $B$ surface with an $A$ surface and an $AB$ interface, as discussed above. For sufficiently small $D_A$ or $D_B$, the $AB$ film is more stable—as suggested from the analysis and discussion of Eq. (8), above. Figure 2(a) shows that the $AB$/$ABA$ transition occurs at approximately 2.5 times the minority component thickness (at large majority component thickness) when $A$ is the minority than when $B$ is the minority. This is in excellent agreement with the analytical prediction of the thickness ra- tio where this transition occurs [see the discussion of Eq. (8)], i.e., $D_A/D_B=\sqrt{7}\approx 2.65$.

### III. MULTILAYER TUBES

We now examine the equilibrium structure of multilayer, cylindrical tubes (see Fig. 1). Unlike in the case of free- standing planar multilayer films where elasticity plays no role, the elastic energy associated with curvature makes an important contribution to the thermodynamics of tubes. The change in geometry from the film to the tube also modifies the

van der Waals energy. The van der Waals energy of a tube of thickness $D$, inner radius $R_0$, and length $L$ [see Fig. 3(a)] can be written in terms of that of the films as (see the Appendix)

$$
\frac{U}{L}=2 \pi R_{0}\left(1+\frac{D}{2 R_{0}}\right) \frac{U^{\mathrm{film}}}{A}, \tag{9}
$$

where we have assumed (and will assume throughout this paper) that the inner radius of the tube, $R_0$, is much larger than the thickness of the tube walls, $D$, and that $L$ is suffi- ciently large that the ends of the tube do not make significant contributions to the total energy. Since the interaction be- tween the surfaces is small (because we only consider cases where $D\gg a$) compared to either the bulk or surface ener- gies, we neglect the correction term of order $D/R_0$. In the comparisons of different structures below, the correction to the surface energy is the leading order correction in going from the film to the tube geometry because the comparisons are made at fixed volume of each material (such that the bulk terms cancel). Inserting the definition of $U^{\mathrm{film}}$ from Eq. (2) and neglecting the correction to the interaction term yields

$$
\frac{U}{L}=2 \pi R(-\beta D+\sigma)\left(1+\frac{D}{2 R_{0}}\right)-2 \pi R \frac{\varepsilon}{D^{2}}. \tag{10}
$$

Bending an elastic film into a cylinder raises the elastic energy of the film by¹⁷
$$
\frac{U_{e}}{L}=2 \pi R_{0}\left[\frac{E D^{3}}{24\left(1-\nu^{2}\right) R_{0}^{2}}\right],\qquad(11)
$$
where $E$ and $\nu$ are the Young's modulus and Poisson ratio, and $R$ is the radius of curvature. Equation (11) is valid for materials where the bonding is strong in all three dimen- sions. The bending energy can be relaxed by introducing misfit dislocations between the atomic planes. There is a trade-off between the energy of the misfit dislocations and the bending energy (i.e., the misfit dislocations lower the bending energy, but raise the interfacial energy¹⁴). Disloca- tions lying along the van der Waals bonded planes in such materials as graphite and MoS₂ tend to have delocalized and, hence, low-energy cores. Therefore, in such materials the bending energy is commonly relaxed by the formation of dislocations between the layers, as discussed in detail in Ref. 14. The bending of such a material is equivalent to bending each of the atomic planes independently:
$$
\frac{U_{e}}{L}=2 \pi R_{0}\left(\eta \frac{D}{R_{0}^{2}}\right),\qquad(12)
$$
where $\eta=E a^{2} /[24(1-\nu^{2})]$. Comparison of Eqs. (11) and (12) shows that the elastic energy increases much more weakly with the tube wall thickness in the incoherent case [Eq. (12)] than in the coherent case [Eq. (11)].

Combining Eqs. (5), (9), and (12) yields a generalized expression for the total energy of the multilayer tube shown in Fig. 3(c):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U}{L}= & -\pi \sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_{t_{i}}\left(R_{i}^{2}-R_{i-1}^{2}\right)+2 \pi R_{0} \sigma_{t_{1}}+2 \pi R_{n} \sigma_{t_{n}} \\
& +2 \pi \sigma_{A B} \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} R_{i}+2 \pi R_{0}\left[-\frac{\varepsilon_{t_{1} t_{n}}}{\left(R_{n}-R_{0}\right)^{2}}-\left(\varepsilon_{A B}\right.\right. \\
& \left.-\varepsilon_{t_{1} t_{1}}\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{i}}{\left(R_{i}-R_{0}\right)^{2}}-\left(\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{t_{n} t_{n}}\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{i+n}}{\left(R_{i}-R_{n}\right)^{2}} \\
& \left.-\left(2 \varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}-\varepsilon_{B B}\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n=2} \sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{\left(R_{i}-R_{j}\right)^{2}}\right] \\
& +2 \pi \sum_{i=1}^{n} \eta_{t_{n}} \frac{R_{i}-R_{i-1}}{R_{i-1}}.
\end{aligned}\qquad(13)
$$

The first term is the bulk energy, the next two are the surface energies, the third represents the interfacial energies, the fifth term (i.e., in square brackets) is the interfacial interaction energy, and the last term is the elastic energy. The first term in the brackets is the interaction between the inner and outer surfaces, the next two are the interactions between the sur- faces and all of the internal interfaces, and the last term in the brackets is the interaction between the internal interfaces.

We can rewrite this expression in terms of the energy of the multilayer film, $U^{\text{film}}$ [Eq. (5)]:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U}{L}= & 2 \pi R \frac{U^{\text {film }}}{A}+2 \pi\left(R_{n}-R\right) \sigma_{t_{n}}+2 \pi \sigma_{A B} \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(R_{i}-R\right) \\
& +\sum_{i=1}^{n} \eta_{t_{n}} \frac{R_{i}-R_{i-1}}{R_{i-1}}.
\end{aligned}\qquad(14)
$$

In making this change we use the fact that the first term in Eq. (13) is simply the bulk energy per unit length: i.e., the first sum is $\beta_{A}N_{A}+\beta_{B}N_{B}$, where $N_{A}$ and $N_{B}$ are the number of atoms of each material type (per unit length). All of the terms in Eq. (14), except for the (flat) film energy, are asso- ciated with the tube geometry and bending and are positive. Therefore, the energy per atom in the infinite multilayer tube is always greater than for the infinite multilayer film. How- ever, for a finite-size system, the edge energies (not consid- ered here) can stabilize the tube with respect to the film.¹⁸,¹⁹

We determine the equilibrium arrangement of materials A and B in the multilayer tube for the special cases where the tube either consists of two or three layers, as seen in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d), respectively. A stability diagram can be con- structed that indicates the equilibrium multilayer tube struc- ture type (AB, BA, ABA, BAB). The axes of these diagrams should, in principle, be the total volume of A and the total volume of B per unit length of the tube. However, such axes are not easily understood in terms of the geometry of the multilayer tubes. Instead, we define the effective thickness $D_{A}$ as the thickness of a flat A film of dimensions $2\pi R_{0}$ $\times D_{A}\times L$ having the same volume as the sum of the A layers volumes in the multilayer tube. While this is somewhat com- plicated, $D_{A}$ is simply equal to the sum of the thickness of the A layers in the multilayer films to within a factor of $[1 + D_{A}/(2R_{0})]$. Since we assume throughout that $D_{A}/R_{0}$ is small for all layers, this is a small correction. $D_{B}$ is defined analogously.

The equilibrium multilayer tube structure is obtained by comparing the total energies obtained in Eq. (14) subject to the constraints that the total amount of material A and mate- rial B are fixed for different internal radii, $R_{0}$. For the three- layer tube, the energy is first minimized with respect to the distribution of materials between the inner and outer layer. Figure 5, below, shows the variation of $D_{A1}/D_{A2}$ with the tube inner radius $R_{0}$ obtained from numerical minimization of $U_{ABA}$. This figure shows that under all conditions, the ratio of the inner to outer layer thickness approach unity from below as $R_{0}\to\infty$ (i.e., a flat film). When the stiffness of material A is less than that of material B, the inner layer thickness is greater than that of the outer layer. This is be- cause the system bending energy is lowest when the radius of curvature of the B region (the material with the larger bend- ing modulus) is largest. Once the equilibrium layer thick- nesses in the three-layer tube is determined, the overall sta- bility diagram is determined by comparison of the equilibrium energies of the two- and three-layer tubes.

Figures 2(b)-2(d) show the stability diagrams for one set of material parameters and four values of $R_0$. For all tube radii, the equilibrium structure is an $AB$ tube provided that at least one of the layer is thin. If both $D_A$ and $D_B$ are large, the equilibrium tube structure is of the $ABA$ type [see the discussion of the $ABA$ to $AB$ transition for flat layers following Eq. (8)]. No $BAB$ regions are seen on any of the stability diagrams for the material parameters employed (see the caption to Fig. 2). For finite tube radii, there is a small range of parameters for which the $BA$ structure is the lowest energy (when $R_0\rightarrow\infty$, the $AB$ and $BA$ geometries are exactly equivalent). For small radius tubes, the $BA$ two-layer tube is unstable with respect to the $AB$ tube structure at large $D_B$. For the material parameters investigated here, there is very little difference between the $R_0/a_{AB}=200$ and 500 stability diagrams [Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)]. Comparison of these stability diagrams with that for the flat film (corresponding to $R_0$ $\rightarrow\infty$ at constant $D_A$ and $D_B$) shows that the only significant difference is associated with the disappearance of the $BA$ structure from the $R_0=\infty$ stability map. In fact, the $BA$ region of stability does not disappear, but rather becomes indistinguishable from the $AB$ case in this limit. Figures 2(e) and 2(f) show the effects of changing the Young's modulus of material $B$ on the stability diagram. These figures are for exactly the same conditions as those in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c), except that in the present case [Figs. 2(d) and 2(e)] the Young's modulus of $B$ is smaller than that of $A$. Comparison of Figs. 2(b) and 2(e) shows that decreasing the modulus of the $B$ layer leads to the complete disappearance of the three-layer stability field and a much larger stability field of the $BA$ tube. On the other hand, when the tube radius is large, changing the Young's modulus of the $B$ layer has almost no effect on the stability diagram [cf. Figs. 2(c) and 2(f)].

We can understand many of the features of the multilayer tube stability diagram by considering the competition between the two- and three-layer tubes in several interesting limits. For the two-layer tube, Eq. (14) reduces to
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U_{A B}}{L}= & 2 \pi\left[R_{0} \frac{U_{A B}^{\text {film }}}{A}+\left(D_{A}+D_{B}\right) \sigma_{B}+D_{A} \sigma_{A B}\right. \\
& \left.+\frac{\eta_{A} D_{A 1}}{R_{0}}+\frac{\eta_{B} D_{B}}{R_{0}+D_{A 1}}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

An equation for the $BA$ tube (i.e., where the $B$ layer has a smaller radius than the $A$ layer—Fig. 1) may be obtained by simply interchanging $A$ and $B$ in Eq. (15). Note that if the total volumes of $A$ and $B$ are conserved; moving the $A$ layer from the inner to outer surface necessarily implies that $D_A$ for the outer layer case is smaller than $D_A$ for the inner layer case. In order to determine whether the $AB$ or $BA$ two-layer tubes are more stable, we investigate the difference in energy between these two geometries:
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{U_{B A}}{L}-\frac{U_{A B}}{L} \\
&= 2 \pi\left[\left(D_{B}+D_{A}\right)\left(\sigma_{A}-\sigma_{B}\right)+\left(D_{B}-D_{A}\right) \sigma_{A B}+2\left(\varepsilon_{B B}\right.\right. \\
&\left.\left.-\varepsilon_{A B}\right) \frac{D_{A}}{D_{B}^{2}}-2\left(\varepsilon_{A A}-\varepsilon_{A B}\right) \frac{D_{B}}{D_{A}^{2}}-\frac{2\left(\eta_{A}-\eta_{B}\right) D_{A} D_{B}}{R_{0}^{2}}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

For small values of the thicknesses, the interface-surface interaction terms dominate this energy difference. As discussed above, the differences $\varepsilon_{BB}-\varepsilon_{AB}$ and $\varepsilon_{AA}-\varepsilon_{AB}$ always have opposite signs (at least in the approximation that $W_{AB}$ $=\sqrt{W_A W_B}$). If $\varepsilon_{AB}>\varepsilon_{AA}$, then if either $D_A$ or $D_B$ is small, the $A$ layer will be on the interior of the tube (i.e., $AB$ is more stable than $BA$). This is because the interface is attracted to the $B$ surface and repelled from the $A$ surface. If the $A$ layer is on the interior, then the interface is closer to the $B$ surface and further from the $A$ surface than if the $B$ layer is on the interior.

In the limit of large tube radius, the elasticity term [i.e., the last term in Eq. (16)] can be neglected. For large $A$ and $B$ layer thicknesses ($D_A$ and $D_B$), the interface-surface interaction terms are negligible. It is only when these conditions are met that the surface and interface energies alone determine which materials are on the interior and exterior of the tubes. Under these conditions, there is a tendency for the material with the lowest surface energy to be on the exterior of the tube (i.e., the surface with largest area). When the tube radius is not large, the elastic term in Eq. (16) cannot be neglected. Examination of this expression shows that when $\eta_A<\eta_B$, the elastic contribution favors the $A$ layer on the tube interior, where the radius of curvature is the smallest—i.e., the bending energy is taken up by the softer, $A$-type material. The elasticity effect is more important when the difference between the bending moduli becomes large.

The only experimental observations of a multilayer vander Waals tubes of which we are aware are for CBN. $^{12}$  Therefore, we must also obtain material parameters for hex- agonal boron nitride $(h-BN)$ . The separation between the van der Waals planes in $h$ -BN is $0.33 ~nm$ and the atomic density is $n=109$ atom $/ nm^{3}$ . While we do not have data on the sur face tension of $h$ -BN, there is experimental evidence that suggests $^{12,23}$ that the surface tension of graphite is less thanthat of $h$ -BN. Given the relationships between $\sigma, W$ , and $\varepsilon$  and the data quoted here, we conclude that $\varepsilon_{graphite }<\varepsilon_{h-BN}$ . Thus the C/BN/C three-layer tube structure is thermody- namically possible, while the BN/C/BN tube is unstable. The only experimentally observed three-layer tubes in the CBN system were of the type C/BN/C (Ref. 12), in agreement with the present predictions. In addition, two-layer BN/C tubes were found in which the inner layer was BN. The existence of two-layer tubes with $BN$ in the inner layer is favored both by the lower surface energy of $h$ -BN relative to graphite and by the fact that the $h$ -BN is less stiff than graphite $^{24}$ [see Eq. (16)]. We also note that since BN is an insulator and graphite is a semimetal, some charge transfer from graphite may occur, producing long-range image forces that were not considered in the present analysis.

Several approximations were employed in the analysis of the stability of multilayer films and tubes presented above. These include the assumption that the system is continuous rather than discrete and that the surface and interface ener- gies are attributable only to van der Waals interactions. The first assumption may lead to errors when the layers contain only a few atomic planes. Nonetheless, a wide range of ex- perience suggests that continuum models routinely lead to at least semiquantitative predictions down to just a few inter- atomic spacings. The assumption that the surface and inter- face energies are determined solely by van der Waals inter- actions is also an approximation. Even in predominantly van der Waals systems, the surface and interfacial energies typi- cally have some contributions from other types of bonding. However, since the present analysis was performed using the surface and interface energies as parameters, the analysis still holds, with the numerical values reflecting the total interfa- cial and surface energies and not just those that arise from the van der Waals interactions alone. For example, changesin the $A$ or $B$ surface energies will not affect the distribution $A B A$ stability boundaries. In the present analysis, we also did not account for the effects of differences in lattice parameters between $A$ and $B$ , which may be manifested as either misfit strain or, if relaxes, by the incoherency of the $A B$ interface. Since the bonding between van der Waals layers is extremely weak, relaxation of misfit strain between layers by the for- mation of interfacial dislocations is very likely. Such relax- ation will only occur if the increase of interfacial energy associated with the introduction of the dislocations is lower than that gained by decreasing the misfit strain energy. Since the van der Waals bonding between the atomic planes meet- ing at the interface is very weak, the dislocation cores are probably spread over a large interfacial area. In very thin films or films with very high misfit, it is this dislocation core energy that dominates the difference in energy between the coherent and incoherent interface and hence the misfit con- tributions simply renormalize the interfacial energy. Unfortu- nately, it is difficult to quantitatively predict these effects without performing atomistic simulations (note that most first-principles, electronic-structure-based methods cannot account for van der Waals interactions). Finally, we note that if the bonding between atomic planes is not determined by van der Waals interactions, then the bending energy is not easily relaxed by the formation of misfit. In this case, the magnitude of the elastic energy and its scaling with layer thickness and film or tube thickness is completely changed[cf. Eqs. (11) and (12)—the elastic energy increases by a factor of $(D / a)^{2}]$ .

In the analysis presented above, we determined the equi- librium tube structure by minimizing the total energy with respect to the distribution of the two types of materials be- tween the layers. A global minimization of the energy would also include minimization with respect to the tube radius $R_{0}$ , which was not done here. We omitted this additional mini- mization because there is evidence to suggest that the overall tube radius may be determined by external factors. synthesis conditions, $^{25}$ in some cases, such as some of the
CBN multilayer tubes discussed above, the tubes may or
may not contain solid cores of non-CBN material (e.g., $HfB_{x}$
in the case of Ref. 12). When solid cores are present, they
routinely determine the internal radii. In such cases, the inner
tube free surface energy and the interface/surface interaction
contributions to the total energy should be modified to ac-
count for the presence of the core material and the change in
the inner surface to a new type of interface.

## VI. CONCLUSION

In the present paper, we have predicted the stability of
two-phase, multilayer tubes and films on the basis of the
competition between the surface and interfacial energies, the
energies of interactions between the interfaces and interfaces
and surfaces, and the bending energies. When only one of
these terms dominates, one can determine the equilibrium
structures using the following rules of thumb (also see Table
I): (a) for two-layer tubes, the material with the higher sur-
face energy will preferentially lie on the tube interior (where
the surface area per unit length is smaller), (b) the elastically
stiffer material tends to form the exterior layer of the tube
(where the curvature is smaller), (c) the $AB$ interface is at-
tracted to the $A$-layer surface if $\varepsilon_{AB}<\varepsilon_{AA}$, thus destabilizing
the $ABA$ film or tube relative to the two-layer film or tube
(similarly for the $BAB$ tube is $\varepsilon_{AB}<\varepsilon_{BB}$), (d) if the interface
energy is large (and positive), the two-layer film or tube is
preferred to the three-layer film or tube, (e) if either layer is
thin, then a two-layer film or tube is stable with respect to a
three-layer structure. Nonetheless, as the layer thicknesses
and materials parameters are varied, the competition between
the different terms is not necessarily controlled by any single
effect (a)-(e). In such cases, there is no alternative for pre-
dicting the stability of multiphase tubes but to apply the
quantitative theory present here. The stability diagrams sum-
marize the quantitative theoretical predictions and are con-
sistent with the simple, intuitive guidelines suggested here.
