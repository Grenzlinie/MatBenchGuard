## ABSTRACT

Direct measurements of bulk and surface properties of liquid alloys at elevated temperatures are often technically difficult or even impossible, and therefore, theoretical models can be used to estimate missing property values. The energetics of mixing in liquid Co-Cr, Cr-Ni and Co-Ni systems has been analysed through the study of the concentration dependence of various thermodynamic, surface (surface tension and surface composition) and structural properties (concentration fluctuations in the long-wavelength limit and chemical short-range order parameter) by the first or the Quasi-Chemical Approximation (QCA) for regular solutions, developed by Bhatia and Singh, in the framework of statistical mechanical theory in conjunction with the Quasi-Lattice Theory (QLT). The results obtained for these binary systems have been extended to study the thermodynamics and surface properties of ternary Co-Cr-Ni liquid alloys.

## 1. Introduction

High corrosion resistance and good high temperature mechanical properties of Co-Cr-Ni based alloys make them suitable for a wide range of applications, such as MCrAlY (M = Ni and/or Co) coating systems [1], biocompatible Co-Cr-Ni-W alloys are used for orthopaedic and vascular implants [2,3], while the Ni-Co-Cr-W-Si-B system is applied in joining processes [4].

The Co-Cr-Ni system exhibits good oxidation resistance and high tensile strength and it is widely used as the main subsystem of multicomponent NiCoCrAlY coating alloys developed to protect gas turbine blades and discs against environmental attack [1]. The chemical composition and specific role of single alloy components were assumed as criteria to classify protective coatings into two main groups. Indeed, Ni-based alloys rich in Al are oxidation resistant, while Co and Co-Ni based alloys with high Cr content are particularly efficient against hot corrosion. A small amount of Y, Si and Hf can significantly improve scale adherence, while refractory Ta or Re have a beneficial effect on oxidation resistance, increasing in both cases the lifetime of coatings [5,6]. Coating behaviour is strongly influenced by the surface composition that is responsible for providing a protective surface layer as well as by the ordering tendency of an alloy in the liquid state. The last one is a valuable characteristic for coating systems since the formation of intermetallic compounds guarantees a better adhesion to the substrate. Accordingly, the improvement of the existing materials and the development of new materials require the knowledge of relevant property data. Despite the Co-Cr-Ni system and its subsystems are of great industrial and technological importance, the data on their thermodynamic and, in particular, thermophysical properties are still incomplete [7-9].

In order to obtain some information on the energetics and the structure of the Co-Cr-Ni ternary system the corresponding data on its binary subsystems are required. The Co-Cr [10-13], the Co-Ni [14-16] and the Cr-Ni [16-18] systems have been studied by many authors and their phase diagrams are well established [13,16]. The Co-Cr and Cr-Ni phase diagrams indicate the presence of the $\sigma$ and $\gamma'$ intermediate phases, respectively, and are more complex with respect to a lens-type phase diagram that describes the Co-Ni system [19]. The thermodynamic properties of the Co-Cr-Ni system have been determined experimentally in the temperature range (1073 to 1573) K, which made it possible to establish several isothermal sections [9]. However, the experimental data concerning phase equilibria between the solid and liquid phases at higher temperatures as well as the liquidus surface projection for the Co-Cr-Ni system are still missing and thus, the latest version of the Co-Cr-Ni phase diagram [9] is not yet complete. As concerns their surface properties, the surface tension data on liquid Co-Ni [20,21] and Cr-Ni [22-24] alloys are reported by few authors, while in the case of Co-Cr and Co-Cr-Ni melts, the corresponding data are lacking. Therefore, it is only possible to estimate the missing values in terms of theoretical framework.

The present paper deals with thermodynamic and surface properties of liquid Co-Cr-Ni alloys with special emphasis on the bulk and surface properties of its subsystems, i.e. the Co-Cr, Cr-Ni and Co-Ni. The concentration and temperature dependence of surface composition and surface tension have been theoretically investigated by using the Quasi-Chemical Approximation (QCA) for regular solutions developed by Bhatia and Singh [25-27]. The results obtained for the surface properties of binaries are very close to the corresponding values calculated by using the Butler model [28]. The last one has been applied to calculate the surface properties of liquid Co-Cr-Ni ternary alloys and the calculations were performed for $T=1873$ K. Thermodynamic solution models, such as the regular solution model or the sub-regular solution model, etc. can be used in conjunction with Butler's equation [29]. It is important to mention that the QCA used in the present work is entirely different from the Modified Quasi-chemical Model developed by Pelton and his co-workers [30-35].

The QCA formalism provides the information on the energetics and structure at atomistic level that be deduced from observable indicators, such as thermodynamic and thermophysical quantities, giving qualitative insight into the mixing processes that occur in an alloy melt. The ordering phenomena in the Co-Cr, Cr-Ni and Co-Ni. binary systems will be discussed in terms of microscopic functions, such as the concentration-concentration fluctuations in the long-wavelength limit, $S_{cc}(0)$, [36] and Warren-Cowley short-range order parameter, $\alpha_{1}$, [37]. $S_{cc}(0)$ elucidates the nature of ordering, while $\alpha_{1}$ indicates the degree of order in the melt [38].

## 2. Theory

### 2.1. The QCA for regular solutions: thermodynamic and surface properties

The formation of binary alloys is the result of the interplay of energetic and structural readjustment of the constituent atoms. On mixing, $A$ and $B$ atoms can prefer to remain self-coordinated forming $A-A$ or $B-B$ pairs, or can exhibit a strong interactive tendency between unlike atoms forming heterocoordinated $A-B$ pairs. Based on this, all liquid binary alloys can be classified according to deviations of their thermodynamic and thermophysical properties from the additive rule of mixing (Raoult's law) into two main groups: segregating (positive deviation) or compound forming (negative deviation) alloys [39-41]. In spite of the fundamental difference existing between the amorphous structure of the liquids and the crystalline structure of the solids, a close similarity of their properties can be observed close to the melting point. Indeed, the arrangement of atoms in a liquid, at least in the neighbourhood of the crystallisation point, must be more or less similar to their arrangement in the corresponding solid phases [41,42]. This can be directly confirmed by diffraction experiments [43]. Chemical order in solid and liquid alloys is mainly governed by short-range forces between the constituent atoms. Several theoretical treatments, such as the Quasi-chemical method [25] and its variants [27,30,34,44], a number of statistical-mechanical approximations [26,45-47], the Bragg-Williams, the cluster variation and the free-energy expansion methods, the Monte Carlo (MC) simulations [48,49] as well as classical molecular dynamics (CMD) together with $ab$ initio molecular dynamics simulations [50] have been developed to investigate the effects of short-range order phenomenon on the mixing behaviour of alloys. Among the aforementioned methods, the simplest method of treating short-range order is the Quasi-chemical method, which is based on the assumption that the total energy can be divided on energies for near neighbour bonds [25]. Pelton and his co-workers introduced the Modified Quasi-chemical Model (MQM) for short-range order in liquids in the pair approximation [30] aiming its applications to calculate the thermodynamic functions and to optimise phase diagrams. Since the 80s until now the Modified Quasi-chemical Model has been applied to several hundred systems of inorganic materials, such as slags [30], molten salts [31], liquid alloys [34,35], etc. The MQM accounts for a composition of maximum short-range ordering in a binary system to be freely chosen, to express the energy of pair formation as a function of composition and to extend the model to multicomponent systems. The excess thermodynamic quantities are described by the common polynomial expansions [32,33].

The scarcity or the lack of experimental data on the thermophysical properties of liquid alloys makes often impossible to use the models that contain the equations with many adjustable coefficients, as those used for the thermodynamic calculations [34,35]. The first attempt to extend the Quasi-chemical method to describe the surface properties taking into account structural arrangement in liquid alloys, considering both, segregating and compound forming alloy systems, was given by Goumiri and Joud [44]. Based on the "surrounded atom" model and the Quasi-Chemical Approximation [25], these authors developed a statistical model by which the surface tension and the surface composition of some segregating and compound forming alloy systems have been calculated, exhibiting a good agreement with existing experimental data. The effects of short-range order on the thermophysical properties, i.e. the surface and transport properties of liquid binary alloys can be analysed by another variant of the quasi-chemical approach, the so called first or the Quasi-Chemical Approximation (QCA), developed by Bhatia and Singh [25-27,47] in the framework of statistical mechanical theory in conjunction with the Quasi-Lattice Theory (QLT). It has been shown that a numerical treatment of the QCA corresponds to the calculation of a partition function for an ensemble analogous to that for the Complex formation model (CFM) [51,52].

Considering the mixing behaviour of liquid binary alloys, Guggenheim [25] demonstrated that the partition function approach from a statistical-mechanical theory and the Quasi Lattice Theory [26] lead to similar results. The generalised mathematical formalism is available for compound forming alloys [51,52] as well as for regular alloys [27], characterised by a complete solubility in the solid and the liquid phases, or eutectic-type systems. Thermodynamic and surface properties of simple alloy systems, such as, for example, the Cu-Ni [53], Co-Ni [54], Ag-Cu [55], etc. have been properly treated by the QCA for regular solutions [27]. The same model was also applied to weakly interacting compound forming alloys, such as the Cr-Ni [56] system, etc., in which the short range order phenomenon is not pronounced. In the present work the basic equations of the QCA formalism [25-27,45,47] for the regular solutions are given.

The grand partition function for the bulk, $\Xi^{b}$, of a binary $A-B$ alloy consisting of $N_{A}(=NC_{A})$ and $N_{B}(=NC_{B})$ atoms of elements $A$ and $B$, respectively, where the total number of atoms, $N$, is equal to $N_{A}+N_{B}$, is expressed by

$$
\Xi^{b}=\sum_{E} q_{A}^{N_{A}}(T) q_{B}^{N_{B}}(T) \exp \left\{\left(\mu_{A} N_{A}+\mu_{B} N_{B}-E\right) / k_{B} T\right\},
\tag{1}
$$

where $C_{i}$ is the concentration of element $i$ in an alloy, $q_{i}^{N}(T)$ and $\mu_{i}$ are atomic partition function and chemical potential of $i$th component ($i=A,B$), $k_{B}$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature and $E$ is the configurational energy of an alloy. In order to solve equation (1) some simplifications are essential [27,47]. The first one is that the interactions between the atoms should be of short range and they are effective only between nearest neighbours. Secondly, the whole set of lattice sites is divided into two groups, i.e. a cluster of a few lattice sites and the remainder, defining a domain 1 and a domain 2, respectively. Based on the above hypotheses, the

grand partition function equation (1) can be expressed, according to to the convolution function formalism, as

$$
\Xi^{b}=\Xi_{1}^{\prime} \cdot \Xi_{2}, \tag{2}
$$

where a prime on $\Xi_{1}^{\prime}$ indicates that the interaction of domain 1 with domain 2 is incorporated in it and thus, $\Xi_{1}^{\prime}$ becomes the quantity of interest. Taking into account [45,47], $\Xi_{1}^{\prime}$ can be expressed as

$$
\Xi_{1}^{\prime}=\sum_{E_{1}} \xi_{A}^{N_{1 A}} \xi_{B}^{N_{1 B}} \phi_{A}^{v_{A}} \phi_{B}^{v_{B}} e^{-E_{1} / k_{B} T}, \tag{3}
$$

where $E_{1}$ is the configurational energy of the cluster $1, \phi_{A}^{v_{A}}$ and $\phi_{B}^{v_{B}}$ are constants, $v_{A}$ and $v_{B}$ are numbers of lattice sites in domains 2 and 1, respectively, that are nearest neighbours of the $A$ and $B$ atoms in the cluster. The $\xi_{A}$ and $\xi_{B}$ are introduced to abbreviate the parts of equation (1), i.e.

$$
\xi_{i}=q_{i}(T) e^{\mu_{i} / k_{B} T}, \tag{4}
$$

where $i=A, B$. Taking into account the definition of $\Xi_{1}^{\prime}$ (equation (3)) together with equation (4), the average values of $A$ and $B$ atoms in the cluster are given by

$$
\left\langle N_{1 A}\right\rangle=k_{B} T \frac{\partial \ln \left(\Xi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial \mu_{A}}=\xi_{A} \frac{\partial \ln \left(\Xi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial \xi_{A}}, \tag{5a}
$$

$$
\left\langle N_{1 B}\right\rangle=k_{B} T \frac{\partial \ln \left(\Xi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial \mu_{B}}=\xi_{B} \frac{\partial \ln \left(\Xi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial \xi_{B}}. \tag{5b}
$$

The ratio of the expressions (5b) and (5a) gives

$$
\frac{\left\langle N_{1 B}\right\rangle}{\left\langle N_{1 A}\right\rangle}=\frac{1-C}{C}=\frac{\xi_{B}}{\xi_{A}} \frac{\partial \ln \left(\Xi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial \xi_{B}} / \frac{\partial \ln \left(\Xi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial \xi_{A}}, \tag{6}
$$

where $C$ and $1-C$ are the average concentrations of $A$ and $B$ atoms in the cluster and must be the same as the concentrations of $A$ and $B$ atoms in the alloy, respectively. Therefore, the ratio (equation (6)) is independent from the cluster size, and thus, the clusters of two different sizes can be used to evaluate the ratio given in equation (6).

For a cluster of one lattice site $E_{1}=0$, and it can be occupied by either an $A$-atom or $B$-atom, while $v_{A}$ and $v_{B}$ can be replaced by the coordination number of the lattice, $Z$, as follows

$$
\Xi_{1}^{\prime(1)}=\xi_{A} \phi_{A}^{Z}+\xi_{B} \phi_{B}^{Z}, \tag{7}
$$

where the superscript (1) refers to the cluster of one atom. When the cluster consists of two lattice sites, the arrangements of $A$ and $B$ atoms on sites 1 and 2 within the cluster are defined in terms of the coordination umbers $v_{A}$ and $v_{B}$. For the self-coordinated pairs $A-A$ and $B-B$ the values of $v_{A}$ and $v_{B}$ result to be $2(Z-1)$, while for heterocoordinated $A-B$ pairs, the corresponding values for both lattice sites are the same, $(Z-1)$ [27]. Under the aforementioned considerations for a cluster of two sites, equation (3) becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Xi_{1}^{\prime(2)}= & \sum_{E_{1}} \xi_{A}^{2} \phi_{A}^{2(Z-1)} e^{-\varepsilon_{A A} / k_{B} T}+\xi_{B}^{2} \phi_{B}^{2(Z-1)} e^{-\varepsilon_{B B} / k_{B} T} \\
& +2 \xi_{A} \xi_{B}\left(\phi_{A} \phi_{B}\right)^{Z-1} e^{-\varepsilon_{A B} / k_{B} T}, \tag{8}
\end{aligned}
$$

where the superscript (2) refers to the cluster of two lattice sites and $\varepsilon_{i j},(i, j=A, B)$ are bonding energies of $i-j$ bond. Taking into account equations (6) and (7), one obtains

$$
\frac{1-C}{C}=\frac{\xi_{B}}{\xi_{A}}\left\{\frac{\phi_{B}}{\phi_{A}}\right\}^{2}. \tag{9}
$$

On the other side, combining equations (6) and (8), one has

$$
\frac{1-C}{C}=\frac{\xi_{B}}{\xi_{A}} \frac{\xi_{B} \phi_{B}^{2(Z-1)} e^{-\varepsilon_{B B} / k_{B} T}+\xi_{A}\left(\phi_{A} \phi_{B}\right)^{Z-1} e^{-\varepsilon_{A B} / k_{B} T}}{\xi_{A} \phi_{A}^{2(Z-1)} e^{-\varepsilon_{A A} / k_{B} T}+\xi_{B}\left(\phi_{A} \phi_{B}\right)^{Z-1} e^{-\varepsilon_{A B} / k_{B} T}}. \tag{10}
$$

After some algebra, the combination of equations (9) and (10) gives

$$
\rho^{2}+\left\{\frac{1-2 C}{\eta C}\right\} \rho-\left\{\frac{1-C}{C}\right\}=0, \tag{11}
$$

where $\rho=\phi_{B} / \phi_{A} \cdot \exp \left\{\left(\varepsilon_{B B}-\varepsilon_{A A}\right) / 2 k_{B} T\right\}$ and $\eta=\exp \left\{\left(\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A}-\varepsilon_{B B}\right) /\right.$ $\left.2 k_{B} T\right\}=\exp \left\{\omega / Z k_{B} T\right\}$ were introduced to abbreviate the parts of equation (11). For the regular solution model, $\omega$ can be given as a linear function of temperature, as

$$
\omega=Z\left\{\varepsilon_{A B}-\varepsilon_{A A} / 2-\varepsilon_{B B} / 2\right\}=a+b T, \tag{12}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the interaction energy parameters that can be calculated from the thermodynamic data. The QCA for regular solution assumes the additivity of pair wise interactions in the melt. The solution of equation (11) is given by

$$
\rho=\frac{\beta-1+2 C}{2 C \exp \left(\omega / Z k_{B} T\right)}, \tag{13}
$$

where the auxiliary variable $\beta$ is defined as

$$
\beta=\left\{4 C(1-C) \exp \left(2 \omega / Z k_{B} T\right)+(1-2 C)^{2}\right\}^{1 / 2}. \tag{14}
$$

Inserting $\omega$ from equation (14) into equation (13), one obtains

$$
\rho=\left\{\frac{(1-C)}{C} \cdot \frac{(\beta+2 C-1)}{(\beta-2 C+1)}\right\}^{1 / 2}, \tag{15}
$$

which is directly related to the ratio of the activity coefficients of the alloy components. Combining the standard thermodynamic relations for the chemical potentials $\mu_{A}$ and $\mu_{B}$, i.e.

$$
\mu_{A}-\mu_{A}^{0}=k_{B} T \cdot \ln \left(C \gamma_{A}\right), \tag{16a}
$$

$$
\mu_{B}-\mu_{B}^{0}=k_{B} T \cdot \ln \left\{(1-C) \gamma_{B}\right\}, \tag{16b}
$$

where $\mu_{A}^{0}$ and $\mu_{B}^{0}$ are the chemical potentials of the pure species $A$ and $B$, respectively, $\gamma_{A}$ and $\gamma_{B}$ are the activity coefficients of the alloy components. In the framework of the QCA for the regular solutions [27], the use of the ordinary partition function gives

$$
\mu_{A}^{0}=-k_{B} T \cdot \ln q_{A}(T)+Z \varepsilon_{A A} / 2, \tag{17a}
$$

$$
\mu_{B}^{0}=-k_{B} T \cdot \ln q_{B}(T)+Z \varepsilon_{B B} / 2. \tag{17b}
$$

Combining equations (16) and (17) and taking into account equations (9) and (15), the ratio of the activity coefficients $\gamma=\gamma_{A} /$ $\gamma_{B}$ can be obtained in the form

$$
\gamma=\left\{\frac{(1-C)}{C} \cdot \frac{(\beta+2 C-1)}{(\beta-2 C+1)}\right\}^{Z / 2}. \tag{18}
$$

For regular alloys equation (18) is the solution of the grand partition function defined by equation (1). The activity coefficients of pure components can be derived from the standard thermodynamic relation for the excess Gibbs free energy

$$
G_{M}^{\mathrm{XS}}=G_{M}-N_{0} k_{B} T\{C \ln (C)+(1-C) \ln (1-C)\}, \tag{19}
$$

where $N_{0}$ is Avogadro's number. It has been shown [26,47] that equations (19) and (18) are related as

$$
\frac{G_{M}^{\mathrm{XS}}}{N_{0} k_{B} T}=\int_{0}^{C} \ln (\gamma) d C. \tag{20}
$$

After integration one obtains

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{G_{M}^{\mathrm{XS}}}{N_{0} k_{B} T}= & C \cdot \ln \left\{\frac{\beta-1+2 C}{C(\beta+1)}\right\}^{Z / 2}+(1-C) \\
& \cdot \ln \left\{\frac{\beta+1-2 C}{(1-C)(\beta+1)}\right\}^{Z / 2}, \tag{21}
\end{aligned}
$$

that depends only on the interaction energy $\omega$ (as a part of $\beta$, equation (14)) and the coordination number $Z$, without adjustable coefficients, that usually are used in other quasi-chemical methods

[39]. Equation (21) corresponds to the standard thermodynamic relation for the excess Gibbs energy of mixing, $G_{M}^{\text{xs}}$, expressed by
$$
G_{M}^{\text{xs}}=R T\left\{C \ln \gamma_{A}+(1-C) \ln \gamma_{B}\right\}, \tag{22}
$$
and thus, the activity coefficients are given as
$$
\gamma_{A}=\left\{\frac{\beta-1+2 C}{C(1+\beta)}\right\}^{Z / 2}, \tag{23}
$$

$$
\gamma_{B}=\left\{\frac{\beta+1-2 C}{(1-C)(1+\beta)}\right\}^{Z / 2} \tag{24}
$$

The QCA for the regular solutions describes quite well the mixing behaviour of liquid binary alloys for which neither the complex formation takes place nor the phase separation occurs. Regular alloys usually mean symmetry of curves describing their mixing properties and microscopic functions around the equiatomic composition, their constituent atoms have similar size and crystal structure. Otherwise, the model can be used as the first approximation to check the mixing properties of binary melts.

In the framework of the QCA, the connection between the bulk and the surface properties has been established through the grand partition functions [25]. Once the grand partition function for the bulk is solved, it is possible to express the grand partition function for the surface phase, $\Xi^{s}$, in the same form as that given by equation (1), in which the subscript and superscript $s$ refer to the corresponding quantities of the surface phase. The grand partition function for the surface, $\Xi^{s}$, and the surface tension, $\sigma$, are directly related as follows,
$$
\Xi^{s}=\exp \left(-\frac{\sigma S}{k_{B} T}\right)=\exp \left(-\frac{\sigma N^{s} \alpha}{k_{B} T}\right), \tag{25}
$$
where $S$ is the surface area and $\alpha(=S / N^{s})$ is the mean surface area of an alloy [57]. The interface between the bulk liquid and its equilibrium vapour is reduced to a monolayer [25,28] and the order energy, $\omega$, is taken as an invariant property of the system. Under the hypothesis that the bulk and surface phases are in thermodynamic equilibrium $(\mu_{i}=\mu_{i}^{s})$, the surface tension for regular alloys can be obtained by
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma= & \sigma_{A}+\frac{k_{B} T(2-p Z)}{2 \alpha} \ln \frac{C^{s}}{C} \\
& +\frac{Z k_{B} T}{2 \alpha}\left\{p \ln \frac{\left(\beta^{s}-1+2 C^{s}\right)(1+\beta)}{(\beta-1+2 C)\left(1+\beta^{s}\right)}-q \ln \frac{(\beta-1+2 C)}{(1+\beta) C}\right\},
\end{aligned} \tag{26a}
$$
or
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma= & \sigma_{B}+\frac{k_{B} T(2-p Z)}{2 \alpha} \ln \frac{\left(1-C^{s}\right)}{(1-C)} \\
& +\frac{Z k_{B} T}{2 \alpha}\left\{p \ln \frac{\left(\beta^{s}+1-2 C^{s}\right)(1+\beta)}{(\beta+1-2 C)\left(1+\beta^{s}\right)}-q \ln \frac{(\beta+1-2 C)}{(1+\beta)(1-C)}\right\},
\end{aligned} \tag{26b}
$$
where $\beta^{s}$ is the function obtained by equation (14) substituting the bulk concentration, $C$, by the surface concentration, $C^{s}$ and $\sigma_{i}$, $(i=A, B)$ are the surface tensions of pure components. $p$ and $q$ are the surface coordination fractions defined as the fractions of the total number of nearest neighbours of an atom in its own layer and that in the adjoining layer. Consider an atom in the surface layer and let the number of its neighbours in this layer be $p \cdot Z$, while the number of its neighbours in the next layer below be $q \cdot Z$, its total number of neighbours being thus $(p+q) \cdot Z$. It is clear that the corresponding total number of neighbours of an interior atom will be $(p+2 q) \cdot Z$, but by definition this number is $Z$. As a consequence, $p+2 q=1$. In a simple cubic lattice, $Z=6$, $p=2 / 3$ and $q=1 / 3$, while in a close packed lattice $Z=12$, $p=1 / 2$, $q=1 / 4$ [25].

Taking into account that the coordination numbers of Ni (fcc), Co (hcp) and Cr (bcc) are 12, 12 and 8, respectively, in the present study the values of $p$ and $q$ parameters are taken as $1 / 2$ and $1 / 4$, respectively.

### 2.2. Microscopic functions

The Bhatia-Thornton [36] formalism The Bhatia-Thornton of fluctuation structure factors over the whole concentration range that is not easy accessible for the diffraction experiments [43,58]. The first microscopic function, i.e. the concentration fluctuations in the long wavelength limit, $S_{cc}(0)$, visualises the nature of mixing in liquid alloys indicating chemical order and segregation, the characteristics of compound forming and segregating alloys, respectively [27]. It is also directly related to the thermophysical properties [59]. $S_{cc}(0)$ can be expressed by the Gibbs energy of mixing, $G_{M}$, or by the activity, $a_{i}(i=A, B)$, as
$$
S_{c c}(0)=R T\left(\frac{\partial^{2} G_{M}}{\partial C_{A}^{2}}\right)_{T, P, N}^{-1}=C_{B} a_{A}\left(\frac{\partial a_{A}}{\partial C_{A}}\right)_{T, P, N}^{-1}=C_{A} a_{B}\left(\frac{\partial a_{B}}{\partial C_{B}}\right)_{T, P, N}^{-1}, \tag{27}
$$

For ideal mixing the energy parameters describing $\omega$, given in equation (12) are equal to zero, and equation (27) becomes
$$
S_{c c}(0, \mathrm{id})=C(1-C). \tag{28}
$$

The mixing behaviour of liquid binary alloys can be deduced from the deviation of $S_{cc}(0)$ from $S_{cc}(0, \mathrm{id})$. The presence of chemical order is indicated by $S_{cc}(0)<S_{cc}(0, \mathrm{id})$; on the contrary, if $S_{cc}(0)>-S_{cc}(0, \mathrm{id})$, the segregation and demixing in liquid alloys take place. The degree of order and segregation in the melt can be quantified [37,38] by the second microscopic function, the Warren-Cowley short-range order parameter, $\alpha_{1}$. In the framework of QLT, the $\alpha_{1}$ and $S_{cc}(0)$ are related to each other through the $\beta$-function (equation (14)) as
$$
\alpha_{1}=\frac{\beta-1}{\beta+1}. \tag{29}
$$

The parameter $\alpha_{1}$ takes the values between -1 and 1 ; the negative values indicate the ordering in the melt, and complete ordering is manifested by $\alpha_{1}^{\min }=-1$. On the contrary, positive values of $\alpha_{1}$ indicate segregation, whereas the phase separation takes place if $\alpha_{1}^{\max }=1$.

### 2.3. Butler's equation

Butler's equation has been extensively used to calculate the surface tension of binary, ternary and multicomponent melts including alloy systems [24,60-65]. In this model, the surface is considered as an additional thermodynamic phase, in equilibrium with the bulk. Assuming the regular solution model, the surface tension of multicomponent liquid alloys, can be calculated by Butler's equation as
$$
\sigma=\sigma_{i}+\frac{R T}{S_{i}} \ln \frac{X_{i}^{s}}{X_{i}^{b}}+\frac{1}{S_{i}}\left\{G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, s}\left(T, X_{j}^{s}\right)-G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, b}\left(T, X_{j}^{b}\right)\right\}, \tag{30}
$$
where $R, T, \sigma_{i}, S_{i}$ are gas constant, temperature, surface tension of pure component $i$, and surface area of component $i$, respectively. The surface area of component $i$ is calculated from Avogadro's number $N_{0}$, the atomic mass $M_{i}$ and the density data $\rho_{i}$, as
$$
S_{i}=1.091 \cdot N_{0}\left(M_{i} / \rho_{i}\right)^{2 / 3}, \tag{31}
$$
$X_{j}^{k}$ denotes an alloy composition where the subscript $j$ and superscript $k$ refer to the corresponding component $j$ in the bulk $b$, or in the surface phase $s$. $G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, b}\left(T, X_{j}^{b}\right)$ and $G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, s}\left(T, X_{j}^{s}\right)$ are partial excess Gibbs energies of component $i$ in the bulk phase and the surface

phase, respectively, both as functions of temperature and composition.

The excess energy term of component $i$ can be derived from standard thermodynamic relationships, in the form

$$
G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}}=G^{\mathrm{xs}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\delta_{i j}-X_{j}\right) \frac{\partial G^{\mathrm{xs}}}{\partial X_{j}},
\tag{32}
$$

where $\delta_{i j}$ is Kronecker's symbol.

Assuming that the free energy of an alloy is always proportional to the number of interactive contacts between neighbouring atoms, $G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, s}$ and $G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, b}$ are related to the respective coordination numbers in the surface layer and the bulk phase as

$$
G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, s}\left(T, X_{j}^{s}\right)=\widetilde{\beta} G_{i}^{\mathrm{xs}, b}\left(T, X_{j}^{s}\right),
\tag{33}
$$

where $\widetilde{\beta}$ is the ratio between the two coordination numbers, *i.e.* a parameter describing the reduced coordination at the surface phase. In typical close-packed solid structures (fcc, hcp) the coordination numbers are 12 and 9 for the bulk and the surface phases, respectively, and thus $\widetilde{\beta}=0.75$. However, in some cases the value of $\widetilde{\beta}$ might be affected by other factors, such as the relaxation of the surface structure, and its values may vary from 0.5 to 0.84 [61]. As it has already been mentioned above, in relation to equation (26) and close-packed solid structures of Co and Ni, for the present calculations $\widetilde{\beta}$ was taken as 0.75.

The excess Gibbs energies of the binary subsystems of the Co-Cr-Ni system are given in the form of Redlich-Kister polynomials, as follows

$$
{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{L}^{A-B}=X_{i} \cdot X_{j} \cdot \sum_{v=0}^{n}\left(A_{v}+B_{v} \cdot T / \mathrm{K}\right) \cdot\left(X_{i}-X_{j}\right)^{v},
\tag{34}
$$

Owing to a lack of experimental data, the excess Gibbs energies of ternary systems can be calculated by combining the corresponding binary values, without any additional ternary contribution, $x_{1} x_{2} x_{3} L_{123}$, as

$$
{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{L}^{A-B-C}=\sum_{i, j} x_{i} x_{j} \cdot \sum_{v=0}^{n}\left(A_{v}+B_{v} \cdot T / \mathrm{K}\right) \cdot\left(x_{i}-x_{j}\right)^{v},
\tag{35}
$$

Equation (35) is known as the Redlich-Kister-Muggianu model and it assumes that the ternary interactions in the liquid phase are small in comparison to those which arise from the binary terms [39].

### 2.4. Chou's model

Having in mind the lack of thermodynamic experimental data for the Co-Cr-Ni liquid phase, Chou's general solution model [66] may be an easy and convenient tool for estimation of those data.

The basic equations of the general solution model for a ternary system are given as follows

$$
\begin{aligned}
{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{L}^{A-B-C}= & \frac{X_{1} X_{2}}{X_{1(12)} X_{2(12)}} \cdot{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{12}\left(X_{1(12)}, X_{2(12)}\right)+\frac{X_{2} X_{3}}{X_{2(23)} X_{3(23)}} \\
& \cdot{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{23}\left(X_{2(23)}, X_{3(23)}\right)+\frac{X_{3} X_{1}}{X_{3(31)} X_{1(31)}} \\
& \cdot{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{31}\left(X_{3(31)}, X_{1(31)}\right),
\end{aligned}
\tag{36}
$$

where $X_{i}$ is the mole fraction of component $i$ in the ternary system, and $X_{i(i j)}$ is the mole fraction of component $i$ in the $i-j$ binary system, which can be calculated by

$$
\begin{aligned}
& X_{1(12)}=X_{1}+\frac{\eta_{1}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}} X_{3}, \\
& X_{2(23)}=X_{2}+\frac{\eta_{2}}{\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}} X_{1} \\
& X_{3(31)}=X_{3}+\frac{\eta_{3}}{\eta_{3}+\eta_{1}} X_{2},
\end{aligned}
\tag{37}
$$

where $\eta_{i} / \eta_{i}+\eta_{j}$ is the similarity coefficient. The parameters $\eta_{i}$ in equation (37) take into account the correlation with other components $(j$ and $k)$ of the ternary system

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \eta_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}\left({ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{12}-{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{13}\right)^{2} d X_{1}, \\
& \eta_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\left({ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{21}-{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{23}\right)^{2} d X_{2}, \\
& \eta_{3}=\int_{0}^{1}\left({ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{31}-{ }^{\mathrm{xs}} G_{32}\right)^{2} d X_{3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{38}
$$

## 3. Results and discussion

Thermodynamic and thermophysical properties of Co-Ni and Cr-Ni liquid alloys have been investigated in [54,56], respectively, while in the case of the Co-Cr, the aforementioned properties have not yet been reported. Accordingly, the properties of liquid Co-Cr alloys will be described in detail. In order to make comparison with literature data, in the present work the calculations were carried out for $T=1873 \mathrm{~K}$. After all calculations for the binaries have been completed, the data obtained were used to calculate the thermodynamic and the surface properties of the Co-Cr-Ni liquid phase.

### 3.1. Thermodynamic properties of Co-Ni, Cr-Ni and Co-Cr liquid alloys

The Co-Cr and Cr-Ni phase diagrams indicate that at $T=1873 \mathrm{~K}$ the corresponding liquid phases exist up to (69 and 75) at.\% of $\mathrm{Cr}$, respectively, while the Co-Ni system is in the liquid state over the whole concentration range [19]. Thermodynamic quantities of mixing suggest different types of interactions in Co-Ni [54] and Co-Cr [56] liquid alloys. Similar binary interactions are observed in Cr-Ni and Co-Cr melts. Indeed, both systems exhibit compound forming tendency and their thermodynamic properties exhibit negative deviations from Raoult's law. On the contrary, the mixing behaviour of liquid Co-Ni alloys is close to the ideal, but very small positive deviations of their mixing properties from ideality indicate repulsive interactions in alloy melts and thereby support weak demixing tendency in the Co-Ni system [54]

For each system the order energy $\omega$, and its parameters $a$ and $b$ (equation (12)) have been calculated from the corresponding thermodynamic data and are given in the form

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{Co}-\mathrm{Ni}} / R T=1.63 \cdot 10^{-1}-4.14 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot T,
\tag{39}
$$

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{Cr}-\mathrm{Ni}} / R T=-8.26 \cdot 10^{-1}-9.45 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot T,
\tag{40}
$$

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{Co}-\mathrm{Cr}} / R T=-1.18 \cdot 10^{0}+3.64 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot T.
\tag{41}
$$

Assuming the energetic term as an invariant of the system [59], the order energies, $\omega$, of Co-Ni, Cr-Ni and Co-Cr liquid alloys have been calculated at the equiatomic composition and their values in RT-units, are 0.0856, -0.843 and -0.499, respectively.

Concerning the Co-Cr system, the optimised data set of the excess Gibbs energy of mixing, $\alpha_{1}$, of liquid Co-Cr alloys [13,67,68] and the activity data [12,69,70] have been used to calculate unknown interaction energy parameters, $a$ and $b$ (equation (41)) by means of the first approximation of the regular solution (QCA) [27]. In all calculations the coordination number, $Z$, for the liquid phase was taken as 10 [25].

### 3.3. Microscopic functions: concentration fluctuations in the long-wavelength limit and chemical short-range order parameter

The ordering phenomena in the three binary systems have been analysed in terms of the concentration fluctuations in the

long-wavelength limit, $S_{cc}(0)$ and the Warren-Cowley short-range order parameter, $\alpha_{1}$, as functions of bulk composition. In order to obtain the information on the structure of Cr-Ni, Co-Ni and Co-Cr alloy melts, in terms of the short range order phenomena, the nature of mixing and the degree of order in these liquid alloys have been analysed in the framework of the QCA for the regular solutions (equations (27)-(29)) for $T=1873$ K. $S_{cc}(0)$ values for the Cr-Ni and Co-Cr systems indicate that $S_{cc}(0) \leq S_{cc}(0$,id) over the whole concentration range and together with negative values of the Warren-Cowley short-range order parameter, $\alpha_{1}$ clearly describe both systems as compound forming, as can be seen from figure 4b and c. The maximum deviation between functions of concentration fluctuations in the long-wavelength limit, $|S_{cc}(0)-S_{cc}(0$,id$)|$, is used as an indicator of the concentration at which the compound formation may take place [27,36]. The calculations of microscopic functions, $S_{cc}(0)$ and $\alpha_{1}$, for liquid Cr-Ni and Co-Cr alloys reproduce fairly well the positions of the energetically favoured compounds, the CrNi₂, and $\sigma$-intermediate phase, respectively. This observation can be further substantiated by the negative values of the Warren-Cowley parameter, $\alpha_{1}$, over the whole concentration range, as is shown in figure 4b and c. This implies a tendency towards clustering and indicates a weakly pronounced heterocoordination, i.e. unlike Ni-Cr or Co-Cr atoms tend to pair as nearest neighbours.

The $S_{cc}(0)$ values for the Co-Ni system calculated for $T=1873$ K clearly indicate that $S_{cc}(0) \geqslant S_{cc}(0$,id) in the whole concentration range, as it is shown in figure 4a. The $S_{cc}(0)$ and $\alpha_{1}$ curves exhibit the maximum values of about 0.2612 and 0.004292 at $C=0.5$, respectively. This implies a tendency of this system towards ideal behaviour and indicates a weakly pronounced homocoordination, i.e. like atoms Co-Co and Ni-Ni tend to pair as nearest neighbours. $S_{cc}(0)$ values that are close to $S_{cc}(0$,id) and small positive values of the Warren-Cowley short range order parameter, $\alpha_{1}$, classify Co-Ni liquid alloys as weakly segregating [59].

### 3.5. Surface properties of liquid Co-Cr-Ni alloys

Combining the corresponding descriptions of the three binary subsystems given in table 1, the surface tension of liquid Co-Cr-Ni ternary alloys has been calculated for $T = 1873$ K applying the Butler model in the regular solution approximation (equations (30)-(35)). The excess Gibbs energy terms of the Co-Ni, Cr-Ni and Co-Cr liquid binary phases were taken from [13,16,17], while the reference data for the pure components, such as the melting temperatures, densities and molar volumes were taken from [41]. In order to reduce the overall error in the calculated surface tension values, the chosen surface tension data of liquid cobalt [72], chromium [73] and nickel [74] were obtained by the same experimental method, i.e. the sessile drop method [71]. The input data used for the calculations of the surface tension of the ternary and its binary subsystems are given in table 1. It is important to mention that in the case of weakly interacting binary systems, such as the Bi-Pb, Bi-Sn, Sn-Pb [76], or the Co-Ni [54], Cr-Ni [56] and Co-Cr, the difference between the surface tension values, obtained by the QCA for regular solutions equations (26a) and (26b) and the corresponding values calculated by the Butler model in the regular solution approximation (equation (30)) is very small. Indeed, for the present results it can be estimated to be below 0.5% on average.

## 4. Conclusions

The mixing behaviour of liquid Co-Cr, Cr-Ni and Co-Ni alloys has been analysed by the QCA for regular solutions. The thermodynamic functions of mixing and the thermophysical properties as well as the microscopic functions classify the Co-Cr and the Cr-Ni as weakly interacting compound forming systems, while the Co-Ni exhibits very weak tendency for demixing. The results obtained for these binary systems have been extended to study the thermodynamics and surface properties of ternary Co-Cr-Ni liquid alloys. To calculate the excess Gibbs free energies of mixing of the liquid Co-Cr-Ni phase, the Redlich-Kister-Muggianu, the Redlich-Kister-Chou and the QCA for regular solutions have been used. The

results obtained by the three models indicate exothermic mixing behaviour of these ternary alloys, as already observed in the case of the binaries Co–Cr and Cr–Ni, suggesting a weak tendency towards complex formation in Co–Cr–Ni melts. The binary descriptions were combined with the reference data for the pure components (the melting temperatures, densities or molar volumes and surface tension) to predict the surface properties of the ternary alloys in the framework of Butler's model. With exception of Co–Ni based alloys with low chromium content, the present study reveals the compound forming tendency of liquid Co–Cr–Ni alloys. The experimental data are needed to compare the results obtained.
