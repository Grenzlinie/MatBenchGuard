# NEW MODELLING OF THE B2 PHASE AND ITS ASSOCIATED MARTENSITIC TRANSFORMATION IN THE Ti-Ni SYSTEM

W. TANG†¹, B. SUNDMAN¹, R. SANDSTRÖM¹ and C. QIU‡²

¹Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Royal Institute of Technology, S-100 44 Stockholm, Sweden and ²Institute of Materials Science, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT 06269, U.S.A.

(Received 24 March 1999; accepted 14 June 1999)

Abstract-A symmetric two-sublattice model (Ni, Ti, Va)₀.₅(Ni, Ti, Va)₀.₅ is applied to describe the intermediate B2 compound in order to cope with the order-disorder transition in the Ti-Ni system. Using this model, the ordered B2 and the disordered Ti-rich b.c.c. are described by a single Gibbs free energy function. The B2 phase is the parent phase of the martensitic transformation in the TiNi shape memory alloys (SMAs), and its thermodynamic properties are then reassessed with emphasis on its composition range that is critical for SMAs. The low temperature B19' phase is also evaluated on the basis of the selected experimental data from the martensitic transformation. Properties related to the transformation are studied in comparison with experimental data. The magnetic contribution is examined for the martensitic transformation. All calculations are in satisfactory agreement with experimental phenomena. © 1999 Acta Metallurgica Inc. Published by Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Titanium-nickel; Shape memory; Thermodynamics; Phase diagrams; Phase equilibria

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The Ti-Ni system has been of great interest mainly due to the unique shape memory effect (SME) and the pseudoelasticity (PE) which occur in the nearequiatomic TiNi alloys. In comparison with other shape memory alloys (SMAs), the TiNi SMAs provide the best combination of material properties, e.g. the maximum recoverable strain and stress, excellent corrosion resistance and the best biocompatibility. They are the most widely used SMAs in the world. The unique SME and PE originate from the so-called thermoelastic martensitic transformation. In the Ti-Ni system, it is the intermetallic compound B2 phase that can undergo a thermoelastic martensitic transformation in the composition range around 50 at.% Ni. The phase diagram and thermodynamic properties of the B2 phase and the associated martensitic transformation product phase B19' are essential not only for understanding the kinetics of the transformation but also for further development of SMAs.

However, the ordinary phase diagram does not include the low temperature phase since it is usually believed that the martensitic transformation product phase is metastable. In the present case, nevertheless, the parent phase B2 is stable down to low temperatures [1], and hence fast cooling is not necessary for preventing its decomposition. Furthermore, it is known that the martensitic transformation takes place regardless of the cooling rate in the Ti-Ni system [2, 3]. So we consider that the B19' phase could be stable. Therefore, our aim was to establish thermodynamic models to describe the parent phase B2 and the low temperature phase B19' and then study properties related to the transformation, such as the $T_0$ temperature, the $M_s$ temperature, and the transformation enthalpy.

The B2 phase with CsCl crystal structure in the Ti-Ni system has been modelled thermodynamically several times [4-8]. Among the previous works Saunders [7] applied the two-sublattice model with the formula (Ni, Va)₀.₅(Ni, Ti)₀.₅ to describe it. This model suggests the major structure imperfections are vacancy defects on the nickel sublattice while the main defects on the titanium sublattice are substitutional Ni atoms. This is in agreement with experimental observation [2]. In fact, this model is a good approximation of reality, but the two sublattices are identical so one should have the same constituents on both. This requirement must also be fulfilled in order to describe the disordering of the B2 phase to the A2 (b.c.c.) phase. In the Ti-Ni system, there is a Ti-rich A2 phase which is the disordered form of the B2 phase and the B2 phase can be considered as an ordered state of the A2 phase. From the physical point of view, A2 and B2 should be described with a single Gibbs free energy ex-

## 2. THERMODYNAMIC MODELLING

### 2.1. B2 and A2 phases

Most SMAs have a superlattice structure with the sublattices of the parent phase being body-centred cubic (b.c.c.), which are called $\beta$-phase alloys. The $\beta$-phase alloys are generally divided into two groups according to the superlattice or composition ratio. One group is the $\beta 2$-phase, which has a CsCl-type B2 superlattice and about 50:50 composition ratio. The parent phase in TiNi SMA belongs to this type of superstructure as shown in Fig. 1 [11]: the Ni atoms fill the corners while the Ti atoms are in the body centre. The formula $(Ni)_{0.5}(Ti)_{0.5}$ represents the completely ordered B2 structure. However, the B2 phase in the Ti-Ni system has a certain compo- sition range. Depending on the defects appearing on the sublattices, there are two types of two-sub- lattice formalisms to model the homogeneity range of the B2 compound. When there are substitutional defects on both sublattices, the formula (A, B)(A, B) is used. If vacancy defects appear in one sublattice and substitutional defects on the other one, the formula (A, Va)(A, B) is applied. According to experimental information mentioned in Section 1, the TiNi B2 phase is the second type and thus Saunders [7] adopted the model $(Ni, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti)_{0.5}$. On the other hand, the B2 phase is an ordered b.c.c. phase, while the terminal Ti-rich phase as shown in the Ti-Ni phase diagram (see Fig. 2) is the disordered b.c.c. (A2) phase. Then one will be able to use a single Gibbs energy function to describe the two phases. Obviously, the model $(Ni, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti)_{0.5}$ cannot reflect the order-disorder transition. Although Bellen *et al.* [8] used a single Gibbs energy function accounting for the two phases, the formalism $(Ni, Ti)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti)_{0.5}(Va)_{3}$ used by them is not proper for the TiNi B2 phase since it assumes that only substitutional defects but no vacancy defects appear on sublattices which is in contrast to experiments.

In the present work, a new model with the for- mula $(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}$ is introduced in line with the treatment of the B2 phase in the Al-Ni system [12]. This symmetric model can describe the phase from perfect ordering state to complete disordering. When the alloy has stoichiometric com- position with complete ordering, the fractions of Ti and Va in the first sublattice will go to zero and Ni and Va in the second will also vanish. Then it becomes a linear compound $(Ni)_{0.5}(Ti)_{0.5}$. When the alloy is in a disordered state, the site fractions of all constituents in both sublattices will be the same and equal to their mole fractions. In this case the model $(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}$ simply becomes (Ni, Ti, Va) that can be used to treat the A2 phase (i.e. disordered B2). For TiNi SMA with compo- sition around the equiatomic range, the site frac- tions of Ti in the first lattice $(y_{Ti}^{a})$ and Va in the second lattice $(y_{Ti}^{b})$ are usually too small; thus $(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}$ turns into $(Ni, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti)_{0.5}$ that is used by Saunders [7]. The relations among these formalisms are schemati- cally demonstrated in Fig. 3.

## 3. SELECTION OF EXPERIMENTAL DATA AND MODEL PARAMETER EVALUATION

In the thermodynamic models described in the preceding section, there are several model parameters to be determined in the present work. These parameters are evaluated using a computer optimization program available in Thermo-Calc [13], which will search for the best fit to the selected experimental data or estimated quantities. The program can treat different kinds of input data simultaneously by minimizing the sum of squares of the differences between the target and the calculated values. Each piece of input data is given a certain weighting factor and it may be changed by trial and error during optimization until all the input information is accounted for within the expected uncertainty.

### 3.1. B2 phase

Phase diagram data concerning the B2 phase in the Ti–Ni system have been used in the previous assessment [7], but some data are to be specially addressed in the present optimization. First of all, the composition range of B2 is very important to the SMA properties. Several experiments reported that the maximum solubility of Ti in B2 is about 50.5 at.% in the temperature range from 1200 to 1500 K [2, 9]. However, the previous calculation [7] gives the value around 51.5 at.% Ti at 1280 K. A difference of 1 at.% may not be so important for other alloy systems but is critical for SMAs since the SMA properties strongly depend on the composition. For instance a difference of 1 at.% in composition, or of $100^\circ\text{C}$ in the anneal temperature, can cause a dramatic change in the behaviour of the SMA. Secondly, it is reported that the B2 phase through long time ageing at 773 K has almost the stoichiometric composition and its martensitic transition temperature is essentially independent of the initial alloy composition [2]. This indicates that the composition range of B2 becomes very narrow at and below 773 K, and may not be larger than 0.2–0.3 at.%. The previous calculation [7] shows a much wider range than this, which makes it difficult to account for the experimental phenomena. In addition, the previous evaluation for the congruent melting temperature of B2 is 1605 K; that is higher than the experimental data which range from 1513 to 1583 K [22]. All these will be taken into account in the new evaluation.

### 3.2. B19' phase

There is no experimental information on proper phase equilibria with the low temperature B19' phase. Instead, information on the martensitic transformation is used. Therefore, the selection of experimental information is vital to the evaluation of the B19' phase. One should choose properties that depend only upon chemical composition. The $T_0$ temperature, defined as the temperature at which B2 and B19' have the same Gibbs free energy, is a common thermodynamic quantity used for model parameter evaluation of the martensitic transformation product phase [23–25]. However, $T_0$ cannot be directly measured. On the other hand, the martensitic transformation start and finish temperatures $(M_{\text{s}}, M_{\text{f}})$ and the reverse transformation start and finish temperatures $(A_{\text{s}}, A_{\text{f}})$ can be measured by means of several approaches. Previously, we discussed at some length how to interpret $T_0$ by the martensitic transformation temperatures [10].

For a non-thermoelastic martensitic transformation, there is no doubt that $T_0$ can be expressed by $(M_{\text{s}} + A_{\text{s}})/2$ since the stored elastic strain energy can be neglected in this case and both the martensitic transformation and its reverse require separate nucleation to start [26]. For the thermoelastic transformation, it is common that $T_0$ is expressed by $(M_{\text{s}} + A_{\text{f}})/2$ [27, 28]. However, Olson and Cohen [29, 30] have shown that it is possible that $T_0 > A_{\text{f}}$. In their treatment, the elastic energy is present even at $M_{\text{s}}$ and $A_{\text{f}}$, and the frictional resistance is negligible. In such an ideally thermoelastic transformation, the first martensite not only nucleates below $T_0$ on cooling but also it reverts below $T_0$ on heating, thus resulting in $T_0 > A_{\text{f}}$. When the frictional resistance is large enough, it can raise $A_{\text{f}}$ above $T_0$, but the hysteresis loop may still not be symmetric about $T_0$. Later, Salzbrenner and Cohen [31] concluded that $T_0 = (M_{\text{s}} + A_{\text{f}})/2$ should be valid only for single crystals; for polycrystals, $T_0$ should be higher than $(M_{\text{s}} + A_{\text{f}})/2$. Recently, the work by Smith *et al.* [32] indicated that $T_0$ was very close to $A_{\text{f}}$ rather than $(M_{\text{s}} + A_{\text{f}})/2$. Consequently, as a first approximation, we took $A_{\text{f}}$ instead of $(M_{\text{s}} + A_{\text{f}})/2$ as the $T_0$ for the parameter evaluation.

The same experimental information shown in Table 1 was used for model parameter evaluation as in the previous work. These data were selected from a large number of experiments on the marten-sitic transformation temperatures ($M_s$, $M_f$, $A_s$ and $A_f$) and transformation enthalpy ($\Delta H$) of TiNi SMA. The reported experimental data differ signifi-cantly from each other, which makes data selection difficult. One critical issue for getting reliable data is to make sure that the reported composition is as close to the true composition of the TiNi matrix as possible. Usually the literature only provides the overall composition. In order to choose the right data, the following principles should be obeyed, as pointed out in our previous paper. (i) Off-stoichio-metric TiNi alloys should be quenched after high temperature solution treatment to ensure that the metastable parent phase maintains the same compo-sition as the high temperature phase without any precipitation. (ii) Interstitial atoms like O, N and C should be kept as low as possible, e.g. O<150 p.p.m. (iii) Wasilewski et al. [2] found that under very slow cooling the martensitic transformation takes place at about 333 K independent of composition in a range close to 50 at.% Ni. Thus, the $M_s$ of Ni-rich alloys should be lower than 333 K and that of Ti-rich alloys should be higher than 333 K.

The experimental information shown in Table 1 for the martensitic phase is mainly at lower tem-peratures. Optimization indicated that such infor-mation is not sufficient to determine all the quantities $^0G^{B19'}_{ij}$ in equations (9)–(12) and interaction parameters $L^{B19'}_{Ni:Ni,Ti}$ and $L^{B19'}_{Ni:Va,Ti}$, especially the temperature dependence. Then the constant $a_1$ in equation (9) is set to zero. A large positive value of 50 000 J/mol is assigned to $a_3$ in equation (11) after trial and error in order to describe b.c.c.(Ni) more stable relative to B19'(Ni). However, the par-ameter $a_4$ in equation (12) cannot be simply set to a value; it is the critical parameter to determine the shape of the B2/B19' metastable phase diagram after the magnetic parameters are added to the dis-ordered B2 phase. Its estimation will be discussed in the next section. In addition, the temperature dependence of $L^{B19'}_{Ni:Ni,Ti}$ and $L^{B19'}_{Ni:Va,Ti}$ was set to the same values as the previous ones which follows Saunders' description for the B2 phase.

Having made the above simplification treatments, the remaining parameters were evaluated during the optimization based on the selected experimental in-formation. All the parameters evaluated in this work are presented in the Appendix; they are used to compute the Ti–Ni phase diagram including the B19' phase, $T_0$ temperature, and other thermody-namic properties.

### 4.2. The B2/B19' equilibrium and the $T_{0}$ temperature

The equilibrium between B2 and B19' was calculated as shown in Fig. 11. The Ni-rich part of this diagram is similar to the previous calculation [10], while the Ti-rich part has a significant change. The B19' phase appears as an island with the two-phase region B2+B19' on each side. Obviously this is due to the symmetry in the model used for the B2 phase. The dotted line represents the $T_{0}$ temperature obtained under the condition that B2 and B19' have the same Gibbs energy under the same composition. It indicates the possible composition range of the metastable B19' which can be obtained by non-equilibrium cooling to temperatures below $T_{0}$. From Fig. 11 it is seen that $T_{0}$ changes greatly with composition. The strong dependency of $T_{0}$ on composition gives a high sensitivity of SMA properties to a variation in composition, especially in Ni-rich alloys. In contrast to the previous result that $T_{0}$ increases with increasing Ti content continuously, Fig. 11 indicates that $T_{0}$ increases with increasing Ti first and then decreases when Ti exceeds 55 at.%. It suggests that the $T_{0}$ temperature has a maximum value in the binary TiNi system even when there is no limit for the solubility of Ti in the B2.

The determination of the parameter $a_{4}$ in equation (12) is related to this metastable phase diagram. According to Fig. 11, the B19' phase region should extend towards the Ti-rich side as far as possible to reach the maximum $T_{0}$ temperature. This requires the parameter $a_{4}$ in equation (12) to be as small as possible. On the other hand, it is also found that $a_{4}$ should be large enough in order to yield a reasonable phase relation between B2 and B19'. Through trial and error and by calculating the B2/B19' phase diagram, $a_{4}$ was finally determined to $22500 \, \text{J/mol}$, which enables a larger $T_{0}$ value while keeping the diagram in reasonable shape.

### 4.3. Martensitic transformation starting temperature $M_s$

The martensitic transformation would start at $T_0$ if there were no energy barriers for nucleation. In reality, there are always some non-chemical forces to resist the transformation. The non-chemical forces come from the reversible elastic strain energy ($\Delta G_{\text{el}}^{\text{P-M}}$) accommodating the transformational shape and volume changes, and from the irreversible frictional energy ($E_{\text{fr}}$) opposing the interfacial motion. Therefore, a driving force is needed to overcome these energy barriers. The driving force is a chemical contribution coming from the difference in Gibbs energy between the parent phase (denoted as P) and martensite (denoted as M), $\Delta G_{\text{ch}}^{\text{P-M}}$. When the driving force and the energy barriers reach equilibrium, the following relation holds:

$$
\Delta G_{\text{ch}}^{\text{P-M}} = \Delta G_{\text{el}}^{\text{P-M}} + E_{\text{fr}}. \tag{13}
$$

The transformation will start and the corresponding temperature is the so-called $M_s$ temperature. We need to estimate the driving force. Based on calorimetric experiments, Ortin and Planes [35, 36] have explicitly evaluated the relationship among the heat $Q$, the latent heat $\Delta H_{\text{ch}}$, the stored elastic enthalpy $\Delta H_{\text{el}}$, and the dissipated energy $E_{\text{fr}}$. According to their findings, for the forward transformation $\text{P} \rightarrow \text{M}$ (exothermal), part of the latent heat released ($-\Delta H_{\text{ch}}^{\text{P-M}}$) is stored as elastic enthalpy $\Delta H_{\text{el}}^{\text{P-M}}$ or spent as frictional work $E_{\text{fr}}^{\text{P-M}}$. Thus, the measured heat is given by

$$
-Q^{\text{P-M}} = -\Delta H_{\text{ch}}^{\text{P-M}} + \Delta H_{\text{el}}^{\text{P-M}} + E_{\text{fr}}^{\text{P-M}}. \tag{14}
$$

For the reverse transformation $\text{M} \rightarrow \text{P}$ (endothermal), the measured heat is as follows:

$$
Q^{\text{M-P}} = \Delta H_{\text{ch}}^{\text{M-P}} - \Delta H_{\text{el}}^{\text{M-P}} + E_{\text{fr}}^{\text{M-P}}. \tag{15}
$$

Smith *et al.* [32] have studied the temperature dependence of the molar heat capacity $C_p$ for a TiNi alloy by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and a stepwise enthalpy technique. By this stepwise enthalpy technique, the heat $Q$ and the latent heat $\Delta H_{\text{ch}}$ can be measured more exactly than through the conventional DSC method. According to their measurements, $Q^{\text{M-P}}$ is 1470 J/ mol, $Q^{\text{P-M}}$ is $-1350$ J/mol, and $\Delta H_{\text{ch}}$ is 1502 J/mol. By inputting these data into equations (14) and (15), one can get $E_{\text{fr}} = 60$ J/mol and $\Delta H_{\text{el}} = 92$ J/mol. Approximately, we let the driving force $\Delta G_{\text{ch}}^{\text{P-M}}$ be equal to $(\Delta H_{\text{el}}^{\text{P-M}} + E_{\text{fr}})$ that is about 150 J/mol.

Since the Gibbs energy functions of the parent and the B19$'$ phases are already known, the $M_s$ temperature can be calculated by setting $\Delta G_{\text{ch}}^{\text{P-M}}$ equal to 150 J/mol. On a tentative basis, we assume that the driving force is 150 J/mol regardless of the composition and internal structure. The predicted $M_s$ vs Ni content is plotted in Fig. 13 together with experimental observations. At the equiatomic composition, the calculated $M_s$ is 333 K that agrees very well with experiments such as Wasilewski *et al.*'s data $\sim 333$ K [2], and with our own data 329–333 K [37]. According to Fig. 13, when the Ni content is larger than 50 at.%, the predicted $M_s$ decreases dramatically when increasing the Ni content at a rate of around 164 K/at.% Ni, which is close to the slope of the recovery temperature reported by Eckelmeyer [38]. A difference from the previous result [10] is noticed in that the changing rate becomes greater when the temperature is below 200 K. This is due to the magnetic contribution that appears strongly at low temperature. The steeper slope at low temperature agrees well with experimental data, which can be taken as an indication that the estimation of the magnetic parameters $T_{\text{Ni,Ti}}$ and $\beta_{\text{Ni,Ti}}$ is suitable.

It is noticed that for $\text{Ni} > 50$ at.%, some experimental data are well above the predicted $M_s$ line. As we discussed before, the discrepancy could not be due to the selected driving force because even a zero driving force would not change the calculated line enough. The experimental enthalpy data from various sources are scattered with respect to Ni content. Considering that the reported composition may not be the real composition of the TiNi B2 but the overall one, we correct the $x_{\text{Ni}}$ value for each point by comparing the reported $M_s$ with the predicted $M_s$ in the light of the fact that $M_s$ is mainly determined by composition. The experimental enthalpy vs corrected $x_{\text{Ni}}$ is then plotted in Fig. 14. It indicates that the experimental data are better correlated to the calculation than before. It means that the transformation enthalpy is also mainly dependent on chemical composition although other effects like elastic strain energy and interfacial friction may exist. On the Ni-rich side, the absolute value of enthalpy decreases when the Ni content increases. At $x_{\text{Ni}} = 0.5$, the calculated chemical enthalpy is equal to 1672 J/mol that is in satisfactory agreement with our experimental data 1597–1710 J/mol [37].

$$

## 5. CONCLUSIONS

1. By taking into account the order-disorder transition between the ordered B2 phase and the disordered A2 phase in the Ti-Ni system, the symmetric model $(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti, Va)_{0.5}$ was applied to describe the B2 phase. Thermodynamic properties of the B2 phase have been reassessed and particular attention was paid to its composition range that is critical for SME. The present calculation gives a more precise description of the B2 composition range than the previous one [7]. The calculated melting point of B2 (1585 K) also agrees better with experimental data (1513-1583 K).

2. The low temperature B19' phase has been re-evaluated using the two-sublattice model $(Ni, Va)_{0.5}(Ni, Ti)_{0.5}$ using experimental information from the martensitic transformation. Since the B2 phase is described as the ordered counterpart of the paramagnetic A2 phase, a magnetic contribution to the Gibbs energy is present in the martensitic transformation. The magnetic effect was estimated by introducing the negative magnetic parameters $T_{Ni,Ti}$ and $\beta_{Ni,Ti}$, which yields a reasonable description of the transformation for Ni-rich alloys at low temperatures.

3. The calculated B2/B19' equilibrium shows that the $T_{0}$ temperature has a maximum value in the binary Ti-Ni system even if there is no limit for the solubility of Ti in the B2. The calculated $T_{0}$ line has similar shape to the previous calculation except that the $T_{0}$ line starts to bend off when Ni > 50.7 at.%.

4. The predicted $M_{s}$ at equiatomic composition is 333 K, which is in very good agreement with experimental data. The calculated $M_{s}$ vs $x_{Ni}$ curve exhibits a similar trend to the previous calculation but the present one fits the experimental data better when Ni > 50.7 at.%.

5. The calculated enthalpy shows a satisfactory correlation with experimental data after the composition is corrected on the basis of the predicted $M_{s}$ vs $x_{Ni}$ relation. The calculated stress rate is consistent with experimental data under the condition that alloys have no work hardening and/or ageing effects. When Ni > 50.7 at.%, the calculated stress rate shows better agreement with experiments compared to the previous calculation.
