# Thermodynamics of Solid $\text{Cu}-\text{Ni}$ Alloys by Knudsen Cell Mass Spectrometry and Re-Calculation of the Phase Diagram ${ }^{*}$

J. Tomiska and A. Neckel

Institut für Physikalische Chemie, Universität Wien, Austria

Copper-Nickel (solid) / Mass Spectrometry / Phase Diagram / Thermodynamics

Solid $\text{Cu}-\text{Ni}$ alloys have been investigated thermodynamically within the whole range of composition by Knudsen Cell Mass Spectrometry. The "Algebraic Intensity-Ratio"-method (A.I.R.) has been used for the determination of the thermodynamic mixing behavior. The thermodynamic activities $a_{j}$ show positive deviations from Raoult's law. The molar enthalpies of mixing $H^{\mathrm{E}}$ and the molar excess Gibbs energies $G^{\mathrm{E}}$ are positive over the whole range of composition. At 1350 K the maximum value of $H^{\mathrm{E}}$ is 2420 J/mol (59.48 at-$\%$ Ni), and the maximum value of $G^{\mathrm{E}}$ is 3220 J/mol (53 at-$\%$ Ni). The excess entropies $S^{\mathrm{E}}$ are negative over the whole range of composition with a minimum value of $-0.719 \mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{mol} \cdot \mathrm{K}$ at 36.71 at-$\%$ Ni. The phase diagram has been re-evaluated by using an analytical procedure which is based on the generalized Newton method for the numerical solution of equations. The calculations are based on the thermodynamic data obtained in this study, on the thermodynamic results from mass spectrometric studies on liquid $\text{Cu}-\text{Ni}$ alloys, and on literature data for the heat capacities and the heats of melting of $\text{Cu}$ and $\text{Ni}$.

## Introduction

Thermodynamic investigations on solid $\text{Cu}-\text{Ni}$ alloys are of particular interest, because the energetic effects caused by the filling of the 3d-shell in Nickel by alloying Copper to Nickel may influence the thermodynamic properties of the solid solutions of $\text{Cu}$ and $\text{Ni}$.

The effective radii of $\text{Cu}$ (0.1278 nm [1]) and of $\text{Ni}$ (0.1246 nm [1]) differ only slightly, and the $\text{Cu}-\text{Ni}$ equilibrium phase diagram shows in the solid state complete miscibility at high temperatures (Fig. 1). So, nearly ideal mixing behavior can be expected. However, the filling of the free d-shell of $\text{Ni}$ can cause anomalies in the composition dependence of the thermodynamic mixing functions [8,9].

## Experimental Method and Data Evaluation
Thermodynamic evaluation of mass spectrometric measurements can be based upon the relation between the activity coefficient $f_{j}$ of the component $j$ in the condensed phase and the ion-current-intensity $J_{j}^{k}$ produced by the isotope $k$ of the atomic species $j$ in the vapor phase of a Knudsen cell

$$
f_{j}=\frac{p_{j}}{x_{j} p_{j}^{0}}=\frac{J_{j}^{k}}{x_{j}} \tilde{B}(T)_{j} C^{-1}
$$

$x_{j} \quad=$ mole fraction of the component $j$ in the condensed phase;
$p_{j} \quad=$ partial vapor pressure of the component $j$ in the alloy;
$p_{j}^{0} \quad=$ vapor pressure of the pure component $j$;
$\tilde{B}(T)_{j}=$ temperature dependent factor containing the vapor pressure $p_{j}^{0}$ and mass spectrometric quantities;
$T \quad=$ absolute temperature [K];
$C \quad=$ sensitivity factor.

Using the thermodynamic relation between the activity coefficients of a binary system and the first derivative of the molar excess Gibbs energy $G^{\mathrm{E}}$ with respect to $x$, the mole fraction of the component 2 $\left(x=x_{2}\right)$, and substituting from Eq. (1) leads to Eq. (2):

$$
\frac{\partial G^{\mathrm{E}}}{\partial x}=R T \ln \frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}=R T \ln \frac{J_{2}}{J_{1}} \frac{(1-x)}{x}-B_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)
$$

$R$ = gas constant.
with

$$
B_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)=\tilde{B}(T)_{2} / \tilde{B}(T)_{1}. \quad(3)
$$

Through this procedure the troublesome sensitivity factor $C$ will be eliminated [20]. In the A.I.R.-method the molar thermodynamic excess functions $Z\left(Z=G^{\mathrm{E}}, H^{\mathrm{E}}, S^{\mathrm{E}}\right)$ are represented by means of a polynomial series, e. g. the Redlich-Kister expansion [21]

$$
Z=x(1-x) \sum_{n=1}^{N} B_{n}^{z}(2 x-1)^{n-1}
$$

$N=$ total number of parameters $B_{n} ; B_{n}=$ adjustable parameters.

Using Eq. (4) $\partial G^{\mathrm{E}} / \partial x$ is formed and substituted in the left hand side of Eq. (2). A rearrangement yields a convenient algebraic regression formula [22] to fit the experimentally determined quantities on the left hand side of Eq. (5)

$$
\begin{aligned}
& R T \ln \frac{J_{2}}{J_{1}} \frac{(1-x)}{x}=B_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)+B_{1}^{\mathrm{G}}(1-2 x) \\
& \quad+\sum_{n=2}^{N} B_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}(2 x-1)^{n-2}\left[(2 n+2)\left(x-x^{2}\right)-1\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The explicite value of the temperature dependent constant $B_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)$ can be determined from the boundary conditions of vanishing molar excess Gibbs energies for the pure components ($x=0$ and $x=1$) as described in Ref. [20].

As it has been shown recently [23,24], the associative law of the addition of real numbers causes necessarily mathematical equivalence among all polynomials of the same degree in $x$. The result of an algebraic best fit of given experimental data depends only on the highest power of $x$ appearing in the regression formula, but not on an arbitrary splitting of the coefficient of any power of $x$ into various terms [24]. From the mathematical point of view the constant $B_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)$ in Eq. (5) constitutes only a part of the $x^{0}$-term. Therefore $B_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)$ can be regarded as an additional adjustable parameter of the regression formula, and the older regression procedure described in Ref. [22] will be simplified considerably: Considering the left hand side of Eq. (5) and the coefficients of all parameters $B_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}$ (now with $n=0,1,2, \ldots, N$) as "measureable variables" allows the substitution of the algorithm derived in Ref. [22] by recently described procedures [24].

The enthalpies of mixing $H^{\mathrm{E}}$ and the excess entropies $S^{\mathrm{E}}$ can be determined from the temperature dependence of the excess Gibbs energies $G^{\mathrm{E}}$. Assuming that $H^{\mathrm{E}}$ and $S^{\mathrm{E}}$ are temperature independent, the Redlich-Kister parameters for $G^{\mathrm{E}}, H^{\mathrm{E}}$, and $S^{\mathrm{E}}$ will be connected by the relation [22]

$$
B_{n}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)=B_{n}^{\mathrm{H}}-T B_{n}^{\mathrm{S}}. \quad(6)
$$

Investigations on several binary systems have proved the usefulness of the described algebraic intensity-ratio (A.I.R.)-method (compare [25]). Recently Bergman et al. [26] also reported the successful application of this technique.

## Experimental Procedure
For the experimental investigations modified commercial equipment was employed (VARIAN-MAT, Bremen, FRG) consisting of a modified Knudsen cell unit MS-22 combined with a mass spectrometer, model CH4 (MS 24 molecular beam ion-source). Details concerning the equipment and general experimental technique have been already published elsewhere [20, 22, 27].

Because of the strong supercooling tendency of $\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Ni}$ alloys, as noted in Ref. [14], the sample preparations had to be handled carefully: The pure components (Cu: 99.999%, Fluka AG, Switzerland; Ni: 99.998%, Koch-Light Lab. Ltd., U.K.) were alloyed in a high vacuum furnace at about 1800 K. Then the samples were annealed in closed quartz tubes for several weeks at 800 K, as proposed by Elford et al. [14]. Finally, the homogeneity of the samples was proved by scanning electron microscopy. The compositions of the samples are given in Table 1.

After charging the temperature of the Knudsen cell was raised slowly up to about 40 degrees below the melting point of the respective sample. The intensity-ratios of $^{58}Ni^{+}/^{63}Cu^{+}$ were measured for samples with $x_{Ni} \geq 0.36$ at about 16 different temperatures within a range of 80 - 150 degrees. The diameter of the effusion orifice for these measurements was 1 mm. The small vapor pressures of the samples with $x_{Ni} \leq 0.2080$ made the use of effusion orifices with a diameter of 1.5 - 2 mm necessary. Also the temperature-range which could be investigated was considerably smaller (60 - 40 degrees). To compensate this disadvantage to a certain degree, the measurements for these five runs were performed at 20 different temperatures.

The ionization energy used was 11 eV and the electron current $20\ \mu A$.
The cell liners were made of $Al_{2}O_{3}$.

$$
\ln \left(J_{\mathrm{Ni}} / J_{\mathrm{Cu}}\right)=d(x)+k(x) / T
\tag{7}
$$

yielded the results presented in Table 1. As Fig. 4 shows, a satisfactory best-fit was achieved using a 2-parameter expansion $(N=2)$ for $G^{E}$. The values of the adjustable parameters $B_{n}$ are summarized in Table 2. The A.I.R.-method also allows one to check the quality of the best fit data by determination of the enthalpies of mixing $H^{E}$ by means of Eq. (8) [20, 22, 27]

$$
R \frac{\partial\left(\ln J_{2} / J_{1}\right)}{\partial(1 / T)}=\frac{\partial H^{\mathrm{E}}}{\partial x}-B_{0}^{\mathrm{H}}.
\tag{8}
$$

The minimum $S^{\text{E}}$-value reported by Rapp and Maak [9] is about $-1.1\ \text{J/mol·K}$ ($x_{\text{Ni}} \approx 0.38$), and Katayama et al. [15] found for this value about $-0.95\ \text{J/mol·K}$ ($x_{\text{Ni}} \approx 0.42$). Vecher and Gerasimov [13] reported a minimum $S^{\text{E}}$-values of $-1.97\ \text{J/mol·K}$ (50 at-% Ni), and a local minimum at 30 at-% Ni ($-1.92\ \text{J/mol·K}$). But is should be kept in mind that the data given by Vecher and Gerasimov [13] are not consistent. Notin et al. [16] determined negative $S^{\text{E}}$-values for compositions $x_{\text{Ni}} < 0.9$ which are in good agreement with the data of this work (except for a small delve at $x_{\text{Ni}} \approx 0.5$), whereas these authors obtained very slight positive $S^{\text{E}}$-values for higher compositions ($x_{\text{Ni}} \geq 0.9$).

## The Cu - Ni Equilibrium Phase Diagram

Calculations of phase boundaries can be based directly upon the thermochemical equilibrium conditions that at constant temperature $T$ the chemical potential $\mu_{j}^{\alpha}$ ("partial free Gibbs energies $G_{j}^{\alpha}$") for each component $j$ must be equal in each of the coexisting phases $\alpha$ [36]. Reducing to a liquid (l) and a solid (s) phase of a binary system yields

$$
\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{l}}\left(\bar{x}^{\mathrm{l}}, T\right)-\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{s}}\left(\bar{x}^{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)=0 \quad(j=1,2)
\tag{10}
$$

($\bar{x}^{\text{l}} =$ equilibrium composition in the liquid phase; $\bar{x}^{\text{s}} =$ equilibrium composition in the solid phase).

The chemical potential $\mu_{j}^{\alpha}$ can be written as

$$
\mu_{j}^{\alpha}(x, T)=\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{E}, \alpha}(x, T)+R T \ln x_{j}^{\alpha}+\mu_{j}^{0, \alpha}(T),
\tag{11}
$$

where $\mu_{j}^{\text{E}}$ denotes the excess chemical potential, and $\mu_{j}^{0}$ the chemical potential of the pure component under the same experimental conditions and in the same state. In order to obtain the necessary data at temperatures at which the considered state of the component $j$ is not stable an extrapolation has to be performed [37]. Describing the heat capacities $C_{p, j}^{\alpha}$ by the common expression

$$
C_{p, j}^{\alpha}=C_{1, j}^{\alpha}+C_{2, j}^{\alpha} T+C_{3, j}^{\alpha} T^{-2},
\tag{12}
$$

and using the temperature of melting $T_{\text{m}, j}$ and the heat of melting $\Delta_{\text{m}} H_{j}$, the temperature dependence of the difference between $\mu^{0,1}$ and $\mu^{0, \text{s}}$ is given [36] by

$$
\Delta \mu_{j}^{0}(T)=\Delta_{\mathrm{m}} H_{j}\left[1-T / T_{\mathrm{m}, j}\right]-\left[M_{j}^{\mathrm{l}}(T)-M_{j}^{\mathrm{s}}(T)\right],
\tag{13}
$$
with
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{j}^{\alpha}(T)= & C_{1, j}^{\alpha}\left[T \ln \left(T / T_{\mathrm{m}, j}\right)+T_{\mathrm{m}, j}-T\right]+ \\
& +0.5\left[C_{2, j}^{\alpha}+C_{3, j}^{\alpha} /\left(T / T_{\mathrm{m}, j}^{2}\right)\right]\left[T_{\mathrm{m}, j}-T\right]^{2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{14}
$$

The excess chemical potentials $\mu_{j}^{\text{E}}$ can be written in terms of the Redlich-Kister expansion as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{j}^{\mathrm{E}, \alpha}(x, T)= & v_{j}^{2}\left[B_{1}^{\mathrm{G}, \alpha}+\sum_{n=2}^{N} B_{n}^{\mathrm{G}, \alpha}\left(2 v_{j}-1\right)^{n-2}\right. \\
& \left.+\left[2 n\left(v_{j}-1\right)+1\right](-1)^{(j+1)(n-1)}\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
$$

$j=1,2;\ v_1=x;\ v_2=(1-x).$

The $B_{n}^{\text{G}}$-values of this study (Table 2) have been used for the solid phase, and the results reported in Ref. [40] for the liquid phase.

Substitution from Eqs. (11), (13) and (15) in Eq. (10) makes evident that the equilibrium conditions at each temperature $T$ represent a system of two transcendental equations of higher degree in $x^{\text{l}}$ and $x^{\text{s}}$. Such a system of equations cannot be solved exactly, and the difficulties of the approximate solution rise with the degree in $x$. This may be the reason why several authors prefer regular solution models which limit the degree of the transcendental equations to third order in $x$. As already described [36], the system of Eq. (10) can be solved by applying the generalized Newton technique [41] without any restrictions concerning the representation of the excess chemical potentials $\mu_{j}^{\text{E}}$. The detailed procedure has been given in Ref. [36].

Following the described method, the calculations yield the equilibrium phase diagram plotted in Figs. 1 and 6 for the Cu – Ni system. Table 5 gives the compositions of the coexisting phases at selected temperatures. In Fig. 1 the results obtained in the present paper are compared with the experimental investigations on the Cu – Ni phase diagram: The liquidus line of this study agrees well with the experimental data of Guertler and Tammann [2], whereas the solidus points reported by these authors are considerable lower. The liquidus points determined by Kurnakow and Zemczuzny [3] are only in agreement with the liquidus line of this study for $x_{\text{Ni}} < 0.6$. For higher Ni-concentrations ($x_{\text{Ni}} > 0.6$) these authors reported melting and solidification at higher temperatures than obtained in this paper. The solidus values for $x_{\text{Ni}} < 0.6$ are lower than the results of this

study. Tafel [4] reported liquidus values which agree only with the data of this study for $x_{\mathrm{Ni}}<0.4$, for higher Ni-concentration the liquidus line of this investigation is lower than the values determined by Tafel [4]. Concerning solidification, there is agreement with the data reported by Tafel [4] for $x_{\mathrm{Ni}} \geq 0.6$. However, for lower Ni-concentrations the solidus values determined by Tafel [4] are considerable lower than the values of this study.

Predel and Mohs [5], and Feest and Doherty [6] determined melting diagrams which are in complete agreement with the present results (Fig. 1). There is also good agreement between the experimental data reported by Schürmann and Schulz [7], and the melting diagram obtained in this study (Fig. 1). Only the liquidus values determined by these authors are slightly higher than those of this paper. The measurements reported by Doherty and Feest [42] have been incorporated in the data by Feest and Doherty [6], and are therefore not characterized separately in Fig. 1. Bastow and Kirkwood [43] determined liquidus and solidus points between 0-20 at-% Ni by micro- analysis. The results of these authors are not plotted in Fig. 1, but the data also agree well with the melting diagram of this study.

Calculations of the phase boundaries of the $\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Ni}$ equilibrium phase diagram have been already performed by several authors [14,18,30,31]. The results of these authors are given in Fig. 6, together with the values obtained in this study. The phase diagram from Hansen and Anderko [29] is also shown in Fig. 6. Comparison of Figs. 1 and 6 shows that the melting diagram of this study better concurs with the newer experimentally determined liquidus and solidus data than all previously calculated phase diagrams.

Concerning the miscibility gap in the solid phase the experimental data is too poor for reasonable comparison: Dey [44] determined the temperatures of demixing for three different solid alloys: $x_{\mathrm{Ni}}=0.25(513 \mathrm{~K}), x_{\mathrm{Ni}}=0.5(753 \mathrm{~K})$, and $x_{\mathrm{Ni}}=0.75$ (673 K). Virjen and Van Dijk [45] obtained for a solid alloy with 56.5 at- $\% \mathrm{Ni}$ a critical temperature of demixing between 593 K and 613 K.

The calculations of this study yields a solid miscibility gap with the critical point of demixing at 72 at- $\% \mathrm{Ni}$, and 695 K (Fig. 6). This is in reasonable agreement with the data calculated by Boyle et al. [32], as it can be seen from Fig. 6. Whereas the computations by Elford et al. [14] yield a miscibility gap only at far lower temperatures (Fig. 6). Recently Notin et al. [46] computed a miscibility gap with the critical point of demixing at nearly the same composition than obtained in this work, namely at $x_{\mathrm{Ni}}=0.75$, but at considerable higher temperature (770 K). These results are not shown in Fig. 6.
