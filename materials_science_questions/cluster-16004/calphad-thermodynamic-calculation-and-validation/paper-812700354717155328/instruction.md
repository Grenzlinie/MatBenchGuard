# APBE Coefficients from DFT-based Cluster Expansion and Monte Carlo for Ni-base Superalloys

## Problem background
In Ni-base superalloys, the ordered L1₂ γ' precipitates provide strengthening through the formation of antiphase boundaries (APBs) when dislocations cut through them. The energy of these APBs (APBE) strongly influences the cross-slip behavior of dislocations and, consequently, the fatigue life and high-temperature performance of the alloy. The APBE depends on the local composition of the γ' phase, which can contain multiple alloying elements (e.g., Ti, Cr, Co, Mo) beyond the base Ni and Al. Understanding the quantitative effect of each solute and their combined interactions on the APBE is essential for rational alloy design. This task aims to compute the composition-dependent APBE of γ' phases using a DFT-based cluster expansion approach combined with Monte Carlo simulations.

## Approach
The computational workflow has three main stages:

1. **Cluster expansion (CE) fitting** – For each relevant alloy system (binary Ni-Al, ternaries Ni-Al-M with M = Ti, Cr, Co, Mo, and the quinary Ni-Al-Ti-Cr-Co-Mo), density functional theory (DFT) total-energy calculations are performed on many small periodic structures using pseudopotentials from the SSSP library. The calculated energies are used to fit a cluster expansion model via the ATAT toolkit, which expresses the energy of any atomistic configuration in terms of effective cluster interactions (ECIs). Cross-validation scores serve as evidence of model quality.

2. **Monte Carlo (MC) simulations of APBE** – Using the fitted CEs, large supercells (2160 atoms) containing a {111} APB are constructed. Metropolis Monte Carlo sampling, which accounts for configurational disorder, is executed for a range of compositions and temperatures to obtain the average APBE relative to that of pure Ni₃Al. This yields the relative APBE (Δγ_APB) as a function of solute content and temperature.

3. **Coefficient fitting** – From the ternary simulation data, a linear model Δγ_APB = Σ K_i X_i (where X_i is the at.% of solute i in the γ' phase) is fitted to extract the per-at.% influence K_i of each element at 500 K. For the multicomponent γ' phases, a master equation is fitted that includes binary cross-terms (products of two solute fractions) and a ternary cross-term (product of three solute fractions) to capture synergistic interactions among solutes. The resulting coefficients are reported in two JSON files.

## Reproduction target
Produce two scored output files:
- `ternary_coefficients.json` – Contains the linear coefficients K_i for Ti, Cr, Co, and Mo at 500 K, obtained by fitting the ternary Δγ_APB data from Monte Carlo simulations. Each coefficient is given in mJ/m² per at.% solute in the γ' phase.
- `multicomponent_coefficients.json` – Contains the full set of coefficients from the master equation at 500 K: the linear K_i (same elements), the binary cross-term coefficients for Ti–Cr, Ti–Co, and Cr–Co, and the ternary cross-term coefficient for Ti–Cr–Co. Units are mJ/m² per at.% (linear) and mJ/m² per at.%² (cross-term).

The fitting must be based on the simulation data generated by the preceding DFT and Monte Carlo steps; simply reporting standard literature values or guessed numbers without executing the workflow is not sufficient.

## How you are scored
A hidden verifier reads your submitted `ternary_coefficients.json` and `multicomponent_coefficients.json` and compares each coefficient to reference values derived from the published work (not visible to you). The comparison enforces correct sign (e.g., a specific solute must have a positive or negative coefficient) and requires that the values lie within an acceptable range defined by hidden tolerances. The final reward (0–1) is the fraction of coefficients that satisfy these criteria, weighted appropriately between the two files. Simply entering the paper's reported numbers without running the DFT+CE+MC pipeline will not produce the correct set of coefficients that pass all hidden checks. Therefore, ensure you perform the full computational workflow and extract the coefficients from the resulting simulation data.
