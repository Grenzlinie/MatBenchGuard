# Variation of Hg incorporation in molecular-beam epitaxially grown HgCdTe structures due to growth front roughness and misoriented substrates: Role of kink sites

Jose Arias

(Received 15 December 1988; accepted 11 February 1989)

Theoretical and experimental results are presented to shed light on the atomistic nature of Hg incorporation during HgCdTe growth. The extremely large difference in the bond strengths of the Cd-Te and Hg-Te forces the choice of growth conditions where Hg incorporation is extremely small and controlled essentially by two key factors: (i) $Te_2$ overpressure and (ii) kink site density at the growing surface. While the role of $Te_2$ overpressure was explored earlier, the role of the second factor will be the subject of this paper. The kink site density is in general time dependent during growth; it can also be varied by choosing different growth directions. Consequently, the Hg incorporation rate, which is closely tied to surface kink site density, is altered. These issues and their importance on HgCdTe alloys and HgCdTe-CdTe heterostructures are explored.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The band-tailoring potential of HgCdTe-CdTe heterostructures has fueled much research in the area of molecular-beam epitaxy (MBE) of this system. $^{1-4}$ In heterostructures, the key issues that determine whether or not the full potential of the structure will be realized are (i) defect density in the layers including extrinsic and intrinsic defects, (ii) interface quality, and (iii) composition uniformity over the entire thickness of the heterostructure. While in HgTe-CdTe systems to a large extent the defect density appears to be controlled by thermodynamic considerations, growth kinetics play an important role in surface/interface quality and the incorporation rate of Hg. To fully understand these important issues, an atomistic understanding of the MBE growth of HgCdTe must be developed. In this paper, we attempt this task by carrying out theoretical and experimental studies on the growth process. Although our theoretical studies based on Monte Carlo techniques have by no means resolved all the atomistic details of growth, important findings that shed new light on potential problems in growth of high-quality HgCdTe-CdTe heterostructures have emerged, along with possible solutions for these problems.

Any detailed understanding of the HgTe-CdTe growth must be able to explain the following previously known experimental observations in addition to the new experimental results discussed in this paper: (i) Hg incorporation at normally used growth temperatures $(\sim 160-200\ ^{\circ}\text{C})$ is approximately controlled by $1-(J_{Cd}/2J_{Te_2})$, where $J$ is the beam flux of the various components$^5$; (ii) under normally used conditions where the CdTe part of the HgCdTe heterostructure is grown by turning off $Te_2$ flux, roughly 10% of Hg gets incorporated$^6$; (iii) Hg incorporation in the "pure" CdTe parts increases if CdTe growth rate is increased$^6$; and (iv) Hg sticking coefficient is in the $\sim 10^{-2}-10^{-4}$ range depending on the growth temperature and crystallographic orientation. $^{1,7}$

The main difficulty in MBE growth of HgTe-CdTe systems arises from the large difference in the HgTe and CdTe bond strengths.$^8$ The effects of this difference in the surface kinetics of Hg and Cd are explored in the next section. In particular, the incorporation process for Hg atoms under normally employed growth conditions is identified and the effect of surface roughness on the incorporation process is quantified. Since at normally employed growth conditions Hg incorporation occurs primarily at the high bonding kink sites, the kink site density plays a key role in Hg incorporation. On the other hand, the Cd incorporation does not require the presence of kink sites. Since kink site density is related to surface roughness, increased surface roughness causes increased Hg incorporation. Kink site density (and Hg incorporation) can also be altered by changing growth directions. Experimental studies discussed in Sec. III bear out these computer results. In Sec. IV, we conclude and discuss some possible ways to reduce surface roughness.

## II. EXPERIMENTAL

The experiments were performed on a Riber 2300 MBE system. Dynamic growth experiments were carried out by reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) intensity oscillation measurements. $^{9-11}$ (100)CdTe substrates that were misoriented $0.47^{\circ}$ (determined by x-ray measurements) were used. Assuming that the CdTe surface relaxes to its minimum energy, namely, that it breaks into monoatomic steps with precise low-index terraces and edges, an average CdTe terrace width of $\sim 395\ \mathring{A}$ was calculated.

The acceleration voltage of the electron gun was 10 keV, and the electron beam was aligned along the [001] azimuth. The incident electron beam was chosen at such a grazing angle that the off-Bragg condition was satisfied [about $1^{\circ}$ for (100)CdTe], resulting in a high sensitivity to step behavior. RHEED intensity oscillations were visible to the unaided eye and measured from the phosphor screen by means of a

### III. THEORETICAL FORMALISM AND EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

Our goal is to develop an understanding of HgTe–CdTe growth on an atomistic scale, using a Monte Carlo approach for this purpose. So far, no computer simulations have been carried out for the HgTe–CdTe system, although a consider- able amount of work has been done for III–V semiconduc- tors,¹²⁻¹⁴ including GaAs, AlAs, and InAs. Since molecular anions and atomic cations are used for both systems and they also have the same zinc-blende structure, we expect the basic growth process to be similar for the III–V and II–VI sys- tems.

We have applied the microscopic model¹²,¹⁴ developed for MBE growth of III–V semiconductors that simulates the atom-by-atom growth of semiconductors (and heterostruc- tures) grown from atomic cations and molecular anions. With some important modifications discussed below, we use this model for the II–VI system. In Fig. 1 we show a concep- tual picture of the MBE growth of HgCdTe compounds. This picture has emerged from experimental and computer simulation work done on III–V compounds and the observa- tion of close similarity with II–VI compounds.

Computer simulations carried out to understand the atomistic nature of MBE growth of III–V semiconductors have led to a physical picture shown in Fig. 2. The key find- ings from these simulations as well as experimental studies on III–V growth are (i) for a given growth rate, there exists a minimum growth temperature $(T_{c}$ of Fig. 2) below which the film grows with a rough surface due to very low surface kinetics of the impinging atoms; (ii) an upper limit in tem- perature exists since, beyond this temperature $(T_{c}$ of Fig. 2), the semiconductor has too many defects that are con- trolled by entropy; and (iii) growth of high-quality heteros- tructures then depends on being able to line up the tempera- ture windows of the two semiconductor components. Since the position of these temperature windows for ideal growth (low defects and smooth surface) is controlled by cation surface kinetics (which is related to the surface bond

Similarly, the evaporation rate for $Hg$ atoms is about six orders of magnitude higher than for $Cd$ atoms. Not only does this cause experimental difficulties in growing HgTe-CdTe heterostructures, but even theoretically this creates a special problem. Due to the high migration and evaporation rates of the $Hg$ component, it is required to study nearly $10^{14}$ events to simulate the growth of 10 monolayers (ML) of HgTe for a $30 \times 30$ site area if conventional Monte Carlo approaches are to be followed. This makes it prohibitively expensive to simulate HgCdTe at usual growth temperatures. To circumvent this problem, we treat the CdTe growth according to the usual Monte Carlo principles (i.e., random atom generation, migration, evaporation) while the $Hg$ incorporation is handled by defining an occupation probability for the $Hg$ atoms for each of the growing surface's cation sites defined through thermodynamics by

$$
P_{\mathrm{Hg}}\left(E_{\mathrm{tot}}\right)=J_{\mathrm{Hg}} / R_{v}\left(E_{\mathrm{tot}}\right), \tag{2}
$$

where $J_{\mathrm{Hg}}$ is the $Hg$ flux and $R_{v}(E_{\mathrm{tot}})$ is the evaporation rate for the site with energy $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$. A random number is then used to describe whether or not a $Hg$ atom is incorporated during the chemisorption of a $Te_{2}$ molecule.

Table II shows clearly that $Hg$ atoms have a high probability of being incorporated at sites where they have strong bondings, e.g., the (2,6) or (2,7) site for the (100) growth. The bonding nature of the growing surface depends very much on the surface quality in terms of smoothness. As can be imagined physically, a smooth surface will primarily have exposed sites with very low energy, i.e., (2,4) site types. On the other hand, a rough surface is expected to have a large concentration of kink-type surface sites [i.e., (2,6) site types].

The implication of these results is that sample misorientation and surface preparation (polishing, chemical etching, thermal heating, buffer layer growth) play major roles in the incorporation of $Hg$ atoms during nucleation and growth of $HgTe$ and $Hg_{1-x} Cd_{x} Te$. The kink site density can be enhanced using off-axis substrates. To demonstrate this, we have grown $Hg_{1-x} Cd_{x} Te(0.20 \geqslant x \geqslant 0.30)$ on the $(211) B^{15}$ and $(111) B^{16}$ orientations; the $(211) B$ surface is misoriented $19.5^{\circ}$ off the $(111) B$ surface towards the nearest (100) plane. Growth was carried out in the $180-200{^\circ}C$ range, and ideal $Hg/Te_{2}$ flux ratios were determined and monitored by RHEED. A $Hg$ deficiency during growth produces polycrystalline material, $^{17}$ and an excess produces microtwin defects $^{17,18}$ that are detrimental to the electrical transport properties of the material. Our results show that ideal $Hg/Te_{2}$ flux ratios for the $(211) B$ orientation are in the 90-145 range, depending on the growth temperature and Cd composition, while those for the $(111) B$ are in the 159-400 range for similar growth conditions. We believe that the lower $Hg$ flux required (a factor of 1.7 to 2.7 lower) to grow single-crystal $Hg_{1-x} Cd_{x} Te$ on the highly stepped $(211) B$ surface is due to the higher incorporation of $Hg$ at kink and step sites, as predicted by our calculations.

With the above motivating factors, we have carried out Monte Carlo simulations to shed light on the $Hg$ incorporation rate as a function of the surface conditions, $Te_{2}$ overpressure, and temperature. In Fig. 4, we show the kink site density of a CdTe surface growing at $1 ML/s$ at three different temperatures. This result is a key to understanding the results presented later. Plotted in Fig. 4 are the evolution of the bonding configurations on a growing surface for a (100) growth when one starts from an atomically flat surface. At very low temperatures where the Cd migration rate is very low [Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)], we note that rapid roughening of the growth surface manifests itself in a high density of surface sites where available bonds are large, i.e., (2,6) or (2,7) configurations. As can be seen, the kink site concentrations approaches $\sim 20\%$ of available surface sites.

In the case where Cd migration is somewhat higher $(\sim T_{s}>180{^\circ}C)$, the kink site density goes through an oscillatory phase, as shown in Fig. 4(c). These oscillations arise from the starting atomically smooth surface, becoming rough as the first monolayer grows, and then again smooth as the first layer fills up. In fact, the commonly observed RHEED oscillations in the growth of CdTe (Figs. 5-7) and HgTe (Fig. 8) originate from the same principle. It may be noted that once the average cation migration distance approaches the naturally occurring surface step densities, the oscillations will disappear. The temperature at which this would occur depends upon the precise orientation of the substrate. This point is further clarified in our discussions on the RHEED oscillations.

This is an important factor to consider in the technology of MBE of HgCdTe because ideal $J_{Hg}/J_{Te_{2}}$ flux ratios to grow a high-quality HgCdTe single crystal would then be time dependent during growth. We have demonstrated this effect by growing thick HgCdTe epitaxial layers, monitoring the growth with RHEED, and changing the $J_{Hg}/J_{Te_{2}}$ flux ratio during growth to maintain single-crystal growth. We have carried out these growth studies on different crystallographic orientations: (111)<i>B</i>, (111)<i>B</i> misoriented 8°, and (211)<i>B</i>. These studies show that ideal $J_{Hg}/J_{Te_{2}}$ flux ratios change continuously with time, as seen in Table III; here, only ideal initial and final flux ratios are shown. An excess Hg-to-Te₂ ratio at constant growth temperature produces microtwin defects that are easily detected by RHEED.¹⁷ Such defects are detrimental to the growth and are avoided by continuously decreasing the Hg flux as the surface step density increases. This effect is especially important where thick epitaxial layers are grown (≥10 μm) because the overall variation of the Hg incorporation at the surface is large. Epitaxial layers grown using this approach¹⁵ have material and device characteristics comparable to those of HgCdTe grown by the more mature technique of liquid phase epitaxy (LPE).

## IV. SUMMARY

Theoretical growth modeling calculations and RHEED dynamic growth experiments of CdTe and HgTe have been presented to understand the He incorporation process on the growth front. At normally used temperatures, growth computer simulations for HgCdTe showed clearly that the Hg incorporation occurs primarily at the kink sites (sites near step edges), where it could be up to two orders of magnitude greater than at the free sites. These theoretical results have been experimentally confirmed by growing HgCdTe on the highly stepped (211) surface.

Computer simulations and RHEED dynamic experiments carried out in the 160–200 °C temperature range also show that, starting from an atomically flat surface, the kink site density of CdTe is time dependent during growth. Having initially an oscillatory nature (two-dimensional island growth mechanism) gradually settles down toward a steady-state value. On the other hand, the growth of HgTe follows a step propagation growth mechanism in which the kink density should be constant, and its value equal to that before deposition. The implication of these results is that during the growth of HgCdTe, the incorporation and evaporation of Hg atoms at the surface are time dependent because the step density is variating; therefore, ideal growth parameters are also time dependent, as we have demonstrated experimentally.

These results are also important in the fabrication of CdTe/HgTe quantum wells and superlattices, where interface roughness at the atomic level can smear out the fine structure expected from an ideal superlattice. Based on the studies presented here, we do not expect the formation of CdTe/HgTe interfaces to be atomically smooth because of the step generation during CdTe growth. Improvement of the interface smoothness may be obtained by using the technique of growth interruptions²¹,²² in order to relax the CdTe surface, or by enhancing the Cd surface kinetics¹³,¹⁴,²³ with external sources (other than the substrate thermal energy) such as light, electrons, ions, etc.
