# Novel statistical lattice model for the supported nanoparticle. Features of the reaction performance influenced by the dynamically changed shape and surface morphology of the supported active particle

The aim of this study is to reveal the mutual influence of the shape and the surface morphology of supported nanoparticles on the reaction kinetics. The analysis has been provided by means of the novel statistical lattice model, which imitates the physicochemical processes that proceed over the supported particles. To simulate the active metal particle the finite Kossel crystal located on the inert support has been chosen. The surface morphology of the particle is defined by distribution of heights of the metal atom columns. The metal atoms attract the nearest neighbour metal atoms and the atoms of the support. The attraction is characterised by interaction energies between the nearest neighbour metal atoms and between the metal atom and the support underneath. The change of morphology is caused by the thermal diffusion of the surface atoms. As a result the equilibrium shape of the particle has been observed to depend on the temperature and the relative ratio of metal-metal and metal-support energies. The model reaction $2A + B_2 \to 2AB$ has been studied, taking into account the roughening of the particle surface and the spillover phenomenon of the adsorbed $A_{ads}$ over the support. It has been shown that the kinetics of the roughened nanometer-sized particle can be notably different from those corresponding to the flat homogeneous surface. The shape of the nanoparticle can change under the influence of the adsorbed layer even in the absence of the adsorbate-metal interactions.

## 1. Introduction

The modeling of the reaction kinetics in heterogeneous cataly- sis is of invaluable importance both for elucidation of the detailed reaction mechanism and for deeper understanding of what happens with real catalysts in the course of the reaction performed on them. But the kinetic simulations in heteroge- neous catalysis are usually carried out for essentially homoge- neous infinite systems. These systems correspond mainly to well-defined faces of a single crystal. However, industrial cata- lysts mostly consist of active nanometer-sized particles depos- ited on the internal surface of more or less inactive supports (e.g., noble metals deposited on $SiO_{2}, Al_{2}O_{3}$ , etc.). In these cases it is incorrect to consider the active surface as a flat homogeneous plane. Such simplifying supposition is often (and, in a number of cases, reasonably so) made by the model- ing of the reactions occurring on the single crystal and bulk catalysts. The possible differences between single crystal facesand supported catalysts could result from the following: $^{1,2}$ (a), synergetic participation in the reaction of different crystal faces of active particle, caused by surface diffusion of adsorbed species from one face to another; (b), possible change of the particles' shape, size and morphology of their surface due to influence of the reaction media (reactant pressures and tem- perature) inducing changes in activity and/or selectivity; (c), the chemical modification of dispersed particles due to metal-support interaction, especially important in the case of the small $(>3 ~nm)$ particles; (d), spillover phenomena over the support surface caused by diffusion of adsorbed species from the support to the active sites or vice versa. These effects become especially evident with decreasing particle size. The complexity of such systems, coupled with the relatively high pressure of reagents, limits the use of sensitive physical techni- ques for surface characterization and makes hard the interpre- tation of reaction kinetics on the supported catalysts compared with a bulk one. The activity and selectivity of the supported catalyst might essentially depend upon not only the particles' shape and size but also the support properties. As follows from the experimental data, the influence of the various surface defects appearing under the action of the reaction media, as well as the change of the shape and size of deposited particles, can significantly determine the catalyst performance. For example, it was stated recently that, in some cases, the nano- particles undergo the reversible adsorbate-induced shape changes $(H_{2}+H_{2}O$ and $CO+H_{2}$ over a $Cu/ZnO$ system stu died by in situ transmission electron microscopy $^{3}$ ) and even disintegrate into atoms (effects of $CO$ and NO adsorption on the morphological changes of $Rh/TiO_{2}$ studied by scanning tunneling microscopy $^{4}$ ). For better understanding of the reac tion kinetics we should take into account these processes and the inherent heterogeneity of supported particles.

However, despite the plentiful experimental evidence of the marked size and shape dependence of reaction performance(see, e.g., recent reviews $^{5,6}$ ) the theoretical approaches for the description and analysis of such phenomena are not numerous. The simulation of the reaction kinetics on the supported nano- particles almost inevitably requires the use of the Monte-Carlo technique, i.e., statistical lattice modeling. Under the recent

Such factors as reactant supply due to the diffusion of reagents over the support (spillover), $^{7}$ interplay of the reaction kinetics on the dif ferent facets of the supported particles due to adsorbed species diffusion between the facets, $^{8}$ the jump-wise reshaping of the active particle under the influence of the adlayer composition $^{9}$ and the influence of geometrical parameters of the supported particles on the activity and selectivity of the multi-route cata- lytic reaction $^{11,12}$ were examined in detail. In particular, in refs. 7 and 8 the reaction kinetics of nm-sized catalytic particles was investigated in two variants—reaction on the different facets of the particle and the possibility of reagent adsorption over the support with subsequent diffusion to the active particle. It has evidently been shown that the kinetics of catalytic reac- tions occurring on the supported particles can be drastically different from the simple superposition of the kinetics inherent to the separate faces of single crystals.

Of special interest is the recent study $^{13}$ where the influence of crystallite size on the activity of a supported catalyst in the case of the Langmuir–Hinshelwood reaction mechanism has been investigated by means of the dynamic Monte-Carlo simula- tion. The active surface was modelled here (following the model proposed in ref. 14) as a set of particles, each one repre- sented as the top projection of a truncated hexagonal pyramid on a flat support surface. Catalytic centers with different con- figuration (edges, corners, etc.) have differing activities in respect of the elementary reaction steps. The ratio between the number of different active centers depends, of course, on the size of the crystallites (the relative concentration of surface defects increase with decreasing particle size). But in this and most of other studies performed by the Monte-Carlo technique the shape and the surface morphology of the supported parti- cle has invariably to be taken in the course of the reaction. These models reflect so-called a priori heterogeneity of the surface, i.e., the prescribed configuration of the active site on the surface does not dynamically depend on the changing environment.

The objectives of the present study are: (1) to elaborate the statistical lattice model of the supported particle, taking into account the dynamic change of the shape and surface mor- phology of the particle under the influence of the reaction media; and (2) to reveal the influence of the particle shape and its surface morphology on the reaction performance.

## 2. Model
Recently the model for the monomolecular and dissociative adsorption on the roughened catalytic surface with dynami- cally changing morphology has been proposed. $^{15,16}$ It is based on the well known "solid-on-solid" model $^{17,18}$ using the "infi nite" Kossel crystal. As far as we know the modelling of the catalytic reaction performance on the nanosized active parti- cles represented by the "finite" Kossel crystals situated on the inactive support surface had been not performed until now. The theoretical analysis of such systems is very compli- cated even in the case of the infinite surface. Therefore the only possible method for their investigation is modelling based on the Monte-Carlo technique.

Several advantages of this technique should be mentioned:
(a) the possibility of independent consideration of the local environment of each active center or adsorbed molecule; (b) simple enough algorithmic realization of almost any concepts describing the occurring physicochemical processes; (c) computer visualization of the simulated surface morphology. It should be pointed out that it is possible to model the considered system on a real scale: the linear size of nano- particles is from several to hundreds of atoms. The analysis will be provided by means of the statistical lattice models imi- tating the physicochemical processes on the supported cataly- tic particles dynamically changing both its shape and surface morphology.

As a model of the catalytic metal particle we shall considerthe Kossel crystal or, more exactly, the SOS (solid-on-solid) model $^{17,18}$ located on a neutral support. The rigid support sur face is imitated by a square lattice $N × N$ with periodic bound ary conditions. The initial active particle consist of the finite number of elementary cells (metal atoms) situated in the col- umns of the cubic lattice $N_{in } × N_{in } × h_{in }$ . In our simulations $N=200, N_{in }=21, h_{in }=10$ atoms, i.e., in this particular case the base width of the initial particle is $N_{in } \sim 8 ~nm$ , and the height $h_{in } \sim 4 ~nm$ . The surface morphology of the particle is defined by distribution of heights of the metal atom columns. The metal atoms attract the nearest neighbour ones and the atoms of the support (scheme in Fig. 1). The attraction is char- acterised by energies: $J_{mm}$ , the interaction energy between the nearest neighbour atoms situated at the same level and one on the other; $J_{ms}$ , the interaction energy between the metal atom and the immobile support underneath. For the sake of simpli- city we will not consider at this stage the diagonal interactions between atoms. The overhangs of atoms in the columns and the appearance of the vacancies in the volume of the crystal are forbidden (Fig. 1). The particle surface morphology is changed due to the thermal diffusion of the surface atoms. As follows from Fig. 1 we considered as a surface atom the upper atom of the Kossel crystal but not a side one. Hence, our model is two-dimensional, the construction of 3-D model is now in progress. To model the diffusion of the metal atoms over the metal and support surfaces the Metropolis algo- rithm $^{19}$ has been used. The diffusion proceeds via transfer of the randomly chosen surface metal atom from any column onto one of the neighbouring ones (for the boundary metal atoms it might be the cell of the support surface). If total energy decreased due to this diffusion transfer, i.e., the trans- ferring atom got a more energetically beneficial position, such an attempt would occur with probability $W=1$ . If the energy increases (the number of the neighbouring metal atoms decreases), the successful attempt happens with probability $W=\exp \{-\Delta E / R T\}<1$ , where $\Delta E$ is the difference between the energies of the initial and resulting configurations. Such a procedure ensures the fulfilment of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution on the probabilities of the possible configurations and warrants getting to the thermodynamically equilibrium state at an infinite number of Monte Carlo steps. In our case one MC step consists of $N^{2}$ attempts to transfer the surface atom. In reality, however, we are forced to restrict our calcula- tion to a sufficiently great but finite number of MC steps at which the system configuration can be considered to be in equilibrium (within statistical fluctuations). Hereinafter we shall mean by "equilibrium" the shape of the particle in just

this "dynamic" sense, taking into account that after each next MC step the shape and the surface morphology of the particle changes slightly but the average characteristics keep safe. In our numerical experiments 50000-70000 MC steps had usually been performed to establish the equilibrium at fixed temperature. It should be borne in mind that the number of surface atoms (active centres) used for the calculation of sur- face coverages by adsorbed species can change from one MC step to another as a result of surface atoms diffusion on the support (for the flat initial particle this number equals $21 × 21=441$ ).

At a sufficiently high temperature the initially cubic shape of the supported crystal begins to change due to increasing of the surface atoms' mobility. The particle's surface becomes more rough: in addition to the increasing number of point defects, collective defects appear, such as steps surrounding relatively planar surface regions (terraces). As a result, some "dynami- cally" equilibrium shape of the particle arises, depending on the temperature, size of the crystal and the ratio of interaction energies $J_{mm} / J_{ms}$ between metal and support atoms. The simu lation shows that, depending on the relative ratios of inter- action energies metal-metal and metal-support, we can observe various pathways of particle shape evolution at differ-ent temperatures: (i) the dispersal of metal particle over thesupport surface in the cases when the ratio $J_{mm} / J_{ms}$ is low;(ii) partial reshaping of the initial cubic crystal into a hemi-sphere at comparable $J_{mm} / J_{ms}$ (but $|J_{mm}|>|J_{ms}|$ ); and (iii) at very high ratios of $J_{mm} / J_{ms}$ , the reduction of the width and the growing of the average height of the supported metal particle.

It is evident that the key factors for our model are the inter- action energies $J_{mm}$ and $J_{ms}$ . Owing to the lack of the reliable information about these values (both experimental and theore- tical) we shall not consider particular supports, metals and adsorbed species. Our aim is to reveal the common regularities of the reaction performance on the supported particles. We estimate the $J_{mm}$ value to be the energy of creation of the pair adatom-vacancy for the flat (100) surface, obtained in the frames of effective medium theory. According to ref. 17 and references therein this value equals $\sim 0.115 eV$ , i.e., $\approx-10 ~kJ$  mole $^{-1}$ . We assume that the attraction metal-metal is stronger than metal-support, (otherwise it is evident that the particle will spread over the support surface at any temperature). In our simulations we accept $J_{mm}=-10 ~kJ ~mole^{-1}$ and $J_{ms}=-2.5 ~kJ ~mole^{-1}$ (sign - correspond to the attraction between the atoms).

## 3. Results and discussion
### Equilibrium shapes of the supported particle
In the frames of the proposed statistical lattice model the equi- librium shapes of the supported nano-sized particle at different temperatures have been studied. The initial shape and the equilibrium shapes of the supported particle simulated at theabove-mentioned particular interaction energies $(J_{mm}=-10$  $kJ mole^{-1}$ and $J_{ms}=-2.5 ~kJ ~mole^{-1}$ ) are shown in Fig. 2.

One can see that at the intermediate range of temperatures(T = 500 K) the surface of the particle is flat enough but the corners and edges are partly rounded. The surface of the metal particle becomes extremely rough at the hemispherical shape(T = 900 K). The beginning of the dispersal of metal particle over the support surface in our model (at the above-mentioned values of $J_{mm}$ and $J_{ms}$ ) is located between $T=900 ~K$ and T = 1100 K. The radius of the dispersal remains unchanged(within statistical fluctuations) during the process of equilibra- tion. When these radii overlap we can observe the coalescence of closely situated particles at high temperatures. Obviously, the temperature range of the shape changing depends on the absolute values of interaction energies $J_{mm}$ and $J_{ms}$ .

### Langmuir-Hinshelwood reaction on the supported particle
The performance of the model reaction $A+B_{2}$ has been stu died taking into account the roughening of the particle surface and the spillover phenomena of the $A_{ads }$ species over the support surface. The reaction is carried out through monomo- lecular adsorption of $A$ , dissociative adsorption of $B_{2}$ , and immediate reaction between neighboring $A_{ads }$ and $B_{ads }$ ; deso rption of adsorbates has been neglected for comparison of simulated kinetic dependencies with the standard case-ZGBmodel. $^{20}$

The following propositions had been made to model the reaction proceeding on the supported catalyst particle (Fig.1): (1) the reaction starts on the equilibrium shape of the par- ticle at a particular temperature; (2) the $B_{2}$ molecule can adsorb (with sticking coefficient equal to 1) with subsequent dissociation only on the two neighboring active sites situated at the same level, $B_{ads }$ remains immobile in all cases as in ref. 20; (3) the $A$ molecule can adsorb (with sticking coefficient equal to 1) and diffuse both on the metal and support surface(spillover); (4) desorption of $A_{ads}$ and $B_{ads}$ is neglected, as in ref. 20; (5) the reaction between $A_{ads}$ and $B_{ads}$ proceeds imme diately they are brought into contact due to adsorption, or Aads diffusion into the two neighboring active sites situated at the same level; (6) the probabilities of adsorbed species $A_{ads }$  diffusion are governed by the above-mentioned Metropolis algorithm; (7) the simulation procedure makes provision fortaking account of the lateral interactions both of $A_{ads}$ or $B_{ads}$  with each other and with neighboring metal atoms with corresponding energies, although in the cases considered below they are not taken into account. Our simulations (studies of the influence of dissociative adsorption on the equilibrium shape and surface morphology of a particle) show that if the interaction energies adsorbate-metal become comparable with metal-metal ones then the nanoparticle undergoes the revers- ible adsorbate-induced reshaping, $^{21}$ and that particle size deter mines the degree of reshaping as in experiments. $^{4}$ In this contribution we intend to examine only the influence of the roughened surface (i.e., the geometrical restrictions on the adsorption and on the reaction) and of the spillover pheno- mena on the kinetic dependencies of the reaction under study. The effect of lateral interactions adsorbate-metal and adsor- bate-adsorbate on the reaction performance will be studied elsewhere. For better statistics we measured the kinetic charac- teristics (coverages and reaction rate) simultaneously from

four separate supported particles preliminarily equilibrated at the given temperature. Also, all the characteristics were averaged over the last 500 Monte-Carlo steps of the equilibration process.

The arriving molecule is chosen to be $A$ with probability $Y_A = P_A/(P_A + P_{B_2})$ ($P_i$ are the partial pressures of the reagents in the gas phase) and $B_2$ with probability $(1-Y_A)$. Thus, $Y_A$ is the $A$ molar ratio. The Monte-Carlo step in the case of the reaction consists of $N^2$ attempts to adsorb $A$ or $B_2$ with immediate reaction between the near-neighboring partners. After each attempt to adsorb an $A$ or $B_2$ molecule several attempts (usually 10) at the diffusion of surface metal atoms and $A_{\text{ads}}$ species were performed to equilibrate both the morphology of particle surface and the adsorbed layer.

The kinetic dependencies of $A_{\text{ads}}$ and $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverages (number of adsorbed atoms normalized on the total number of the active sites) *versus* molar ratio $Y_A$ obtained at different variants of the reaction performance (with or without $A_{\text{ads}}$ species diffusion and/or metal atoms diffusion) has been compared with ones of the Ziff-Gulari-Barshad model. $^{20}$ In our case the ZGB-model corresponds to the performance of the reaction only on the initial flat uniform surface (Fig. 2a) without any diffusion of adsorbates and metal atoms (Fig. 3a). Similar to $^{20}$ we observe the continuous kinetic phase transition at $Y_A \approx 0.39$ (transition from the metal surface completely covered by $B_{\text{ads}}$) and the first order kinetic phase transition (KPhT-1) at $Y_A \approx 0.52$ when $A_{\text{ads}}$ and $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverages undergo a stepwise change. The reaction window where the reaction rate is nonzero exists just in this interval, as in ref. 20 (upper part of Fig. 3a). Note that contrary to ref. 20 our active surface has no periodic boundary conditions.

Introducing into the model the $A_{\text{ads}}$ molecules' adsorption and diffusion over the metal and support surface (Fig. 3b), leads to considerable decrease of the unit $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverage region observed in the ZGB model and to a minor shift (from 0.51 to 0.48) of the reaction rate maximum to the smaller values of $Y_A$. Let us note that in the "basic" ZGB model increasing of the $A_{\text{ads}}$ diffusion intensity leads to a shift of the KPhT-1 to bigger values of the molar ratio $Y_A.^{22,23}$ (contrary to the behavior of our model), but does not influence the position of the continuous kinetic phase transition. On the other hand, introducing into the ZGB model only very slow diffusion of the $B_{\text{ads}}$ atoms leads to the complete disappearance of the region with the unit $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverage. $^{24}$ The foregoing effect is connected with an additional source of $A_{\text{ads}}$ molecules due to the spillover from the support to the active particle and was revealed for the first time by MC simulations in ref. 7. In that way, the spillover effect leads to the effective increase of the $A$ reagent partial pressure. Two reaction channels exist in this case. The first channel is the diffusion one. The reaction occurs *via* the adsorption of $A$-molecules on the support followed by diffusion to the particle and proceeds on the boundaries of the $B_{\text{ads}}$ atoms island with the dense core (Fig. 6a). The diffusion channel takes place at all values of $Y_A$, starting from very low $Y_A$. The second channel is an adsorption one that leads to the rarefaction of the dense core of adsorbed $B_{\text{ads}}$ island.

Let us discuss briefly the mechanism of unit $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverage formation at low $Y_A$. If diffusion is absent the single vacant sites inevitably existing in the randomly formed $B_{\text{ads}}$ layer eventually become two sites due to $A$ adsorption and further reaction with the neighboring $B_{\text{ads}}$ atom. The newly formed double sites are then filled preferentially with $B_{\text{ads}}$ ($P_{B_2} \gg P_A$). As long as such process requires the availability of $A_{\text{ads}}$ molecules, the unit $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverage can occur only at $Y_A \neq 0$. The continuous kinetic phase transition in the ZGB model appears at $Y_A \approx 0.39$ (Fig. 3a), when $A$ reagent begins to win in the competition for the vacant sites. The adsorption of $A$ species over the support surface with subsequent diffusion to the particle leads to the appearance of the additional source of $A_{\text{ads}}$ molecules due to the spillover from the support to the particle. This leads, in turn, to the shift of the continuous kinetic phase transition to the smaller values of $Y_A$ ($\approx 0.15$).

When the reaction proceeds on the non-regular roughened surfaces (dynamically equilibrated particle at $T = 500$ K Fig. 2b), all the regions with sharp stepwise changing of adsorbates coverage disappear Fig. 4 a-c (in this case the number of active sites convenient for $B_2$ adsorption is restricted and $\Theta_{\text{B}}$ is never equal to unity). Fig. 4a corresponds to the case when the reaction proceeds on the preliminary roughened but then frozen (rigid) surface of the catalytic particle and the metal atoms and $A_{\text{ads}}$ molecules are immobile (compare with Fig. 3a). The steady-state kinetic dependencies become smooth enough and in this case the reaction proceeds through the adsorption route only. It is very surprising but up to $Y_A \approx 0.4$ the steady-state reaction rate equals zero in spite of nonzero values of both $A_{\text{ads}}$ and $B_{\text{ads}}$ coverage of the particle surface. The same feature can be observed in Figs. 4b and 4c, illustrating correspondingly the cases of $A_{\text{ads}}$ adsorption and diffusion over the support (b) and the addition of the metal atoms diffusion (c). The explanation of this phenomenon follows from the distribution of $A_{\text{ads}}$ and $B_{\text{ads}}$ over the active sites of the roughened particle (Fig. 6b). One could see that the $B_{\text{ads}}$ adlayer is located on the flat patches of the particle surface and the $A_{\text{ads}}$ atoms are situated predominantly on the single defect sites of the active particle. At $Y_A < 0.4$ the equilibration process leads to the situation when all active sites of the particle are occupied ($\Theta_{\text{A}} + \Theta_{\text{B}} = 1$) but $A_{\text{ads}}$ and $B_{\text{ads}}$ atoms are sitting on the different heights of the metal columns. Therefore, according to the rules prescribed by ZGB model (the reaction between $A_{\text{ads}}$ and $B_{\text{ads}}$ proceeds when they are brought into the two neighboring active sites of the catalyst surface situated at the same level), the steady-state reaction rate should be zero. Note that when we switch the $Y_A$ from one value to the another, the reaction rate is nonzero under the above mentioned conditions only in the very beginning of the dynamic equilibration process.

In this case the kinetic dependencies (Fig. 7a) differ from those considering the spillover effect (Figs. 4b and 4c), first because of the more narrow reaction window, second because of the behavior of steady-state $A_{\text{ads}}$ ($Y_A$) and $B_{\text{ads}}$ ($Y_A$) kinetic dependencies. If we remove all the adsorbed species from the particle surface without changing its shape (that is possible in the computer experiments) then the particle reverts after the process of equilibration to its equilibrium shape, similar to that in Fig. 2b. Thus it has been shown (in the frames of the proposed model) that the shape of the active particle can change under the influence of the adsorbed layer, even in the absence of the adsorbate–metal interactions.

## 4. Conclusion
A statistical lattice model has been elaborated which permits one to take into account the change in the shape and surface morphology of the supported catalytic particle under the influence of the reaction media. It has been shown that the reaction kinetics on the nano-sized particle can be qualitatively and quantitatively different from those corresponding to the flat infinite surface due only to the change of shape and the surface morphology of the supported particle and spillover phenomena. We believe that the proposed approach, very likely too simplified now, can offer the new routes to model the catalytic reactions closer to reality.
