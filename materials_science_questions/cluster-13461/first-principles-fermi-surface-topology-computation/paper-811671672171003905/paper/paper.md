# Coexistence of one- and three-dimensional Fermi surfaces and heavy cyclotron mass in the molecular conductor (DMe-DCNQI)₂Cu

S. Uji, T. Terashima, and H. Aoki
National Research Institute for Metals, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305, Japan

J. S. Brooks
Physics Department, Boston University, Boston, Massachusetts 02215

R. Kato, H. Sawa, S. Aonuma, M. Tamura, and M. Kinoshita
Institute for Solid State Physics, The University of Tokyo, Roppongi, Minato-ku, Tokyo 106, Japan

(Received 27 June 1994; revised manuscript received 17 August 1994)

The Fermi surface of the molecular conductor (DMe-DCNQI)₂Cu (DMe-DCNQI=2,5-dimethyl- N,N'-dicyanoquinonediimine) has been investigated by the de Haas-van Alphen method. Seven different oscillations with heavy cyclotron masses are observed at frequencies lower than 3000 T. These effects arise from the three-dimensional (3D) Fermi surface which is dominated mainly by the Cu $3d$ orbital character. Many oscillations with frequency higher than 3000 T are detected for the magnetic field in the $ab$ plane-an effect which demonstrates the coexistence of the 1D and 3D Fermi surfaces.

Molecular conductors have attracted great interest due to their unique physical properties. These phenomena, which include superconductivity, spin- or charge-density wave states, are closely related to their low-dimensional electronic character. Much recent activity has centered on the understanding of the ground states of the onedimensional (1D) TMTSF (tetramethyltetraselenafulvalene) and two-dimensional (2D) BEDT-TTF [bis(ethylenedithio)tetrathiafulvalene] molecular conductors. Although there have been stimulating developments in their synthesis, the electronic structure of most molecular conductors has remained low dimensional. In this paper we present direct evidence for a 3D Fermi surface in a molecular conductor.

Recently, a molecular conductor containing Cu ions (DMe-DCNQI)₂Cu(DMe-DCNQI = 2,5-dimethyl-N,N'- dicyanoquinonediimine) was synthesized.¹ The crystal structure has a tetragonal symmetry with the space group $I4_1/a$. The planar DCNQI molecules are stacked in 1D columns along the tetragonal $c$ axis and they are interconnected by the tetrahedrally coordinated Cu ions.²,³ The 1D Fermi surfaces of this system are formed by the $p\pi$ orbitals of the DCNQI molecules. The Cu ions form a seven hold diamondlike lattice, and are found to be in a mixed valence state between $Cu^{2+}$ and $Cu^{+}$ states.⁴⁻⁸ The results show that the $3d$ level of the Cu ions lies close to the Fermi level. Consequently, if the band picture is available for the $3d$ electrons, the 3D energy band is expected to exist in addition to the 1D $p\pi$ energy band.²,⁷ The existence of the 3D energy band has been also suggested by other groups.³,⁸

Recently, a metal-insulator-metal reentrant transition is found in the partially deuterated system $^{10}$ and in the alloy system with the fully deuterated DMe-DCNQI. $^{14}$ The interesting feature is that the 3D static order of the valence of the $Cu$ ions $(... In order to investigate the Fermi surface, we have car- ried out the de Haas-van Alphen (dHvA) measurements for the single crystals of the undeuterated (DMe- DCNQI) $_{2} Cu$ . The samples are metallic down to $0.05 ~K$ . We observed many dHvA oscillations and determined the angular dependences of the frequencies. The results are compared with the calculated band structure.

Figure 1(a) presents the dHvA oscillation $(\alpha)$ at $0.05 ~K$  for the magnetic field parallel to the tetragonal $c$ axis. The dHvA measurements were done by a standard field modulation technique. The Fourier transform (FT) spec- trum is shown in the inset of the figure. The frequency of $477 ~T$ corresponds to $27 \%$ of the area of the Brillouin zone. On the other hand, we can observe many oscilla- tions when the magnetic field is applied in the $a b$ plane. The dHvA oscillations for $\omega=26^{\circ}$ are shown in Fig. 1(b), where $\omega$ is the angle between the magnetic field and the[110] direction. In the FT spectrum, we see low- frequency oscillation $(\delta_{2})$ , its second harmonic $(2 \delta_{2})$ , and high-frequency ones $(\varepsilon_{5}$ and $\varepsilon_{6})$ .

The angular dependences of the low frequencies(F<3000 T) are shown in Fig. 2. We find seven low- frequency oscillations $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta_{1}-\delta_{4})$ . The high frequency oscillations $\varepsilon_{1}, \varepsilon_{2},...(F>3000 ~T)$ are ob served only in the $a b$ plane (Fig. 3). In contrast to the low-frequency oscillations, the high-frequency oscilla- tions are detected in relatively limited angle ranges. The $\varepsilon_{9}$ and $\varepsilon_{10}$ oscillations, for instance, are observable only for $\omega=36^{\circ}-37^{\circ}$ and suddenly disappear for $\omega=35^{\circ}$ and38°

To understand the Fermi surface in detail, the elec- tronic band structure of this system is carefully calculat- ed by the tight-binding approximation. $^{2,6}$ The lattice pa rameters and the atomic coordinates determined at $20 ~K$ are used. $^{3}$ For the calculation, we consider the LUMO(lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) of the DCNQI molecules and the $d_{x y}$ orbital. The $d_{x z}$ and $d_{y z}$ orbitals have no significant contribution to the band structure be- cause their energy levels are lower than that of the $d_{x y}$ or bital. $^{2}$ Therefore, we neglected the $d_{x z}$ and $d_{y z}$ orbitals for simplicity. The results of the calculated band structure are presented in Fig. 4.

The band structure consists of the 1D and 3D energy bands. The finite overlap integrals between the $p \pi$ and $3 d$ orbitals remove the degeneracy of the $p \pi$ bands and generate a multiple Fermi surface. The 1D Fermi surface FS1 is purely $p \pi$ in character and is doubly degenerated.Another 1D Fermi surface FS2 is also formed by the $p \pi$ band, but is warped due to the $\pi-d$ overlap integrals. FS3 is the 3D Fermi surface and arises mainly from the nar- row $3 d$ band. We note that FS2 and FS3 touch at the point $P$ .

The calculated angular dependences of all the low frequencies are shown by the solid lines in Fig. 2. In spite of the simplicity of the band calculation, the agreement with the experimental results is very good.

Next, we consider the origin of the high-frequency oscillations ($F > 3000$ T) observed for the magnetic field in the $ab$ plane. It is difficult to explain the high-frequency oscillations only in terms of the orbits on FS3. Since FS2 and FS3 touch at $P$, the cyclotron orbits extending over FS2 and FS3 via the point $P$ are possible. The open circles in Fig. 3 exhibit the calculated results corresponding to the possible cyclotron orbits. The proposed cyclotron orbits of $\varepsilon_{5}$ and $\varepsilon_{8}$ are shown in Fig. 6. The frequencies of the $\varepsilon$ series seem to be explained by the above model. The calculated values are sensitive to the shapes of FS2 and FS3, so that the main reason for the disagreement with the experimental results is probably due to the details not included in the band calculation.

In the $\varepsilon$ series, some oscillations are detected in relatively wide angle ranges. The fact is explained by the magnetic breakdown effect$^{18}$ around the point $P$. Actually, the band calculation suggests that the magnetic breakdown around $P$ is possible above 10 T. There is a pronounced tendency that higher-frequency oscillation is observable in narrower angle range. The result is well described by the prediction of the band calculation that the larger cross-sectional area has the extremal value in the narrower angle range. We believe that the observation of the high-frequency oscillations demonstrates the coexistence of the 1D and 3D Fermi surfaces.

The band structure indicates that the magnetic breakdown around the point $P$ takes place more easily for $\omega \approx 45^{\circ}$ than for $\omega \approx 0^{\circ}$. This fact means that the observation of the dHvA oscillations in the low $\omega$ range is relatively difficult. This is a possible reason why the high-frequency oscillations are not observed for $\omega < 20^{\circ}$ On the other hand, the curvature factor of the Fermi surface should be considered$^{18}$ because the oscillation amplitude is proportional to the inverse of the curvature factor. However, it is impossible to make a meaningful estimation of the factor. Further improved band calculation may clarify this point.

We now turn to the cyclotron masses of the oscillations. The cyclotron masses are obtained by measuring the oscillation amplitudes in the temperature range between 0.05 and 0.3 K. The experimental effective masses $m_{c}^{\text{exp}}$ and the calculated band masses $m_{b}^{\text{cal}}$ are listed in Table I. Reliable estimations of the effective masses corresponding to the high-frequency oscillations cannot be obtained. Band-structure calculations of the type used in this work usually predict both Fermi surface shapes and the relative sizes of the effective masses reliably. However, there is an inherent uncertainty (factor of 2-3) in the absolute sizes of the predicted effective masses, which are sensitive to the input parameters. While the good agreement between calculation and experiment is therefore fortuitous, it may be said that the calculations do indicate that the mixing between the $p\pi$ and $3d$ orbitals provides a very substantial contribution to the large effective masses observed. The absence of the other measurements of the transfer integrals (e.g., optical) precludes a quantitative comparison of experiment and theory, so that little can be said about the influence of many-body effects in normalizing the effective mass.

In conclusion, the molecular conductor (DMe-DCNQI)$_2$Cu has the characteristic 1D and 3D Fermi surfaces, which are mainly formed by the $p\pi$ bands of the DCNQI molecules and the $3d$ bands of the Cu ions, respectively. The heavy cyclotron masses may arise from the density of states enhanced by the mixing between the $p\pi$ and the $3d$ orbitals.

The dHvA measurements were carried out by a top loading dilution refrigerator system with a superconducting magnet in National Research Institute for Metals.
