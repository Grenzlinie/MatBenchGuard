# FREE ENERGY OF FORMATION OF DROPLETS FROM VAPOR AND DEPENDENCE OF SURFACE TENSION ON RADIUS

FARID F. ABRAHAM

IBM Scientific Center, 2670 Hanover Street, Palo Alto, California 94304, U.S.A.

and

G. M. POUND

Materials Science Department, Stanford University, Stanford, California 94304, U.S.A.

Received 27 October 1967

It is demonstrated that a heretofore neglected contribution to the chemical potential of a small droplet arises from any variation of surface tension with radius. This contribution is evaluated by integration of the Gibbs-Duhem equation for several assumed relationships between surface tension and radius. It is concluded that the dependence of the free energy of formation of critical nuclei on surface tension is definitely not cubic, and exact relationships are given for the several assumed relationships. In the case of homogeneous nucleation of droplets from water vapor, the increase in surface tension required to bring about agreement between theory and cloud chamber observations is more nearly $50\%$ rather than $17\%$ as heretofore believed.

## 1. Introduction

According to Lothe and Pound $^{1,2,3)}$ and Abraham and Pound $^{4)}$, the equilibrium concentration of clusters in a supersaturated vapor is given by

$$
c(n)=\left(\frac{2\pi nmkT}{h^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{(2kT)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\pi I^{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\hbar^{3}}\frac{1}{q_{\text{rep}}}\exp\frac{-\Delta F^{0}(n)}{kT},
\tag{1}
$$

in which $n$ is the molecular number of the cluster, $m$ is the mass of a single molecule, $I$ is the moment of inertia of the cluster (assumed to be spherical), and $q_{\text{rep}}$ is the "replacement" factor $^{2,4)}$.

In terms of the capillarity approximation, in which macroscopic thermodynamic properties of a bulk equilibrium phase are ascribed to the small cluster, the radius of the cluster of critical size for nucleation is given by the Gibbs-Thomson formula†

$$
r^{*}=2\sigma_{0}v_{0}/kT\ln S,
\tag{2}
$$

where $\sigma_{0}$ is the surface tension of the bulk liquid with molecular volume $v_{0}$ and the supersaturation ratio $S$ equals the ratio of actual partial pressure to saturation vapor pressure over bulk liquid, $p/p_{0}$. The Helmholtz "free energy of formation" of the critical cluster from the vapor was evaluated by Gibbs as

$$
\Delta F^{0}(n^{*})=\frac{4\pi r^{*2}\sigma_{0}}{3}=\frac{16\pi v_{0}^{2}\sigma_{0}^{3}}{3(kT\ln S)^{2}}.
\tag{3}
$$

In order to bring theory into agreement with some of the cloud chamber observations of supersaturations necessary for the onset of appreciable nucleation rates, it is sometimes hypothesized that the surface tension for the bulk liquid appearing in eq. (3) should be replaced by a quantity $\sigma(r^{*})$ which is a function of the radius of the critical nucleus. Thus in view of eq. (3), it is sometimes asserted $^{5,6)}$ that the correct free energy of formation $\Delta F(n^{*})$ is

$$
\Delta F(n^{*})=[\sigma(r^{*})/\sigma_{0}]^{3}\Delta F^{0}(n^{*}).
\tag{4}
$$

According to this formula, an increase in $\sigma(r^{*})$ of about $17\%$ over that of the bulk phase is required to bring about agreement between theory and observation in the case of water vapor. It is the purpose of the present paper to show that eq. (4) is incorrect and to derive the correct expressions for several dependences of surface tension on curvature. Integration of the Gibbs-Duhem relation using the Tolman $^{7)**}$ expression for

† Small contributions from translation and rotation of the critical nucleus and from its replacement factor will be ignored in this treatment.

** We assume the same functional form but require that $\sigma(r)$ increases as $r$ becomes smaller.

$\sigma(r)$ yields a result which reduces, to a better approximation, to
$$\Delta F(n^{*}) = [\sigma(r^{*})/\sigma_{0}] \Delta F^{0}(n^{*}). \tag{5}$$

Further, eq. (5) is found to be exact if the Preston⁸) dependence is used. In this case the increase in $\sigma(r^{*})$ over $\sigma_{0}$ required to bring agreement with observation in the case of water vapor is $60\%$ instead of $17\%$. Other dependences without physical bases have been investigated to exhibit the mathematical character of the problem. These turn out not to be well approximated by either eqs. (4) or (5).

### 2. Integration of the Gibbs-Duhem relation

The Gibbs-Duhem relation for a droplet at constant temperature and variable volume $V$, molecular number $n$, and surface area $A$ is⁹)
$$-V \mathrm{d} P+n \mathrm{d} \mu+A \mathrm{d} \sigma=0, \tag{6}$$
where $P$ is pressure, $\mu$ is chemical potential, and the dependence of both intensive and extensive parameters on $n$ is implicit. To a good approximation, the molecular volume is a constant, $V/n = v_{0}$, and thus
$$\mathrm{d} \mu=v_{0} \mathrm{d} P-v_{0}(A/V) \mathrm{d} \sigma. \tag{7}$$

For the case of a spherical drop, $A/V = 3/r$, and accordingly
$$\mathrm{d} \mu=v_{0} \mathrm{d} P-(3v_{0}/r) \mathrm{d} \sigma. \tag{8}$$

In order to proceed further, one must now assume the form of the dependence of surface tension on radius of curvature. It is still by no means clear what this dependence should be. Direct experiments have been made by Reinhold and Rücker¹⁰) on the surface tension of thin films of soap solution. They have interpreted their measurements as indicating substantially constant values of surface tension down to a thickness of $5 \times 10^{-6}$ cm, followed for thinner films first by decreases in surface tension of several percent and then later by increases in surface tension $(\sim 1 \times 10^{-6}$ cm). Sir J. J. Thomson¹¹) assumed that the surface tension of water droplets would pass through a minimum followed by one or more maxima on going to smaller and smaller droplets. Benson and Shuttleworth¹²), using a second nearest-neighbor approximation, predicted less than a $15\%$ reduction in the surface tension of small fragments of atomic crystals containing of the order of 13 atoms. On the other hand, Oriani and Sundquist⁵) estimate a $20$-$25\%$ increase in surface tension due to surface curvature from a "broken-bond" model, where the number of molecules of the droplet ranged from 10 to 116. On the basis of a quasi-thermodynamic argument, Tolman⁷) proposed the relationship
$$\sigma(r)=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{1+2\delta/r}, \quad \frac{2\delta}{r} \ll 1, \tag{9}$$
in which $\delta$ is the thickness of the transition layer at the surface, presumed to be several molecular diameters. A general statistical mechanical theory of the interfacial phenomena by Kirkwood and Buff¹³) and Buff¹⁴,¹⁵) confirmed the results of Tolman's quasi-thermodynamic theory and leads to the conclusion that the surface tension of small drops decreases with increasing curvature, as long as $2\delta/r \ll 1$. At this point, is is well worth mentioning a remark by Tolman⁷): "Under the circumstances, an important part of our empirical interest must actually lie in the indirect verification of theoretical results (on surface tension) if they prove successful in explaining important features of the phenomena of droplet behavior in laboratory apparatus, in engineering appliances, and in the earth's atmosphere".

### 3. The Tolman functional form

For the purpose of demonstrating the general effect of a variable surface tension, let us assume the functional form of Tolman
$$\sigma(r)=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{1+a/r}, \tag{10}$$
where $a$ is an arbitrary constant. Since $a$ may be of either sign, the following result is applicable to situations in which the surface tension either increases or decreases with radius of curvature. Substituting eq. (10) in eq. (8) and integrating from $r = \infty$ to $r = r^{*}$, we obtain
$$\mu(r^{*})-\mu(\infty)=v_{0}[P(r^{*})-P(\infty)]+Q(r^{*}), \tag{11}$$
where
$$Q(r^{*})=\frac{-3v_{0}\sigma(r^{*})}{r^{*}}+\frac{3v_{0}\sigma_{0}}{a}\ln\left(1+\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right). \tag{12}$$

From the equality of chemical potentials between the liquid and vapor states,
$$\mu(r^{*})-\mu(\infty)=k T \ln S.\qquad(13)$$

Since $P(r^{*})-P(\infty)=2 \sigma(r^{*}) / r^{*}$ (Laplace's equation), eq. (11) reduces to
$$k T \ln S=\frac{-v_{0} \sigma_{0}}{r^{*}}\left(1+\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right)^{-1}+\frac{3 v_{0} \sigma_{0}}{a} \ln \left(1+\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right).\qquad(14)$$

We can solve eq. (14) for $r^{*}$ in terms of the ratio $a / r^{*}$ and insert the result in the exact expression for the free energy of formation of a droplet of $n^{*}$ molecules (see e.g., ref. 9):
$$\Delta F\left(n^{*}\right)=\frac{4}{3} \pi r^{* 2} \sigma\left(r^{*}\right).\qquad(15)$$

This yields
$$\frac{\Delta F\left(n^{*}\right)}{\Delta F^{0}\left(n^{*}\right)}=\frac{\sigma\left(r^{*}\right)}{\sigma_{0}}\left[\frac{3}{2} \frac{r^{*}}{a} \ln \left(1+\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right)^{-1}\right]^{2},\qquad(16)$$
where $\Delta F_{0}(n^{*})$ is given by eq. (3).

For small ratios of $a / r^{*}$, this reduces in adequate approximation to eq. (5). However, for the interesting case of a water drop cited in the Introduction (i.e., $\Delta F(n^{*}) / \Delta F_{0}(n^{*})=1.6$), we determine that $a / r^{*}=$ $-0.288$ by solving eq. (16) exactly. Therefore, eq. (16) requires an increase of $\sim 40 \%$ in $\sigma(r)$. This is to be compared with the $60 \%$ increase in $\sigma(r)$ required by eq. (5) (linear dependence on $\sigma(r)$ ) and the $17 \%$ increase in $\sigma(r)$ required by eq. (4) (cubic dependence on $\sigma(r)$ ) in order to bring about agreement of theory with observation on water vapor.

### 4. The Preston functional form
In developing a semi-empirical formula for the binding energy of the atomic nucleus, Preston $^{8}$ (p. 125, eq. (6-3)) obtains a "surface-tension" term of the form
$$\sigma(r)=\sigma_{0}\left(1+\frac{b}{r^{2}}\right)\qquad(17)$$
in which $b$ is a negative constant. We assume that $b$ may be positive or negative. Upon performing the Gibbs-Duhem integration and proceeding just as indicated in the preceding paragraph, one finds eq. (5) exactly, with no approximation. In this situation the required increase in surface tension for the case of water is exactly $60 \%$ instead of the erroneous value of17% obtained from eq.(4).

### 5. A general expression for any power of $a / r$
Let us arbitrarily assume a general dependence of surface tension on radius of the form
$$\sigma(r)=\sigma_{0}\left[1 \pm\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^{m}\right],\qquad(18)$$
in which $m$ is an integer. The procedure, described above, leads to the relation
$$\frac{\Delta F\left(n^{*}\right)}{\Delta F^{0}\left(n^{*}\right)}=\frac{\sigma\left(r^{*}\right)}{\sigma_{0}}\left[1 \pm\left(\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right)^{m}\left(1-\frac{3}{2} \frac{m}{m+1}\right)\right]^{2}. \quad(19)$$

The roots, $a / r^{*}$, of eq. (19) for the positive sign and $\Delta F(n^{*}) / \Delta F_{0}(n^{*})=1.6$ were solved numerically as a function of $m$, and the results, along with the corresponding values of $\sigma(r^{*}) / \sigma_{0}$, are given in table 1. It is seen that only in the case $m=2$, the Preston equation(17), is the agreement with eq. (5) good (in fact exact). For all other values of $m$, neither eq. (4) nor (5) is a good approximation; i.e., $\sigma(r^{*}) / \sigma_{0}$ is neither 1.2 nor 1.6.

Finally, assuming a positive sign in eq. (19) and setting the right-hand side of this equation equal to $1+h$, we obtain
$$h=-\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right)^{2 m}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{a}{r^{*}}\right)^{3 m},\qquad(20)$$

for $m \gg 1$. One notes that $a/r > 1$ for positive $h$. For $a/r > 1, m \gg 1$, the last term dominates because of the high power. In this case, $a/r^{*} \cong (4h)^{1/3m}$. Therefore $a/r \to 1$ for $m \gg 1$, whereupon $\sigma(r^{*}) \to 2\sigma_{0}$.

We conclude, from the analysis presented in this study, that one is required to invoke a much larger increase in surface tension $(\sim 50\%)$ for a critical water droplet than heretofore believed. Therefore it is more difficult to reconcile theory with experiment by postulating an "ad hoc" increase of surface tension with curvature, because the required increase in $\sigma(r)$ is a large percentage of the bulk value. One way out of this problem is to recognize that the surface free energy can be of a much more subtle nature than just the product of macroscopic surface tension and spherical surface area. However, we do not undertake an analysis of this aspect in the present paper.
