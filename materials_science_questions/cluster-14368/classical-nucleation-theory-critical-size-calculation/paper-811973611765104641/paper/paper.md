## Abstract
The droplet formation in a supersaturated vapor which may occur during the expansion of an excited blob of nuclear matter in the metastable region at subnuclear densities is investigated. The free energy change accompanying the formation of a drop is calculated as a function of droplet radius for various saturation ratios on the basis of Fisher's model. The results are related to the experimental data.

## 1. Introduction
The droplet formation in hot nuclear matter, is one of the most interesting phenomena in heavy ion reactions and astrophysical objects. Liquid-gas phase instabilities and droplet formation in nuclear reactions have been extensively discussed in the context of relativistic [1,2] and non-relativistic models [3–7]. A compressed and hot blob of nuclear matter is formed in the collision of two heavy-ions or two slabs of nuclear matter. Assuming this blob to be close to thermodynamic equilibrium it will expand and enter the region of subnuclear densities where it becomes unstable to density inhomogeneities. However, heavy ion reactions are naturally time dependent phenomena so that, it is suspect whether or not there is sufficient time to reach a thermodynamic equilibrium. We know that the total time spent in the high density ($n$) and temperature ($T$) domain is only about 10 fm/c so that phase transition can not fully develop in that region. At low densities and temperatures ($n<n_{\rm o}$ and $T<T_{\rm c}$, $n_{\rm o}$ denotes the saturation density and $T_{\rm c}$ the critical temperature), however, time scales are much longer than 1000 fm/c, so that there should be sufficient time to reach a state of thermal and chemical equilibrium. In recent years, several groups have investigated whether the break up of nuclear matter into several intermediate hot droplets is a phase transition and if so, whether it is related to the liquid-gas-type phase transition. Even if there exist some theoretical and experimental evidence of this phase transition [8–10], so far much uncertainty remains in its nature. Recently, strong circumstantial evidence for this transition, has been found and discoverable quantities such as vapor pressure, evaporation enthalpy, and surface energy are calculated [9] on the basis of Fisher's model [11]. Here in this paper, we present a calculation of the free energy change accompanying the formation of a primary hot nuclear droplet versus the radius of the droplets at various supersaturation ratios on the basis of Fisher's droplet model.

The development of density inhomogeneities in any initially homogeneous matter can be classified as spinodal decomposition and nucleation of droplets. Spinodal decomposition occurs as a result of growth of small amplitude fluctuations to such an extent that the matter is disrupted and it breaks up within the isothermal spinodal line. During the nucleation of localized region, however, droplets may be formed as a result of large amplitude fluctuations everywhere within the coexistence line. We presented our results on the spinodal and coexistence lines of nuclear matter in Ref. [6], where the critical temperature $T_{\rm c}$ and the critical density $n_{\rm c}$ are found to be 16.6 MeV and $0.055\ \text{fm}^{-3}$ respectively. In this paper, we will not pretend to give a detailed account of the fragmentation, but rather to investigate droplet formation in the metastable region which is located between the coexistence and spinodal lines where $T<T_{\rm c}$ and $n<n_{\rm c}$.

## 2. Droplet formation in a nucleonic vapor
The vapor phase can be prepared in the supersaturated state of metastable region of $n<n_{\rm c}$ and then droplets of liquid phase will be prepared outside the coexistence line. In order to study the evolution of a liquid droplet formation, we choose two points in the vapor and liquid phases, where the pressures of liquid and gas phases are equal but the densities are not. For a sufficiently slow process in a system of the Gibbs free energy $G$, volume $V$, pressure $P$, entropy $S'$, number of particle $N$ and chemical potential $\mu$, we know the relation,

$$
{\rm d}G = V{\rm d}P - S'{\rm d}T + \mu{\rm d}N. \tag{1}
$$

Assuming that the number of particles $N$ is conserved we can write down ${\rm d}G = V{\rm d}P$ at fixed $T$. One may calculate the vapor pressure of a pressurized liquid by using the fact that at equilibrium $\mu_{\rm l} = \mu_{\rm g}$ and for any change that preserves equilibrium we can write ${\rm d}\mu_{\rm g} = {\rm d}\mu_{\rm l}$. If the pressure $P$ on the liquid is increased by ${\rm d}P$, the chemical potential of the liquid changes by ${\rm d}\mu_{\rm l} = V{\rm d}P$ and the chemical potential of the vapor changes by ${\rm d}\mu_{\rm g} = V{\rm d}p$ where ${\rm d}p$ is the change in its vapor pressure $p$. If we use the ideal gas relationship $pV = NkT$ for the nucleonic gas then we can write

$$
{\rm d}\mu_{\rm g} = (NkT/p){\rm d}p, \tag{2}
$$

$$
{\rm d}\mu_{\rm l} = V{\rm d}P. \tag{3}
$$

Equating the two changes in chemical potential and integrating with respect to the fact that when there is an additional pressure $\Delta P$ on the liquid, the vapor pressure will be $p_2$ (when there is no additional pressure acting on the

liquid, the pressure experienced by the liquid is equal to the normal vapor pressure $p_1$); we obtain

$$
NkT \int_{p_1}^{p_2} \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{p} = \int_{p_1}^{p_1+\Delta P} V\mathrm{d}P, \tag{4}
$$

$$
VnkT \ln(p_2/p_1) = V\Delta P \tag{5}
$$

where $p_1/p_2$ is called supersaturation ratio denoted by $S$ and $N=nV$. This equation represents the free energy change for the transfer at constant temperature and pressure of a droplet from the vapor into the liquid phase. In the context of molecular physics it is a good approximation to use the ideal gas relationship between chemical potential and pressure at fixed $T$. However, one should bear in mind that this generally will not be a very good approximation for a nearly degenerate Fermi gas such as nuclear matter.

The probability of formation of the droplets which can be estimated by calculating the free energy change $\Delta G$ accompanying the formation of a drop of radius $r$ and containing $A$ nucleons, is proportional to $\exp(-\Delta G/T)$. Here $\Delta G$ is given by

$$
\Delta G = 4\pi r^2 \sigma - \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \right) nT \ln S + 3 T \tau \ln(r/r_\mathrm{o}) \tag{6}
$$

where it has been assumed that the droplet is essentially of uniform density so that $r=r_\mathrm{o}A^{1/3}$ and, particle number density of hot liquid drop is choosen $0.15 \mathrm{fm}^{-3}$ at $T=10\,\mathrm{MeV}$, which is very close to the equilibrium density $n=0.16 \mathrm{fm}^{-3}$. The surface tension for nuclear matter at low temperatures is $\sigma \approx 1\,\mathrm{MeV} \cdot \mathrm{fm}^{-2}$. Here, we should remark that $\sigma$ might be significantly reduced at high temperatures but, one should keep in mind that the cold fragments are formed after evaporating a few particles from the primary hot droplets. The critical exponent of nuclear matter is $\tau=2.2$ which has been predicted both theoretically and experimentally [8].
In order to see what our results yield, we have, in Fig. 1, determined a plot of $\Delta G$ against $r$ for various supersaturation ratios at $T=10\,\mathrm{MeV}$, by means of Eq. (6). The first term on the righthand side of Eq. (6) gives the change in free energy on formation the liquid/vapor interface, the second term arises from the transition from vapor to liquid, and the third term has been added to take account of the fact that the surface closes on itself which reduces the total entropy associated with the surface [11]. Differentiation of Eq. (6) with respect to $r$, yields

$$
8\pi \sigma r^* - 4\pi nT \ln S r^{*2} + \frac{3T\tau}{r^*} = 0. \tag{7}
$$

Here, we have obtained the values of the critical radius $r^*$ and the free energy increase $\Delta G^*$ corresponding to $r=r^*$, numerically from Eq. (6). The results for $r^*$ are $2.645\,\mathrm{fm}$, $2.006\,\mathrm{fm}$ and $1.769\,\mathrm{fm}$ for $S=2$, 3 and 4 respectively, and $S=1$ means that there is no droplet formation. The results given in Fig. 1 are very similar to the figure for critical droplets of water [12].

In recent years, several groups have addressed that the data on fragment yields support that heavy ion reactions are consistent with droplet formation in a supersaturated vapor. For instance, in Fig. 2, we have shown the data of ALADIN collaboration for the charge distribution of Au reactions on C, Al, Cu and Pb at $600\,\mathrm{MeV}$/nucleon [13], where it is assumed that the cross section for the production of a charged fragment is proportional to the number of fragments of a given nucleon number. The minimum in each curve corresponds to the maximum probability of the formation of a critical sized droplet. One may easily calculate the droplet radius from the value of minimum $Z$ relating to $A$ and corresponding supersaturation ratio from Fig. 1. As can be seen also in Refs [14,15], size of the critical droplets are increasing with excitation energies and it also depends on the nature of each nuclei. ### 3. Conclusions
In summary, we have presented a transparent derivation of the mechanism for the droplet formation process on the basis of Fisher's model. We have, for simplicity, neglected some physical effects, such as Coulomb interaction, one-body dissipation and the additional entropy contri- bution of light particles (p, n, d, t, $^3$He...). Actually, we should remark that the hot nuclear droplets evaporate particles and change the distribution of protons and neutrons. Therefore, one should involve the effects of Coulomb and evaporation for a more complete calculation. Thus, our results can be quite significant, especially for making a comparison with experimental data of heavy ion reactions, in the yield of the fragments of $A \geq 6$, when assuming that binary fission is not dominant for producing the yield of the fragments. For the lighter fragments, the four-body alpha-clustering could be more important at low densities. Alpha-clustering could have a relatively small effects on stable matter at $T \geq 10$ MeV, but, it has a large effect on $T \leq 5$ MeV low-density nuclear matter. The one-body dissipation (interaction with the nuclear surface) could also be very important especially for $T \leq 5\,\text{MeV}$.

In conclusion, further analysis of the above mentioned effects would be worthwhile.
