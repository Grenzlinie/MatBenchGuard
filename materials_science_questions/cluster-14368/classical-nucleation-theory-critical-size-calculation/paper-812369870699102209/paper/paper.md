## Abstract
Several mechanisms for the production of polar stratospheric cloud (PSC) particles are investigated using the classical theories of nucleation and freezing and the multicomponent condensation theory. These mechanisms invoke particle compositions ranging from binary $(H_{2}SO_{4}/H_{2}O)$ solution, solid sulfuric acid tetrahydrate (SAT) and ternary $(HNO_{3}/H_{2}O/H_{2}SO_{4})$ solution to binary $(HNO_{3}/H_{2}O)$ solution and solid nitric acid trihydrate (NAT). Empirical relations, derived from classical nucleation studies, are used to calculate the surface energies required in calculations of nucleation and freezing. Using these data, we calculate that the nucleation of nitric acid solutions or solid phases onto SAT particles is not efficient. Homogeneous freezing of SAT or NAT from ternary solutions does not occur under stratospheric conditions. Homogeneous freezing of water ice can occur at temperatures near the frost point of pure water. Heterogeneous freezing is a strong function of the contact parameter between the emergent crystal and the initiating seed particle. Heterogeneous freezing of the stratospheric aerosol to SAT and NAT at temperatures above the frost point is not ruled out by our calculations. If formed, NAT can deplete the gas phase nitric acid concentration, by condensational growth, more efficiently than ternary droplets. We conclude that the most likely route to type 1 PSC particles is via condensational growth of ternary solution droplets followed by rapid freezing to NAT, SAT, and water ice at temperatures near the ice frost point. The particles formed are then stable and can reduce nitric acid vapor pressures to the saturation vapor pressure over NAT at all temperatures below the NAT point. Such a mechanism is consistent with observations.

## Introduction
Heterogeneous chemical reactions on the surface of polar stratospheric cloud (PSC) particles are believed to initiate the catalytic destruction of ozone in the polar lower stratosphere. It is important to know, therefore, the phase and composition of PSC particles so that their reactivity may be studied. To date, it has been established that (1) two broad classes of PSC exist, one of which (type 1) forms at some temperature $(T_{PSC})$ above the frost-point, and the other of which forms at the frost point $(T_{ice})$ [Poole and McCormick, 1988a]; (2) type 1 PSC particles contain large amounts of nitric acid [Fahey et al., 1989; Pueschel et al., 1989]; and (3) some type 1 particles (type 1a) are relatively large and anisometric, others (type 1b) are smaller and more spherical [Toon et al., 1990].

Several proposals have been put forward regarding the phase and composition of type 1 PSCs (below we will refer to composition only, since the phase of a proposed composition is implicit in its name). These proposed compositions are nitric acid trihydrate (NAT) [Toon et al., 1986; Hanson and Mauersberger, 1988a]; nitric acid dihydrate (NAD) [Ritzhaupt and Devlin, 1991; Worsnop et al., 1992]; supercooled binary $HNO_{3}$-$H_{2}O$ solution (SBS) [Hanson, 1990]; supercooled ternary $HNO_{3}$-$H_{2}SO_{4}$-$H_{2}O$ solution (STS) [Zhang et al., 1993; Carslaw et al., 1994; Tabazadeh et al., 1994]; or glassy solid solution (NAG). This last composition has only been observed in the laboratory at very low temperatures [Koehler et al., 1992; Ritzhaupt and Devlin, 1991] and so will not be discussed below. The liquid "background" aerosol at cold temperatures can dissolve sufficient nitric acid that its large particle fraction may register a "PSC" signal on remote sensing devices such as lidars and satellite instruments [Carslaw et al., 1994; Drdla et al., 1994].

The facts that nitric acid solutions do not freeze into NAT easily at temperatures around 250 K in the labo- ratory [Hanson and Mauersberger, 1988a] and that the initial mole fractions of vapor deposited nitric acid films are much less than the mole fraction of $HNO_3$ in NAT [Marti and Mauersberger, 1993] also support the idea that a substantial nucleation barrier exists to the for- mation of NAT.

# Nucleation, Freezing, and Condensation

## Vapour-Liquid Nucleation

According to the general formula of statistical physics the probability of a fluctuation producing an embryo is proportional to $\exp(-\Delta G^*/kT)$, where $\Delta G^*$ is the minimum work needed to form the nucleus (the "crit- ical cluster"), $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $T$ is temperature in Kelvins. The homogeneous binary nu- cleation rate $I$ can be predicted as

$$
I=N_{\mathrm{vap}} R_{\mathrm{av}} Z \exp \frac{-\Delta G^{*}}{k T}. \tag{1}
$$

Here $N_{\text{vap}}$ is the total concentration of the vapor in the gas phase. The details of the numerical calculation of the average condensation rate $R_{\mathrm{av}}$ and the Zeldovich nonequilibrium factor $Z$ are given in our recent paper [Kulmala and Laaksonen, 1990].

The Gibbs free energy change $\Delta G^*$ of the critical clus- ter is obtained from the Kelvin equations [e.g., Wilemski 1984]:

$$
\Delta G_{\text {Homo }}^{*}=\frac{4}{3} \pi r^{* 2} \sigma. \tag{2}
$$

Here $r^*$ is the radius of the critical cluster and $\sigma$ is the surface tension, usually referred to below as the liquid- vapour surface energy. Surface energies are discussed in detail in section 3.2.

If the nucleation takes place heterogeneously, i.e., is initiated by a preexisting particle, the theory is some- what altered. The Gibbs free energy of formation of a critical cluster from a binary mixture of vapors onto a curved surface is given by the expression [Fletcher, 1958]:

$$
\Delta G_{\mathrm{Het}}^{*}=\frac{1}{2} \Delta G_{\mathrm{Homo}}^{*} f(m, x). \tag{3}
$$

Here

$$
\begin{gathered}
f(m, x)=1+\left(\frac{1-m x}{g}\right)^{3}+ \\
x^{3}\left[2-3\left(\frac{x-m}{g}\right)+\left(\frac{x-m}{g}\right)^{3}\right]+3 m x^{2}\left(\frac{x-m}{g}-1\right) \quad(4)
\end{gathered}
$$

with

$$
g=\left(1+x^{2}-2 m x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}. \tag{5}
$$


$x = \frac{R_p}{r^*}$ and $R_p$ the radius of the solid surface. The contact angle $\theta$ is given by $\cos\theta = m = \frac{\sigma_{13}-\sigma_{23}}{\sigma_{12}}$ where $\sigma_{ij}$ is the surface free energy between the phases $i$ and $j$. The gas phase is indexed by 1, the cluster by 2 and the substrate by 3. Below, we will drop the second subscript in $\sigma_{ij}$ if phase $j$ is vapour in air. The contact parameter $m$ is discussed in more detail in section 3.3.

The nucleation rate for heterogeneous nucleation ($\text{m}^{-2}\ \text{s}^{-1}$) is given by (see *Lazaridis et al.* [1992] for details of the numerics)
$$
I_{\text{Het}} = N^{\text{ads}} R_{\text{av}} Z \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G_{\text{Het}}^*}{kT}\right). \tag{6}
$$
where $N^{\text{ads}}$ is the number of vapor molecules adsorbed on the substrate per unit area. The nucleation probability for one particle within some "nucleation time," $t$, is [e.g., *Lazaridis et al.*, 1992]
$$
P = 1 - \exp\left(-4\pi R_{\text{p}}^2 I_{\text{Het}} t\right). \tag{7}
$$

### Vapor-Solid Nucleation

When the nucleating cluster is in a crystalline phase, as with NAT (and SAT, were it to be formed directly from the vapor), the mole fraction of the cluster is known, and the theory is somewhat simplified. Let the crystal density of NAT be $\rho_{\text{NAT}}$ and the mass of one NAT "molecule" be $m_{\text{NAT}}$. Denoting acid with $a$ and water with $w$, the number of "molecules" in a cluster is $n_{\text{NAT}} = n_{\text{a}} = n_{\text{w}}/3$. So
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G(n_{\text{a}}, n_{\text{b}}) &= -3n_{\text{NAT}}kT\ln\frac{p_{\text{w}}}{p_{\text{w,xt}}} - \\
&n_{\text{NAT}}kT\ln\frac{p_{\text{a}}}{p_{\text{a,xt}}} + 4\pi r^2 \sigma_{\text{NAT}} \tag{8} \\
&= -n_{\text{NAT}}kT\ln\frac{p_{\text{w}}^3 p_{\text{a}}}{K_{\text{t}}} + 4\pi r^2 \sigma_{\text{NAT}} \tag{9}
\end{aligned}
$$
where $p_i$ is the partial vapor in the gaseous phase and $p_{i,\text{xt}}$ is the equilibrium vapor pressure over the crystal. Now, $p_{i,\text{xt}} = p_{i,\text{NAT}}$ and $p_{\text{w,NAT}}^3 p_{\text{a,NAT}} = K_{\text{t}}$ where $K_{\text{t}}$ is the equilibrium constant for NAT [*Hanson and Mauersberger*, 1988a; *Smith*, 1990].

We will also use the relation $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_{\text{NAT}} = n_{\text{NAT}} m_{\text{NAT}}$, and have for the critical cluster
$$
r_{\text{NAT}}^* = \frac{2\sigma_{\text{NAT}} m_{\text{NAT}}}{kT \rho_{\text{NAT}} \ln S_{\text{NAT}}}, \tag{10}
$$
where $S_{\text{NAT}} = \frac{p_{\text{w}}^3 p_{\text{a}}}{K_{\text{t}}}$. The energy barrier is
$$
\Delta G_{\text{NAT}}^* = \frac{4}{3}\pi(r_{\text{NAT}}^*)^2 \sigma_{\text{NAT}}. \tag{11}
$$

These expressions are equivalent to that given by *Arnold* [1992]; we have simply made more explicit their derivation the from classical nucleation theory [see *Volmer*, 1939].

### Freezing

The freezing rate, i.e., the rate of appearance of crystalline nuclei in a certain liquid volume, can be calculated theoretically using classical nucleation theory [*Turnbull*, 1950]. The expression for the homogeneous freezing rate is
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\text{f}} &= n(kT/h)\exp(-\Delta G_{\text{a}}/kT) \times \\
&\exp\left(-\frac{(16\pi/3)\sigma_{\text{sl}}^3 N_{\text{Av}}^2 v^2}{kT \Delta H_{\text{f}}^2(T-T_0)^2/T_0^2}\right) \tag{12}
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\sigma_{\text{sl}}$ is the surface energy between the solid and the liquid phases, $N_{\text{Av}}$ is the Avogadro number, $v$ is the liquid volume per molecule, $\Delta H_{\text{f}}$ denotes the enthalpy of freezing per mole, $T_0$ is the melting temperature, $n$ is the molecular concentration in the liquid phase, $h$ is Planck's constant, and $\Delta G_{\text{a}}$ is the diffusion-activation energy across the phase boundary [e.g., *Pruppacher and Klett*, 1978]. Equation (12) has been derived for one-component systems, but it also applies to the formation of two-component solid phases (such as SAT and NAT) out of stoichiometric solutions. If the solution is not stoichiometric, the argument of the second exponential factor in eq. (12) (which is equal to $-\Delta G_{\text{Homo}}^*/kT$) must be replaced by [see *Pruppacher and Klett*, 1978; *Jensen et al.*, 1991]
$$
-\Delta G_{\text{Homo}}^*/kT = -\frac{(16\pi/3)\sigma_{\text{sl}}^3}{kT} \times \frac{1}{\left(\Delta H_{\text{f}}(T-T_0)/T_0 + RT\left(\frac{1-\chi_{\text{s}}}{\chi_{\text{s}}}\ln\frac{A_{\text{wl}}}{A_{\text{ws}}} + \ln\frac{A_{\text{al}}}{A_{\text{as}}}\right)\right)^2} \tag{13}
$$
where $\chi$ is the acid mole fraction. The Gibbs free energy could, of course, be expressed in terms of vapor pressures rather than supercooling [e.g., *Byers*, 1965; *Luo et al.*, 1992].

If there are foreign particles present in the liquid which can initiate the crystal formation, the freezing takes place heterogeneously, and the rate is obtained from
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\text{f}} &= n_{\text{s}}(kT/h)\exp(-\Delta G_{\text{a}}/kT) \times \\
&\exp(-\Delta G_{\text{Homo}}^* f(m, x)/2kT). \tag{14}
\end{aligned}
$$
Here the function $f(m, x)$ is calculated using eqs. (4) and (5), and $n_{\text{s}}$ denotes the number of molecules in contact with the substrate per unit area.

### Condensation

Supercooled sulfuric acid-water droplets may grow by binary water-nitric acid condensation. Sulfuric acid is assumed to be a nonvolatile substance. In the case of binary condensation the condensational growth of a droplet (with total mass $M_{\text{d}} = \Sigma_i M_{\text{di}}$) is governed by expressions given by *Kulmala et al.* [1993a, b]. The mass fluxes toward a droplet are obtained using a quasi-steady-state approach, replacing the explicit droplet temperature dependence by the dependence on the products of latent heats and mass fluxes, linearizing

with respect to mass fluxes and neglecting the effects of the Stefan flow and the radiative heat transport.

NAT condensation is considered in the following way: the mole fraction of the acid in the particle is constant (0.25) and the ratio of molar fluxes to the crystal is, therefore, also constant (i.e., $(M_{\nabla 2} I_{1})/(M_{\nabla 1} I_{2}) = 3$, where $I_{i}$ is the mass flux and the subscript 1 denotes water). The one unknown mass flux can be determined by means of the ordinary unary mass flux expression [e.g., *Pruppacher and Klett*, 1978; *Kulmala and Vesala*, 1991]. The surface kinetic resistance [*MacKenzie and Haynes*, 1992], which is caused by the requirement for impinging molecules to be accommodated at active sites in the crystal lattice, is not considered here. It is estimated to be small when supersaturations are large, as is the case in type 1 PSC formation under conditions of rapid cooling.

# Physicochemical Data

## Densities

The densities of ice and supercooled water were calculated using expressions given by *Pruppacher and Klett* [1978]. The density of NAT at 85 K is equal to 1.62 g cm⁻³ [*Taesler et al.*, 1975]. The density of SAT at 83 K is equal to 1.59 g cm⁻³ [Kjallman and Olovsson, 1972]. There is probably a small temperature dependence for these crystal densities. This dependence is not considered here and will, in any case, result in only a small change in the nucleation probability. The density of nitric acid-water binary solutions were adopted from *Granzhan and Laktionova* [1975]. The density of sulfuric acid solutions were calculated using a polynomial fit to the data presented by *Perry and Green* [1984] and *Jaecker-Voirol* [1988]. The density of STS is estimated simply as the mass fraction average of the separate values for binary solutions (note that our simulations are not very sensitive to the liquid density).

## Surface Energies and Diffusion-Activation Energies

It can be seen from eqs. (1), (2), and (12) above that the nucleation and freezing rates are extremely sensitive to the surface energies between the relevant phases. Temperature and composition dependent expressions for the liquid-vapor surface energy per unit area (i.e., surface tension) of each liquid can be found in the literature. For sulfuric acid-water solutions we use [*Sabinina and Terpugov*, 1935]

\end{aligned}
$$

where $\chi$ is the acid mole fraction and $0.15 < \chi <$ 0.25. For nitric acid-water we have [*Granzhan and Lak-tionova*, 1975]

Equations (15)-(18) are based on surface energy measurements performed at temperatures above 283 K, and it is therefore somewhat questionable to use them at stratospheric temperatures. We discuss the temperature dependences of the liquid-vapor surface energies below, together with the temperature dependences of the solid-vapor surface energies.

There are a number of methods available for the estimation of liquid-solid surface energies. One method involves the calculation of unused hydrogen bonds at an interface: this has been successfully applied to the freezing of pure water [*Pruppacher and Klett*, 1978] and attempted for SAT [*Luo et al.*, 1992, 1994]. However, to be used accurately, this method requires information on lattice parameters, crystal habit, and surface closure (annealing of the interface to reduce the number of unused bonds) which is not generally available for NAT and SAT. Furthermore, it is not clear that nucleated clusters will form with the same habit as seen in macroscopic crystals [*Duff et al.*, 1978]. For these reasons we prefer to use the empirical correlation of *Turnbull* [1950], who measured the maximum supercoolings of various metals, semimetals, and water. He used the theory for homogeneous freezing (equation (12)) and, by assuming that the freezing rate in his samples (liquid drops with a diameter of 50 $\mu$m suspended in oil) was 0.1 per droplet per second, he was able to calculate the surface energies from eq. (12) (the actual value of the freezing rate is not very important as the rate is a very steep function of the temperature). Turnbull noticed that the various substances obey the empirical relation

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{sl}}=b \Delta H_{f} /\left(N_{\mathrm{Av}} v^{2 / 3}\right) \tag{19}
$$

where $b$ is 0.45 for metals and 0.32 for water and semimetals. These empirical constants have recently received support from a theoretical model [*Liu*, 1993]. Note that the surface energy in eq. (19) refers directly to the parameter in the freezing rate expression (eq. (12)); this cannot be said with certainty of surface energies determined in some other way. It is thus possible to calculate at a given temperature the surface energies of SAT and NAT relative to their respective liquid phases using eq. (19) with $b = 0.32$ and then determine the maximum supercooling of the liquids using eq. (12), or eq. (14) if the freezing is heterogeneous. When substituting into eqs. (12) and (14), one must for the sake of consistency, use exactly the same freezing rate expression that Turnbull used when deriving his empirical relation. In particular, a value of $10^{-2}$ should be used for $\exp(-\Delta G_{\mathrm{a}}/kT)$ at the homogeneous freezing temperature, with all substances (strictly speaking, the surface energies calculated with eq. (19) are only applicable when this particular assumption is used). In this

way one ensures that the calculated freezing behavior of any given substance is in accordance with the experimental freezing behaviour of the various substances studied by Turnbull. The diffusion-activation energy is temperature dependent, however, and in order to calculate heterogeneous freezing rates at higher temperatures than the homogeneous freezing temperature, we take the temperature dependence of $\Delta G_{\mathrm{a}}$ to be the same as that obtained from the empirical relation given by Hirschfelder et al. [1954]; we thus use

$$
\Delta G_{\mathrm{a}}=c\left(\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{vap}}-R T}{2.45 N_{\mathrm{Av}}}\right)
\tag{20}
$$

where $\Delta H_{\mathrm{vap}}$ is the partial molar enthalpy of vaporization of $\mathrm{HNO}_{3}$ or $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ from the substance which is freezing, and $c$ is determined from $\exp \left(-\Delta G_{\mathrm{a}} / k T\right)=$ $10^{-2}$ at the homogeneous freezing temperature.

Our choice of $b=0.32$ in eq. (19) for SAT and NAT reflects the fact that most of the molecules in these crystal lattices are actually water. It should be noted, however, that eq. (19) is an empirical relation based on measurements with one-component systems, whereas those solutions considered here are binary. It is probable that crystal formation in a binary system will have an additional resistance to growth, reflecting the fact that unlike in a one-component system, the right kind of molecule is not always readily available at the surface to join the emergent crystal lattice. Furthermore, even in one-component systems other studies have found significantly lower homogeneous freezing temperatures than Turnbull [see, for example, Oztoby 1988, and references therein]. We base our freezing calculations of SAT and NAT on Turnbull's experimental freezing temperatures, and if those temperatures are higher than the actual freezing temperatures of the substances he studied, then, presumably, our calculated freezing temperatures are also higher than the actual freezing temperatures of SAT and NAT. Consequently, the freezing temperatures presented below ought to be viewed as upper limits rather than as exact results (similarly, the liquid-solid surface energies should be viewed as lower limits.)

The surface energies given by equation (19) are, strictly speaking, temperature independent; Turnbull [1950] correlated the surface energies calculated at homogeneous freezing temperatures with enthalpies of fusion calculated at melting temperatures. Our aim, however, is to perform heterogeneous freezing and nucleation calculations at temperatures differing from the homogeneous freezing temperature. We therefore take the enthalpy of fusion in eq. (19) to be temperature dependent (see below) in order to have our $\sigma_{\mathrm{sl}}$ temperature dependent as well. This results in slightly different surface energy values at the homogeneous freezing temperature than with a temperature independent fusion enthalpy. Our justification for taking the temperature dependence of the enthalpy of fusion into account, is twofold. First, the water-ice surface energy we calculate at the homogeneous freezing temperature is somewhat too low (compared with that calculated by Turnbull) and, consequently, the freezing temperature itself is somewhat too high: the error is about 5 K. Our view that we are at the lower limit with the surface energy and at the upper limit with the freezing temperature is thus further supported. Secondly, there is some experimental evidence that the temperature dependence we calculate for $\sigma_{\mathrm{sl}}$ is approximately correct. Wood and Walton [1970] measured the homogeneous freezing of water over a limited temperature range in the vicinity of 237 K and deduced from their measurements the temperature dependence of the surface energy between water and ice. At 236.6 K their result was $24.2 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$, and the extrapolated value at 273.15 K was $32 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$, in reasonable agreement with the value of Hardy [1977] of $29 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$, measured at 273.15 K with the grain boundary groove method. Our corresponding values calculated with eq. (19) are 23.8 at 236.6 K and 31.2 at 273.15 K.

The enthalpies and molar volumes in eq. (19) vary with composition, so eq. (19) could, in principle, also be used for nonstoichiometric compositions. In nonstoichiometric solutions, of composition $\chi$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}(\chi)= & \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}\left(\chi_{0}\right)+\chi_{0}\left(H_{m}^{1}\left(\chi_{0}\right)-H_{m}^{1}(\chi)\right)+ \\
& \left(1-\chi_{0}\right)\left(H_{m}^{2}\left(\chi_{0}\right)-H_{m}^{2}(\chi)\right) \quad(21)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\chi_{0}$ is the stoichiometric mole fraction and $H_{m}^{1}$ and $H_{m}^{2}$ are the partial molar heats of mixing of components 1 and 2, respectively. We considered the freezing of water-ice in dilute sulfuric acid solutions and found unreasonably high surface energies compared with the surface energy of pure water. We therefore prefer to approximate the liquid-solid surface energies of nonstoichiometric solutions by those calculated for the stoichiometric ones (apparently, Jensen et al. [1991] also used this approximation). The true liquid-solid energies will most probably grow somewhat as the liquid composition is changed away from the stoichiometric one, and thus there is even more reason to regard our freezing temperatures calculated for nonstoichiometric solutions as upper limits.

The liquid-solid surface energies calculated from eq. (19) are used together with the liquid-vapor surface energies, calculated from eqs. (15)-(18), in order to determine the solid-vapor surface energies. If one assumes that a liquid wets its crystal completely, Antonoff's rule gives [Adamson, 1982]

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{sv}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{sl}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{lv}}
\tag{22}
$$

where $s$ refers to solid, $l$ to liquid, and $v$ to vapor. The use of Antonoff's rule can be criticized on the grounds that in the case of water-ice for example, a liquid-like layer is thought to exist on its surface down to about 240 K, which implies that the sum of the solid-liquid and liquid-vapor energies is less than the solid-vapor energy. The deviation from Antonoff's rule is, however, well within the uncertainty in the determination of the solid-liquid and solid-vapor energies.

Determining the dependence of the solid-vapor surface energies on temperature is not straightforward. Just as the solid-liquid surface energy is proportional to the enthalpy of freezing, the solid-vapor energy should be proportional to the enthalpy of sublimation. The enthalpy of sublimation has (at least in the case of water) a much smaller temperature dependence than the enthalpies of vaporization and freezing, suggesting that the solid-vapor surface energy is almost independent of temperature. The independence of the solid-vapor surface energy with respect to temperature makes physical sense, because the crystal structure does not change when temperature changes, and the surface energy is a very weak function of vapor properties. If a liquidlike film does exist on the crystal surface, there might be a considerable temperature factor in the true surface energy of the system. This problem should not apply to the surface energy of nucleating embryos, however, because the liquidlike layer (the depth of which can be several molecular diameters [see *Jellinek, 1967*]) is a property of macroscopic crystal surfaces rather than crystal nuclei consisting of about 100 molecules. It therefore seems likely that the temperature dependences of the surface energies of ice and NAT used by *Drdla and Turco [1991]* (which are essentially the same as those of water and nitric acid-water solution, respectively) are too large. The temperature dependences we obtain for the $\sigma_{sv}$ of NAT and SAT using eq. (22) are of the opposite sign to those of the corresponding liquid-vapor surface energies. This is contrary to expectations and could be an indication that the temperature dependences of the surface energy of SBS [Granzhan and Laktionova, 1975] and sulfuric acid-water liquid solution [Sabinina and Terpugov, 1935] are not correct at low temperatures. Fortunately, the positive temperature dependences of our solid-vapor surface energies ensure that we calculate lower limits. Hence lower-limit surface energies are calculated from

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{SAT}}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{SAT,sa}}(T) + \sigma_{\mathrm{sa}}(T) \tag{23}
$$

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{NAT}}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{NAT,SBS}}(T) + \sigma_{\mathrm{SBS}}(T), \tag{24}
$$

where the subscript $sa$ refers to sulfuric acid solution. The sensitivity of our calculations to the $\sigma_{sv}$ values is tested by using values calculated at the melting temperatures of each crystal (this would correspond to the case in which the solid-vapor energies are temperature independent). The melting points of pure NAT and SAT are 254.63 K and 244.88 K. For other liquid compositions the solidification temperature is given by the liquidus curves of the phase diagrams [Kuster and Kre mann, 1904; *Gable et al., 1950*].

Thus for the sensitivity analysis we use

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{SAT}}'(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{SAT}}(245K) \tag{25}
$$

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{NAT}}'(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{NAT}}(254K), \tag{26}
$$

and for the sake of consistency

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{SBS}}'(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{NAT}}'(T) - \sigma_{\mathrm{NAT,SBS}}(T). \tag{27}
$$

Some of these values can be compared with values that other investigators have used. *Poole and McCormick [1988b]* gave a value of 88 erg cm$^{-2}$ for $\sigma_{\mathrm{NAT}}$ at 190 K; this was later used by *Wofsy et al. [1990]*, and it is very close to that calculated from the temperature dependent expression of *Drdla and Turco [1991]*. ## Contact Parameters

There is no information available on the surface energy or contact parameters of solid-solid interfaces such as NAT-SAT, nor any information available on the liquid-solid interface between SBS and SAT. To arrive at estimates, we use Young's equation [Adamson, 1982]

$$
m = \frac{\sigma_{13} - \sigma_{23}}{\sigma_{12}}, \tag{28}
$$

where 1 denotes the substrate, 2 denotes the embryo, and 3 denotes the parent phase. We can thus write for the systems under consideration:

$$
\sigma_{\text{SAT,NAT}} = \sigma_{\text{SAT}} - (m_{\text{SAT,NAT}})(\sigma_{\text{NAT}}) \tag{29}
$$

$$
\sigma_{\text{SAT,SBS}} = \sigma_{\text{SAT}} - (m_{\text{SAT,SBS}})(\sigma_{\text{SBS}}), \tag{30}
$$

where $\sigma_{\text{SAT}}$ is obtained from eq. (23), $\sigma_{\text{NAT}}$ from eq. (24), and $\sigma_{\text{SBS}}$ from eq. (18) above.

Figure 2 shows the surface energies between NAT and SAT, and SBS and SAT, as a function of $m$ at 192 K. The figure also shows calculations using $\sigma_{\text{SAT,NAT}}'$ and $\sigma_{\text{SAT,SBS}}'$ , which are determined using eqs. (25)-( 27) (see also Table 1). We expect in general, the magnitude of the various surface energies in a particular system will increase in the order $\sigma_{\text{s,l}} < \sigma_{\text{s,s}} < \sigma_{\text{l,v}} < \sigma_{\text{s,v}}$. This expectation arises because solids can accommodate different bond distances less easily than liquids and because vapors can provide fewer interactions with the other phase than liquids. We assume, therefore, that the surface energy between NAT and SAT is at least as high as that between SBS and SAT, which, in turn, should be higher than the surface energy between SBS and NAT (that is, we expect the surface energy between a liquid and its own crystal to be lower than the surface energy between the same liquid and some other crystal). We thus expect that $\sigma_{\text{SAT,NAT}} \geq \sigma_{\text{NAT,SBS}}$, from which we can get an upper limit for the contact parameter between NAT and SAT at a given temperature: at 192 K we have $m_{\text{SAT,NAT}} \leq 0.57$ (0.64 using the $\sigma'$ values). In the same way we conclude that $\sigma_{\text{SAT,SBS}} \geq \sigma_{\text{NAT,SBS}}$, and at 192 K, $m_{\text{SAT,SBS}} \leq 0.90$ (0.96). Increasing $\sigma_{\text{SAT}},\sigma_{\text{NAT}}$, and $\sigma_{\text{SBS}}$ by 8-9 erg cm$^{-2}$ thus has quite a small effect on the upper limits of the contact parameter. If one used $b = 0.45$ instead of $b = 0.32$ in eq. (19), the limits would shift toward lower $m$, as the effect of increasing $\sigma_{\text{NAT,SBS}}$ would be larger than the effect of increasing the solid-vapor energies. Temperature has a very small effect on the upper limit of $m$: at 210 K we find $m_{\text{SAT,NAT}} = 0.55$ and at 180 K, $m_{\text{SAT,NAT}} = 0.58$.

## Enthalpies of Freezing and Vaporization

The following relationships were used to calculate the enthalpies of freezing and vaporization:

$$
\Delta H_{\text{f}} = \Delta H_{\text{o}} + \Delta C_{\text{p}}(T - T_{\text{o}}) \tag{31}
$$

$$
\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = \Delta H_{\text{T0}} + \Delta C_{\text{p}}(T - T0) \tag{32}
$$

where $\Delta H_{\text{o}}$ is the enthalpy of freezing at the melting temperature, $T_{\text{o}}$, of NAT or SAT [Forsythe and Giauque, 1942; *Hanson and Mauersberger*, 1988a; *Giauque et al.*, 1960]. Enthalpies of vaporization for SBS use eq. (32), where T0 is the midpoint of the range of temperatures over which the vapor pressure functions are calculated [Hanson, 1990; *Hanson and Mauersberger*, 1988a].

Enthalpies of vaporization for sulfuric acid-water solutions were calculated using the method of *Jaecker-Voirol* [1988]. The partial mixing enthalpies for the ternary solution were estimated from the activity coefficients (calculated according to *Zhang et al.* [1993]) by differentiating them with respect to temperature [e.g., *Prausnitz et al.*, 1986].

## Vapor Pressures

The vapor pressures of NAT and SBS were calculated using data from *Hanson and Mauersberger* [1988a, b] and *Hanson* [1990]. Vapor pressures for the sulfuric acid-water system were calculated using the expression derived by *Kulmala and Laaksonen* [1990] from the data of *Ayers et al.* [1980] and *Giauque et al.* [1960]. The vapor pressures over ternary solutions were calculated using nonideal solution thermodynamics and were supplied to us by T. Peter and B. Luo (MaxPlanck Institute for Chemistry, Mainz, Germany, private communication, 1994). Nonideal solution calculations provide a thermodynamically consistent framework for the extrapolation of measured properties of a solution that is not provided by simple polynomial fits to data [Carslaw et al., 1994].

Below, the various pathways outlined in Figure 1 are investigated. We begin with heterogeneous nucleation of nitric acid aerosol on SAT since that is the mechanism most often assumed. We then present calculations of the condensational growth of NAT and STS to outline the temperature thresholds at which they can be considered to form PSCs. Finally we deal with the homogeneous and heterogeneous freezing of STS to investigate the possibility of PSC formation via a "condensation-freezing" mechanism.

## Heterogeneous Nucleation of SBS and NAT

The heterogeneous nucleation of SBS and NAT can be calculated using eq. (6) with the appropriate ex- pressions for $\Delta G^{*}$. Figure 3 shows the nucleation prob ability calculated at 192 K for each composition as a function of $m$ . The nucleation time is 1 hour. Va por concentrations are 10 parts-per-billion by volume (ppbv) and 4.5 parts-per-million by volume (ppmv) for $HNO_{3}$ and $H_{2} O$ . The surface properties of the pre existing particle, i.e., SAT, are not involved in this cal- culation. To connect the information of Figure 3 to nucleation on SAT, one has to consider the contact pa- rameter between SAT and the nucleating substances, which is given in Figure 2. For given values of $m$ and temperature, equilibrium vapor pressures dictate that NAT be easier to form than SBS. Therefore the thresh- old value of $m$ above which the nucleation probability becomes unity is lower for NAT than for SBS. NAT will not nucleate if $m$ is less than 0.78 at 100 mbar; at 50 mbar the contact parameter has to be higher than 0.9 for nucleation to take place. In Figure 2 the contact parameter between NAT and SAT was estimated to be below 0.64 at 192 K, clearly less than that needed for NAT nucleation to take place.

Similarly, we calculate that SBS will not nucleate if its contact parameter is less than 0.96 at 100 mbar. At50 mbar, $m$ would have to be 1 for SBS nucleation to be possible. In Figure $2, m \leq 0.96$ for SAT and SBS at 192 K, which would rule out the possibility of SBS nucleation.

The vapor pressure of NAD is between the vapor pres- sures of NAT and SBS, and therefore the heterogeneous nucleation of NAD will have a behavior intermediate between that of NAT and SBS. Because of lack of data we are not able to calculate NAD surface energies, nor the actual contact parameters that would allow NAD nucleation. However, we can consider the limiting case in which the nucleation behavior of NAD is taken to be similar to that of NAT. At 192 K and 50 mbar the contact parameter between NAD and SAT would then have to be above 0.9 in order to make nucleation pos- sible (see Figure 3). For simplicity, let us assume that the difference between $\sigma_{NAT}$ and $\sigma_{NAD}$ is the same as the difference between $\sigma_{NAT,SBS}$ and $\sigma_{NAD,SBS}$ . Withreasoning similar to that used in Figure $2(\sigma_{SAT,NAD}=$  sAT - (msAT,NAD)( NAD) ≥ NAD,SBS), it is easy to show that the NAD energies would have to be more than $20 erg cm^{-2}$ lower than the NAT energies, in or der to have the contact parameter between NAD and SAT above 0.9. This, in turn, would mean that the en- thalpy of freezing of NAD in eq. (19) would have to be only about half of that of NAT, which seems unlikely. Our results indicate that PSC formation does not pro- ceed via any kind of binary nucleation of nitric acid and water vapors on pure SAT aerosols.

Our heterogeneous nucleation calculations can be com- pared with one previous study. Wofsy et al. [1990] ex- amined temperatures of PSC formation and concluded that classical nucleation theory required a lower limit of $m$ of 0.85 for NAT nucleation at 50 mbar. This value of $m$ is somewhat lower than the value of 0.9 estimated above. The explanation for this discrepancy is in the35 erg/cm² difference in the NAT surface energies used(see above).

However, the condensation of water and nitric acid is a fairly slow process under stratospheric conditions (from several hours to a few days) and thus the importance of large STS droplets in NAT freezing remains somewhat unclear. On the other hand, condensational growth is a steep function of temperature and our results give an upper limit for temperature where condensation can occur. The condensational growth process becomes even slower if the droplet does not contain sulfuric acid, a situation which would follow the nucleation of SBS on a SAT particle. In both cases the droplet compositions will remain relatively constant after about 1000 s, as would be expected from nonideal solution thermodynamic calculations [B. Luo and T. Peter, MaxPlanck Institute for Chemistry, Mainz, Germany, private communication, 1994]. Figure 4b shows that at 193 K and 100 mbar, liquid droplets can grow to rather large sizes within 10-15 min.

NAT particles could form by the freezing of submicron STS droplets to NAT and subsequent growth of these particles. This route would be quite efficient after the onset of freezing, since the threshold temperature for NAT stability and growth is significantly higher than for marked STS stability and growth (Figure 4a). Hence at a given temperature the growth rate of NAT particles is substantially greater than the growth rate of STS droplets because the supersaturation of the vapors over NAT is greater than that over STS.

When the growth of particles proceeds, the vapors are depleted from the gas phase until the gas phase is in equilibrium with the solid phase. If the particle number concentration is high, the gas temperature will increase as latent heat is released. These effects have not been included in the above calculations because our aim is to find the upper limit of the condensation temperature and the relative rates of NAT and ternary droplet growth.

The main conclusion of the freezing experiments of Molina et al. [1993] was that nitric acid promotes the freezing of the ternary samples. Whether or not freezing took place homogeneously in the experiments cannot be ascertained easily. Our theoretical results suggest that the freezing seen by Molina et al. was in most cases het- erogeneous: the homogeneous freezing temperature we calculate for a $20 \% HNO_{3}$ solution is about 34 K lower than the experimental freezing temperature (to account for this discrepancy we would have to set b=0.23 in eq.(19); while this would not be unphysical, we are aware of no substances with such low b-values). On the other hand, the fact that some samples had to be cooled down to very low temperatures lends support to the homoge- neous freezing temperatures we have calculated, at least in the case of SAT.

There are some features of the experimental setup of Molina et al. which support the heterogeneous freez- ing interpretation. Firstly, the samples were very large compared to those normally used in experiments con- ducted to find the homogeneous freezing temperatures of liquids. It is known to be very difficult to keep liq- uid samples clear of impurity particles. In freezing ex- periments, samples are usually dispersed into droplets which are small enough that one can be confident that most of them are free of impurities. Secondly, the sam- ples of Molina et al. were confined within glass walls, which can promote heterogeneous freezing (cf. Turn- bull [1950], who suspended his samples in oil to avoid wall effects). Thirdly, the experiment results show that similar samples froze at different temperatures.

Our conclusion about the comparison of the theoret-ical results with the experiments of Molina et al. [1993] is that either our theoretical homogeneous freezing tem- peratures for NAT are grossly too low or the freezing monitored by Molina et al. took place heterogeneously. In the latter case, the tendency of $HNO_{3}$ to make the so lidification easier suggests that the NAT lattice matches the impurity surface better than the SAT surface does. We disagree with Molina et al. on one point: they con- clude that because the possible impurities (in their ex- periments) can only increase the nucleation rates, their experiments provide an upper limit for the freezing tem- peratures of stratospheric $H_{2} SO_{4}$ aerosols. This can only be true if it is known with certainty that the strato- spheric freezing takes place only homogeneously and, to our knowledge, nobody has proved this yet. Some the- oretical studies [Laaksonen and Kulmala, 1991; Hamill et al., 1990] suggest that a fraction of the sulfuric acid aerosols could be formed by homogeneous nucleation in winter polar stratospheres and would thus contain no foreign particles which could later promote heteroge- neous freezing. This may also be the case with fresh volcanic clouds [Sheridan et al., 1992]. However, it seems likely that most of the background aerosols have been nucleated around dust particles, originating from volcanic eruptions or meteoric sources, and these dust particles could later act as freezing nuclei. The contact parameter between SAT and the seed particle then dic- tates whether or not freezing will take place at a given temperature, and it would be possible for two differ- ent $H_{2} SO_{4}-H_{2} O$ droplets having the same size, liquid composition, and thermal history to freeze at different temperatures.

We would also like to point out that it is not cer- tain that crystallization of NAT out of ternary solution droplets is the first step of the stratospheric freezing process, even though Molina et al. [1993] conclude that this is likely. If the freezing seen by Molina et al. was indeed heterogeneous, the fact that NAT crystallized earlier than SAT only gives information about the im- purity surfaces in the experiments; no information is available about the nature of the stratospheric impu- rity surfaces and whether they are closer to SAT or to NAT lattices.

If our analysis of surface energies is broadly correct, then we can deduce the following points about the pro- cesses outlined in Figure 1: nucleation of nitric acid aerosols onto SAT particles is inefficient; and the freez- ing temperature of liquid particles is strongly dependent on the contact parameter between the emergent crys- tal and the underlying seed particle. Our calculations of the multicomponent condensational growth of PSCs(the theory of which is on a much firmer ground than the nucleation theory) show that once formed, conden- sation can produce ternary solution particles at temper- atures 2-3 K above the ice frost point and large particles of NAT at higher temperatures. This result, based on the thermodynamic calculations of Carslaw et al., is in agreement with the thermodynamic calculations given recently by Tabazadeh et al. [1994].

It would seem, then, that the most likely route to PSC formation is via the condensational growth of liq- uid droplets. As the temperature in an air parcel falls below the NAT point, enough nitric acid is dissolved to permit freezing of NAT (and SAT) but only if the droplets contain seed particles whose contact parame- ters with the crystalline phases are sufficiently large. If the temperature drops sufficiently close to the ice frost point and the droplets are still in the liquid phase, for- mation of water ice will take place (a situation which

[1990]: smaller, more spherical droplets (type 1b) correspond to the "condensation stage" of the process; while larger, anisometric (type 1a) particles correspond to the final "frozen stage." If heterogeneous freezing occurs for only a small percentage of particles, then only a few particles may reach the freezing stage. These few particles may grow large enough to sediment and, hence, cause denitrification without dehydration, as seen in the Arctic [Fahey et al., 1990]. If no freezing occurs, the total volume of condensation-stage particles can become large at temperatures 2-3 K above the ice frost point. Freezing may be homogeneous, to form water ice, or heterogeneous, to form NAT and SAT.

Besides PSC particles of types 1a and 1b, a condensation/freezing mechanism also accounts for high supersaturations relative to NAT vapor pressure [Rosen et al., 1989; Arnold, 1992] and liquid-like behavior of aerosol particles at temperatures below the NAT point [Dye et al., 1992; Carslaw et al., 1994]. However, detailed numerical modeling of specific PSC events would be required in order to check whether the time dependences of the proposed mechanism are the same as those observed.

The exact effect that the revised mechanism will have on modeling studies of the atmosphere remains to be investigated. However, it is already clear that models which treat type 1 PSCs as having a NAT surface immediately when temperatures fall below the thermodynamic threshold for NAT formation will overestimate the time an air parcel is exposed to NAT surfaces and underestimate the time of exposure to (increasingly dilute) $HNO_3$/$H_2O$/$H_2SO_4$ solution surfaces.
