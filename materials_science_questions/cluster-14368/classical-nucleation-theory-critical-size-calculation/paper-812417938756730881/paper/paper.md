# Nucleation in a nonuniform vapor*

Carol Becker and H. Reiss

Department of Chemistry, University of California, Los Angeles, California 90024
(Received 14 May 1976)

Conventional nucleation theory describes nucleus formation in a uniform vapor as the addition or loss of single molecules from clusters of a given size. However, under nonuniform conditions, clusters can also be removed by diffusing away from the region of interest. Although there are many practical situations in which nucleation does occur under nonuniform conditions, the conventional theory cannot account for the loss of clusters by diffusion. It is therefore the purpose of this paper (1) to formulate a new theory which does include loss by diffusion and (2) to make a quantitative study of the diffusion effect in order to delineate the range of conditions under which it becomes important. The new theory is derived in the context of a typical diffusion cloud chamber by considering the diffusion loss from the narrow slab where nucleation occurs. The supersaturations which give rise to a unit steady rate of nucleation are then compared with and without the diffusion term for model compounds under a variety of conditions. These quantitative studies on typical model compounds show that the diffusion effect first becomes important for vapor pressures as low as $10^{-8}$ torr. Thus, in the cloud chamber, nonuniformities do not cause serious errors. More importantly, in polluted atmospheres, even very low concentrations of vapors of involatile organic substances (e.g., sulfinic acids) can nucleate and give rise to the particulate matter commonly found in photochemical smog.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Conventional nucleation theory¹ deals with a uniform vapor, i.e., one in which the supersaturation is constant throughout the entire volume under consideration. Under such circumstances, the process of nucleus formation by the growth of clusters, through the reversible stepwise addition of single molecules, may be regarded as "conservative." In a conservative process, a cluster of a given size, once formed, can only be removed by having it change its size (through the acquisition or loss of a molecule), but not by having it diffuse away. To put it in another way, in a uniform vapor, a cluster which diffuses away will, on the average, be replaced by an identical cluster diffusing to the volume element under consideration, so that the net diffusion process need not be considered. On the other hand, there are many practical situations in which nucleation does occur under nonuniform conditions.

One of the most common situations involves measurements in a diffusion cloud chamber²,³,⁴ where the supersaturation is not a constant but varies with elevation. Ideally, in the chamber, nucleation takes place only in a narrow planar slab within which the supersaturation barely exceeds the critical value. Thus clusters forming within this slab may diffuse away to regions of lower supersaturation before they attain nucleus size.

Another situation occurs in the atmosphere where a condensable vapor may be produced locally by a chemical reaction. An example would be the production of involatile sulfinic acids (in a polluted atmosphere) by the reaction of olefins and sulfur dioxide. This process is considered important in the production of particulate matter in photochemical smog.⁵

In conventional nucleation theory it is assumed that the rate of nucleus formation attains the "steady state," and the quantitative theory is based on this premise. On the other hand, the achievement of the steady state, especially when nucleation occurs at very low vapor pressures (which require extremely low saturation pressures), may take place only after an extremely long time. Several studies dealing with various aspects of the transient approach to the steady state are now available.⁶⁻¹⁰ Although these papers address different aspects of the problem, they are all in essential agreement, and the last (Ref. 10) in particular shows that at vapor pressures as low as $10^{-11}$ torr the time to steady state may be as long as several hundred seconds. The particular "real" instance in which such low saturation pressures are involved occurs in the nucleation of supersaturated water vapor by traces of sulfuric acid.¹¹⁻¹⁵ However, there may be other examples.

When the time to steady state becomes long then the problem of nonuniformity must be nonnegligible, because growing clusters have considerable time to diffuse away before the nucleus size is reached. Thus nonuniformity is just another face of the same coin corresponding to the nonachievement of steady state. However, it does have a simple aspect, as we shall see, in that its study involves only ordinary differential equations in size space, whereas the study of transients involves partial differential equations in both size and time. In this paper we present a quantitative study of the effects of nonuniformity in order to delineate the range of conditions under which it becomes important.

## II. FORMULATION OF THE PROBLEM

For definiteness, we consider a case of plane parallel symmetry such as might occur in a diffusion cloud chamber. As a matter of fact, we might just as well consider the problem in the context of a typical cloud chamber. Thus we consider the arrangement of Fig. 1.

Here we show the two plates, $P_1$ and $P_2$, of the cloud chamber. Elevation in the chamber is denoted by x. The supersaturation, $\theta(x)$, is also shown in its dependence on elevation. Nucleation is assumed to occur in the narrow slab of thickness h, a distance $L_1$ above plate $P_1$ and $L_2$ below plate $P_2$.

Consider a cluster containing n molecules, produced

![](./images/812417938756730881_3.jpg)

FIG. 1. Arrangement for formulating the nonuniform problem.

within the slab. Such a cluster will have the opportunity to diffuse (through the supporting gas, usually helium) out of the slab. Since the concentration of clusters de- pends very critically on the supersaturation, and $\theta$ de creases rapidly on either side of the slab, we can ex- pect that the availability of clusters (in the regions im- mediately adjacent to the slab) to replace the cluster which escaped is minimal. However, if we assume theescaped cluster to have a long lifetime (which it doesn't) then the diffusion process itself may be regarded as the source of gradients of cluster concentration, which we take to be zero at $P_{1}$ and $P_{2}$ where the vapor is only saturated. The gradient set up in this manner will be less than that which actually prevails at the steady state of transport in the chamber when the disappearance of clusters due to actual finite lifetimes is taken into ac- count. Thus the loss from the slab, by diffusion, cal- culated on this basis will be less than the actual loss, since the driving force for diffusion is less. With this simplification, then, we will be calculating a lower lim- it for the perturbation due to nonuniformity; in the ac- tual case it will be even more important than we esti- mate. In still another simplification we assume that the binary diffusion coefficient, $D$ , for the cluster-helium gas mixture is independent of size.

Now, in the cloud chamber, experiments are per- formed under steady state conditions. The fact that the steady state for the rate of production of nuclei may re- quire considerable time for its establishment is only reflected by the size of the perturbation due to nonuni- formity. If the steady state concentration, in the slab, of clusters containing $n$ molecules is $f_{n}$ while it is zeroat $P_{1}$ and $P_{2}$ , the diffusion gradients towards $P_{1}$ and $P_{2}$  will be $f_{n} / L_{1}$ and $f_{n} / L_{2}$ , respectively. The diffusion loss per unit time from a unit area of slab will then be
$$Q_{n}=D\left(\frac{f_{n}}{L_{1}}+\frac{f_{n}}{L_{2}}\right)=D f_{n}\left(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}\right). \quad(1)$$

Since the slab has thickness $h$ , the loss per unit volume will be
$$\frac{Q_{n}}{h}=\frac{D f_{n}}{h}\left(\frac{1}{L_{1}}+\frac{1}{L_{2}}\right)=\frac{D f_{n}}{h L}, \quad(2)$$
where $L$ is an equivalent length,
$$L=L_{1} L_{2} /\left(L_{1}+L_{2}\right). \quad(3)$$

Next we consider the net steady rate of production of clusters of size $n$ within the slab. For this purpose we invoke the conventional theory, $^{1}$ which describes the case where there is no nonuniformity. Thus we denote by $S_{n}$ , the surface area of a cluster containing $n$ mole cules, and by $\beta$ , the rate, based on the Maxwell-Boltz mann distribution of velocities, at which one square centimeter of (plane) surface is hit by molecules. We have,
$$\beta=P / \sqrt{2 \pi m k T}, \quad(4)$$
where $P$ is the pressure due to single molecules (clus ters containing only one molecule-monomers), $m$ , the molecular mass, $k$ , the Boltzmann constant, and $T$ , the temperature. Then the rate, per unit volume, that clusters of size $n$ become clusters of size $n+1$ is
$$\beta S_{n} f_{n}, \quad(5)$$
while the rate at which clusters of size $n+1$ lose a mol ecule to become clusters of size $n$ is
$$\gamma_{n+1} S_{n+1} f_{n+1}, \quad(6)$$
where $\gamma_{n+1}$ is the rate of evaporation per square centi meter. The net rate of passage from $n$ to $n+1$ is then
$$J_{n}=\beta S_{n} f_{n}-\gamma_{n+1} S_{n+1} f_{n+1}, \quad(7)$$
i.e., the difference between Eq. (5) and Eq. (6).

To determine $\gamma_{n+1}$ , detailed balancing is invoked. A formal constraint is introduced which disallows clus- ters exceeding the nuclear size. The system then fallsinto equilibrium such that $J_{n}=0$ , and $f_{n} \to N_{n}$ , where $N_{n}$  is the equilibrium concentration of clusters of size $n$ . Introducing these conditions into Eq. (7) yields
$$\gamma_{n+1}=\beta S_{n} N_{n} / S_{n+1} N_{n+1}. \quad(8)$$

By resubstituting Eq. (8) into (7) we obtain the familiarresult $^{1}$ 
$$J_{n}=-\beta S_{n} N_{n}\left(f_{n+1} / N_{n+1}-f_{n} / N_{n}\right). \quad(9)$$

For the continuum the flux of clusters through size space is
$$J(n)=-\beta S(n) N(n) \frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{f(n)}{N(n)}\right).\qquad(10)$$

When the system is uniform, this is the only flux which needs to be considered, and in the steady state $J_{n}$ is a constant, independent of $n$ . Setting $J_{n}=J$ , a constant, provides the equation by means of which the problem is solved in the usual, uniform system. How- ever, in the nonuniform case, in the steady state, the sum of all the fluxes to "size $n$ " must be zero. Thus we have
$$J_{n-1}-J_{n}-Q_{n} / h=0\qquad(11)$$
or, again invoking the continuum,
$$-\frac{\partial J(n)}{\partial n}-\frac{Q_{n}}{h}=0.\qquad(12)$$

Substitution from Eqs. (2) and (10) yields,
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left[\beta S N \frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{f}{N}\right)\right]-\frac{D N}{h L}\left(\frac{f}{N}\right)=0.\qquad(13)$$

This is the fundamental equation for the nonuniform case.

Equation (13) must be solved for $f / N$ subject to thetwo point boundary condition:
$$f / N \rightarrow 1 \quad n=1, \quad(14)$$

$$f/N\to 0\ \ n=\infty.$$


Once $(f/N)(n)$ is known, we can determine the rate $J$ at any size $n$. In particular, if $n^*$ denotes the nucleus size, the steady rate of nucleation, $J^*$, can be expressed as

$$
J^{*}=J\left(n^{*}\right)=-\beta S^{*} N^{*}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{f}{N}\right)\right]_{n=n^{*}}. \tag{15}
$$

The equilibrium distribution of clusters, $N(n)$, is given, as usual, $^{1}$ by

$$
N(n)=P_{g} \exp \left\{-(1 / k T)\left[-n k T \ln \theta+4 \pi \sigma(3 v / 4 \pi)^{2 / 3} n^{2 / 3}\right]\right\}, \tag{16}
$$

in which $P_{g}=P / k T$ is the concentration of monomers in the vapor, $\theta$ is the supersaturation ratio, $\sigma$ is the interfacial tension between liquid and vapor, and $v$ is the volume per molecule in the bulk liquid phase.

Note, that in the case $D \to 0$, so there can be no escape by diffusion, the solution of Eq. (13) is merely $J(n)=J$, a constant, and we return to the case of a uniform system. It is the perturbation due to the last term on the left of Eq. (13) whose magnitude is under investigation in this paper. Note that Eq. (13) is an ordinary differential equation in size space. As indicated earlier, we are investigating an effect related to the time required to establish the steady state, but without having to solve a partial differential equation in both size and time.

### III. SOLUTION OF BOUNDARY VALUE PROBLEM AND DISCUSSION

The boundary value problem comprised by Eqs. (13)-(16) was solved numerically, as documented in the Appendix, for model compounds under a variety of conditions. The model compounds were assumed to have a value of $D$ equal to $0.29 \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{sec}^{-1}$. This value, although calculated for ethane sulfinic acid by the method of Fuller et al., $^{16}$ is not atypical for molecules commonly studied by diffusion cloud chamber methods. Since only order of magnitude estimates of the behaviors of real systems were of interest, $h$ was set equal to $0.1 \mathrm{~cm}$, and $L$ was set equal to $2.0 \mathrm{~cm}$-reasonable, typical values for a cloud chamber. Furthermore, $v$ was set equal to $1.8 \times 10^{-22} \mathrm{~cm}^{3}$ and $\sigma$, at $20{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, set equal to $30.0 \mathrm{dyn} \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.

For simplicity, when several temperatures were involved, $\sigma, v$, and $D$ were considered invariant. However, $P_{e}$, the saturation pressure of the model compound, varies considerably (i.e., exponentially) with temperature. It was therefore felt that $P_{e}$ should be made to vary in a manner physically consistent with the magnitude of $\sigma$. Toward this purpose we made use of Langmuir's approximate relation $^{17}$ between interfacial tension and heat of vaporization. This relation is obtained by assuming that the vaporization of one mole of liquid is equivalent to the process of dividing the liquid into spherical drops. After subdivision, the number of drops is equal to the number of molecules in one mole of vapor, where each drop has the volume per molecule (in the liquid) $v$. Then $\lambda$, the molar heat of vaporization, is assumed to be equal to the surface energy of the equivalent drop system.

If $r$ is the radius of the drop, this surface energy is estimated by the surface free energy $N_{0} 4 \pi r^{2} \sigma$ where $N_{0}$ is Avogadro's number. Thus

$$
\lambda \simeq N_{0} 4 \pi(3 v / 4 \pi)^{2 / 3} \sigma, \tag{17}
$$

where we have used the relation

$$
v=\frac{4}{3} \pi r^{3}. \tag{18}
$$

If we substitute the expression for $\lambda$ [Eq. (17)] into the Clapeyron-Clausius equation,

$$
P_{e}=P_{e}^{0} \exp \left[-\frac{\lambda}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_{0}}\right)\right], \tag{19}
$$

the vapor pressure at any temperature is determined as soon as a reasonable value of $P_{e}^{0}$ is selected.

In cases where we studied a given model compound at various temperatures, we chose $P_{e}^{0}$ to be $10^{-5}$ torr at $T_{0}=20^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, approximately comparable to the saturation pressure of a typical sulfinic acid. In all cases we chose the molecular mass, $m=1.6 \times 10^{-22} \mathrm{~g}$, corresponding to the molecular weight of ethane sulfinic acid, $94.13 \mathrm{~g} \mathrm{~mole}^{-1}$.

As the Appendix indicates, the numerical solutions were sensitive to grid size and to how far past $n^{*}$ we chose "infinity" for the purpose of satisfying the second of the boundary conditions [see Eq. (14)]. If this point was chosen as $2 n^{*}$, the results were quite insensitive to the selection of any larger value of $n$. However, the question of grid size is another matter. Our results still contain an error of about $3 \%$ due to prohibitive computing costs for step size smaller than $6.4 \times 10^{-3}$.

Fortunately, the magnitude of the error due to step size could be checked by comparing our numerical results when $D$ in Eq. (13) is set equal to zero with the conventional results obtainable by the usual theory. $^{1}$ Incidentally, the conventional result is based on a peak integration method, known to be accurate. $^{18}$ It was reassuring to find that the results obtained by numerical solution of Eq. (13) (when $D$ is set equal to zero) are quantitatively identical with those obtained from the usual peak integration.

Figure 2 illustrates results for a series of model compounds, at a single temperature, namely, $T=20^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. These compounds differ only in their $P_{e}$ values. The ordinate represents the critical supersaturation, $\theta^{*}$, required to produce a nucleation rate of 1 drop $\mathrm{cm}^{-3} \cdot \mathrm{sec}^{-1}$ while the abscissa represents the $P_{e}$ values of the various compounds. The solid curve is a plot of $\theta^{*}$ versus $P_{e}$. It can be noticed that the critical supersaturation rises precipitously as $P_{e}$ becomes small. The numbers along the curve are the numbers of molecules in the critical nucleus at each point.

The dashed curve in Fig. 2 represents values of $\theta^{*}$ obtained by assuming $D$ in Eq. (13) to be zero, i.e., the critical supersaturations to be expected in the conventional, uniform system. The most striking aspect of the Figure is the low value of $P_{e}$ required before a substantial difference in $\theta^{*}$ between the uniform and nonuniform systems is observed. Thus, at $P_{e}=10^{-8}$ torr, the critical supersaturation for the nonuniform system

exceeds that for the uniform by only $25\%$. For $P_{e}=10^{-11}$ torr, however, $\theta^{*}$ for the nonuniform system exceeds that for the uniform by $315\%$.

These general results are consistent with Ref. 10, dealing with the time to steady state in which substantial times are first predicted for vapor pressures in the neighborhood of $10^{-11}$ torr.

Figure 3 shows the results for a single model compound at various temperatures. As indicated previously, this compound is assumed to have a $P_{e}$ value of $10^{-5}$ torr at $20^{\circ}C$, and to have a heat of vaporization given by Eq. (17). In the Figure (a log-log plot) the solid curve again corresponds to a nonuniform system while the dashed curve represents the uniform case. Here we plot the actual pressure $P$ giving rise to a unit rate of nucleation against $P_{e}$, the saturation pressure. The numbers along the curves are the various temperatures to which the associated points on the curve correspond. We see that for a model compound, physically consistent to the extent of Eq. (17), there is very little difference between the uniform and nonuniform cases. Even at such low $P_{e}$ values as $10^{-12}$ torr, the difference in $\theta^{*}$ is only $15\%$.

The conclusion of this study must therefore be that in conventional diffusion cloud chamber experiments, unavoidable nonuniformities do not give rise to serious errors. Furthermore, even more important, in polluted atmospheres, even very low concentrations of vapors of involatile organic substances (e.g., sulfinic acids) can nucleate and give rise to particulate matter, thereby supporting current hypotheses.

## APPENDIX: METHOD FOR NUMERICAL SOLUTION OF EQS. (13) AND (15)

The equation [Eq. (13)],
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left[\beta S N \frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{f}{N}\right)\right]-\frac{D N}{h L}\left(\frac{f}{N}\right)=0, \tag{A1}
$$
is a linear differential equation for $(f/N)(n)$ on the domain $1 \leq n < \infty$. The boundary conditions for (A1) are
$$
\begin{aligned}
& f/N \to 1 \text{ as } n \to 1, \\
& f/N \to 0 \text{ as } n \to \infty.
\end{aligned} \tag{A2}
$$

Using the product rule for differentiation, we can rewrite Eq. (A1) as
$$
g(n) \frac{\partial^{2} u}{\partial n^{2}}+h(n) \frac{\partial u}{\partial n}-d(n) u=0 \tag{A3}
$$
where
$$
\begin{aligned}
& u=f/N, \\
& g(n)=\beta S N, \\
& h(n)=\beta\left(S \frac{\partial N}{\partial n}+N \frac{\partial S}{\partial n}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
d(n)=D N/h L
$$

The first step is to discretize the problem by dividing the interval $1 \leq n \leq 2n^{*}$ into $N$ subintervals of length

$n=(2n^{*}-1)/N$ and approximating the derivatives (at grid point $i$) by the corresponding central finite differences, i.e.,

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} &= \frac{u_{i+1}-u_{i-1}}{2\Delta n} , \\
\frac{\partial^{2} u}{\partial n^{2}} &= \frac{u_{i-1}-2u_{i}+u_{i+1}}{(\Delta n)^{2}} .
\end{align} \tag{A4}
$$

By directly subsituting Eqs. (A4) into (A3) and grouping together terms in $u_{i-1}$, $u_{i}$, and $u_{i+1}$, we get

$$(GF-HF)u_{i-1}-(2GF+DF)u_{i}+(GF+HF)u_{i+1}=0 \tag{A5}$$

where

$$
\begin{align}
GF &= g(n_{i})/(\Delta n)^{2} , \\
HF &= h(n_{i})/2(\Delta n) ,
\end{align} \tag{A6}
$$

and

$$DF=d(n_{i}) .$$

We have now reduced the problem to one of solving a set of simultaneous linear equations of the form

$$a_{i}u_{i-1}+b_{i}u_{i}+c_{i}u_{i+1}=0 \tag{A7}$$

In our case, there are $N-1$ equations for $N+1$ unknowns $u_{i}$. The remaining two equations are supplied by the boundary conditions (A2). Since the system described by (A7) has a maximum of three variables per equation, the matrix of coefficients, $a$, $b$, and $c$, is a simple tridiagonal matrix of size $(N+1)$ by $(N+1)$. The system (A7) is readily solvable by a Gaussian elimination method tailored to a set of equations with a coefficient matrix of band structure (GELB). Consequently, we use the SSP subroutine GELB$^{19}$ with $N=909$ to solve Eq. (A1) for $(f/N)(n)$.

The rate $J^{*}$ at the nucleus size $n^{*}$ can now be determined from the equation [Eq. (15)],

$$J^{*}=J(n^{*})=-\beta S^{*}N*\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{f}{N}\right)\right]_{n=n^{*}} \tag{A8}$$

As before, the first-order derivative is replaced by the appropriate finite difference formula. We then calculate the steady rate of nucleation $(J^{*})$ both with and without the diffusion term. To find the critical supersaturation $(\theta^{*})$ for the diffusion case, we compute $J^{*}$ with the diffusion term for a grid of supersaturations. The supersaturation which corresponds to a rate of $1\ \mathrm{cm}^{-3} \cdot \mathrm{sec}^{-1}$ is chosen as $\theta^{*}$. Thus $\theta^{*}$ is known with and without diffusion, and the effect of diffusion on $\theta^{*}$ can be studied for various sets of parameters.

All computations are carried out on a PDP11-45 DEC computer. Errors due to step size are 3% or less. Errors due to truncating the domain at $2n^{*}$ are about $\frac{1}{500}$ the errors due to step size.
