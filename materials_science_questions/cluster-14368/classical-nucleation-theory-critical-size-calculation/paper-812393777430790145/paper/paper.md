# HETEROMOLECULAR NUCLEATION IN THE SULFURIC ACID-WATER SYSTEM

A. JAECKER-VOIROL and P. MIRABEL

Département de Chimie, Université Louis Pasteur, B.P. 296R8, 67000 Strasbourg, France

(First received 5 July 1988 and received for publication 8 February 1989)

**Abstract**—New predictions for the nucleation rates in the $\mathrm{H_2SO_4-H_2O}$ mixture are presented. These new calculations take into account the influence of hydrate formation in the gas phase as well as new values for the vapor pressure of $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$. The results cover a large range of relative humidities (r.h.) (20-100%) and of temperatures $(-50$ to $100^\circ\text{C})$.

**Key word index:** Nucleation, sulfuric acid, sulfate formation.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Heteromolecular (or binary) homogeneous nucleation from a binary mixture of $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ and water vapor has received much attention in the past since this process for gas-to-particle conversion plays a major role in the atmosphere.

In particular, this process has been invoked to explain the formation of the stratospheric aerosol layer, the formation of $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$ in power plant plumes or in volcano plumes.

The theory of heteromolecular homogeneous nucleation was developed in several papers, based on the pioneering works by Flood (1934), who performed the first experiments in binary systems, and Reiss (1950), who showed how to fully generalize single component nucleation theory. This theory was later improved by Stauffer (1976). Application of binary nucleation to the $\mathrm{H_2SO_4-H_2O}$ mixture was first studied by Doyle (1961), followed by Kiang and Stauffer (1973), Mirabel and Katz (1974), Ithoh *et al.* (1977). All these authors predicted nucleation rates under conditions encountered in the lower atmosphere, and confirmed the effectiveness of $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ as a powerful nucleating agent for water. Further improvements in the theory were brought about by Heist and Reiss (1974), Shugard *et al.* (1975), Shugard and Reiss (1976) and Schelling and Reiss (1981), who included the effect of hydration of $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ molecules.

Since then a remarkably large number of kinetic models appeared, which predict the temporal evolution of the number concentration, the size distribution, chemical composition etc. of the newly formed aerosols.

Most of the above work has been performed assuming an equilibrium vapor pressure for pure $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ of $10^{-6}$ or $3.6 \times 10^{-4}$ torr at 298 K. These last values were either 'suggested' by Doyle $(10^{-6}$ torr) or calculated by Gmitro and Vermeulen in 1964 $(3.6 \times 10^{-4}$ torr). It is only since 1979 that this equilibrium vapor pressure has been known with some accuracy $(2.5 \times 10^{-5}$ torr at $23^\circ\text{C}$, Roedel (1979); 1.4 $\times 10^{-5}$ torr at $25^\circ\text{C}$, Ayers *et al.* (1980); $3.1 \times 10^{-5}$ torr at $25^\circ\text{C}$, Chu and Morrison (1980)). Furthermore, with the exception of the work of McGraw (1982), *all* the models ignore hydrate formation in the gas phase, which can lead to serious errors in the calculated nucleation rate.

Finally, in two recent articles (Jaecker-Voirol *et al.*, 1987; Jaecker-Voirol and Mirabel, 1988), we have re-examined the energetics of nucleation in the $\mathrm{H_2SO_4-H_2O}$ mixture and performed nucleation rate calculations, for a limited set of conditions.

By examining all the articles cited above, it is possible for the reader who is interested in nucleation in the $\mathrm{H_2SO_4-H_2O}$ mixture to perform his own calculation but it is time-consuming to use all these complicated equations and to collect and analyze all the necessary thermodynamic data.

Thus it is the purpose of this paper to provide an extensive enough listing of nucleation rates in the $\mathrm{H_2SO_4-H_2O}$ mixture, under a large variety of temperatures, r.h. and acid concentrations, so that through simple interpolation, it will be possible to obtain results for atmospheric applications, extending from stratospheric conditions to power plant plumes.

## 2. THEORY AND THERMODYNAMIC DATA

A detailed description of the theory used here to calculate nucleation rates in the $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ system has already been given in two recent papers (Jaecker-Voirol *et al.* 1987; Jaecker-Voirol and Mirabel, 1988) and will not be repeated here. We simply recall that the rate of nucleation $J$ is given by the equation:

$$
J=C \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G^{*}}{kT}\right) \tag{1}
$$

where $C$ is a slowly varying, mainly kinetic, frequency

factor, $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ is the temperature, and $\Delta G^{*}$ is the free energy required to form a mixed critical nucleus from the vapor phase.

The free energy $\Delta G^{*}$ which corresponds to a saddle point on the free energy surface has the following form:
$$
\Delta G^{*}=\frac{16 \pi \gamma^{3} V^{2}}{3\left(R T \ln S^{*}\right)^{2}} \tag{2}
$$
where $\gamma$ and $V$ represent the respective values of the surface tension and the mean molar volume of the droplet at the saddle point composition $X^{*}$ (in this article, $X$ refers to the mole fraction of the acid). The critical supersaturation $S^{*}$ for the mixture can be expressed as a function of $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$, the supersaturations of components 1 and 2, by
$$
S^{*}=S_{1}{ }^{\left(1-X^{*}\right)} S_{2}^{X^{*}}. \tag{3}
$$

Although Equation (2) appears to be a generalization of single component nucleation theory, its use is difficult since it requires the knowledge of $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ which in turn depends on the composition of the droplet. It is however, possible to determine $\Delta G^{*}$ (see, for example, Mirabel and Katz (1974) and Jaecker and Mirabel (1988)) in terms of the activities $a_{i, \mathrm{~g}}$ of the components in the gas phase. If $P_{i}$ is the actual partial pressure of component $i$ in the gas phase, and $P_{i}^{0}$ its equilibrium vapor pressure over the pure liquid, then the activity $a_{i, \mathrm{~g}}$ is defined as $P_{i} / P_{i}^{0}$. For example, a water activity of unity corresponds to a r.h. of $100 \%$. An additional complication arises with $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ since, in the vapor, its molecules are far less numerous than those of water and are distributed throughout several species of hydrate. It is then impractical to use activities and instead, we present our results in terms of the total number of acid molecules $N_{\mathrm{a}}$.

The correct evaluation of $C$ and $\Delta G^{*}$ requires the knowledge of many physical parameters, both as functions of temperature and concentration. Among the most crucial data are the differences of chemical potential between liquid and gaseous state for both constituents, the surface tension, molar and partial molar volumes of the solution, and equilibrium constants for successive additions of water molecules to an $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ molecule [see Equations (2), (8) and (10) of Jaecker-Voirol and Mirabel (1988)].

### Chemical potential
The chemical potentials of both components in the liquid phase as functions of concentration and temperature are needed. These data have been taken, at $298 \mathrm{~K}$, from the compilation of Giauque *et al.* (1960). Calculations at other temperatures than $298 \mathrm{~K}$ have been performed with the help of the Van't Hoff equation, i.e. for a component $i$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}\left(\ln \frac{\mu_{i}-\mu_{i}^{0}}{T}\right) & =\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} T}\left(\ln \frac{\Delta \mu_{i}}{T}\right) \\
& =-\frac{\bar{H}_{i}-\bar{H}_{i}^{0}}{R T^{2}}=-\frac{\Delta \bar{H}_{i}}{R T^{2}}
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
$$
where $\Delta \bar{H}_{i}$ is the partial molar enthalpy of mixing and where the superscript $^{0}$ refers to the standard state, taken as the pure liquid. Since in the range of temperature employed in this study, $\Delta \bar{H}_{i}$ cannot be considered constant, its values at other temperatures than $298 \mathrm{~K}$ were calculated according to:
$$
\Delta \bar{H}_{i}(T)=\Delta \bar{H}_{i}(298)+\int_{298}^{T} \Delta \overline{C p}_{i} \mathrm{~d} T \tag{5}
$$
where $C p_{i}$ is the partial molar specific heat at constant pressure. Both $\Delta \bar{H}_{i}(298)$ and $\overline{C p}_{i}(298)$ are given by Giauque *et al.* (1960).

### Surface tension
The surface tensions for the mixture are taken from the work of Sabinina and Terpugow (1935). Their data at 10 and $50^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ (surface tension vs mole fraction) have been fitted by appropriate polynomials so that the differences between the original values and the fitted values never exceeded $0.3 \%$. Data at other temperatures than 0 and $50^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ were then calculated by means of linear interpolation or extrapolation. This last interpolation introduced an error of at most $0.3 \%$.

### Molar and partial molar volumes
Molar volumes in the $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}-\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ mixture have been calculated from the data on density and composition at 0 and $60^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in the *Chemical Engineer's Handbook*. Calculated values were then fitted by appropriate polynomials so that the differences between values from the compilation and the fitted values never exceeded $0.1 \%$. Data at other temperatures than 0 and $60^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ were then calculated as functions of temperature by linear interpolation or extrapolation. Once again the maximum error introduced by the linearization is at the most $0.3 \%$. In both cases, partial molar volumes were determined numerically from the well known relations:
$$
\bar{V}_{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}}=V-X \frac{\partial V}{\partial X} \tag{6}
$$
$$
\bar{V}_{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}}=V+(1-X) \frac{\partial V}{\partial X}.
$$

### Vapor pressures
Equilibrium vapor pressures for pure $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ are taken from Ayers *et al.* (1980). Their equation leads to a value of $1.4 \times 10^{-5}$ torr at $25^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $5.1 \times 10^{-11}$ torr at $-55^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. For pure water, equilibrium pressures were calculated from the equation proposed by Lowe (1977) for temperatures in the range -50 to $50^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. At higher temperatures, the compilation in the Landolt- Börnstein tables were used.

Partial vapor pressures for both components, at any composition, can then be estimated from the following relationship
$$
P_{i}=P_{i}^{0} \exp \left(\frac{\Delta \mu_{i}}{k T}\right). \tag{7}
$$

### Hydration constants

Equilibrium constants as a function of temperature for successive addition of water molecules to an $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ molecule are needed. To our knowledge, such data are not available and were estimated according to equation 39 of Jaecker-Voirol *et al.* (1987). The lack of known data for these constants may be a major source of uncertainty.

### 3. RESULTS

For details of the calculation, the interested reader is referred to the following papers:

Hydration in the gas phase, see Heist and Reiss (1974) Jaecker-Voirol *et al.* (1987).
Location of the saddle point, i.e. composition of the critical nucleus, see Heist and Reiss (1974), Mirabel and Reiss (1987) and Jaecker-Voirol and Mirabel (1988).
Calculation of the frequency factor $C$, see Shugard *et al.* (1974), Mirabel and Clavelin (1978) and Jaecker-Voirol and Mirabel (1988). Note that to simplify the calculations, the 'Zeldovich factor' which appears in $C$ was set to a fixed value of 0.25. This approximation causes only negligible error.

The figure clearly shows the influence of the equilib- rium vapor pressure $P_{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}}^{0}$ and of hydrate formation. When $P_{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}}^{0}$ is reduced (from $3.6 \times 10^{-4}$ to 3.1 $\times 10^{-5}$) while $N_{\mathrm{a}}$ remains constant, both the activity of the acid in the gas phase and the nucleation rate increase. In order to maintain the unit rate of nuclea- tion, fewer acid molecules are required, and $N_{\mathrm{a}}$ must be reduced. On the contrary, the introduction of hydrates has a stabilizing effect on the vapor and reduces the nucleation rate. Then, a greater total number of acid molecules are required to maintain a unit rate of nucleation.

Finally, it is interesting to compare these predic- tions to the available experimental results. One of us, (Mirabel and Clavelin, 1978), measured nucleation rate at 298 K, using a redesigned upward thermal diffusion cloud chamber, with $\mathrm{He}$ or $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ as carrier gases. However, due to the long time required for the droplets to grow to 'visible size', this experimental set up allowed only measurement for r.h. $>100 \%$.

In Figure 4 of Jaecker and Mirabel (1988), we compared the predictions of the theory with the experimental results. As it can be seen from that figure, experiments and theory which includes hydrate for- mation compare very well, for r.h. $>100 \%$. There is no doubt that, in the atmosphere where the r.h. is at most $100 \%$ and where plenty of time is available for growth, this good agreement should remain valid.
