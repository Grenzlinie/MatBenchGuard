# Polarization bremsstrahlung study of the surface of xenon clusters:
## The pseudocrystalline state

E. V. Gnatchenko, A. N. Nechay, $^{\text{a)}}$ A. A. Tkachenko, and V. N. Samovarov

B. I. Verkin Institute of Low Temperature Physics and Technology, National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine,
pr. Lenina 47, Kharkov 61103, Ukraine
(Submitted July 30, 2012; revised September 18, 2012)
Fiz. Nizk. Temp. **38**, 1446–1454 (December 2012)

The half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile is measured as a function of the number $W(N)$ of atoms in free clusters of xenon. The clusters are excited by electrons with energies of 0.7 and 0.3 keV, which preferentially probe the core and surface, respectively, of the clusters. It turns out the $W(N)$ curves are entirely different for electrons with energies of 0.7 and 0.3 keV. An analysis of this difference indicates that the crystalline fcc core of clusters with sizes $N \approx 2000/8000$ atoms/cluster ($R \approx 30$–$50\,\mathring{\text{A}}$) is covered with a noncrystalline shell. This layer is examined in terms of the possible formation of glassy and pseudocrystalline states. For the first time a pseudocrystalline state is found in inert gas clusters. In this state the system is structurally unstable and constantly fluctuates between the liquid and solid phases. © 2012 American Institute of Physics. [http://dx.doi.org/10.1063/1.4770519]

## 1. Introduction

Clusters are systems with a finite number of particles and are characterized by a large fraction of surface atoms. Even in quite large atomic metal and Lennard-Jones clusters with radii $R=40\,\mathring{\text{A}}$, surface atoms constitute roughly 10% of the total number of atoms. Consequently, the surface subsystem has a significant influence on the physical and chemical properties of clusters. The structure of the surface layers of a cluster can differ significantly from that of its inner bulk layers. For example, the possible freezing of a glassy state on cluster surfaces at high cooling rates has been discussed. $^{1}$ The heating of a Lennard-Jones fcc cluster (200 atoms) has been traced numerically $^{2}$ and it was shown that, within a certain temperature range, the crystalline fcc core coexists with a noncrystalline shell that has a *pdp*-structure with a fifth order axis of symmetry. Most papers in this area are devoted to studies of surface melting of clusters, which essentially means that, for temperatures below the melting point of Lennard-Jones and metallic clusters, their surface layer can be in a quasiliquid (liquid) state (the premelting effect). $^{3–6}$ Experiments on surface melting are mostly done with metallic clusters. A calorimetric technique for determining the temperature and heat of fusion has been developed for metallic clusters free of a substrate produced by vacuum condensation of metal vapor. $^{7}$ This makes it possible to detect the existence of a liquid layer on the cluster surface. Studies of a so-called pseudocrystalline state in metallic clusters is of special interest. $^{6–8,11}$ The transition to a pseudocrystalline state can be ascribed to a number of cluster effects, since it occurs in clusters with radii below a critical $R_{c}$. $^{10,11}$ The surface layer of clusters can also be in a pseudocrystalline state. $^{11}$ A system in a pseudocrystalline state is structurally unstable and continuously fluctuates between liquid and solid phases. This behavior is analogous to the dynamic coexistence of phases in small metallic and Lennard-Jones clusters with approximately 100 or fewer particles, where the cluster is liquid part of the time and spends the rest of the time in a solid state. $^{3,12}$

Up to now there have been no experimental studies of the surface state of inert gas clusters. This paper reports an effort to obtain information on the structure of the surface layer of xenon clusters that are free of a substrate. The clusters were produced by adiabatic expansion of the gas in a vacuum through a supersonic nozzle. Fast electron diffraction is widely used to study the structure of free homogeneous and heterogeneous inert gas clusters. $^{13–15}$ Electron diffraction patterns only provide information on the structure, structural transitions, and compositional concentrations of the core of clusters, and cannot be used to study their surface layer. On the other hand, it is very difficult to apply traditional methods for studying the surfaces of massive samples to free inert gas clusters.

Polarization bremsstrahlung spectroscopy is used here for the first time to diagnose the structural state of the surface and core of clusters. Polarization bremsstrahlung arises during the interaction of electrons (and other charged particles) with gaseous or solid targets as a result of oscillations of the dipole moment of the atoms induced by the variable field of incident electrons. $^{16}$ Experimental proof of the existence of polarization bremsstrahlung has been obtained in spectroscopic studies of the scattering of electrons with intermediate energies on beams of xenon atoms. $^{17}$ Ordinary bremsstrahlung is produced by another mechanism and is associated with emission by an electron as it is slowed down in the field of a target. As opposed to the spectrum of ordinary bremsstrahlung, the polarization bremsstrahlung spectrum has a resonance profile in the ultrasoft x-ray region, 60–200 eV ($\lambda=207$–$62\,\mathring{\text{A}}$). $^{16–18}$

During excitation of xenon clusters by 0.7-keV electrons we have found $^{19}$ that the width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile becomes significantly smaller on going from icosahedral to crystalline clusters, i.e., the polarization bremsstrahlung profile can be used to diagnose the structural state of the clusters.

In this paper, besides the 0.7 keV electrons we use electrons with low energies of 0.3 keV, which have a considerably larger effect on the excitation of the surface layer of clusters. It turned out that the dependence of the half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile on the number $W(N)$ of atoms in a cluster is very different for 0.3 and

0.7keV electrons. A discussion of this difference indicates that the crystalline fcc core of clusters with 2000-8000 atoms/cluster (sizes $R \approx 30-50 \AA$) is covered by a noncrystalline shell. It may be assumed that this result holds for all inert gas (except He) clusters produced by adiabatic expansion of the gas into a vacuum.

The nature of the noncrystalline shell is examined for two possible surface structures: glassy and pseudocrystalline states. Under the conditions of our experiments, the rates of cooling $(\approx 2 \cdot 10^{7} ~K / c)$ of the liquid droplets and "hot" solid clusters are found to be insufficient to freeze crystalline structures in a glassy state on the surface. For the first time we propose and justify another possibility for inert gas clusters, where the surface shell is in a pseudocrystalline state that is typical of clusters.

It is also found that the large differences in the observed W(N) curves for electrons with energies of 0.3 and 0.7keV can be described fairly well in terms of the half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile calculated using a model that we have developed. $^{19}$ The effect of the density $\rho$ of the medium and the Onsager parameter $\eta$ (which specifies the change in the polarizability of an atom as it moves from a vacuum into a medium) on the half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile has been studied. It appears that W(N)for the core of the clusters is controlled by the increase in $\eta$  for a constant value of $\rho$ . For the surface the situation is the opposite; i.e., W(N) is controlled by the increase in $\rho$ for fixed $\eta$ . Additional theoretical studies, such as by molecular dynamics techniques, may establish the relation between this behavior of $\rho$ and $\eta$ and the nature of noncrystalline shells, e.g., those in a pseudocrystalline state.

### 2. Experiment
The experimental technique for studying polarization bremsstrahlung during scattering of intermediate-energy electrons on atomic and cluster beams has been discussed in detail in Refs. 18 and 19. Here we discuss the main features of this experiment.

Atomic and cluster beams of xenon were produced by adiabatic expansion of the gas into a vacuum through a conical supersonic nozzle with the following parameters: full aperture angle of the cone $2 \alpha=9.5^{\circ}$ , critical cross section diameter 0.3 mm, and ratio of the areas of the outlet and critical cross sections $S_{ex}=59.3$ . By reducing the temperature $T_{0}$ of the gas at the inlet to the nozzle (450-180 K) and raising its pressure $P_{0}(0.3-1 atm)$ , it was possible to go from atomic to cluster beams, while varying the size of the clusters. Studies were conducted on a sequence from atoms, to icosahedral clusters, to crystalline clusters with an fcc structure up to sizes $N \approx 12000$ atoms/cluster $(R \approx 56 \AA)$ . The average number N of atoms in a cluster was determined using the well known Hagen relations $N=\varphi(P_{0}, T_{0})$ with the geometrical parameters of the nozzle taken into account. $^{20}$ The error indetermining N using these relations is $\pm 30 \%$ for $N>10^{3}$  atoms/cluster, and $\pm 50 \%$ for smaller sizes. The clusters were excited by electrons at a distance of 10 mm from the nozzle cutoff, where the cluster parameters are stable. The temperature $T_{cl}$ of the xenon clusters in the supersonic beams was $79 \pm 8 K$ , based on measurements of the lattice parameter by electron diffraction. $^{7,13}$

$$

where R(E) is the mean free path of electrons with energy E and x is the distance from the sample surface. For the electron energies used here, we take $R(0.3 keV) \approx 4.5 nm$ and $R(0.7 keV) ≈8 nm .^{22}$ Figure 1 shows the calculated D(x). We specify a thickness $x ≈6 \AA$ for the surface layer; for an fcc xenon cluster this corresponds to a layer for which the number of nearest neighbors is less than 12 but greater than 6 withoutthermal vacancies. $^{23 *}$ Then the ratio of the areas under the D(x) curves for this thickness to the total area under each curve is proportional to the probability of exciting polarization bremsstrahlung in the surface layer. The contribution of 0.3keV electrons to the excitation of the surface is twice that of 0.7keV electrons, and this difference increases for thicker layers.

The curves are normalized to the half width of the profile for an atomic beam. It can be seen that these curves differ substantially. Crystalline clusters of xenon with an fcc structure are formed in the beam when the number of atoms per cluster exceeds $\approx 1500$ ($R \approx 27\,\mathring{\text{A}}$).$^{14}$ We note that electron diffraction data show that fcc clusters of the inert gases with sizes $R < 100\,\mathring{\text{A}}$ are made up of single domains.$^{15}$ Thus, strong narrowing of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile for 0.7 keV electrons generally occurs when they are probing the crystalline fcc core of the clusters. At the same time, for the 0.3 keV electrons the half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile changes very little on going from a beam of atoms to noncrystalline and crystalline clusters. To within the limits of error of the experiments, these changes may be only 3% for sizes of 8000 atoms/cluster (Fig. 2). This indicates that the fcc clusters of xenon with $N \approx 2000$–8000 atoms/cluster have a noncrystalline surface shell.

## 4. Discussion of the nature of the surface shell of the clusters
### The glassy state
The formation of a glassy state is determined to a great extent by the rate of cooling of the system of particles. We now estimate the rate of cooling of liquid droplets and “hot” solid clusters formed near the triple point of xenon ($T_{\text{tr}} = 161.4\,\text{K}$) during the initial stage of formation of fcc clusters with a temperature $T_{\text{cl}} = 80\,\text{K}$.

Cooling of gas with a temperature $T_0$ begins at the inlet to a conical supersonic nozzle. Neglecting heat release owing to condensation, the gas temperature inside the nozzle is given by$^{25}$

$$
T_x = T_0 \left[ \frac{2}{\gamma+1} \left( 1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_x^2 \right) \right]^{-1}. \tag{2}
$$

The ratio of the specific heats $\gamma = C_P/C_V = 1.66$ for xenon at $T = 300\,\text{K}$ and $M_x$ is the Mach number, which increases as the flow accelerates. At the critical cross section (the inlet to the nozzle) $M_x = 1$. For an arbitrary cross section with radius $r_x$ inside the nozzle and $\gamma = 1.66$, we have$^{25}$

$$
S = \left( \frac{r_x}{r_0} \right)^2 \approx \frac{0.55}{M_x} (1 + 0.33 M_x^2)^2, \tag{3}
$$

where $r_0$ is the radius of the critical cross section. Equation (3) relates $M_x$ to the distance $x$ from the critical cross section. Given $M_x(x)$ for the geometric parameters of our nozzle, we can find $T(x)$ from Eq. (2). We take the effect of heat release during formation of the liquid droplet and the subsequent clusterization by replacing the initial temperature $T_0$ in Eq. (2) with an effective temperature $T^* = T_0 + (2\varepsilon/3)$,$^{26}$ where $\varepsilon=1500\,\text{K}$ is the energy of condensation per xenon atom. For $S = 59.3$ and $T^* = 1200\,\text{K}$, Eqs. (2) and (3) imply that $M_x \equiv M_{\text{ex}} \simeq 9.7$ and the temperature of the gas flow is about 50 K at the outlet cross section of the nozzle; that is, cluster formation at 80 K is completed inside the nozzle. With Eqs. (2) and (3) it is easy to show that at a distance $x = 3.5\,\text{mm}$ from the inlet cross section $M_x \equiv M \simeq 4.85$, while $T = T_{\text{tr}} \approx 162\,\text{K}$ for an initial temperature $T^* = 1200\,\text{K}$.

We obtain the following estimate for the characteristic cooling time near $T_{\text{tr}}$:

$$
\tau \approx \left( \frac{1}{T_{\text{tr}}} \frac{dT}{dx} \frac{dx}{dt} \right)^{-1}. \tag{4}
$$

The flow velocity is given by $^{25}$
$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = M \left( \frac{2\gamma k}{m} T \right)^{1/2} \left( 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2 \right)^{-1/2}. \tag{5}
$$

Here $k$ is the Boltzmann constant and $m$ is the atomic mass of xenon. Using Eqs. (2)-(5), we find that near $T_{\text{tr}}$ the cooling rate is roughly $2 \cdot 10^7$ K/s and the characteristic cooling time is $\tau \approx 8 \cdot 10^{-6}$ s.

In order for a glassy state to develop on the surface, the following entirely obvious inequality must be satisfied: $t < \tau_D$, where $\tau_D$ is the characteristic time for atoms to move in the surface layer. Before obtaining an estimate for $\tau_D$, some aspects of the thermal excitation of surface atoms in clusters should be noted. At zero temperature all the shells of the cluster are filled. With rising temperature, atoms in the surface shells move to outer unfilled shells and stick to the cluster, forming a system of adsorbed atoms. These atoms can move quite freely over the cluster surface with diffusion coefficients close to those for the liquid phase and recombine with vacancies produced by thermal excitation of the cluster. As the temperature increases, the number of these excited atoms (vacancies) increases exponentially. Near $T=T_{\text{tr}}$ the surface shells of the cluster become very "brittle." In order for the glassy state to freeze, the characteristic diffusion time for the vacancies (atoms) in the quasiliquid surface layer of thickness $\ell$,
$$
\tau_D = \frac{\ell^2}{D}
$$
must be significantly greater than the cooling time, i.e., $\tau_D \gg \tau$.

The diffusion coefficient for the vacancies (atoms) is
$$
D = d_0 \exp \left( -\frac{E_a}{T} \right), \tag{6}
$$
and for xenon $E_a=600$ K and $d_0=10^{-3}$ cm$^2$/s.$^1$ Near $T_{\text{tr}}$ for a layer of thickness $\ell=10$ Å, the characteristic diffusion time $\tau_D \approx 10^{-9}$ s, i.e., the very strong inequality $\tau_D \ll \tau$ applies. Thus, under our experimental conditions a glassy state does not form in the surface shell of the xenon clusters.

### The pseudocrystalline state

We now discuss the other possible state of the surface layer. It is known that as the size of clusters is reduced, their melting temperature also decreases. For clusters with radius $R$ this dimensional effect is described (in the approximation of equal densities of the solid and liquid phases, $\rho_s \approx \rho_l$) by$^{27}$
$$
\frac{T_m^{\text{cl}}}{T_m} = \left( 1 - \frac{2(\sigma_s - \sigma_l)}{q\rho_s R} \right) = \left( 1 - \frac{R_0}{R} \right), \tag{7}
$$
where $T_m$ and $T_m^{\text{cl}}$ are the melting temperatures of the bulk crystal and a cluster, respectively. (Based on the thermodynamics of small systems$^{29}$ it has been shown$^{28}$ that the numerical coefficient in Eq. (7) may be 3, rather than 2, as implied by the Gibb-Thomson relation.) For xenon the quantities in Eq. (7) have the following values: surface tension of the fcc crystal $\sigma_S=61$ erg/cm$^2$ (80 K),$^{30}$ surface tension of the liquid phase $\sigma_l=18.8$ erg/cm$^2$,$^{23}$ specific heat of fusion $q=3.7 \cdot 10^{-14}$ erg,$^{23}$ and number density of the solid phase $\rho_S=1.7 \cdot 10^{22}$ cm$^2$ (80 K).$^{23}$ Then $R_0=13$ Å in Eq. (7) and for clusters with 3000 atoms ($R \approx 35$ Å) the melting temperature $T_m^{\text{cl}}=101$ K and for $N=6000$ atoms/cluster ($R \approx 44$ Å), $T_m^{\text{cl}}=113$ K.

It should be noted that different kinds of structural defects reduce the melting temperature. Packing defects have a particular influence. They are the sources of Shockley partial dislocations, which are caused by the splitting of whole dislocations and spatially delimit packing defects. Their effect on fcc crystals has been studied$^{31}$ with an analysis of the data employing the Lindemann criterion $L$ given by the ratio of the mean square displacement of an atom to the interatomic distance at the melting point. It was shown that near Shockley dislocations the local Lindemann parameter approaches $L \approx 0.18$, or twice the value in the bulk crystal ($\approx 0.085$) and the condition for local melting $L>0.085$ is retained out to three lattice parameters on each side from the defect. A strong influence of packing defects (dislocations) has also been observed in experiments on the premelting of copper nanowires with lengths of 1000 nm and diameters of 20 nm.$^{32}$ It turned out that surface melting sets in at $T \approx 0.4 T_m$, or well below the melting temperature of copper, $T_m=1284$ K.

Inert gas clusters produced in supersonic jets are characterized by a high density of packing defects of the deformation type (i.e., by the ratio of defect planes to the total number of densely packed planes). The defects develop during the cooling of "hot" clusters in the first stage of their formation from liquid droplets. In addition, in this stage the clusters can coagulate and this also produces packing defects. The high density of packing defects is a result of their low formation energies in solidified inert gases ($\approx 1$ erg/cm$^2$ for xenon), as well as of the anomalously large vibration amplitudes of the atoms in apical coordination spheres that is typical of fcc inert gas clusters.$^{33}$ The width of a dislocation increases as the formation energy for a packing defect decreases, i.e., it affects an ever larger volume of the sample. For xenon clusters with $N=3000$ and 6000 atoms/cluster the densities of defects are 0.06 and 0.05, respectively, according to electron diffraction, and on the average each cluster contains four defect planes that intersect one another.$^{14}$ Under these conditions the melting temperature of the clusters can be very close to their temperature, 80 K, and we have to speak of surface melting of the clusters.

Surface melting of bulk crystals has already been studied for a long time, both theoretically and by various experimental techniques, in metals, molecular media, and solidified inert gases.$^{34}$ It has been shown experimentally and theoretically$^{35}$ that in solid xenon melting of surface (111) and (100) facets sets in at $T_S=0.8 T_m$ (129 K), where $T_m=161.4$ K is the homogeneous melting temperature of xenon. Deeper facets become more unstable beginning at $T_S$ and an interface develops between the crystalline phase and the liquid (quasiliquid) layer. As noted above, surface melting of metallic clusters has been studied extensively. For example, it has been found that when Sn clusters free of a substrate and with

radii of 50–100 Å are heated, the liquid surface layer occupies an extremely large volume of the cluster with a thickness of 18 Å.⁴

The surface layer is in a liquid (quasiliquid) state only in clusters with radii greater than a critical $R_c$.$^{10,11}$ When a cluster is smaller than the critical $R_c$, the surface layer may enter a pseudocrystalline state.¹¹

Experimental work on the pseudocrystalline state in metallic clusters has opened up a new area in the physics of small objects. One of the first efforts was an electron microscopy study of gold clusters with sizes of about 20 Å (Ref. 8) (the theory¹¹ gives $R_c=37$ Å). It was noted early on⁸ that, from the standpoint of traditional concepts of matter, the observed state of the clusters could not be ascribed to either a liquid or a solid phase. In phase diagrams of clusters as a function of their sizes, the pseudocrystalline state lies between the regions of the liquid state and the crystalline state, as well as the crystalline state with an icosahedral state.⁹ The system fluctuates continuously between these different states, since the large entropy of small clusters does not permit stabilization of just one state.³⁶ As the radius of the clusters and their temperature are increased, the range of sizes where the pseudocrystalline state exists rapidly becomes narrower.⁹

The existence of a critical cluster size is determined by the following factors. As we have already discussed, when the size of clusters is reduced their melting temperature $T_m^{\text{cl}}$ also decreases. However, the surface melting temperature $T_S$ is a weaker function of the cluster radius. As a result, for $R < R_c$, the inequality $T_s > T_m^{\text{cl}}$, holds; this specifies the temperature range over which a pseudocrystalline layer exists. Thus, for xenon clusters with sizes of 6000 atoms/cluster the melting temperature $T_m^{\text{cl}} = 113$ K, but since $T_S$ varies little on going from a bulk sample to a cluster, at 129 K it exceeds $T_m^{\text{cl}}$.

Based on the theory of Ref. 11, we can write down the following size dependence $T_S(R)$ for $R \geq R_c$ in xenon clusters

$$
T_s=0.8 T_{m}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left[1+\frac{0.66 R_{c}}{3.7 R-R_{c}}\right].\qquad(8)
$$

For $R \to \infty$ ($T_m^{\text{cl}} = T_m$) we obtain the surface melting temperature of bulk xenon $T_s = 0.8T_m$ (129 K), and for $R=R_c$ the boundary condition $T_s = T_m^{\text{cl}}$ for formation of a pseudocrystalline layer is satisfied. With this same theory¹¹ we can calculate the critical radius for xenon clusters $R_c = 55$ Å. For comparison, we note that the theory¹¹ gives the following values for some low-melting metals: $R_c = 52$ Å for Pb clusters, 94 Å for Sb clusters, and 38 Å for Sn clusters (with experimental values of 25 Å (Ref. 10) and 35 Å (Ref. 37)). Thus, for the fcc clusters of xenon with 2000–8000 atoms ($R \approx 30$–50 Å) studied here, a pseudocrystalline layer can develop on their surfaces and exist until the clusters have melted completely. The existence of this layer explains the weak dependence of the half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile on the number of atoms in a cluster for 0.3 keV electrons. As noted above, to within the experimental error, these changes may be only 3% for sizes below 8000 atoms/cluster (see Fig. 2). When $N > 8000$ atoms/cluster, however, the polarization bremsstrahlung profile definitely begins to narrow sharply for 0.3 keV electrons. Over the range from 8000 to 12 000 atoms/cluster the profile becomes almost 25% narrower while the cluster radius increases by only 10%.

The radii are larger than critical in this range of cluster sizes for xenon and we may assume that the structural state of the surface layer undergoes a readjustment.

### 5. Model calculations of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile

We have developed a computational approach¹⁹ for modelling the effect of the dielectric constant of a medium on the polarization bremsstrahlung profile. It is known that the polarization bremsstrahlung cross section $\omega d\sigma/d\omega$ is determined by the polarizability $\alpha(\omega)=\alpha'+i\alpha''$ of atoms,¹⁶,¹⁸ i.e.,

$$
\left(\omega \frac{d \sigma(\omega)}{d \omega}\right) \propto \omega^{4}|\alpha(\omega)|^{2} \propto \omega^{2} \Omega\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}, \varepsilon^{\prime \prime}\right).\qquad(9)
$$

On the other hand, the polarizability of an atom can be related to the photoabsorption cross section $\Omega(\varepsilon',\varepsilon'')$, which depends on the dielectric constant of the medium, $\varepsilon=\varepsilon'+i\varepsilon''$.¹⁹,³⁸ The Onsager relation gives the coupling between the dielectric constant and the polarizability of an isolated atom³⁹

$$
\varepsilon(\omega)=1+4 \pi \rho \alpha(\omega) \frac{3 \varepsilon(\omega)}{2 \varepsilon(\omega)+1-2(\varepsilon(\omega)-1) \alpha(\omega) / \eta^{3}}.\quad(10)
$$

The Onsager parameter $\eta$ characterizes the variation in the polarizability of an atom when it is moved from a vacuum into a medium and is determined by the degree of ordering of the atoms in the medium. In general, the density $\rho$ of the medium in a cluster can reflect both the density of the core and the density of the surface layer. As a result, the half width of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile $W(\eta,\rho)$ depends on the Onsager parameter and the density of the medium.

Figure 3 is a calculated three-dimensional plot of $W(\eta,\rho)$. Two simple cuts in this figure correspond to the following conditions: (1) $\rho=$ const but $\eta$ does not increase and (2) $\eta=$ const but $\rho$ increases. The figure shows that the first condition gives a good qualitative and quantitative description of the behavior of the experimental $W(N)$ for electrons with an energy of 0.7 keV and $\rho=1.7 \cdot 10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, which corresponds to the density of solid xenon. Thus, when $\eta$ is raised from 2 to $7 \cdot 10^{-8}$ cm, the half width decreases by roughly 25%. The increase in the Onsager parameter most likely reflects the relaxation of the crystalline core as the cluster size increases. ## 6. Conclusions

We have measured the half widths of the polarization bremsstrahlung profile as a function of the number of atoms in free xenon clusters, $W(N)$, with excitation by 0.7 and 0.3 keV electrons, which mainly probe the core and surface, respectively, of the clusters. It turned out that the $W(N)$ curves are very different for electrons with energies of 0.7 and 0.3 keV. These differences have been analyzed and it was found that for cluster sizes $N \approx 2000$–8000 atoms/cluster ($R \approx 30$–$50\ \mathring{\text{A}}$) their fcc crystalline cores are coated with a noncrystalline surface layer. The nature of this layer has been examined in terms of the formation of glassy and pseudocrystalline states. It was concluded that there is a surface pseudocrystalline state that had not been observed previously for inert gas clusters.

Calculations provide a fairly good description of the measured polarization bremsstrahlung profile widths as a function $W(N)$ of the number of atoms in the clusters. The behavior of $W(N)$ for the surface layers of clusters, as opposed to their cores, is found to be controlled by an increase in the density $\rho$ with a fixed value of the Onsager parameter $\eta$. Further theoretical studies may establish a correlation of this behavior of the parameters $\rho$ and $\eta$ with the pseudocrystalline nature of the surface of the shell.
