# Pulsed clearing of a synthetic aqueous aerosol by $CO_2$ laser radiation

A. V. Kuzikovskii, L. K. Chistyakova, and V. I. Kokhanov

Institute of Atmospheric Optics, Siberian Branch of the Academy of Sciences of the USSR, Tomsk
(Submitted December 18, 1980)
Kvantovaya Elektron. (Moscow) 8, 2090-2096 (October 1981)

An experimental investigation was made of the action of a microsecond pulse in the $10.6 \mu$ range on the dispersive properties of a fine-droplet synthetic aqueous fog. It was found that when a certain threshold pulse energy was reached, a state of total clearing occurred and remained stable for a time governed by the wind- induced drift of the fog. At subthreshold pulse energies, secondary condensation occurred causing clouding of the medium at the $0.63 \mu$ wavelength. An optothermodynamic analysis was made of the clearing process and the recondensation kinetics was analyzed.

PACS numbers: 42.68.Vs, 42.60.He

Investigations aimed at providing the physical basis of the method of radiation clearing of aqueous aerosols (see, for example, reviews presented in Refs. 1 and2) have shown that, for realistic beam energy charac- teristics in subexplosive particle evaporation re- gimes, the clearing rate cannot always compete with the wind-induced clouding of the channel and recon- densation. Thus, it is interesting to study fast clearing regimes involving explosive evaporation of material in unloading waves. The rate of explosive processes is such that the transparency of precipitation can also be controlled efficiently.

A theory taking into account the energy potential of existing pulsed $CO_{2}$ lasers has dealt with extreme situations such as supercritical explosions accom- panied by instantaneous energy release $^{3}$ and opto thermodynamic transitions for pulses approximating real ones. $^{4}$ However, because the lack of an equation which satisfactorily describes the state of water over the whole range of variation of the parameters, cal- culations cannot give reliable predictions of the con- sequences of the irradiation of water by high-intensity pulses.

In order to eliminate these problems, it is necessary to measure the pulse energy fluxes for which total evaporation of scatterers occurs and to compare the measured level with the optical breakdown threshold of an aerosol. It is also necessary to investigate the influence of recondensation on the lifetime of the cleared state and on the transmission, if supersaturation occurs as a result of irradiation.

Published results of experimental investigations of the action of pulsed $CO_2$ laser radiation $^{5-8}$ have been obtained at intensities insufficient for supercritical explosion of all droplets in the volumes under study. This was both due to the parameters of the beam at the entrance of the medium and due to nonlinear atten- uation of the radiation as a result of the appreciable (~1) initial optical thickness. This last factor also makes it impossible to give a unique interpretation of the behavior of the local optical characteristics of a fog on which the method of pulsed clearing of aerosols is based.

The present paper reports measurements of the in- tensity of visible radiation scattered by an aqueous aerosol of small optical thickness in a zone irradiated by TEA $CO_2$ laser radiation with an energy density of up to $50 J/cm^2$.

## EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

The apparatus is shown schematically in Fig. 1. A fog of low optical thickness was produced in a generator 1 in the form of an aerosol jet 2.5 mm in diameter having a velocity of 8 m/sec. The most probable drop- let radius was $2.3\ \mu$ and the maximum radius was $\sim5\ \mu$. These microstructure parameters are found fairly frequently in liquid droplet fogs and clouds of various types but are most characteristic of radia- tion fogs. The microstructure of the fog was deter- mined using a sampling method.

The aerosol jet was directed into the focusing re- gion of a $CO_2$ laser beam perpendicular to the direc- tion of the beam so that the cross section of the jet was within the focusing cone. The radiation source was an atmospheric-pressure gas-discharge $CO_2$ laser with a pulse energy of up to 10 J and duration of 300 nsec at half-maximum intensity. The radiation energy was measured using an IMO-2 calorimetric detector 2 in each experiment. The pulse shape was recorded at irregular intervals using an FP-5 photo- detector and remained constant in the whole series of experiments. The radiation energy density was varied between 5 and $50 J/cm^2$ by changing the pump energy and using polyethylene terephthalate filters $60\ \mu$ thick having a $\sim$50% transmission coefficient.

FIG. 1. Schematic diagram of the apparatus.

A probe beam from an He-Ne laser 3 was directed into the interaction zone perpendicular to the aerosol jet at an angle of $\sim45^{\circ}$ with respect to the direction of the laser beam. The radiation scattered at an angle of $15^{\circ}$ was fed to an FÉU-38 photomultiplier 5 using an optical microscope system 4. The photomultiplier signal was recorded using an S8-2 oscilloscope 6 which was triggered by a discharge trigger pulse in the $CO_2$ laser 7 in order to synchronize the irradiation and recording processes. The linear field of view of the microscope was ~1 mm so that only the scattered radiation from the interaction zone could be recorded. Extraneous illumination from a spark 11 produced at the focus of the lens 8 and from the discharge in the gas cell of the irradiating laser was suppressed by interference filters.

The occurrence of a spark in the focal region pro- vided another possible method of recording the clearing by taking a photograph of the aerosol jet. In this case, a camera 9 recorded the integrated effect over the lifetime of the spark which functioned as a source of illumination synchronized with the irradiation.

For energy densities of less than $25 J/cm^2$ in the interaction zone, no spark occurred at the focus. The effect was then photographed using an SKS-1M high-speed camera 10 and He-Ne laser radiation scattered at an angle of $\sim10^{\circ}$ was used. Photographs at speeds of $\sim4\times10^3$ frames/sec also gave a direct estimate of the clearing lifetime.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 2 shows an integrated photograph of a fog jet exposed to a radiation pulse having an energy density $\gtrsim25 J/cm^2$. The exposure time determined by the luminescence lifetime of the spark was $5\ \mu sec$. Clearing was indicated by a lack of scattering in the zone where the jet and the region occupied by the radia- tion intersected. Mention may be made of the enhanced brightness at the edges of the cleared zone which was evidently due to a decrease in the intensity at the beam edges and due to resultant clouding of the fog.

Total clearing is found on exposure to densities higher than $\sim 20 \mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$. Several stages can be distinguished on the oscillograms. As a result of the action of the pulse, a certain minimum (with respect to the following stages) scattering level is observed in all cases. The duration of this stage is $\sim 50 \mu \mathrm{sec}$, which is substantially longer than the irradiation time $(\sim 1.5 \mu \mathrm{sec})$ and the time taken for gasdynamic expansion of the droplet but it satisfactorily agrees with the calculated lifetime for the scattering by shock waves. $^{9}$ If the minimum of the scattered radiation intensity observed in the initial stage of the process is finite (curves 2-7 in Fig. 3), the interaction zone subsequently undergoes clouding at a rate which depends on the incident energy density. The lifetime of the perturbed transmission in the observation zone is governed by the velocity of the jet and under the experimental conditions is $0.3 \mathrm{msec}$. If the duration of the clouding stage is within this range (curves 4-7), a stage of stable transmission is observed; in the opposite case (curves 2 and 3), the clouding process is "cut off" by the motion of the jet which simulates wind-induced drift. In the case of total clearing (curve 1), this state is conserved up to a time governed by the wind-induced drift.

It can be seen that total clearing is found for a threshold radiation energy density of approximately 30 $\mathrm{J} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$. Provided that the bulk coefficient of absorption of radiation by condensed water is constant $(\alpha_{\text {abs }} \approx 800 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1})$, this would correspond to the absorption of $24 \mathrm{~kJ}$ of radiation energy per unit volume, i.e., 10.6 times the specific heat of evaporation at the normal boiling point. It has been established theoretically $^{4}$ that total evaporation of a particle takes place at a radiation energy density higher than $25 \mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$. In this case, the energy absorbed during the irradiation process is twice the normal heat of evaporation. Thus, both theory and experiment indicate a decrease in the absorption efficiency during the irradiation process, although the dependence $\alpha_{\text {abs }} \sim \rho^{2}$ assumed in the calculations ( $\rho$ is the density of the material) is evidently too strong since a calculated threshold of $25 \mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ was obtained for pulses of $\sim 10$ nsec leading edge duration, whereas the experimental value was $\sim 100$ nsec. Thus, from the energy point of view a good agreement is observed between theory and experiment but in this case the pulse leading edge duration is used as the fitting parameter.

All the oscillograms shown in Fig. 3 clearly indicate aftereffects since the pulse duration is shorter than the temporal recording precision. However, it should be noted that in all cases, clouding is observed in the initial stage of the process corresponding to the time of action of the pulse. According to theoretical predictions, $^{4,9}$ this clouding in the visible part of the spectrum is not only due to the scattering by shock waves, noted above, but is also due to thermal expansion of the droplet which has a significant influence during the initial stage of the explosion.

Important factors are the lifetime of the cleared state and the reasons for the decay of this state. Thus, the following reasoning is aimed at interpreting the second stage of the process (clouding). It can be seen from the oscillograms that clouding is only observed under conditions of partial clearing of the fog, i.e., in the presence of condensed particles. The origin of these particles may be established by means of a qualitative optothermodynamic analysis of the process. On the phase $p-V$ diagram (Fig. 4) the following transitions take place as a function of the rate of energy release in the droplet. Transition 1 corresponds to supercritical explosion for an energy input twice the normal heat of evaporation. In this case, the final state is near the saturation curve $S$ for an ambient gas pressure $p_{0}$. For an energy input $1.5 Q<Q<2 Q$, explosion also takes place in the supercritical region (transition 2) but the adiabatic unloading curve intersects the absolute thermodynamic instability boundary $B$. Supersaturation is removed within $\sim 1$ nsec (Refs. 10 and 11), which is substantially shorter than the inertial confinement time of the material. In this process, the entropy of the droplet material undergoes a jump and a transition $\Delta_{1}$ takes place to the isotherm $T_{2}$ of a two-phase medium. Thus, a thermodynamic equilibrium is established within the sphere of the explosion products. However, the resultant two-phase medium is in mechanical nonequilibrium with respect to the surrounding atmosphere. As a result, further gasdynamic relaxation takes place until the pressures are equalized. A characteristic feature of the irradiating pulses is the presence of a low-intensity "tail". Thus, it is assumed that the process ends with an isobaric-isothermal transition to the point $e_{2}$. Transition 3 corresponds to subcritical explosion. In this case, a two-phase medium is formed by the jump $\Delta_{2}$ when the left-hand branch of the spinodal $B$ corresponding to a superheated

We shall show that diffusive and thermal relaxation of the final spheres may result in supersaturation in the interaction zone. The relationship between supersaturation and the parameters of the medium is expressed by

$$

Here, supersaturation is defined as $\delta_{0}=1-\rho_{0} / \rho$, where $\rho$ is the vapor density. Equation (1) was obtained by averaging the enthalpy and density of the vapor after explosion of the particles over the interaction volume. Dependences of supersaturation on the water content for three temperatures of the explosion products are plotted in Fig. 5 where curve 1 corresponds to the saturation state of the explosion products and curves 2 and 3 correspond to the superheated vapor. Attention is drawn to the existence of a certain limiting water content, which depends on the temperature of the explosion products, above which no supersaturation occurs. However, this limit is in the range of water content not found in the atmosphere. In the experiment under discussion the water content was $1.9 \mathrm{~g} / \mathrm{cm}^{3}$. Curves 1-3 in Fig. 5, plotted as a function of the water content and initial temperature of the fog, determine the temperature of the products above which no supersaturation occurs. It can be seen that, for a water content of $\sim 2 \mathrm{~g} / \mathrm{cm}^{3}$, this temperature is appreciably higher than the calculated value $^{4}$ and supersaturation is observed experimentally in all cases. The supersaturation level is $\sim 0.1$.

This level is insufficient for homogeneous recon- densation. Thus, only the possibility of heterogeneous clouding is discussed. From the point of view of heterogenous recondensation, there is a fundamental difference between the state $e_{1}$, which concludes transition 1 (see Fig. 4), and the states $e_{2}$ and $e_{3}$. In the states $e_{2}$ and $e_{3}$ the final sphere contains a large number of homogeneously formed droplets with a concentration characteristic of homogeneous formation $\left(\sim 10^{8}-10^{14} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}\right)$, whereas the state $e_{1}$ corresponds to total evaporation of the material and, when supersaturation occurs, only condensation nuclei (whose concentration generally does not exceed $10^{5} \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ ) can act as heterogeneous growth centers.

In the experiments the concentration of fog droplets was $3.3 \times 10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ with a root-mean-cube radius of $2.4 \mu$. Assuming that $90 \%$ of the water content evaporates and the final sphere contains an average of $10^{2}$ particles (this corresponds to a concentration of $2 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ ), we have an initial particle radius of $\sim 0.24 \mu$ and an average scattering cross section per unit volume at the $0.63 \mu$ wavelength $\sigma_{s} \approx 0.60 \cdot 10^{-2} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$, which corresponds to a jet of optical thickness 0.15

$\times10^{-2}$ i.e., to some degree of clearing (the initial optical thickness was $\sim0.3\times10^{-2}$). In the case of supersaturation $\delta_{0}^{0}=0.1$ under these conditions, the maximum radius is $a_{0}\approx0.38\ \mu$ and the maximum scattering cross section is $\sigma_{s}\approx3.75\cdot10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm^{2}}$ (the optical thickness is $\sim0.94\times10^{-2}$), i.e., clouding takes place.

In order to obtain a correct estimate of the clouding time, it is necessary to bear in mind that this is governed by two processes: the growth kinetics of the droplets and the diffusion of vapor from the final sphere. The actual clouding time corresponds to the slower process. The diffusion time is $(10r_{0})^{2}/4D_{v}\sim10\ \mu\mathrm{sec}$. The droplet growth time can be estimated using Eq. Assuming, as for estimates of the scattering cross section, that $N=2\cdot10^{3}$ $\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, $\delta_{0}^{0}=0.1$, $a_{0}=0.38\ \mu$, $r_{0}=0.24\ \mu$, we find that for $\alpha=0.03$ the droplets grow to $0.9a_{0}$ over $123\ \mu\mathrm{sec}$. The clouding time is thus within the observation interval $(300\ \mu\mathrm{sec})$ and it is governed by the droplet growth kinetics.

The recondensation time corresponding to the $e_{1}$ state is estimated from Eq. (4) with no stipulations. It has been noted that in this case, the concentration of active nuclei does not exceed $10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$. The corresponding recondensation time is no less than $\sim1$ sec, i.e., over the observation time no effective scatterers are formed in the interaction zone.

Thus, these estimates show that oscillograms 2-7 in Fig. 3 correspond to optothermodynamic transitions 2 and 3 in Fig. 4 and oscillogram 1 corresponds to optothermodynamic transition 1.

## CONCLUSIONS
These results indicate that a fine-droplet fog can be completely cleared by microsecond TEA $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ laser radiation. The totally cleared state remains stable for a time governed by the wind-induced clouding of the interaction zone. The required energy density (30 $\mathrm{J/cm^{2}}$) indicates a low local efficiency of utilization of the incident energy, which is due to clearing of the water as a nonlinear material. This means that the interaction zone in real aerosols may be lengthened substantially, i.e., the energy utilization efficiency for large optical thicknesses may be increased. The results indicate that this possibility evidently is not realized because of losses due to the scattering of radiation by shock waves. The energy threshold for total clearing is lower than the optical breakdown threshold. In the case of partial clearing, an aftereffect is observed in the form of clouding due to heterogeneous recondensation on droplets formed homogeneously as a result of explosions, contained in the final sphere. Supersaturation in the interaction zone occurs as a result of diffusive and thermal relaxation of the final spheres.
