# Theoretical and experimental investigations of the micronization of organic solids by rapid expansion of supercritical solutions

B. Helfgen *, M. Türk, K. Schaber

Institut für Technische Thermodynamik und Kältetechnik, Universität Karlsruhe (TH), Richard-Willstätter-Allee 2, 76128 Karlsruhe, Germany

## Abstract

The RESS-Process (Rapid Expansion of Supercritical Solutions) is an innovative and promising technology to produce small particles and offers interesting applications for difficult-to-comminute species such as certain pharmaceuticals. Our RESS-experiments are carried out with an apparatus suitable for temperatures up to 600 K and pressures up to 60 MPa. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) and trifluoromethane (CHF₃) have been used as supercritical solvents and cholesterol and benzoic acid as solutes. Besides the experimental investigations, the RESS-Process is modelled numerically considering the three parts capillary inlet-capillary-microscale free jet. The evolution of fluid pressure and temperature along the expansion path are used to calculate the supersaturation of the real mixtures CO₂/cholesterol, CO₂/benzoic acid and CHF₃/benzoic acid and the respective nucleation rates. The results of these calculations are theoretical nucleation rates of about $10^{26}\ \mathrm{cm}^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ at residence times of $10^{-7}\ \mathrm{s}$ in the free jet. © 2000 Elsevier Science S.A. All rights reserved.

Keywords: RESS; Micronization; Cholesterol; Benzoic acid; CO₂; CHF₃; Supersaturation; Nucleation

## 1. Introduction

The interest on supercritical fluids has increased rapidly over the last few years. One of the processes making use of the positive characteristics of a supercritical fluid as a solvent like high solvent capacity and compressibility is the RESS-Process (Rapid Expansion of Supercritical Solutions). In this process, a solution in the supercritical state is expanded rapidly through a throttling device, e.g., a capillary nozzle. Due to rapid changes in density and therefore solvent power, the solution becomes highly supersaturated and consequently particles of high purity with small sizes (see Fig. 1a and b) and relatively narrow size distribution are formed. The process is applicable to different areas like the pharmaceutic, polymer or ceramic industry [1–4]. The advantages of the RESS-Process over other processes for particle generation with supercritical fluids is the possibility to produce particles lower than $1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ in contrast to the PGSS-Process, where particle size is of some microns, and to obtain solvent-free particles in contrast to the GAS- or PCA-Process, where liquid solvents besides the supercritical fluid are required.

At our institute, we investigated the influence of various parameters like pre-expansion temperature and pressure and nozzle temperature on the RESS-Process using CO₂ and CHF₃ as solvents and cholesterol and benzoic acid as solutes [5–8]. The apparatus used for the experiments is suitable for temperatures up to 600 K and pressures up to 60 MPa. The particle size and particle number concentration are measured in situ and online with a Three-Wave-length-Extinction-Measurement-Technique (3-WEM) [9] at our apparatus in contrast to the offline measurement techniques normally cited in literature like optical microscopy and SEM. The results of the experiments showed different dependences on the researched parameters.

Besides the experimental work, a model was developed to describe the process in the capillary nozzle and the free jet region. The aim of the theoretical work is to understand the complex process of the particle formation, which cannot be explained in a sufficient way until now. ## 2. Experimental setup and results

The flow apparatus for the RESS-Process is shown in Fig. 2. The gaseous solvent is cleaned in a column with activated carbon and a molecular sieve, condensed and pressurized to the desired pressure with a diaphragm pump. The resulting mass flow rate is measured with a mass flow meter. After heating the solvent to the extraction tempera- ture, it passes through the extraction column, which is packed with the solute. The supercritical solution flows through a heated sampling valve to the heated high pres- sure vessel to reduce the flow velocity and to adjust the pre-expansion temperature. The following expansion through the laser-drilled capillary nozzle with an inner diameter of about $45\ \mu$m and a length of $350\ \mu$m into the expansion chamber under ambient conditions results in particle formation. The particle diameter and particle num- ber concentration are measured online with a 3-WEM at a variable distance to the nozzle exit. To evaluate the results of the 3-WEM measurements, comparative particle size measurements with different measurement techniques were carried out [12], which gave corresponding results. Besides the online measurements, isokinetic probes can be taken out of the expansion chamber for offline measurements.

### 3. Modelling

Because of low solute concentrations ($y^*{\leq}4{\times}10^{-3}$), the modelling can be performed considering only the pure solvent. The model includes the three parts capillary inlet, the capillary nozzle and the supersonic free jet region to the Mach disk. The flow over the distance $x$ is described by one-dimensional, steady-state mass, momentum and energy balances (Eqs. (1)-(3)). In the capillary inlet adiabatic flow is assumed whereas in the capillary itself and in the free jet heat exchange is included. The unknown entrance velocity at the capillary inlet is determined by the fact that at the capillary exit ($x/L=1$ with $L=L_{\rm Inlet}+L_{\rm Nozzle}$) sonic velocity $w_{\rm S}$ (Eq. (4)) ($\rm Ma=w/w_{\rm S}=1$) must be reached. The distance from the nozzle exit to the Mach disk, $L_{\rm M}$, and the diameter of the Mach disk, $D_{\rm M}$, are calculated using Eqs. (5) and (6) [13,14]. For calculating the thermodynamic properties of the solvent, we use the egB-EoS (Eq. (7)). For comparison, we made calculations with the precise fundamental equation of Span and Wagner for ${\rm CO}_2$ [15]. The differences between the calculated values, of the sonic velocity $w_{\rm S}$, for example, were always less than 3% [7]. The advantages of the egB-Eos are the wide application possibilities and the reliability in describing the supercritical densities and the caloric properties. In contrary to the egB-EoS, cubic equation of states do not give accurate results of the caloric properties [7,16].

$$
w \frac{\mathrm{d} P}{\mathrm{~d} x}+\rho \frac{\mathrm{d} w}{\mathrm{~d} x}+\frac{\rho w}{A} \frac{\mathrm{d} A}{\mathrm{~d} x}=0 \tag{1}
$$

$$
\rho w \frac{\mathrm{d} w}{\mathrm{~d} x}+\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{~d} x}=-\frac{2 f w^{2} p}{D_{\text {Nozzle }}}
\tag{2}
$$

$$
w \frac{\mathrm{d} w}{\mathrm{~d} x}+\frac{\mathrm{d} h}{\mathrm{~d} x}=\frac{\mathrm{d} q}{\mathrm{~d} x}
\tag{3}
$$

$$
w_{\mathrm{S}}=\sqrt{\frac{c_{\mathrm{p}}}{c_{\mathrm{v}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} p}{\mathrm{~d} \rho}\right)_{T}}
\tag{4}
$$

$$
\frac{L_{\mathrm{M}}}{D_{\text {Nozzle }}}=0.67 \sqrt{\frac{p_{0}}{p_{\text {Exp.ch. }}}}
\tag{5}
$$

$$
\frac{D_{\mathrm{M}}}{L_{\mathrm{M}}}=0.5625
\tag{6}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z(\tau, \delta)=1 & +\delta B_{1}+\delta^{2} B_{2}+\delta^{3} B_{3}+\delta^{4} B_{4}+\delta^{5} B_{5} \\
& +\delta^{2}\left(B_{6}+\delta^{2} B_{7}\right) \exp \left(-\delta^{2}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

In the egB-EoS, the compression factor $Z$ is a function of the reciprocal reduced temperature $\tau=T_{\mathrm{C}} / T$ and of the reduced density $\delta=\rho / \rho_{\mathrm{C}}$, using the coefficients $B_{i}=$ $B_{i}\left(\tau, e_{j}\right)$ with $e_{j}=e_{j}(\omega, \chi)$, which depend on the Pitzer factor $\omega$ and the Stiel factor $\chi$ [10].

As mentioned above, it is not possible to evaluate the results of the modelling by comparing them with measurements in the microscale region. Because of the discontinuity at the Mach disk, the comparison with measured quantities in the expansion chamber is as well not permissible. Only the measured mass flow rate can be taken into consideration to evaluate the modelling but due to the very small measurements of the capillary nozzle, the nozzle diameter is not known exactly. This fact shows the need to determine accurately the mass flow rate in dependence of accurately known nozzle diameters and compare it with the modelling, which is done at the moment at our institute.

In contrary to the pressure, where no clear difference between the two solvents can be seen, the temperature is visibly higher inside the capillary nozzle (capillary entrance $x / L=0.485$ and capillary exit $x / L=1$ ) in case of $\mathrm{CHF}_{3}$. The calculated fluid density plotted in Fig. 6 decreases continuously as expected inside the capillary nozzle followed by a sharp decrease at the transition to the free jet. The fluid velocity shows the opposite behaviour (Fig. 7). It increases slowly inside the capillary, reaching sonic velocity at the exit and a Mach number of about 2.5 just before the Mach disk with velocities of around 500 and $600 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}$ for $\mathrm{CHF}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$, respectively. In order to study particle formation, the supersaturation $S$ and the nucleation rate $J$ must be calculated and therefore the knowledge of the phase behaviour of the real supercritical mixture is necessary. Fig. 8 shows calculated solubilities of benzoic acid in $\text{CO}_2$ and in $\text{CHF}_3$ at extraction temperature (318 K) and at the approximate fluid temperature at the capillary nozzle exit. In the pressure range up to 10 MPa the solubility of benzoic acid in $\text{CO}_2$ is markedly lower than in $\text{CHF}_3$ whereas for pressures greater than 10 MPa an opposite behaviour is observed. Furthermore, at intermediate pressures where the solubility strongly increases with increasing pressure at constant temperature a retrograde behaviour (decreasing solubility with increasing temperature) in the vicinity of the solvents critical point is noted. For reasons of clarity, the analogous curves for cholesterol are not shown here. The solubility of cholesterol in $\text{CO}_2$ is about two orders of magnitude lower than the solubility of benzoic acid in $\text{CO}_2$.

With the calculated pressure and temperature changes of the pure fluid along the expansion path, the supersaturation $S$ of the solute in the real mixtures $\text{CO}_2$/cholesterol, $\text{CO}_2$/benzoic acid and $\text{CHF}_3$/benzoic acid were calculated using a modified PR-EoS [11,17,18]. The supersaturation $S$ is defined according to Eq. (8) as the ratio of the equilibrium mole fraction $y^*$ at extraction conditions $T_{\text{Extr}}$ and $p_{\text{Extr}}$ times the fugacity coefficient $\Phi$ at $y_{\text{Extr}}^*$ to the equilibrium mole fraction $y^*$ at prevailing temperature and pressure times the fugacity coefficient $\Phi$ at $y^*$.
$$
\begin{aligned}
S= & \Phi\left(T, p, y_{\text{Extr}}^*\right) y^*\left(T_{\text{Extr}}, p_{\text{Extr}}\right) \\
& /\left(\Phi\left(T, p, y^*\right) y^*(T, p)\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

The results of the supersaturation for the systems $\text{CO}_2$/cholesterol, $\text{CO}_2$/benzoic acid and $\text{CHF}_3$/benzoic acid are presented in Fig. 9. Already inside the capillary nozzle, a supersaturation of about 10 is reached for the systems $\text{CO}_2$/cholesterol and $\text{CO}_2$/benzoic acid so nucleation could not be excluded. The difference in the curves for benzoic acid in the solvents $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{CHF}_3$ is mainly a consequence of the different phase behaviour. For the two mixtures in question, the retrograde region covers the range between 16 and 8.5 MPa in case of $\text{CO}_2$/benzoic acid and from about 10 to 5.5 MPa for $\text{CHF}_3$/benzoic acid. This leads to an earlier and stronger decrease in solubility, which results in the higher supersaturation for $\text{CO}_2$/benzoic acid in comparison to $\text{CHF}_3$/benzoic acid. However, the lower temperature of $\text{CO}_2$ inside the capillary nozzle reduces this effect. For $\text{CO}_2$/cholesterol, the solubility decreases stronger with respect to pressure than for $\text{CO}_2$/benzoic acid, which is the explanation for the higher supersaturation along the expansion path.

The maximum theoretical supersaturations are in the range between $10^6$ and almost $10^9$, which is the result of the sharp temperature drop in the free jet. The objective of the modelling is to determine the location of nucleation, therefore, a nucleation theory has to be applied. As a first approach to calculate the nucleation rate $J$ (Eq. (9)), the classical nucleation theory was used.
$$
J=K n \exp \left[-\Delta G^{*} /(k T)\right]
\tag{9}
$$

$$
K=\theta \kappa \alpha_{\mathrm{C}} n \pi r^{* 2} c
\tag{9a}
$$

$$
\Delta G^{*}=4 / 3 \pi \sigma r^{* 2}
\tag{9b}
$$

$$
r^{*}=2 \sigma v_{\mathrm{S}} /(k T \ln S).
\tag{9c}
$$

The non-isothermal factor $\theta$ was set to 1 (dilute mixture), the condensation coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}$ to 0.1 according to Springer [19] and for the solid-fluid interfacial tension $\sigma$ a value of 0.02 N/m was used.

For instance, $S$ equals approximately 3 at the capillary exit in case of $CHF_3$/benzoic acid and approximately 31 in case of $CO_2$/benzoic acid (see Fig. 9). The corresponding nucleation rates are $6 \times 10^{16}$ and about $10^{26}$ $\text{cm}^{-3}$ $\text{s}^{-1}$, respectively. The nucleation rate in case of $CO_2$/benzoic acid reaches high values already at the beginning of the capillary nozzle. According to the classical nucleation theory, this should result in particle formation inside the nozzle. For the other two systems, the nucleation rate increases at the transition from the capillary nozzle to the supersonic free jet so particle formation would be expected to occur here. As in the case of benzoic acid, the structure of the resulting particles obviously does not depend on the solvent type for the systems investigated. It is not possible that in one case benzoic acid particles are formed at the beginning of the capillary and in the other case only at the end with totally different surrounding conditions. This shows the need to improve the already to real mixtures fitted assumptions in the classical nucleation theory.

## 4. Conclusions

RESS-experiments were carried out for the materials cholesterol and benzoic acid using $CO_2$ and $CHF_3$ as solvents. The resulting particle sizes were in the range of 100-400 nm for cholesterol and 200-1400 nm for benzoic acid showing different dependences of the varied parameters like the pre-expansion conditions.

Modelling the microscale region of the RESS-Process must take the place of lacking possibilities to measure any quantity inside the capillary nozzle or the initial stage of the supersonic free jet. Therefore, evaluating the model is of great importance and is carried out at the moment at our institute.

The one-dimensional flow model for the pure solvent includes heat exchange and friction in the capillary nozzle, isentropic flow in the nozzle inlet area and heat exchange in the supersonic free jet. The results show drastic changes of the process parameters $(p, T, \rho, w)$ at the transition from the capillary nozzle to the supersonic free jet, which lead to a fast increase in supersaturation in this region.

Due to the rapid changes in process parameters, assumptions taken for modelling the process of nucleation have to be carefully checked. It was shown that the application of the classical nucleation theory does not lead to acceptable results in relation to the process. Further experimental and theoretical work should result in better understanding of the underlying phenomena and pointing out the relationship between the process conditions and the product quality.
