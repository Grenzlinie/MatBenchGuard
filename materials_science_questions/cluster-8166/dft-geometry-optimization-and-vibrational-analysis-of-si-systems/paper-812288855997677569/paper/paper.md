A tetrahedral model is presented to explain the bonding properties of nonstoichiometric amorphous silicon oxynitride ($a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$) alloys, grown under highly nonequilibrium conditions, whose structures obey neither the random bonding model nor the random mixture model. Based on our approach, a numerical procedure is proposed to obtain the relative atomic percentages of each component structural phase from the deconvolution of the high-resolution x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region. The tetrahedral model is then used to study the bonding properties of $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films grown by electron-cyclotron resonance plasma-enhanced chemical-vapor deposition, having relatively low values of the O/Si atomic ratio ($\leq$0.37) incorporated in their networks. The experimental results show that five tetrahedral phases (tetrahedrons Si–Si$_4$, Si–Si$_2$ON, Si–N$_4$, Si–Si$_3$O, and Si–O$_4$) are present in $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films with low N/Si atomic ratios ($\leq$0.93), while only three phases (Si–SiON$_2$, Si–N$_4$, and Si–O$_2$N$_2$) are present in samples with higher N/Si atomic ratios ($\geq$1.12). The Si$_3$N$_4$ phase is the most important bonding unit and it is the only phase present in all our samples. These results are corroborated by survey scans and by comparison with the high-resolution XPS spectra in the N 1$s$ region. They support the validity of the model proposed for $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ alloys and the XPS analysis, correlated with growth conditions, presented in this work. © 2005 American Institute of Physics.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Due to their technological importance in microelectron-ics and optoelectronics applications, $^{1,2}$ SiO$_x$N$_y$ thin films have been the subject of increasing research interest in recent years. $^{3}$ For example, SiO$_x$N$_y$ films with a variable visible refractive index are obtained by changing the oxygen and nitrogen content inside their networks which is of interest for integrated optics. $^{4}$ These SiO$_x$N$_y$ films may induce low stress on silicon substrates, $^{5}$ which is useful for micromechanical devices. Also, the excellent passivating properties of silicon oxynitride on the surface of silicon allow their use as active layers in transistors and memory devices. $^{6}$ Despite the industrial interest in SiO$_x$N$_y$, several important aspects of this material remain unclear. For example, the bonding structure of nonstoichiometric $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films remains poorly understood, which limits the further optimization of their optical, electrical, and mechanical properties. To the best of our knowledge, the composition of the bonds and the network structure for these films are still not well understood and little effort has so far been dedicated to clarify this problem. Furthermore, among those few works that have been published on this topic, $^{7-11}$ the study of the structural properties of $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films has been limited to the identification of some well-known stable phases without making a coordinated effort to establish all the phases including the metastable structures that are present in their networks, in particular, those that might be present in low-temperature growth.

According to the commonly accepted view, there are two different approaches for modeling the structure of amorphous nonstoichiometric alloys: the random bonding model (RBM) and the random mixture model (RMM). $^{11-14}$ Both are based on silicon-centered tetrahedrons and in-plane triply coordinated nitrogen as basic bonding structures. According to the RBM, the bonding structure of $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films should be a statistically controlled mixture of several Si-centered tetrahedrons occurring with a probability which considers the nearest N and O atoms neighboring the Si sites to be randomly bonded to the Si atom. For RMM, the SiO$_x$N$_y$ film should be a random mixture in which the more stable SiO$_2$ and Si$_3$N$_4$ phases predominate over other bonding units. Therefore, it is expected that RMM will model rather well the bonding structure of SiO$_x$N$_y$ films grown at very high temperatures where the laws of thermodynamics are more likely to control their structural properties. Notice that, according to the phase diagram of the Si–N–O system, stable Si$_3$N$_4$ and SiO$_2$ phases can coexist separated by Si$_2$N$_2$O in the bulk under equilibrium conditions. $^{15}$ At low growth temperatures, it is likely that Si$_3$N$_4$ and SiO$_2$ also coexist with other metastable bonding configurations made by the plasma or by ion impact fragments, $^{16}$ more in agreement with RBM.

However, when growing at low temperatures in a plasma environment, or by other highly nonequilibrium process, the presence of bonding units in which the Si atoms are not randomly bonded to O and/or N atoms cannot be discarded. Depending on the flow ratio of the precursors during film growth, several bonding structures can be present to form a mixture of Si-centered tetrahedrons that do not obey RBM or RMM, as will be explained in more detail in Sec. II. The proposal advanced in this paper is that $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films grown at low temperatures in a highly nonequilibrium process, such as a plasma environment, should be considered as a multiple-phase structure which can only be fully explained with a more general model than RBM and RMM.

At low growth temperatures, the low surface mobility of species arriving from the vapor phase favors the presence of multiphase structures which do not obey RBM or RMM. In fact, the structure of $a$-SiO$_x$:H films has already been reported as a sort of mixture of RBM and RMM structures.$^{17}$ Also, although the microstructure of $a$-SiN$_x$ films depends on the preparation technique,$^{18}$ the bonding properties of $a$-SiN$_x$ films grown by low-pressure chemical-vapor deposition (LPCVD) cannot be explained with RBM or RMM.$^{19}$ Those results present experimental evidence of bonding structures that do not correspond to the two extreme cases considered in RBM and RMM, and thus justify the development of a more general model to analyze the bonding properties of films grown under highly nonequilibrium conditions. In this work we propose an extension of the random mixture model (ERMM) to identify and to study quantitatively all the structural phases incorporated in $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films grown by chemical-vapor deposition (CVD) in an electron-cyclotron resonance (ECR) plasma. Once the bonding structure is established, the calculations by ERMM of the relative atomic percentages of structural phases present in ECRCVD SiO$_x$N$_y$ films is based on the deconvolution of high-resolution x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region. The calculations are then used to aid the interpretation of XPS data in correlation with growth conditions. A method has also been developed to corroborate those results using survey scans and the high-resolution XPS spectra in the N $1s$ region. Although the extended model which we present contains both RMM and RBM as limiting cases, there is no instance in which the XPS data from our films can be explained by either one of these limited models, as will be clear from our results.

This article is the first part of a more general study of the structural properties of ECRCVD SiO$_x$N$_y$ films deposited at low temperature on several substrates using various gas flow compositions in a dilute mixture of SiH$_4$+N$_2$ in Ar. Section II describes in detail the model proposed to interpret the XPS data and the calculations of the binding energies ($E_B$) in spectral regions associated with Si $2p_{3/2}$ and N $1s$ core levels of all the possible component phases for hydrogen-free $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films. In Sec. III we briefly review the description of our ECRCVD system, and the procedure for film preparation,$^{5}$ to establish the necessary base for Sec. IV, which describes the analysis procedure used to obtain and identify the component peaks in the regions Si $2p_{3/2}$ and N $1s$ of the XPS spectra. Section IV also presents the results of this analysis for a series of films, which will be correlated with growth conditions and will also be compared with those obtained from the survey scans for a self-consistency check in Sec. V. We summarize our conclusions in Sec. VI.

## II. TETRAHEDRAL MODEL PROPOSED FOR AMORPHOUS SiO$_x$N$_y$ FILMS

In the model to be presented in this section we will only consider hydrogen-free $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films, since from the study of the mechanical properties of our films, we have obtained indirect evidence that the hydrogen concentration, if any, is very low. In fact, according to our recent work,$^{5}$ where we studied the intrinsic stress of $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films deposited by ECRCVD on silicon substrates using a dilute SiH$_4$ and N$_2$ mixture in Ar, the observed tensile stress generation in some of our films is a result in direct conflict with hydrogen incorporation. Since we believe that H does not control the intrinsic stress and that its atomic percentage remains relatively low in our films, we consider it unnecessary to further complicate our present model by considering the presence of hydrogen.

Plasma growth produces nonequilibrium mixtures on an atomic scale, having Si-centered tetrahedrons as their simplest subunits. The fundamental starting point for the description of a material using a tetrahedral model is the choice of the most adequate tetrahedral structures among all the possible candidates. This selection is usually based on chemical arguments. For example, a bond-type is excluded when the related bonding energy is significantly lower than that associated to other bonds, which are therefore favored, given their greater stability. For hydrogen-free $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films, the network consists of several Si-centered tetrahedrons. Each Si atom at the center of a tetrahedron has four valence electrons available, each of which can participate in a bond to one of the following three units: Si(4), O(2), and N(3); here we have indicated in parentheses the number of valence electrons which each of these units has available for bonding to the network. When a sufficient number of atoms of N or O are incorporated in an $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ network, heterobonds such as Si–N and Si–O bonds are preferably formed,$^{20}$ while no or very few homobonds such as Si–Si, N–N, and O–O bonds are formed. This is due to the relatively weak bonding energies of the homobonds [$E_B$(Si–Si)=1.8 eV, $E_B$(N–N)=1.6 eV, and $E_B$(O–O)=1.4 eV (Ref. 21)] which are considerably smaller than those of heterobonds [$E_B$(Si–N)=3.5 eV and $E_B$(Si–O)=3.8 eV (Ref. 22)]. When an insufficient number of atoms of N or O are incorporated in $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films, it is, however, possible to create intrinsic defects such as Si–Si bonds. In such case, given the energetic consideration, and since only Si, O, and N are available for bonding to the central Si atom, there are at most 15 distinct Si-centered tetrahedrons, Si–Si$_{4-(v+\eta)}$O$_v$N$_\eta$ ($v+\eta$$\leqslant$4, and $v$, $\eta$=0,1,2,3,4), which can be constructed from these bonding units. Therefore, we consider a model where 15 structural phases are possible for hydrogen-free $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films. For example, if the presence of nitrogen in the deposition chamber is considerably larger than that of oxygen, as is the case of our films because oxygen is accidentally introduced in rather small amounts, then the Si–N bonds will occur with higher frequency or probability than the Si–O bonds. In fact, this is one of the main reasons why the RBM is not satisfied, since then the statistics is biased by the overwhelming presence of nitrogen. In other words, the different configurations obtained to place $4-(\upsilon+\eta)$ silicon atoms, $\upsilon$ oxygen atoms, and $\eta$ nitrogen atoms, bonded to a central Si atom, are not indistinguishable, since the relative abundance of nitrogen would favor the Si–N bonds.

Once the tetrahedral units have been selected, the next step in the identification of component peaks in the XPS spectra is the calculation of the binding energies [$E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})$ and $E_B(\text{N }1s)$] for each one of the component tetrahedrons in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ and N $1s$ regions of the spectra. In this work, the calculations of $E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})$ are based on the assumption that the displacement of the binding energy is linear with the increase of the coordination number of oxygen or nitrogen; i.e., $\text{Si-Si}_{4-\upsilon}\text{O}_{\upsilon}(\upsilon=0-4)$ is located between tetrahedrons corresponding to $a\text{-Si}$ and $a\text{-SiO}_2$, whose $E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})$ were chosen to be 99.6 eV (Ref. 23) and 103.6 eV,24,25 respectively. Therefore, the binding energy for $\text{Si-Si}_{4-\upsilon}\text{O}_{\upsilon}$ is $E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=[99.6+(103.6-99.6)\upsilon/4]$ eV. Similarly, $\text{Si-O}_{\upsilon}\text{N}_{4-\upsilon}(\upsilon=0-4)$ is located between tetrahedrons corresponding to $a\text{-Si}_3\text{N}_4$ [$E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=102.0$ eV (Ref. 26)] and $a\text{-SiO}_2$. Thus, the binding energy for $\text{Si-O}_{4-\upsilon}\text{N}_{\upsilon}$ will be $E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=[102.0+(103.6-102.0)\upsilon/4]$ eV. For the tetrahedrons that are not located between two well-known phases, their binding energies are calculated by the following procedure. Consider the $\text{Si-SiO}_{\upsilon}\text{N}_{3-\upsilon}(\upsilon=0-3)$ units, which are located between bonding units corresponding to $\text{Si-SiN}_3[E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=101.4$ eV] and $\text{Si-SiO}_3[E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=102.6$ eV]. Its binding energy will be $E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=[101.4+(102.6-101.4)\upsilon/3]$ eV. We list in Table I the binding energies corresponding to all possible $\text{Si-Si}_{4-(\upsilon+\eta)}\text{O}_{\upsilon}\text{N}_{\eta}$ tetrahedrons for hydrogen-free $a\text{-SiO}_x\text{N}_y$ films, calculated following the above procedure.

Scarce experimental data is available for the XPS energy position of the component peaks of $a\text{-SiO}_x\text{N}_y$ films in the N $1s$ spectral region. Notice that the calculation procedure of $E_B(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})$ previously explained requires the knowledge of the binding energies of at least two structural phases. Therefore, this procedure cannot be used to calculate the binding energies $E_B(\text{N }1s)$ corresponding to component tetrahedrons in the N $1s$ spectral region. Calculations of the displacement $\Delta E_B(\text{N }1s)$ with respect to $\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$ [$E_B(\text{N }1s)=397.7$ eV (Ref. 27)] are based on the charge transfer model of Hasegawa <i>et al.</i>,28 which requires (a) to establish the bonding units for these tetrahedrons, followed by (b) the calculation of the binding energies using the partial charges for the N atom in each one of those bonding units.

We now establish the bonding units for the 15 tetrahedrons of interest. The first step is to determine the fraction $f_{\text{Si}}$ of Si atoms in the $a\text{-SiO}_x\text{N}_y$ alloy which can bond to a given Si atom. This fraction is given by14

$$
f_{\text{Si}}=1-\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{3}{4}y. \tag{1}
$$

The terms $x/2$ and $3y/4$ of the previous equation represent the fractions of Si atoms that are required to satisfy the bonds of all the O and N atoms present in the alloy, respectively. Thus, these Si atoms are effectively unavailable to bond to other Si atoms. Since O and N can only bond to the central Si atom, the fractions $f_{\text{O}}$ and $f_{\text{N}}$ of O and N atoms that can bond to a Si atom are given by, respectively,

$$
f_{\text{O}}=x, \tag{2}
$$

$$
f_{\text{N}}=y. \tag{3}
$$

Silicon has four valence electrons, the oxygen two, while the nitrogen has three valence electrons available for bonding. Therefore, the fractions $F$ of the total number of atoms that belong to each species (Si, O, and N) and can bond to a central Si atom are given by12,14

$$
F_{\text{Si}}=\frac{4f_{\text{Si}}}{4f_{\text{Si}}+2f_{\text{O}}+3f_{\text{N}}}=\frac{4-2x-3y}{4}, \tag{4}
$$

$$
F_{\text{O}}=\frac{2f_{\text{O}}}{4f_{\text{Si}}+2f_{\text{O}}+3f_{\text{N}}}=\frac{x}{2}, \tag{5}
$$

Using these equations and basic bonds of tetrahedrons, it is possible to build their bonding units $\mathrm{Si}_{k} \mathrm{O}_{m} \mathrm{N}_{p}$; i.e., according to the bonding unit of $\mathrm{Si}-\mathrm{Si}_{2} \mathrm{ON}$, two silicon atoms, an oxygen atom, and a nitrogen atom bond to a central atom of silicon. Therefore,
$$
\frac{F_{\mathrm{Si}}}{F_{\mathrm{O}}}=\frac{4-2 x-3 y}{2 x}=2 ; \quad \frac{F_{\mathrm{Si}}}{F_{\mathrm{N}}}=\frac{4-2 x-3 y}{3 y}=2. \qquad (7)
$$

The solutions of these equations are $x=1 / 2$ and $y=1 / 3$, that is to say, $\mathrm{Si}_{6} \mathrm{O}_{3} \mathrm{N}_{2}$. For binary alloys, i.e., the $\mathrm{Si}-\mathrm{O}_{4}$ tetrahedron, there are no atoms of silicon and nitrogen bonding to a central silicon atom. Therefore, $F_{\mathrm{Si}}=0$ and $F_{\mathrm{N}}=0$ and their solutions are, respectively, $x=2$ and $y=0$, that is to say, $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$. The bonding units for all the other $\mathrm{Si}-\mathrm{Si}_{4-(v+\eta)} \mathrm{O}_{v} \mathrm{N}_{\eta}$ tetrahedrons were built using the same procedure and are also listed in Table I.

For binary alloys i.e., $\mathrm{SiN}_{x}$ films, the calculation of the chemical shift of the $\mathrm{N} 1 s\left[\Delta E_{B}(\mathrm{~N} \text { 1s) }]\right.$ level is based on the charge transfer model of Hasegawa et al. $^{28}$ According to this model, $\Delta E_{B}(\mathrm{~N} \text { 1s) }$ is proportional to the partial charge $\left(P_{\mathrm{N}}\right)$ on the $\mathrm{N}$ atom. The addition of a negative unit of $P_{\mathrm{N}}$ shifts $E_{B}(\mathrm{~N} \text { 1s) }$ toward a lower binding energy by $11 \mathrm{eV}^{28}$ Therefore, the position of the $\mathrm{N} 1 s$ peak corresponding to the $\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{N}_{4}$ phase can be used to estimate the $\mathrm{N} 1 s$ positions of each tetrahedron in binary alloys. Gritsenko et al. $^{11}$ extended the Hasegawa model $^{28}$ to ternary alloys. According to the model developed by Gritsenko et al. $^{11}$ for a tetrahedron with bonding unit $\mathrm{Si}_{k} \mathrm{O}_{m} \mathrm{N}_{p}$, the partial charge $P_{\mathrm{N}}$ can be deter mined by
$$
P_{\mathrm{N}}=\frac{S_{\mathrm{SiON}}-S_{\mathrm{N}}}{2.08 S_{\mathrm{N}}^{1 / 2}}, \qquad (8)
$$
where
$$
S_{\mathrm{SiON}}=\left(S_{\mathrm{Si}}^{k} S_{\mathrm{O}}^{m} S_{\mathrm{N}}^{p}\right)^{1 /(k+m+p)} \qquad (9)
$$
is the Sanderson electronegativity in the bonding unit of $\mathrm{Si}_{k} \mathrm{O}_{m} \mathrm{N}_{p}$. The Sanderson electronegativity for $\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{O}$, and $\mathrm{N}$ atoms are $S_{\mathrm{Si}}=2.84, S_{\mathrm{O}}=5.21$, and $S_{\mathrm{N}}=4.49$, respectively. $^{29}$ Using Eqs. (8) and (9), together with the linear relationship between $\Delta E_{B}(\mathrm{~N} \text { 1s) }$ and the variation of the partial charges on the $\mathrm{N}$ atom with respect to $\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{N}_{4}$, we calculated the shifts of $E_{B}(\mathrm{~N} \text { 1s) }$ with respect to $\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{N}_{4}$ for all the Si-centered tetrahedrons containing $\mathrm{N}$; they are listed in Table I.

### III. EXPERIMENTS

The ECRCVD system used in the present work has been described in detail in a preceding publication. $^{5}$ Briefly, it consists of a two-chamber stainless-steel vacuum system designed for the ECRCVD process and an AsTEX 2107 diverging field ECR plasma source, which provides incident microwave power up to $700 \mathrm{~W}$ at $2.54 \mathrm{GHz}$. The source includes a directional coupler with power meters to measure forward and reflected power. The ECR plasma chamber is a quartz bell jar of $10-\mathrm{cm}$ diameter and a height of $15 \mathrm{~cm}$. The deposition chamber is a spherical steel container of $50-\mathrm{cm}$ diameter. Our ECRCVD system also includes a loading chamber and a load-lock system to maintain ultrahigh-vacuum conditions. During the deposition process the substrate was not intentionally heated. We have not monitored the temperature during film growth; however, successful thickness measurements by profilometry using the lift-off technique suggest that the deposition temperature was below $120{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ under the conditions used in this study. $^{5}$ The deposition chambers and the sample entry were pumped during film growth with a diffusion pump, which has a conductance of $1000 \mathrm{l} / \mathrm{s}$ at the substrate position. The background pressure in the deposition chamber was lower than $2 \times 10^{-7}$ Torr. Electronic purity grade $\mathrm{Ar}, \mathrm{N}_{2}$, and $\mathrm{SiH}_{4}$ were used for the film deposition; flows were controlled and measured with electronic mass flow controllers. The silicon oxynitride films were fabricated on silicon substrates [Si:P, $N_{D}=(1-2) \times 10^{19} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ ]. All these 2-in.-diameter substrates were (100) oriented. The cleaning procedures to prepare the surfaces of the substrate before the deposition were explained in detail in a preceding publication. $^{5}$ The deposition conditions for the film series presented in this study were chosen to obtain a constant value of the $\mathrm{O} / \mathrm{Si}$ atomic ratio. Those conditions were presented in Ref. 5 and are repeated in Table II. Microwave power was maintained at $500 \mathrm{~W}$ for the deposition of all the films.

### IV. ANALYSIS PROCEDURE AND RESULTS

The samples were analyzed using a VG ESCALab 3 MKII. Electrons were excited using nonmonochromatic Mg $K_\alpha$ radiation at 1253.6 eV, with an experimentally determined spectral resolution of 0.7 eV and a standard error of the measurement of <0.1 eV. About 60 Å of depth was probed using a detector perpendicular to the surface. The analyzed surface was 2×3 mm². The pressure in the analysis chamber was maintained at 10⁻⁹ Torr. Relative concentrations of elements were obtained from survey scans in the range of 0–1000 eV with the spectrometer working with 50-eV pass energy and by dividing the integrated intensity values by their sensitivity factors (0.25 for C 1s, 0.66 for O 1s, 0.42 for N 1s, and 0.27 for Si $2p_{3/2}$) taken from Ref. 30 and verified in our laboratory. The O/Si and N/Si atomic ratios which were obtained from those relative concentrations of elements are also listed in Table II.

According to the proposed ERMM, in order to identify the structural phases present in hydrogen-free $a$-SiOₓNᵧ films it is necessary to discriminate the 15 possible phases listed in Table I, whose energy positions in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ XPS spectra differ by a value of ≧0.2 eV (see Table I). It is then necessary to determine the energy positions of the component peaks obtained from the deconvolution procedure of these spectra with an error ≤0.2 eV. The calibration of our x-ray photoelectron spectrometer followed the procedure reported by Seah et al.,³¹,³² who used the standards E902-94 (1999) and E1015-90 of the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) to show that a VG ESCALab II can be used to obtain the position of a peak with an energy resolution equal to 0.2 eV. Furthermore, we used an in-house nonlinear least-squares program whose construction was based on Refs. 33–36 applying a Shirley-type background subtraction for C, N, and O and a linear background for Si, we corrected all peaks positions for adventitious carbon, at 285.0 eV in binding energy, to adjust for charging effects, and, finally, calibrated the energy scale with the standards for the binding energies corresponding to Cu, Ag, and Au. Thus, we can assure an error of ± 0.2 eV in the energy positions of the component peaks obtained from the deconvolution of our Si $2p_{3/2}$ XPS spectra. This was demonstrated in Ref. 37, where a displacement of 0.1 eV of the Si peak due to the Staebler–Wronski effect was observed.

High-resolution spectra were obtained for Si, N, O, and C with the spectrometer working with 20-eV pass energy. Using the methodology previously explained, the envelopes measured for Si and N have been separated into their component peaks to identify the chemical environments of silicon and nitrogen in the SiOₓNᵧ film network. As mentioned in Sec. II, the position of $a$-Si (Si–Si₄ tetrahedron) was chosen to be at 99.6 eV, in agreement with the literature,²³ while the position of SiO₂ (Si–O₄ tetrahedron) was chosen to be at 103.6 eV,²⁴,²⁵ the position of Si₃N₄ (Si–N₄ tetrahedron) at 102.0 eV,²⁶ and the $E_{B}$(Si $2p_{3/2}$) of all the other structural phases was calculated assuming that its displacement is linear with the increase of the coordination number of the oxygen or nitrogen. Each possible state or bonding unit was fitted using symmetric Gaussian–Lorentzian peaks. The full widths at half maximum (FWHMs) for the component peaks varied within a small range of values, from 1.45 to 1.65 eV, while the FWHM of the peak for each element was maintained at a fixed value during the XPS analysis of all our $a$-SiOₓNᵧ films (1.6 eV for the C 1s peak, 1.7 eV for O 1s, 1.8 eV for N 1s, and 1.45 eV for the Si $2p_{3/2}$ peak corresponding to $a$-Si). For all the other component peaks, we assumed that the FWHM increases by a constant value for each bonded nitrogen or oxygen atom;²⁴,³⁸ i.e., 1.45 eV for the Si–Si₄ tetrahedron, 1.50 eV for the Si–Si₃N (or Si–Si₃O) tetrahedron, 1.55 eV for the Si–Si₂N₂ (or Si–Si₂O₂) tetrahedron, 1.60 eV for the Si–SiN₃ (Si–SiO₃) tetrahedron, and 1.65 eV for the Si–N₄ (Si–O₄) tetrahedron.

### A. High-resolution XPS spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region
According to Fig. 1 and Table III, eight component peaks were obtained in the XPS Si $2p_{3/2}$ spectral region. The peak at 99.6 eV, named Si⁰ in this figure, is only present in films (SiN46, SiN47, and SiN48) having the lowest values of N/Si (≤0.93 Table II). Its relative intensity decreases with increasing N/Si atomic ratio (Fig. 1 and Table II). Notice that all the films have low and almost constant values of O/Si (≤0.37; Table II). The peak located at $100.6\pm 0.2$ eV follows the same behavior of the previous peak, but less rapidly. It disappears at the same time as the peak related to SiO₂ (Si–O₄ tetrahedron), which is positioned at 103.6 eV. These results suggest that the first two peaks are related to tetrahedrons Si–Si₄ (a-Si) and Si–Si₃O (Si₂O), respectively, as corroborated by their binding energies (Table I). It is important to point out that Si⁰ is not related to the Si $2p_{3/2}$ peak of the silicon substrate. It has already been shown that the escape depth for Si $2p_{3/2}$ electrons in an oxynitride film can be given to a good approximation by a linear interpolation between those for pure silicon dioxide and nitride films, λₒₓ [2.96 nm (Ref. 23)] and λₙᵢₜ [1.73 nm (Ref. 23)], respectively. For both extreme casess, the Si $2p_{3/2}$ electron escape depth is smaller than the thickness of our SiOₓNᵧ films (d≥80 nm) by more than an order of magnitude. Furthermore, this same peak is also observed on films grown on InP substrates. Si⁰ should then be related to Si–Si bonds present in our films.

The peak positioned at 102.0 eV is present in all the spectra and corresponds to the Si–N₄ tetrahedron [Si₃N₄, $E_{B}(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=102.0$ eV; Table I]. Therefore, according to Fig. 1, Si₃N₄ is the most important bonding unit present in all our samples, while SiO₂ is the less important phase. For the first three films (SiN46, SiN47, and SiN48), the intensity of the peak positioned at 103.6 eV and related to SiO₂ is low compared with the others. Starting from SiN49, this peak moves towards lower energy and increases its intensity. This result can be explained by considering the low and almost constant values of O/Si in this film series and the possible replacement of an oxygen atom by nitrogen in the Si–O₄ tetrahedron at high values of the N/Si atomic ratio. The relative atomic percentage of the Si–O₄ tetrahedron will mainly depend on the sputtering of oxygen species from the deposition and plasma chamber walls.⁵ At higher values of N/Si, this percentage can also depend on the substitution of oxygen atoms by nitrogen thus transforming the Si–O₄ tetrahedron into more complex tetrahedrons containing N in amounts depending on the nitrogen flow (or N/Si atomic ratio). For example, at moderate values of the N/Si atomic ratio, the Si–O₄ tetrahedron transforms to Si–NO₃ [Si₆O₉N₂, $E_{B}(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=103.2$ eV; Table I]. Then, at high values of N/Si, SiO₂ should disappear to become Si₆O₉N₂ due to low amounts of SiO₂ incorporated in our films (≤8.3%; Table III), as is the case for SiN49. It is also expected that a new substitution of an oxygen atom by a nitrogen atom in the Si–O₃N tetrahedron transforms it to Si–O₂N₂ [Si₃O₃N₂, $E_{B}(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=102.8$ eV; Table I], as shown by our results for films SiN50 and SiN51. We believe that the peak located at 103.2 eV is then associated to the Si–O₃N tetrahedron, while the peak located at 102.8–102.9 eV is associated to the Si–O₂N₂ tetrahedron.

For the three films (SiN46, SiN47, and SiN48) that have the lowest values of (N+O)/Si, the relative intensity of the peak located at 101.2–101.4 eV presents the same behavior as the Si⁰ peak (Fig. 1 and Table III). In the other two films (SiN50 and SiN51), this peak is displaced at 101.6–101.7 eV and its relative intensity increases with increasing N/O atomic ratio. We note that the formation of Si–Si₂ON [Si₆O₃N₂, $E_{B}(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=101.2$ eV; Table I] requires the incorporation of a higher concentration of silicon to the film network and, therefore, the relative intensity of the peak related to this structural phase should qualitatively follow the Si⁰ peak. This is clearly the case for the peak located at 101.2–101.4 eV in the spectra corresponding to SiN46, SiN47, and SiN48 (Fig. 1). However, the disappearance of the Si⁰ peak does not necessarily imply the absence of Si–Si₂ON in the SiOₓNᵧ network. This is the case for SiN49, where the amount of silicon is not enough to create Si–Si bonds, but it is enough to form Si–Si₂ON due to the relatively high value of N/Si (=1.05) and/or the disappearance of the Si₂O phase (Si–Si₃O tetrahedron). In fact, the replacement of a silicon atom by nitrogen in the Si–Si₃O tetrahedron could transform it to Si–Si₂ON. Due to that substitution, the peak located at $100.6\pm 0.2$ eV and related to the Si₂O phase should disappear to become Si–Si₂ON (Fig. 1 and Table III). Therefore, the peak located at 101.2–101.4 eV is then associated to the Si–Si₂ON tetrahedron. As for the displacement of the peaks for films SiN50 and SiN51, it can be explained by the replacement of a silicon atom by nitrogen in the Si–Si₂ON tetrahedron. This substitution transforms the Si–Si₂ON tetrahedron to Si–SiON₂ [Si₆O₃N₄, $E_{B}(\text{Si }2p_{3/2})=101.8$ eV; Table I] and, therefore, the Si₆O₃N₂ bonding unit should disappear to become Si₆O₃N₄ at higher values of N/Si and low values of O/Si. The relative atomic percentage of the peak related to Si₆O₃N₄ or the Si–SiON₂ tetrahedron should increase with increasing N/O atomic ratio, as shown by our results for films SiN50 and SiN51. The peak located at 101.6–101.7 eV is then associated to the Si–SiON₂ tetrahedron. ### B. The high-resolution XPS spectra in the N 1s region

According to the results obtained from the XPS spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region, there are five structural phases in our $a$-$SiO_{x}N_{y}$ films containing nitrogen incorporated in their networks (Si–Si₂ON, Si–SiON₂, Si–N₄, Si–O₂N₂, and Si–O₃N; Table III). However, the number of component peaks obtained from the XPS spectra in the N 1s region were only three ($I_{1}$, $I_{2}$, and $I_{3}$; Fig. 3 and Table II). Therefore, in the deconvolution of the XPS spectra in the N 1s region, each component peak should correspond to an overlapping of other peaks related to several structural phases. According to Table I, the displacement with respect to the Si₃N₄ phase of the N 1s binding energy for Si–Si₂ON (Si₆O₃N₂) and Si–SiON₂ (Si₆O₃N₄) is smaller than the experimental error of ±0.2 eV. Since $E_{B}(\text{N 1}s)=397.7$ eV for Si₃N₄,²⁷ the $I_{1}$ peak localized at 397.8±0.2 eV in the N 1s region of our XPS spectra could then be the superposition of the peaks corresponding to Si₃N₄, Si₆O₃N₂, and Si₆O₃N₄. While the $I_{2}$ peak, localized at 398.6±0.2 eV in our XPS spectra, could be an overlap of peaks corresponding to Si–O₂N₂ and Si–O₃N (Si₃O₃N₂ and Si₆O₉N₂, respectively) whose binding energies are indistinguishable within an experimental error of ±0.2 eV (Table I). Notice that, according to Table I and using $E_{B}(\text{N 1}s)=397.7$ eV for the Si₃N₄ bonding unit, the binding energies corresponding to Si–O₂N₂ and Si–O₃N are 398.5 and 398.8 eV, respectively, which are similar to 398.6±0.2 eV. To corroborate these hypotheses, the normalized integrated intensities of $I_{1}$ and $I_{2}$ were calculated from the results obtained from the XPS spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region and compared to those calculated from the XPS spectra in the N 1s region. The numerical procedure to calculate the normalized integrated intensities of these peaks from the XPS spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region is explained below.

Each component peak $I_{i}(i=1,2)$ obtained from the deconvolution of the XPS spectra in the N 1s region represents the superposition of several bonding units. And, as was mentioned previously, the normalized integrated intensity $\chi_{j}$ obtained from the deconvolution procedure of the XPS spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region, corresponding to each component phase, represents the (normalized) molecule number incorporated in the film. Therefore, the normalized integrated intensity of $I_{i}$ is the normalized total number of molecules corresponding to all the bonding units related to the $I_{i}$ peak. That is to say,
$$
I_{1}=\frac{\chi_{\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{N}_{4}}+\chi_{\mathrm{Si}_{4} \mathrm{N}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{2}}+\chi_{\mathrm{Si}_{6} \mathrm{N}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \chi_{j}},
\tag{13}
$$
and
$$
I_{2}=\frac{\chi_{\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{N}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{2}}+\chi_{\mathrm{Si}_{6} \mathrm{N}_{9} \mathrm{O}_{2}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \chi_{j}}.
\tag{14}
$$

## VI. CONCLUSION

An extension of the random mixture model (ERMM) has been developed to study the bonding properties of $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films. This model considers hydrogen-free $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films as a multiphase network of 15 possible distinct Si-centered tetrahedrons, $\text{Si-Si}_{4-(v+\eta)}\text{O}_v\text{N}_\eta$ ($v+\eta\leqslant4$ and $v,\,\eta$=0,1,2,3,4), which include intrinsic defects such as Si-Si bonds ($\text{Si-Si}_4$ tetrahedron), and stable SiO$_2$ ($\text{Si-O}_4$ tetrahedron) and $\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$ ($\text{Si-N}_4$ tetrahedron) phases. Based on this ERMM, the relative atomic percentage of each tetrahedral phase incorporated in the film network is obtained from the deconvolution of the high-resolution XPS spectra in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ region. A method has also been presented to corroborate the results obtained by comparing with survey scans and also with the high-resolution XPS spectra in the N 1s region. The binding energies of the component tetrahedrons in the Si $2p_{3/2}$ spectral region are calculated under the assumption that the displacement of these energies are linear with the increase of the coordination number of oxygen or nitrogen, while the calculations for binding energies in the N 1s spectral region are based on the partial charges for the N atom in each one of those component tetrahedrons, as was already proposed by Hasegawa $et\ al.^{28}$ The ERMM is used to study the bonding properties of a series of $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ films deposited by ECRCVD on silicon substrates using a diluted mixture of $\text{SiH}_4$+$\text{N}_2$ in Ar at constant values of the $\text{SiH}_4$ flow, the total gas flow, and the total gas pressure. Our results show that not all the 15 possible tetrahedrons are simultaneously present in each film and that the relative atomic percentages of the tetrahedrons incorporated depend strongly on the growth conditions. Five tetrahedral phases ($\text{Si-Si}_4$, $\text{Si-Si}_2\text{ON}$, $\text{Si-N}_4$, $\text{Si-Si}_3\text{O}$, and $\text{Si-O}_4$) are present in ECRCVD SiO$_x$N$_y$ films grown at low $\text{N}_2$/$\text{SiH}_4$ flow ratios ($\leqslant$1.2), while only three phases are present at highest $\text{N}_2$/$\text{SiH}_4$ flow ratios ($\leqslant$1.6). The stable $\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$ phase is the most important bonding unit and it is the only phase present in all our samples. As mentioned, these results are in agreement with those obtained from survey scans and the high- resolution XPS spectra in the N 1s region, which shows the validity of ERMM proposed for $a$-SiO$_x$N$_y$ alloys and also of the analysis presented in this work.
