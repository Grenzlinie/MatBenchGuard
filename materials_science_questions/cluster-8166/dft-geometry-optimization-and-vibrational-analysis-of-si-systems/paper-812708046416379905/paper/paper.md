## Abstract
Silicon analogues of bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes are known to exhibit a bond stretch isomerism where the bridgehead Si—Si bond changes in both length and geometry. The bridgehead silicon atom of long bond (lb) isomers exhibits an unusual geometry where all the bonds are located on the same hemisphere, making the geometry of the bridgehead Si—Si bond the so-called inverted $\sigma$ bond in contrast with the typical $\sigma$ bond geometry found in the short bond (sb) isomers. Herein, the bonding nature of bridgehead Si—Si bonds was theoretically investigated in detail using valence bond theory calculation at SL-BOVB/6-31G(d) level of theory. Our calculation demonstrates that the bond type of the bridgehead bond depends on the geometry around the bridgehead atoms with those in sb and lb isomers classified as covalent (COV) and charge-shift (CS) bonds, respectively. The different bond types between two bond stretch isomer indicate that the bonding nature is switchable in one molecule. The detailed examination of the bond stretch isomerization in parent 1,3-disilabicyclo[1.1.0]butane demonstrated that the enhanced CS bond nature in the lb isomer is mainly attributed to the geometry inversion of the $\sigma$ bond rather than the bond elongation. This switchable dual bonding nature provides an important platform to compare covalent and CS bond on equal footing for both theoretical and experimental investigations.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Molecules with unusual structure or bonding situation have long been the subject of intense experimental and theoretical research. This is because understanding the underlying physical phenomena behind them could lead to new chemistry and novel applications. One of such phenomena is the bond stretch isomerism, the isomer where more than one stable configuration of the same molecule exists with only major distinct difference in the bond length. Many instances of bond stretch isomerization have been theorized or reported for organic, $^{[1,2]}$ main group elements, $^{[3-9]}$ and transition metal compounds. $^{[10-15]}$ Among such structures is the silicon analogues of bicyclo[1.1.0]butane (Scheme 1a). The predictions of two isomeric structures for 1,3-disilabicyclo[1.1.0]butanes **1** and tetrasilabicyclo[1.1.0] butanes **2** have been well documented in the literatures. $^{[17-20]}$ This phenomenon is classified as bond stretch isomerization due to the difference in bridge Si—Si bond length between the two isomers. This causes the direction of the bridge Si−Si bond to be diametrically inverted from those of the typical $\sigma$ bonds, generally regarded as inverted $\sigma$ bond. The unusual bonding parameter is especially intriguing considering the long bond isomers are predicted to be more stable than the short bond isomers in both **1** and **2**. Typically, the stability is rationalized as the result of strained energy relaxation in small bicyclic rings that should compensate the destabilization of the bridgehead bond.[²⁰,²¹] This rationalization, however, does not explain the inverted geometry of the bridgehead silicon atoms unique to long bond isomers. Planar geometry with the substituents directing away from each other would decreases the steric repulsion between the substituents on the bridgehead atoms more than the predicted inverted geometry.

In order to understand bond geometry inversion phenomenon during bond stretch isomerization in **1** and **2**, it is beneficial to revisit related compounds with inverted $\sigma$ bonds and analyze the underlying factor which stabilizes inverted geometry. One of the most studied systems with inverted $\sigma$ bond is the central C−C bond in [1.1.1] propellane **3** (Scheme 1b).[²²⁻³¹] The bonding situation between atoms with inverted geometry is rationalized as the interaction between the smaller lobes of hybridized atomic orbitals (HAOs) on bridgehead carbon atoms. Recently, the valence bond (VB) theory analysis of the **3** was performed by Wu and coworkers.[²⁹] It was determined that the interaction in the inverted C−C bond in **3** is a type of chemical bond called charge shift (CS) bond. The definition of a CS bond proposed by Shaik et al is a chemical bond which the majority of stabilization energy arises from resonance energy between covalent and ionic VB structures, the so-called CS resonance energy ($\text{RE}_\text{CS}$).[³²] Although this definition is heavily reliant on VB theory, recent developments have shown equivalency between VB and molecular orbital theory in describing systems with CS bond.[³³] Currently, a number of both homonuclear and heteronuclear chemical bonds have been identified as CS bond.[¹⁶,³⁴⁻⁴⁰]

The possible mechanism behind the bonding energy in CS bond has already been described in detail by Hiberty et al.[⁴¹] In summary, $\text{RE}_\text{CS}$ is associated with the Pauli repulsion between the bond forming HAOs and its surrounding. In the case of **3**, the major repulsion is between the HAOs and the peripheral C−C bonds (Scheme 1C).[¹⁶] This effect is called covalent bond weakening repulsion (CBWR). CBWR increases the potential energy of the bonding orbitals and disrupts the electron sharing, and hence decreases covalent bond energy. Hiberty et al examined the effects of these changes to the balance of the virial ratio of the system. They concluded that, to restore the balance of kinetic and potential energy according to virial theorem, an increase in $\text{RE}_\text{CS}$ which also have the effect of lowering kinetic energy occurs. This gives the justification for the dominance of $\text{RE}_\text{CS}$ over covalent bond energy leading to CS bonding nature of the inverted C−C bond in **3**.

Given that the inverted $\sigma$ bond of carbon has been identified as a CS bond, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the inverted $\sigma$ bond of silicon, a heavier group 14 element, is also a CS bond as well. In this sense, compounds **1** and **2** with their bond stretch isomerization provide a very intriguing model system to study the effect of bond geometry inversion in silicon. Although the bond stretch isomerization has been theoretically investigated several times, there has been no report on theoretical calculation to determine whether the bridge Si−Si bond in either isomer is covalent or CS in nature. The closely related C−C bridge bond in bicyclo[1.1.0]butane **4** was previously demonstrated to be a covalent bond.[¹⁶] While the geometry around the bridgehead carbon of bicyclo[1.1.0]butane analogues can be influenced by the substituents both on the bridgehead and the peripheral methylene carbon,[⁴²,⁴³] **4** does not exhibit bond stretch isomerization without changing any substituent like its silicon analogues. The geometry around the bridgehead carbon atoms of **4** is almost planar and does not exhibit strong geometry inversion as the long bond isomers of **1** and **2**. Herein, we report the ab initio VB theory calculations aimed at classifying the nature of interaction between bridgehead silicon atoms in **1** and **2** for both long bond and short bond isomers.

### 1.1 | Computational details

The structural optimization and all DFT calculations were performed using Gaussian 09 program suite.⁽⁴⁴⁾ The hybrid GGA functional PBE0 combined with 6-31G (d) basis set was selected as the optimization method due to the capability to produce a stable bond stretch isomer optimized structure for all compounds while reproducing structures capable of the similar results reported by previous studies. The symmetry of all molecules with bicyclo [1.1.0]butane scaffold is $C_{2V}$. Vibrational analysis was performed on all structures to ensure no imaginary vibrational frequency for all stationary structures and only one imaginary vibrational frequency for all transition state structures. All VB theory calculations were carried out using XMVB software version 2.1.⁽⁴⁵⁻⁴⁶⁾ In both **1** and **2**, only the bridge Si—Si bond is treated using breathing-orbital VB theory⁽⁴⁷⁾ calculation at SL-BOVB level while all other bond pairs are treated at GVB level. This yielded three VB structures (one covalent and two ionic) which are relevant to the description of the bridge Si—Si bond in the fashion described in Scheme 2. This level of calculation has been employed in the study of the inverted C—C bond in [1.1.1]propellane in Wu et al.⁽²⁹⁾

The major issue regarding the bond energy calculation in **1** and **2** is the definition of the nonbonded reference state which is not trivial for bicyclic systems. We followed the method used by Shaik et al and defined the non-bonded reference using the structure of VB wave function (Scheme 2). In VB theory, a chemical bond originates from interaction between two HAOs of the interacting atoms. The interaction is composed of one covalent VB structure and two ionic VB structures. The covalent structure can be decomposed further into two neutral components with opposite localized spin on each HAO. These neutral components, though not Eigenfunctions of spin operator, are Eigenfunctions of Hamiltonian operator which means that energy is defined for these components. Previously, Shaik et al have demonstrated that energy of one neutral component can be taken as non-bonding reference state called quasi-classical (QC) state. The energy difference between the QC state and the spin-paired state or the state in which only the covalent VB structure considered is regarded as covalent bond energy ($D_{COV}$). The mixing of covalent and ionic states leads to further stabilization from CS resonance energy ($RE_{CS}$). ## 2 | RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

### 2.1 | Structural parameter

The PBE0/6-31G(d) optimized structures of the two bond stretch isomers in **1** and **2** are examined (Table 1). In order to quantify the geometry around the bridgehead atoms, the angle $\alpha$, defined as the angle between Si—H bond and X—Si—X plane (Scheme 3), is measured. If the angle is negative, all the bonds are in the same hemisphere of the silicon atom and the bridge bond can be classified as inverted $\sigma$ bonds. If $\alpha$ is positive, then the geometry is normal. We consider $\alpha$ between $-15^{\circ}$ and $15^{\circ}$ as planar. The result in Table 1 showed that the bridgehead atoms of both **1-lb** and **2-lb** exhibit clear geometry inversion from typical $\sigma$ bond with large negative $\alpha$ angles [$-$64.1° (**1-lb**) and $-$ 62.8° (**2-lb**)]. The bridgehead silicon atoms in **1-sb** have typical $\sigma$ bond geometry with positive $\alpha$ angle (22.8°). The bridgehead silicon atoms of **2-sb**, on the other hand, have planar geometry with small negative $\alpha$ angle ($-$10.4°). The transition states of the bond stretch isomerization (**1-ts** and **2-ts**) were also obtained with planar bridgehead silicon atoms [$-$9.7° (**1-ts**) and $-$ 12.3° (**2-ts**)]. Our definition and results of $\alpha$ are contrasted with the hemispheroidality parameter ($\varphi$) proposed by Scheschkewitz.$^{[48]}$ Both parameters give the same classification of the geometry as the negative value of $\alpha$ is the necessary and sufficient condition for negative $\varphi$ for the bridge bond in bicyclo[1.1.0]butane scaffold. However, we adopted the use of $\alpha$ in order to quantify the geometry in the form of angle related to the difference between the two bond stretch isomers.

### 2.2 | Bonding situation

Before proceeding to analyze the bonding interaction in **1** and **2**, it is imperative to argue for the existence of a bond between the two bridgehead silicon atoms especially in the long bond isomers with inverted bond geometry. Previously, Schoeller and coworkers argued that the bridgehead atoms of **2-lb** are not directly bonded and the stabilization interaction mainly arises from orbital interaction through the peripheral atoms.$^{[17]}$ The main argument put forth in their work is the LCAO analysis and energy comparison with related species. While we acknowledge their reasoning, we would like to point out that both **1-lb** and **2-lb** are known to exhibit biradicaloid nature which is generally associated with nondynamical electron correlation effects. This put forth the issue whether analysis based upon mean field theory such as LCAO analysis or HF calculation can provide adequate qualitative representation of the bonding situation in the long bond isomers.

To answer this question, the stability of **1** and **2** singlet ground states over diradical triplet states at (U)HF/cc-pVTZ and (U)CCSD(T)/cc-pVTZ level of theories was evaluated in the form of vertical single triplet energy gap ($\Delta\text{E}_{\text{S-T}}$). In order to eliminate the effect from structural changes, the coordinate of the structures was fixed to the PBE0/6-31G(d) optimized geometry of the singlet ground state. Table 2 displays the results of $\Delta\text{E}_{\text{S-T}}$ evaluation. As shown, the HF calculation provided qualitatively good $\Delta\text{E}_{\text{S-T}}$ estimation compared with CCSD(T) for the short bond isomers but not for long bond isomers. This is

especially true for **1-lb** where $\Delta E_{\text{S-T}}$ was calculated to be only $0.7\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ at HF level in contrast with $150.2\ \text{kJ}$ $\text{mol}^{-1}$ at CCSD(T) level. For **2-lb**, the discrepancy between HF result $(103.4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1})$ and CCSD(T) result $(210.3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1})$ is not as severe; however, the HF calculation still missed more than half of the stabilization energy for the singlet state. This is a good evidence for the other major stabilization interaction not accounted for by the mean field theories, including LCAO analysis.

As the situation of regarding energetics in long bond isomers became more apparent, we return to our main issue regarding bonding situation in **1** and **2**. Unfortunately, the $\Delta E_{\text{S-T}}$ is not the most appropriate tool to answer this question as large value of $\Delta E_{\text{S-T}}$ does not directly imply an existence of a chemical bond. However, the unpaired electrons in the lowest triplet states of **1-lb** and **2-lb** are mainly localized on the bridgehead silicon atoms (Table S11). We interpreted the localization of spin densities in triplet state coupled with the magnitude of the $\Delta E_{\text{S-T}}$ as evidence for a strong interaction between the two bridgehead atoms in the long bond isomers in the singlet ground state.

Next, we proceed to analyze the interaction between the bridgehead silicon atoms of **1** and **2** using ab initio VB theory single point calculation at SL-BOVB/6-31G (d) level on the optimized structures. The weights of the covalent and ionic VB structures obtained from the calculation are displayed in Table S14. The dominant VB structure in all cases is the covalent structure. The weights of covalent structure in the **1-lb** (0.675) and **2-lb** (0.661) are slightly lower than that of **1-sb** (0.697) and **2-sb** (0.782). The dominance of covalent VB structure in the bonding situation is consistent with the result of the inverted C—C bond in **3**. The lower contribution from covalent VB structure could signal a higher importance of ionic VB structures in the long bond isomers. However, both the long bond and short bond isomers of **1** and **2** have significantly higher contribution from covalent VB structure compared with Si—Si bond in disilane (0.556). It is also important to emphasize that the classification of a chemical bond into covalent or CS bond type is made by measuring the contribution of each energy component to the bond energy rather than the weights of VB structures.

Whether this is the characteristic of Si—Si $\sigma$ bonds in strained bicyclic ring systems or not requires further study on a number of different compounds which lies beyond the scope of this work.

Using the decomposition of $D_{in\ situ}$ to $D_{COV}$ and $RE_{CS}$, the covalent and CS nature of Si—Si $\sigma$ bonds can be assessed. By the definition of Shaik et al, a bond is considered as a CS bond if the $RE_{CS}$ contribute more than 50% to $D_{in\ situ}.^{[29]}$ The values of $D_{COV}$ and $RE_{CS}$ are shown in the second and third columns of Table 2, respectively, and the percent contribution of $RE_{CS}$ to $D_{in\ situ}$ ($\%RE_{CS}$) is displayed in the fourth column. As shown, $\%RE_{CS}$ of the reference Si—Si $\sigma$ bonds in disilane is 21%, the bond classified as a covalent bond. The value is comparable to that of C—C bond in ethane (22%). The bridge Si—Si short bond isomers **1-sb** and **2-sb** are also classified as a covalent bond with $\%RE_{CS}$ of 34% and 42%, respectively. These results are similar to the bridge C—C bond in **4** which was reported to have $\%RE_{CS}$ of 35%. On the other hand, the bridge Si—Si bond in **1-lb** and **2-lb** is unambiguously a CS bond with $RE_{CS}$ contribution of 75% and 61% to $D_{in\ situ}$, respectively. The $\%RE_{CS}$ values are even larger than 57% calculated for the inverted C—C bond in **3**. More importantly when considering the bond stretch isomerization in **1** and **2**, this is the first report of system in which a chemical bond can change type from covalent to CS depending on the isomeric structure. We named this behavior as "switchable dual bonding nature." The behavior is distinct from previous reported systems where only one equilibrium structure can be located. These systems, thus, can exhibit only one bond type, either a covalent or a CS bond. Both **1** and **2** have two stable equilibrium structures. Furthermore, the Si—Si bridge bond in **1** and **2** can switch between covalent or CS bond depending on the equilibrium structure without changing substituents. Up until now, the comparison between covalent and CS bonds is usually made by comparing chemical bonds from different species. The dual bonding nature of **1** and **2** allows for the comparison of CS bond and covalent bond of the same species with slightly different structural parameter. Furthermore, stable analogues of **1-lb**$^{[49,50]}$ and **2-sb**$^{[51,52]}$ have been isolated and characterized. This revelation, thus, reveals the potential for studying reactivity and other properties of Si—Si covalent and CS bonds experimentally.

Note an important difference between the changes in each component of $D_{in\ situ}$ of **1** and **2**. In **1**, the $RE_{CS}$ clearly increases from **1-sb** (67.5 kJ mol⁻¹) to **1-lb** (115.3 kJ mol⁻¹) while $D_{COV}$ decreases. The $RE_{CS}$ in **2**, on the other hand, stays rather constant and even slightly decreases from **2-sb** (115.2 kJ mol⁻¹) to **2-lb** (103.8 kJ mol⁻¹), while $D_{COV}$ decreases in similar magnitude with **1**. To obtain a clearer perspective, the transition states of the bond stretch isomerization (**1-ts** and **2-ts**) were located for both bicyclic systems and its nature confirmed with vibrational analysis and IRC path connecting the saddle point to both bond stretch isomers. The structure of the transition state **2-ts** is very close to the short bond isomer **2-sb**. This is apparent in both structural parameters and bond energy analysis of the two structures. The energetics difference between **2-sb** and **2-ts** calculated at PBE0/6-31G(d) level is 0.03 kJ mol⁻¹. This is in line with previous studies which found a very low energy barrier for isomerization from **2-sb** to **2-lb**. $^{[53,54]}$ The transition state **1-ts**, on the other hand, displays an increase of overall $D_{in\ situ}$ with $D_{COV}$ in similar magnitude with **1-sb** and $RE_{CS}$ close to that of **1-lb**. The structure of **1-ts** is also clearly distinct from **1-sb**. This suggests a fundamental difference between the situation of **1-sb** and **2-sb**. We will return to discuss this issue later as the factors governing changing bond type in the bond stretch isomerization became more apparent.

### 2.3 | Bond stretch isomerization

In order to gain insight into the situation of the bridge Si—Si bond in **1** and **2**, we revisit the bond stretch isomerization process to analyze the process through the lens of changing bond type. The conventional rationale of bond stretch isomerization is the relaxation of strain energy from the bicyclic system from short bond isomer to long bond isomer. $^{[18,20]}$ The CS bond nature of the bridge Si—Si bond fits in to the picture as a mechanism providing the stabilization interaction between the bridgehead silicon, making the system a biradicaloid rather than a singlet biradical. However, it is not apparent how the bond transforms from a covalent bond in the short bond isomers to a CS bond in the long bond isomers during the course of isomerization process. To elucidate this issue, SL-BOVB single point calculations along the IRC path of the bond stretch isomerization were performed in order to observe the mechanism of changing bond type during the process. As the shallow potential well for **2-sb** structure complicates the analysis (Figure S6), we focus

our effort on the bond stretch isomerization of $\boldsymbol{1}$ which exhibit clear distinction between each key structure of the bond stretch isomerization process.

The result of VB theory single point calculations on the structure along the bond stretch isomerization process IRC path of $\boldsymbol{1}$ are displayed in Figure 1. The first important information is the change in $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ of the bridge Si—Si bond during the isomerization process. Surprisingly, the value of $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ at the transition state is actually greater than that at the equilibrium structure of either short bond or long bond isomer. This suggests that bond stretch isomerization is neither the process where the bridge Si—Si bond cleaved and reformed nor the gradually weakening of the bridge bond as the $\sigma$ bond elongates. It is more accurate to characterize the bond stretch isomerization in $\boldsymbol{1}$ as the process where bond transforms between covalent and CS bond type.

![](./images/812708046416379905_6.jpg)

FIGURE 1 A, Energetics from valence bond theory single point calculations and B, $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ of the bridge Si—Si bond along with its decomposition to $\mathrm{D_{COV}}$ and $\mathrm{RE_{CS}}$ along the IRC path of bond stretch isomerization from $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb to $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb

Decomposing $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ into $\mathrm{D_{COV}}$ and $\mathrm{RE_{CS}}$ revealed that the bridge Si—Si bond at the transition state is a covalent bond. This shows that the transition state of bond stretch isomerization is not the crossing point where the bond type changes. The crossing point is located slightly towards the long bond isomer along the IRC path. This leads to the question of the major structural governing factor determining whether a bond is a covalent or CS. A previous study on C—C bonds shows the CBWR effect as the major driving force in the increase in $\mathrm{RE_{CS}}$ of the central bond in $\boldsymbol{3.^{[16]}}$ On the other hand, the study on C—N bond dissociation in ammonium compounds demonstrated that bond length can also influence the composition of bond energy. $^{[35]}$

To understand the effects of structural changes on the bond energy, PES scan along the changing bridge Si—Si bond distance ($r_{\text{Si—Si}}$) for both $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb and $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb was performed, and $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ of the bond along with its components was evaluated (Figure 2). The inversion of the bond geometry did not occur along the scan, thus allowed for the study of the bond length changes without the consideration of the change in geometry around the bridgehead atoms. Surprisingly, the behavior of $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ in $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb and $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb during the scan are different. In $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb, $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ decreases drastically as $r_{\text{Si—Si}}$ increases, rapidly approaching bond dissociation (Figure 2B). This differs from $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb where $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ slowly decreases over the change in $r_{\text{Si—Si}}$ and did not approach dissociation even after separate for more than $2.7$ Å (Figure 2A).

Decomposition of $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ into its components revealed that the different behavior between $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb and $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb can be ascribed to the different behavior of the major component of bond energy as $r_{\text{Si—Si}}$ increases. The value of $\mathrm{D_{COV}}$ in $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb decreases rapidly while $\mathrm{RE_{CS}}$ in $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb gradually decreases. The change in percent contribution of each component to $\mathrm{D_{in\ situ}}$ also differs between the two isomers. For $\boldsymbol{1}$-sb, the $\% \mathrm{RE_{CS}}$ of the bond clearly changes as the bond length increases, approaching 50% as the bond distance approach dissociation limit and all interaction energy tend to zero. The behavior is completely different from $\boldsymbol{1}$-lb which $\% \mathrm{RE_{CS}}$ remain generally unchanged over the PES scan. Most importantly, in both isomers, there is no sign of changing bond type between covalent and CS except at the dissociation limit. This shows that the change in bond type observed during bond stretch isomerization is not due to the change in bond length.

![](./images/812708046416379905_7.jpg)

FIGURE 2 $D_{\text{in situ}}$ of the bridge Si—Si bond along with its decomposition to $D_{\text{COV}}$ and $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ along the PES scan of A, $r_{\text{Si-Si}}$ in **1-lb**, B, $r_{\text{Si-Si}}$ in **1-sb**, and C, $\alpha$ in **1** with fixed $r_{\text{Si-Si}}$ at 2.395 Å

Next, the effect of geometry inversion to the bridge Si—Si bond in **1** is explored by varying the angle $\alpha$ while keeping the $C_{2V}$ symmetry. Figure 2C displays $D_{\text{in situ}}$ and its decomposition to $D_{\text{COV}}$ and $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$. In order to eliminate the effect of changing bond length, the bridge Si—Si bond lengths were fixed to 2.395 Å, the average $r_{\text{Si-Si}}$ between short bond and long bond isomer. The scan revealed a different behavior of $D_{\text{COV}}$ and $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ as geometry around the bridgehead silicon atoms changes. The value of $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ increases almost uniformly as the geometry became inverted. The value of $D_{\text{COV}}$, on the other hand, has a maximum value around slightly negative $\alpha$. This can be rationalized as the difference in the mechanism of stabilization behind $D_{\text{COV}}$ and $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$. Using qualitative VB analysis with localized orbitals, the value of $D_{\text{COV}}$ is related to the overlap between the bond forming HAOs (S) and the reduced resonance integral ($\beta$) via the equation.$^{[55]}$

$$
D_{\text{COV}}=2\beta\text{S}/(1+\text{S}^{2}). \tag{1}
$$

Thus, the change in geometry which affects the orientation of the bond forming HAOs and the overlap between the two orbitals will impact $D_{\text{COV}}$ greatly. The maximum of $D_{\text{COV}}$ on the scan correlates well with the region where there is a good overlap between the two HAOs. On the other hand, $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ is expected to rise to compensate the increase in repulsion from CBWR effect. The inverted geometry around the bridgehead atom increases the repulsion between Si—H bonding orbitals and the HAOs forming the bridge Si—Si bond. Thus, the uniform increase in $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ as the geometry around bridgehead Si agrees well with the rationalization of CBWR. The behavior of $D_{\text{in situ}}$ in this case can be explained by the competing contribution from $D_{\text{COV}}$ and $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$. The local maximum in $D_{\text{in situ}}$ around $\alpha=-10^{\circ}$ is due to the increase in $D_{\text{COV}}$ in the region. On the hand, the overall increase in $D_{\text{in situ}}$ as the $\alpha$ decreases can be directly ascribed to the behavior of $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$. Notice that during the bond geometry inversion, the change in bond type occurs even with fixed $r_{\text{Si-Si}}$. This demonstrated that the bond geometry inversion is the major driving force over change in bond type. The effect of geometry over each bond energy components also ties into the different nature of **1-sb** and **2-sb** discussed vide supra. As shown in Table 1, the angle $\alpha$ in **2-sb** is slightly negative $(-10.4^{\circ})$ rather than positive in the case of **1-sb** $(22.8^{\circ})$. Thus, the relatively high value of $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ in **2-sb** compared with **1-sb** can be directly attributed to the adoption of planar geometry of the bridgehead silicon atoms in **2-sb** rather than normal geometry in **1-sb**. In the case of **2** where the value of $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ is high in both the short bond isomer and the long bond isomer, the main driving force of the bond stretch isomerization is not the $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$. However, the $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ clearly influences the geometry of each isomer strongly and should be taken into consideration when rationalizing the structure of other related compounds. With the understanding regarding different behavior of $D_{\text{COV}}$ and $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$, we can now answer our opening question regarding the physical phenomenon behind the bond inversion of **1-lb** and **2-lb**. The reason behind the inverted geometry around the bridgehead atoms in these molecules is to maximize the $\text{RE}_{\text{CS}}$ which stabilize the molecule. This shows that the CS bonding paradigm should be taken into consideration when discussing the interactions in nonclassical structures in silicon containing compounds.

## 3 | CONCLUSIONS

We have demonstrated that the Si—Si bridge bonds in silicon analogues of bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes **1** and **2** can exhibit either covalent or CS bonding nature depending on the structural parameter around the bridgehead silicon. To our knowledge, this is the first explicit confirmation on the CS bonding in Si—Si bond using VB theory

calculation. Up until now, the known silicon containing bond with CS nature determined by VB theory was $H_3Si-Cl.^{[34,39]}$ The CS bonding in Si-Si silabicyclo[1.1.0] butane systems is potentially important in rationalization of silicon compounds with nonclassical bonding structures. Furthermore, the changing bond type phenomenon, dual bonding nature, is unique to these systems due to the bond stretch isomerism involved. Compound with convertible bonding nature between covalent and CS is unprecedented. We believe that the systems of molecules reported in this work will be very important for clarification of the differences between covalent and CS bonds due to very close relationship between the bond stretch isomers. Furthermore, the short bond isomer of tetrasilabicyclo[1.1.0]butane $^{[51,52]}$ and long bond isomer of 1,3-disilabicyclo[1.1.0]butane $^{[49,50]}$ have been reported in literatures. This would open new horizon for experimental chemists to examine the two bond types beyond the capability of theoretical investigation.
