# THEORETICAL STUDY OF A SILYLENE-OXYGEN ADDUCT.
## IS A SILANONE OXIDE STRUCTURE ($\text{H}_2\text{SiOO}$)
## KINETICALLY STABLE IN THE SINGLET STATE?

Shigeru NAGASE, Takako KUDO
Department of Chemistry, Faculty of Education, Yokohama National University, Yokohama 240, Japan

Takeshi AKASAKA and Wataru ANDO
Department of Chemistry, University of Tsukuba, Tsukuba 305, Japan

Received 10 July 1989; in final form 7 August 1989

In an attempt to reinforce our recent experiments on silylene-oxygen adducts, the structure and kinetic stability of silanone oxide ($\text{H}_2\text{SiOO}$) in the singlet state have been investigated at several levels of theory. It is found that silanone oxide has a non-planar, pyramidal structure and undergoes rapid cyclization to siladioxirane, unlike the corresponding carbon compound ($\text{H}_2\text{COO}$).

## 1. Introduction

Silylenes ($\text{R}_2\text{Si}:$), divalent silicon species, play an important role as reactive intermediates in a variety of reactions of organosilicon compounds, as do carbenes ($\text{R}_2\text{C}:$) in organic reactions [1]. Thus, the reactivity of silylenes has been extensively investigated for addition and insertion reactions [1]. In recent years there has been a great deal of interest in the oxidation of silylenes. Several authors [2] have measured the decay rate of silylenes in the presence of oxygen by monitoring the disappearance of the characteristic spectrum of silylenes. These decay rate measurements indicate that silylenes react with oxygen. Although no direct evidence is yet available, it has often been postulated that the reaction of silylenes with oxygen probably proceeds through an initial silylene-oxygen adduct with a subsequent complex chemistry [2].

We have recently carried out the low-temperature matrix isolation and FT-IR spectroscopic characterization of an initial adduct formed in the oxidation of dimesitylsilylene confirming that dimesitylsilylene is rapidly trapped with oxygen [3]. From isotopic labeling experiments and ab initio calculations, the adduct was found to be in the triplet state with a silanone oxide ($\text{R}_2\text{SiOO}$) structure. This is in interesting contrast with the corresponding carbon compounds, carbonyl oxides ($\text{R}_2\text{COO}$), which can be generated in the singlet state by the reaction of carbenes with oxygen or the oxidation of diazoalkanes and can be observed directly by laser flash photolysis in solution and by IR spectroscopy in matrices (see a number of references in refs. [3,4]). In addition, singlet carbonyl oxides are important intermediates in the ozonolysis of olefins [5].

To resolve the discrepancy between the carbon and silicon compounds, we have undertaken ab initio calculations on the singlet parent compound ($\text{H}_2\text{SiOO}$) and its transformation to a cyclic isomer at several levels of theory. Our primary aim is to characterize the singlet potential energy surface and contrast it with that of $\text{H}_2\text{COO}$.

## 2. Computational details

The geometries of stationary points on the singlet potential energy surface of $\text{H}_2\text{SiOO}$ were fully optimized with the split-valence d-polarized 6-31G(d) basis set [6] using analytic energy gradients. Since singlet biradical character cannot be treated satisfactorily by single-determinant Hartree-Fock (HF) theory, second-order Møller-Plesset perturbation

(MP2) theory [7] was employed in the geometry optimization with all electrons correlated. In order to improve the energies, single-point calculations were carried out with the 6-31G(d) and larger 6-31G(2d, p) [8] basis sets using MP$n$ ($n$=2-4) perturbation theory up to full fourth order (MP4SDTQ) [9] with frozen core. We also carried out single-point configuration interaction (CI) calculations including all single and double excitations from the HF single determinant with size-consistent corrections due to unlinked clusters made using Pople's method [10].

Geometries were also optimized using the generalized valence bond (GVB) method [11] with the 6-31G(d) basis set and analytic energy gradients. The perfect pairing restriction was applied and three pairs, the Si-O and O-O $\sigma$ bonds and the $\pi$ pair, were allowed to correlate in order to describe the transformation of the biradical $\ce{H_{2}SiOO}$ at a consistent level. Energies were also calculated with the larger 6-31G(2d, p) basis set. In order to relax the perfect pairing restriction and to include interpair electron correlation neglected in the GVB method, complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) calculations [12] were carried out on the GVB/6-31G(d) optimized geometries using the GVB natural orbitals as the initial guess with the six electrons distributed in the six active orbitals. In an attempt to characterize the degrees of bonding and singlet biradical character, the bond index between atoms and the effective number of spins localized on atoms [13] were calculated using the CASSCF wavefunctions: the bond index gives a value close to 1 for typical single bonds [13].

Optimized geometries were identified as equilibrium or transition structures by checking the number of negative eigenvalues of the MP2/6-31G(d) and GVB/6-31G(d) Hessian (force constant) matrices obtained from numerical differentiation of the analytic energy gradients. We also calculated harmonic vibrational frequencies and infrared intensities. Throughout this paper, the symbol "//" means "at the geometry of".

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Silanone oxide

Since $\ce{H_{2}COO}$ is known to have a planar structure of $C_\text{s}$ symmetry in its singlet ground state [14], we first optimized the geometry of $\ce{H_{2}SiOO}$ with a $C_\text{s}$ symmetry constraint. The optimized geometries at the MP2/6-31G(d) and GVB/6-31G(d) levels are shown in fig. 1.

The GVB wavefunction reveals that silanone oxide ($\ce{H_{2}SiO^{a}O^{b}}$) has considerable singlet biradical character, as indicated by the small overlap ($S$=0.201) between the $\pi$ orbitals localized mainly on the Si and terminal $\ce{O^{b}}$ atoms, while the overlap of each orbital forming the $\ce{Si-O^{a}}$ ($S$=0.826) and $\ce{O^{a}-O^{b}}$ ($S$=0.735) $\sigma$ bonds are normal. The effective spin localized on the Si and terminal $\ce{O^{b}}$ atoms were calculated to be 0.749 and 0.769, respectively, using the CASSCF/6-31G(d) wavefunction. In other words, the $\ce{-SiH_{2}}$ moiety in $\ce{H_{2}SiOO}$ has a strong silyl radical character. For comparison, we have also optimized the geometry of carbonyl oxide ($\ce{H_{2}CO^{a}O^{b}}$) at the GVB/6-31G(d) level and calculated the corresponding orbital overlaps of $S(\ce{C-O^{b}})$=0.420, $S(\ce{C-O^{a}})$=0.842, and $S(\ce{O^{a}-O^{b}})$=0.767 and the effective localized spin on the C (0.407) and terminal $\ce{O^{b}}$ (0.409) atoms. These suggest that $\ce{H_{2}SiOO}$ has a more marked singlet biradical character than $\ce{H_{2}COO}$.

Furthermore, it was found that the planar structure of $\ce{H_{2}SiOO}$ has one single imaginary frequency and hence is not a minimum on the potential energy surface at both MP2/6-31G(d) and GVB/6-31G(d) levels, unlike $\ce{H_{2}COO}$. Reoptimization removing the $C_\text{s}$ symmetry constraint produced a strong pyramidalization at the silicon atom and a nonplanar structure with $C_1$ symmetry was obtained, as shown in figs. 2a and 3a. The energy differences favoring the nonplanar structure are insensitive to the basis set used. However, the differences are calculated to be considerably smaller at the MP and CI levels. In order to clarify whether this is due to geometrical differences, we performed single-point MPn/6-31G(d) calculations on the GVB optimized planar and nonplanar geometries. However, the calculated energy differences were again smaller: 3.4 (n=2), 0.8 (n=3), and 7.9 (n=4) kcal/mol at the MPn/6-31G(d)//GVB/6-31G(d) level. This suggests that the MP and CI methods based on single-determinant HF theory tend to overestimated the stability of the planar structure. Such a trend may not be surprising since single-point HF/6-31G(d) calculations on the MP2 and GVB optimized geometries make the planar structure more stable than the nonplanar structure by 14.3 and 14.5 kcal/mol, respectively. It appears that restricted HF orbitals overestimated the stabilization due to π electron delocalization in the planar form. In addition, it should be mentioned that no significant minimum corresponding to a silanone oxide structure was located even in the planar form at the HF/6-31G(d) level without electron correlation.

The O-O bond length in siladioxirane is $1.628 \AA$ at the MP2/6-31G(d) level and $1.810 \AA$ at the GVB/6-31G(d) level. These values are considerably larger than the microwave spectroscopic (1.516 $\AA$ [18]) and calculated (1.521-1.550 $\AA$ [14,17]) values in the corresponding cyclic carbon compound, dioxirane, probably because of the higher strain in three- membered silicon rings [19] and longer SiO bonds. In siladioxirane the GVB orbital overlaps are $S(O-O)=0.458$ and $S(Si-O)=0.832$ , while the bond indices $(B)$ calculated at the CASSCF level are $B(O-O)=0.552$ and $B(Si-O)=0.875$ . These suggest that the O-O bond in siladioxirane is considerably weakened due to the singlet biradical character (the number of spins on each O is 0.405), though the remaining two $Si-O$ bonds are normal. This also contrasts with the stronger O-O bond in dioxirane.

### 3.3. Transition state for the isomerization
We are now in a position to clarify whether the cyclization of silanone oxide to the more stable siladioxirane is hindered by a significant barrier. According to recent calculations at the MP and CASSCF levels, there is a barrier of $\approx 23 kcal / mol$ for the corresponding reaction of carbonyl oxide in the singlet state [16,17].

As figs. 2b and 3b show, we have located the transition state for the isomerization of silanone oxide to siladioxirane at the MP2/6-31G(d) and GVB/6-31G(d) levels. At both levels the located transition structures resemble silanone oxide more closely than they do siladioxirane, reflecting the fact that the isomerization is highly exothermic. On going from silanone oxide $(H_{2} SiO^{a} O^{b})$ to the transition state, the CASSCF bond index of $B(Si-O^{b})$ increases from0.087 to 0.264, indicating the creation of significant bonding between the $Si$ and terminal $O^{b}$ atoms, while $B(Si-O^{a})$ changes slightly from 0.761 to 0.753 and B(Oa-Ob) decreases from 0.947 to 0.847.

As expected from the "early" transition state, the barrier height for the isomerization of silanone oxide was calculated to be only 6.5,7.0 and $5.0 kcal / mol$ , respectively, at the $MPn(n=2,3$ , and 4 $) / 6$ 31G(d)//MP2/6-31G(d) level and 5.4-5.8 kcal/ mol at the CI/6-31G(d)//MP2/6-31G(d) level, asshown in table 2. At the $MPn / 6-31(2 ~d, p) / / MP 2 /$ 6-31G(d) level it is lowered to 5.6,6.6, and $4.0 kcal /$  mol, respectively, for $n=2,3$ , and 4. As table 3 shows,the barrier is further decreased to 2.2 and $2.5 kcal /$ mol at the GVB/6-31(d) and GVB/6-31(2d, p)/GVB/6-31G(d) levels, respectively, while theCASSCF/6-31G(d)//GVB/6-31G(d) value is 2.4 kcal/mol. These calculated values of 2-6 kcal/mol are sufficiently small for the isomerization to proceed rapidly even at low temperatures.

In theoretical studies [17,20] of the isomerization of carbonyl oxide, it is emphasized that the reaction is not initiated by simply closing the COO angle but by pyramidalization at the $C$ atom. In this respect, it is interesting to recall that silanone oxide already has a pyramidalized structure in the singlet state.

In summary, singlet silanone oxides are kinetically unstable towards isomerization to siladioxiranes, unlike singlet carbonyl oxides. As the high exothermicity of the isomerization suggests, the resultant siladioxiranes are "hot" with considerable excess energy and are subject to complex decomposition(probably initiated by cleavage of their weak O-O bonds) if they are not effectively deactivated by collision. This may explain one of reasons why we were unable to detect stable singlet siladioxiranes in our recent experiment [3], though singlet silanone oxides can be generated from intersystem crossing oftriplet silanone oxides.
