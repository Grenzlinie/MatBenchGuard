# A theoretical study of the formation of the $\boldsymbol{\beta}$-SiC (001)/Si (001) heterojunction

B I Craig

Surveillance Research Laboratory, Defence Science and Technology Organisation,
PO Box 1500, Salisbury, South Australia 5108, Australia

Received 17 July 1992, accepted for publication 4 September 1992

Abstract. In this paper a theoretical study of the formation of a heterojunction between a Si (001) substrate and a very thin film of the more atomically dense $\beta$-SiC (001) is presented. The structures that describe the deposition of the first layer of carbon and then the subsequent deposition of two further layers, one of silicon and the other carbon, to form the polar SiC film are discussed. In each case the atomic structure is determined from minimizing the total energy with respect to variations in the atomic coordinates. The formation of the interface between the polar SiC film and the silicon substrate is found to give rise to a distinct dipole within the top two layers of the silicon substrate. Valence electron charge is transferred from the first silicon layer to the second silicon layer.

## 1. Introduction

The growth of semiconductor structures from group-IV materials via chemical vapour phase deposition (CVD) is gaining widespread interest. Materials derived from silane and hydrocarbons such as the growth of $\beta$-SiC on silicon substrates [1-10] have numerous applications. One important example is the heterojunction bipolar transistor [1]. Other materials derived from silane and germane, such as SiGe alloys and superlattices [11], also have potential applications. However, due to the lattice mismatch between the substrate and the deposited material, many of these structures contain significant strain-induced features at the interface. It is therefore useful to examine the types of structure that may arise from the mismatch between these lattice structures and also the important corresponding electronic structure. These features include the modified bonding geometries at the interface and the distortions within the adjoining layers [12].

In this paper a theoretical study of the formation of the heterojunction between a Si (001) substrate and the more atomically dense $\beta$-SiC (001) film is presented. The structures arising from firstly the deposition of the first layer of carbon and secondly the subsequent deposition of further layers to form a very thin SiC film are examined. The important induced dipole in the valence electron charge distribution at the interface between Si and the deposited $\beta$-SiC film is also described. The step in the electrostatic potential that is derived from this dipole aligns the two valence bandstructures of Si and SiC.

The lattice constant of the zinc-blende $\beta$-SiC structure is approximately $80\%$ of the Si lattice constant. This large mismatch between these two structures gives rise to defect structures within the layers of SiC immediately above the silicon surface. Distortions within the silicon substrate may also occur immediately below the interface. The formation of the polar electronic structure of $\beta$-SiC on top of the silicon material is also interesting. Calculations of the bulk electronic structure of $\beta$-SiC $[13,14]$ determine the carbon atom to be negatively charged and the silicon atom to be positively charged. Those silicon atoms close to the interface might also be polarized by the $\beta$-SiC epilayer.

In the initial stage of the growth of the SiC film the first layer of carbon is formed from the hydrocarbons adsorbed on top of the Si (001) substrate. The experimental study of the initial deposition of $\mathrm{C}_{2}\mathrm{H}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{2}\mathrm{H}_{4}$ has described the bridging of silicon surface atoms by carbon pairs [8-10]. Such structures have been examined in recent theoretical work [15]. The subsequent C-C bond scission of some of the carbon pairs, leading to the formation of a thin SiC layer, has been observed $[10,16]$. The silicon atoms are thus bridged either by single carbon atoms in the manner expected for the zinc-blende structure or sometimes by the important remaining carbon pairs. The ability to bridge two silicon atoms by a carbon pair, rather than always by a single carbon atom, allows the deposited carbon layer to contain more atoms than the underlying silicon surface layer. A mixture of $25\%$ carbon pairs and $75\%$ carbon singles yields an adsorbed carbon layer with an average interatomic distance, between carbon atoms in the bridging direction, very close to the bulk $\beta$-SiC value.

In the present theoretical treatment we therefore model the formation of the more atomically dense SiC material on top of the Si (001) substrate by a $(4\times1)$ unit cell. The initial carbon layer, formed from the CVD

process of hydrocarbons on top of the Si (001) surface, is estimated to contain five carbon atoms in the carbon bridging $(1,-1,0)$ direction for every four surface silicon atoms. These carbon atoms, contained within $CH_{2}$ groups, are attached to the dangling bonds of the silicon surface. The initial estimate for the structure, illustrated in figure 1, is assumed to bridge every fourth pair of silicon atoms with a carbon pair whilst the remaining silicon atom pairs are bridged by a single carbon atom. The atomic positions are then varied until a minimum in the total energy is obtained. This optimized structure represents the type of atomic arrangement that may be formed during the deposition of a layer of carbon from the chemisorption of hydrocarbons.

This model $(4 \times 1)$ structure, where every fourth pair of Si atoms is bridged by the carbon pair, is an ideal representation of the initial carbon layer. The carbon pairs can be expected to be more randomly distributed across the surface. Furthermore, as the CVD process proceeds at relatively high temperatures, some defect structures such as those within amorphous solids may be formed. One aim of the recent experimental work [4] is to determine CVD methods at lower temperatures that yield improved SiC film stoichiometry. The current model provides a basic useful example of the formation of the more atomically dense and ordered carbon layer on top of the silicon surface.

The deposition of subsequent layers of silicon and carbon respectively, form the SiC structure. The eventual bridging of the carbon atoms, in the $(1,1,0)$ direction, by similar silicon singles and pairs is needed to decrease the average interatomic spacing in this direction from the Si (001) value of $3.84 \AA$ to the smaller SiC (001) value of $3.09 \AA$. Experiments with scanning tunnelling microscopy (STM) show that an increase in the thickness of SiC films grown on Si (100) leads to a decrease in the atomic spacing, from $3.3 \AA$ for an $80 \AA$ thickness to the bulk spacing for a $1000 \AA$ thickness [7]. A $(1 \times 1)$ structure, typical of a coverage of $CH_{2}$ groups, is observed in the STM image.

In this paper we discuss the results obtained from theoretical calculations of the more atomically dense structures in the $(1,-1,0)$ direction. The formation of the initial carbon terminated layer is treated first. A second structure where the addition of a silicon layer and another carbon layer, from silane and hydrocarbons, to form a very thin SiC film is then discussed. The theoretical method is described in section 2 and the results and discussion are presented in sections 3 and 4. The overall conclusions are given in section 5.

## 2. Theoretical method

Our method is the SLAB-MINDO molecular orbital technique used previously to study the $(2 \times 1)$ [17] and $C(2 \times 2)$ [18] phases of $\beta$-SiC (001) and the $(1 \times 1)$ dihydride structures of some group-IV surfaces [19]. The method calculates the energetically preferred structure, which is periodic across the surface, by minimizing the total energy with respect to the atomic coordinates of those atoms describing the surface layer, the underlying layers and the chemisorbed species. In the current problem we use a $(4 \times 1)$ unit. A self-consistent iterative procedure is employed to obtain the convergent solution of the modified Fock equations for both the total energy and the electronic structure.

The total energy is given by the expression

$$
U=\frac{1}{2 N} \sum_{k}^{N} \sum_{p q} P_{p q}(k)\left(H_{p q}(k)+F_{p q}(k)\right)+\sum_{A>B} C\left(R_{A}, R_{B}\right)
\tag{1}
$$

where $P$, $H$ and $F$ are the respective valence electron density, core Hamiltonian and Fock matrices. These matrices are functions of the wavevectors $k$ belonging to the Brillouin zone. The second term in equation (1) describes the core-core repulsion between sites $A$ and $B$. The valence electron charge density $n_{A}$ on each site $A$ is obtained by summing those diagonal density matrix elements

$$
n_{A}+\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k}^{N} \sum_{p}^{A} P_{p p}(k)
\tag{2}
$$

belonging to orbitals $p$ sited at $A$. Calculations of the bulk electronic properties of $\beta$-SiC determine $n_{A}$ to be 4.44e for carbon and 3.56e for silicon [14]. The value of $n_{A}$ for bulk silicon is of course equal to 4e. The local density of states of an atom or a layer of atoms can be obtained from the one-electron eigenvalue spectrum derived from the MINDO method. The calculations of the bulk valence electron band structure of $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\beta$-SiC by the periodic MINDO method [14] are in broad agreement with experimental measurements.

A film of six Si (001) layers is used to model the silicon substrate. Hydrogen atoms are adsorbed onto the lower surface to form the $(1\times1)$ dihydride structure. The SiC film is grown on the top surface. The first structure, illustrated in figure 1, contains five carbon atoms on top of the four silicon surface layers of the $(4\times1)$ unit cell. Each carbon atom has two hydrogen atoms attached. The occurrence of such hydrogen can be expected during the CVD process. The two hydrogen atoms form a $\text{CH}_2$ structure which, as illustrated in figure 1, may be tilted by the tilt angle $\phi(\text{HCH})$ from the vertical plane. Two silicon atoms (labelled $1'$) are bridged in the $(1,-1,0)$ direction by a carbon pair (labelled $\text{C}_3$) and the remaining bridge sites in this $(4\times1)$ cell are occupied by a single carbon atom. The positions of the first four silicon layers and the carbon atoms along with the attached hydrogen are allowed to vary until a minimum in the total energy is obtained.

The second structure contains the further addition of another silicon layer and another carbon layer on top. The top carbon layer is again hydrogen terminated. The deposited silicon layer is sandwiched between the two carbon layers. Each of these three layers contains five atoms per unit cell. The atomic coordinates of these layers, the attached hydrogen and the top two Si (001) substrate layers are allowed to vary until the minimum in the total energy is obtained. The underlying silicon atoms (third layer and below) are kept in the positions determined for the first structure. The structure of this very thin SiC film on top of the Si (001) substrate is illustrated in figure 2. The Si-C-Si structure, like the $\text{CH}_2$ structure, may be tilted by an angle $\phi(\text{Si-C-Si})$ from the vertical plane.

In each of the two structures the top five layers of group-IV material have been allowed to find their minimum energy positions within an assumed $(4\times1)$ unit cell. Each calculation converges to one type of defect structure within the $(4\times1)$ cell. Other structures, such as the dislocation observed in GaAs/Si (4% mismatch) by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HREM) [12], may also occur in some number. The experimental growth at temperatures close to $1000\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ may well produce a variety of defect structures. Further HREM experiments will aid in the description of the structure formed at the interface. The approximations employed in this work have been made to enable a basic structural determination of the important Si (001)-SiC (001) interface to be undertaken.

The $C_{3}-C_{3}$ bond has increased in size from $1.534 \AA$ for a single carbon layer coverage to $1.614 \AA$ for the three-layer coverage. This increase in bond length accommodates some of the distortions within the deposited SiC layer. These distortions are illustrated by the increase in the values of the tilt angles for the $Si-C-Si$ structure, as compared with the $CH_{2}$ group, that have followed from the addition of the top carbon layer. As an example, the addition of the atom labelled $C_{6}$ between the two $Si_{3}$ atoms leads to an increase of $10^{\circ}$ in the tilt angle $\phi_{3}$ as compared with the first structure (figure 1).

The displacements of the silicon atoms within the Si (100) substrate, from the ideal bulk positions, for the second structure are displayed in table 2 along with those for the first structure. The directions of these displacements in the $(0,0,1)$ and $(1,-1,0)$ directions are shown in figure 2. The values of these displacements are generally similar for the two structures. However, a further significant increase in the displacement of the atoms labelled $1'$ is observed following the deposition of three layers as compared with that first obtained for the single carbon layer. The increased movement of these atoms is again due to accommodating the additional strain in the lattice that is produced by the additional SiC layers.

The valence electron populations for the second structure are displayed in table 2 and can be compared with those for the first structure. The addition of the silicon layer and then the top carbon layer clearly gives rise to a substantially more polarized valence electron structure. The top carbon layer is negatively charged with an average electron population of 4.2e. The electron population of the initial (lower) carbon layer has increased to a similar value, while the silicon layer sandwiched between the two carbon layers has lost substantial electron charge. The atoms within the top layer of the Si (001) substrate (labelled 1 and $1'$ in figure 2) are similarly depleted of electrons, essentially given to atoms within the second layer (labelled 2 and $2'$), and thereby forming a noticeable dipole. The magnitude of this dipole is nearly seven times greater than the dipole obtained for the first structure.

There has been no significant movement of electron charge either to or from the substrate. The total valence electron population (per unit cell) for the three deposited layers and the attached hydrogen) is 69.98e which is only 0.02e less than expected neutral value.

These results point to a qualitative picture of the interface where the first two layers of the initial silicon substrate play an important role. The dipole distribution of the valence electronic charge within these two layers requires further investigation. The step in the electrostatic potential derived from this dipole aligns the valence band structures of the deposited SiC film with the underlying Si substrate. We therefore examine the density of states of the various layers to gain further insight into the electronic properties of the interface. It must be remembered that the deposited SiC film is very thin and has only gained the bulk $\beta$-SiC interatomic spacing in the $(1,-1,0)$ direction.

In figure 3 the density of states (DOS) for the SiC-Si interface is displayed. This DOS has been obtained from the set of 16 special wavevectors of the first Brillouin zone, for which the total energy expression has been evaluated by equation (1). The DOS for the three parts of the structure (including the attached hydrogen), namely the deposited SiC film, the top two Si layers of the Si (001) substrate and the remaining four Si (001) layers of the substrate are each displayed. The largest peaks, occurring approximately 3 eV below the valence band maximum, are associated with the $C-H$ and $Si-H$ bonds at the respective top and bottom surfaces of the nine-layer slab.

The Si substrate DOS extends for the expected 13 eV width of the bulk Si valence band structure. On the other hand the DOS for the deposited SiC film extends by the larger 18.5 eV width, which is smaller than the 22 eV valence band width calculated by the periodic MINDO method for $\beta$-SiC [14]. The smaller value of the DOS for the deposited SiC film results from small film thickness and the larger bond distortions within the SiC film than in bulk $\beta$-SiC.

The DOS of the top two Si substrate layers extends for the 18.5 eV width and has the features of both the deposited SiC film and the underlying Si substrate. The electrostatic potential energy derived from the dipole within these two layers has aligned the DOS of the deposited SiC film to be closer to that of the Si substrate. Although the potential energy step induced by such a dipole is large and of the order of several eV, the valence band structure maxima (VBM) for the two materials are close together.

The SiC VBM is nearly 0.5 eV above the Si VBM. The

experimental measurement of this band offset determines the SiC VBM to be 0.5 eV below the Si VBM [1]. However, the very thin SiC film considered in the current examination is too thin and distorted in the (1,1,0) direction to be considered as a good example for comparison with this experiment. An increased number of layers, along with the smaller $\beta$-SiC spacing in the (1,1,0) direction, will give rise to an increase in the number of atoms in each layer. This larger number of atoms will increase the overlap between atomic potential wells and thereby lower the potential energy within the SiC epilayer towards the bulk value. A more suitable alignment of the two VBM may therefore result. The modelling of this considerably larger system is too difficult at this stage.

However, the current results have clearly identified the important dipole within the top two layers of the Si substrate and the role played in the alignment of the bandstructures of the two materials. In addition, no transfer of valence electron charge either to or from the substrate has occurred. This result is also important as some models for the alignment of the VBM consider such charge transfer as the origin of the electrostatic dipole. These results will be discussed in more detail in the next section.

## 4. Discussion

The work presented in this paper considers the structures that minimize the total energy. An ideal $(4 \times 1)$ model for the interface has been assumed in these calculations. The approximations within this work are made in order that examination of the structural and electronic properties of the Si/SiC interface can be undertaken. The resulting geometries show significant distortions of the local coordination of near neighbours from the ideal tetrahedral geometry for those atoms located at the interface. All the atoms near the interface are still bonded to four near neighbours. However, the experimental growth of the SiC layer on top of the Si (001) substrate occurs at $700-1100\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ [1,4,5,20]. At these temperatures some of the more strained bonds may break or may have failed to originally form during the CVD process. The resulting dangling bonds allow sites for potential hydrogen attachment [21] or the formation of locally amorphous-like structures.

The geometrical structure of those atoms at and close to the interface plays an important role in the resulting local electronic structure at the interface. In particular the present work has obtained a large and significant dipole in the valence electron structure within the top two layers of the Si (001) substrate. The resulting step in the potential energy determines the valence band offset between the deposited SiC material and this substrate. We may therefore consider the possibility of a change in the valence band offset between two semiconductors following a local chemical change at the interface. This type of variation in the offset has already been observed [22,23] in some semiconductor heterojunctions.

The role of dipoles in the formation of the heterojunction offset has formed the basis of several model theories [24-27]. This paper has presented the results of the self-consistent solution of the electronic rearrangement at the interface for a minimum energy arrangement of the atomic structure. We have employed a semi-empirical model for the Fock matrix and an assumed $(4 \times 1)$ unit cell for a periodic two-dimensional system, and therefore the results do not deal with all the features. However, this work provides an important insight into the various atomic arrangements occurring at a strained interface and the resulting behaviour of the valence electronic structure.

The results for the SiC/Si junction show that the dipole has not resulted from the significant movement of valence electron charge across the interface between the Si (001) surface and the deposited material. On the other hand the dipole has formed mostly within the first two layers of the substrate. An alternative qualitative view of these results, however, is to consider the original top Si (001) layer as part of a new four-layer SiC structure. Then the dipole is centred close to the interface of this four-layer SiC structure and the underlying Si material.

An important result of the present work is to confirm the view [20] that the chemical treatment and resulting structures at the SiC/Si interface may lead to significant changes in the properties of the heterojunction. The recent use of fluorine [20] has led to improved current gain for the SiC/Si device. The addition of fluorine may lead either to termination of the unwanted Si dangling bonds that form during the deposition process or to a more ordered material growth at the interface. Chlorine has also been used to improve the quality of the deposited material at lower temperatures [4]. The other potentially useful halogen, bromine, has rarely been studied in silicon surface chemistry [28,29] and may have some application for the desired lower temperature and more ordered growth of group-IV materials. The Si-Br bonds are weaker and a lower temperature is therefore required for the bromine to be desorbed and provide sites for new SiC bonds.

## 5. Conclusions

The formation of the strained SiC (001)/Si (001) heterojunction has been studied. The atomic structure that minimizes the total energy for the assumed $(4 \times 1)$ unit cell contains significant lattice distortions. The magnitude of these distortions at the interface, as expressed by the stretch of the C-C bond and the displacements of the underlying Si atoms, increases as the number of deposited layers is increased from one to three. The distortions in the SiC bonding are expressed by the increased length of the Si-C bond and values of bond angles much greater than the tetrahedral value. Such distortions are generally smaller in the $(1,-1,0)$ direction, where an increase in the atomic packing to that expected for $\beta$-SiC (001) has occurred, as compared with the (1,1,0) direction where the original Si (001)-like spacing is maintained.
