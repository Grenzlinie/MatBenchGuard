# Reconstruction of small Si cluster after ethylene adsorption:
## A full-potential linear-muffin-tin-orbital molecular-dynamics study

Min Qiu, Ming Jiang, Yu-Jun Zhao, and Pei-Lin Cao
Department of Physics and State Key Laboratory of Silicon Materials, Zhejiang University,
Hangzhou 310027, People's Republic of China

(Received 6 August 1998; accepted 4 March 1999)

Using full-potential linear-muffin-tin-orbital method, we have performed molecular-dynamics simulations for the ethylene adsorption on the Si₅₋₇-cluster surfaces. The calculations show that the most favored adsorption site is the short bridge site for Si₅ cluster, with the adsorption energy 1.78 eV. The adsorption structure of ethylene molecule is similar to that of the dimer-maintained structure for C₂H₄+Si(100)-(2×1). It indicates that ethylene is di-σ bonded to the Si₅ cluster. At the same time, the Si₅ cluster reconstructs after ethylene adsorption. The three-center bond among side atoms breaks, and new bonds form. For Si₆ cluster, the most favored adsorption site is the atop site on the side atom. After adsorption, Si₆ cluster reconstructs from tetragonal bipyramid to edge-capped trigonal bipyramid. The short bridge is the only available site to adsorb ethylene for Si₇ cluster. © 1999 American Institute of Physics. [S0021-9606(99)51720-0]

## I. INTRODUCTION

Silicon is an important material in the semiconductor industry. Particularly, if current miniaturization trends continue, the minimum structure size of industrial instruments will approach the scale of the silicon clusters in a couple of decades.¹ Accordingly, the properties of silicon clusters have been of considerable interest in physics and chemistry. Many experimental studies have been performed to examine the reactions of silicon cluster with ethylene,²⁻⁷ acetylene,⁸ water,⁸,⁹ ammonia,⁸,¹⁰⁻¹⁵ and oxygen.⁷,⁸,¹⁶ All the results show that the reactivity of silicon clusters in range of 2–70 atoms is very different from solid silicon. For a review we refer to the paper of Jarrold.¹

The experimental studies of reactions of Si clusters cation with ethylene²⁻⁷ show that the reaction rates are in large variations with cluster size. The binding energies vary between 0.8 and 2.0 eV. The authors think that the binding energies of this magnitude are too small to be accounted for by strong di-σ bonding, suggesting that the ethylene molecule is bound to the silicon clusters by a weaker π-bonding interaction. However, recent studies of ethylene adsorption on reconstructed Si(100)-(2×1) surface (both the experiments¹⁷⁻²⁰ and theoretical calculations)²¹⁻²³ show that ethylene is di-σ bonded to the Si dimer with two kinds of bond types: dimer-maintained structure and dimer-cleaved structure. The experimental adsorption energy of the former structure is 1.65 eV.¹⁸ This value is just in the range of the ethylene adsorption energy for silicon clusters (0.8–2.0 eV), so we think ethylene may be di-σ bonded with some silicon clusters.

Some theoretical studies have been performed on the reactivity of silicon clusters. Krack and Jug²⁴ have studied ammonia adsorption with neutral Si₅ and Si₁₀ clusters by the semiemprical molecular orbital (MO) method SINDO1 and the ab initio program GAUSSIAN 90. We have studied the nature of NH₃ molecule adsorption and dissociation on neutral Si-cluster surfaces by DV-X_α method,²⁵,²⁶ and performed full-potential linear-muffin-tin-orbital (FP-LMTO) molecular-dynamics studies for the NH₃ and H₂O adsorption on Si₅₋₇-cluster surfaces.²⁷,²⁸ However, we have not found any theoretical studies of ethylene adsorption on silicon-cluster surfaces. We believe that a theoretical study of silicon clusters will lead to a deeper understanding of important scientific issues.

In the present work, we will study ethylene adsorption on a neutral-Si₅₋₇-cluster surface by full-potential linear-muffin-tin-orbital (FP-LMTO) molecular-dynamics simulations. Experimental attention is focused on the studies of chemistry of cluster anions or cations because it is relatively easy to size select cluster ions before reactions.²⁻¹⁶ However, it is appropriate to study the adsorption on the neutral-cluster surfaces which are, in many cases, more scientifically interesting in the terms of different behavior of clusters and extended surfaces. Also our theoretical studies will give a preparation for the future experimental studies of neutral silicon study.

## II. METHOD AND MODELS

Full-potential linear-muffin-tin-orbital (FP-LMTO) method²⁹⁻³² is based on the density-functional formalism and uses the Kohn–Sham (KS)³³ self-consistent field procedure with one-electron orbitals expanded in a basis of linear muffin-tin orbitals (LMTOs).³⁴,³⁵ In such method, space is divided into two parts: nonoverlapping muffin-tin spheres centered at the nuclei and the remaining so-called interstitial region. The linear muffin-tin orbitals χ, which are augmented Hankel functions, are³⁵ (in the right part of the equation, the subscript RL is omitted):

$$
\chi_{R L}=
\begin{cases}
\Phi+(-\Phi \times W\{J, \Phi\} / W\{J, \Phi\}+\Phi)\{-2[W\{J, \Phi\} W\{K, \Phi\}+W\{J, \Phi\} S W\{J, \Phi\}]\} & \text { inside the muffin-tin sphere } \\
K^{i} \times 2 W\{\Phi, J\} & \text { interstitial region }
\end{cases},
\tag{1}
$$

where
$$
W\{f, g\} \equiv s^{2}\left[f(s) \frac{d}{d s} g(s)-g(s) \frac{d}{d s} f(s)\right],
\tag{2}
$$

$$
\Phi=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial \epsilon} \Phi\right|_{\epsilon=\epsilon_{R L}},
\tag{3}
$$

and the functions $\Phi$ (radial function multiplied by spherical harmonics) are solutions of the one-electron Schrödinger equation for an energy $\epsilon_{R L}$ inside the muffin-tin sphere (radius $s$). $J$, $K$ are the Bessel function and Hankel function, respectively, and $K^{i}$ is the interstitial Hankel function. $^{35} S$ is the structure matrix. The second augmented item for wave-function inside the muffin-tin sphere is chosen to make the basis states continuous and energy independent, and to have continuous first derivatives.

While this basis set defines the LMTO method, LMTO calculations often employ rather severe approximations, such as the atomic sphere approximation (ASA). Such approximations are clearly inadequate for structures with large interstitial regions or with low coordination symmetry, as in the present cluster calculations. Here we use a completely general form for the potential and density in which the potential is expressed as a linear combination of radial functions multiplied by spherical harmonics in all the space, including muffin-tin sphere and interstitial regions. $^{35}$ However, the most difficult task is to calculate the interstitial-potential matrix elements. In this paper we use a very efficient approach for this. As in Ref. 35, Hankel functions are used to represent interstitial quantities, but here the three-center integrals are reduced to linear combinations of two-center integrals (which are easy to evaluate analytically) by numerical means. $^{29}$

Up to now, most molecular-dynamics calculations have been done using the plane-wave pseudopotential (PW-PP) method, because in this approach the forces are trivial to obtain. But a problem is that the straightforward PW-PP approach is only suited to systems with weakly scattering $s p$ electrons. It is therefore desirable to obtain force theorems for more sophisticated approaches which can be applied more generally, such as FP-LMTO method. Here we use a systematic technique presented by Methfessel and Schilfgaarde for deriving force theorems in arbitrary basis sets. $^{31,32}$ In such technique, the forces are not calculated directly from the derivatives of the total energy function, but calculated from a Harris energy function. $^{36}$ The Harris and KS energies are equal to first-order respective to variations around the self-consistent density, therefore the energy derivatives are equal at the certain point. However, the Harris energy function has simpler expression, and is easy to calculate the energy derivatives. The details are somewhat tedious, and one can refer to Ref. 31. After we get the forces on the nuclei, we can do molecular-dynamics calculations with the scheme described by Car and Parrinello. $^{37}$

Based on our FP-LMTO molecular-dynamics study, the lowest energy structures of $\mathrm{Si}_{5-7}$ clusters are: trigonal bipyramid $\left(D_{3 h}\right)$, tetragonal bipyramid $\left(D_{4 h}\right)$, and pentagonal bi- pyramid $\left(D_{5 h}\right)$ (see Fig. 1). This is in good agreement with the earlier theoretical results. $^{38-43}$ The structure parameters of these clusters are shown in Table I. For $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}$ molecule, the calculated $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}$ bond length is $1.32 \AA$, $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ bond length is $1.09 \AA$, and $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ angle is $122.0^{\circ}$. (These values will be adopted to compare with those of $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}$ after adsorption.) The data are in good agreement with the experimental values: $1.34 \AA$, $1.09 \AA$, and $121.3^{\circ} .{ }^{44}$

### A. Si₅

According to our molecular-dynamics simulations, Si₅ cluster can adsorb ethylene molecule on all of the following four sites: the atop site on the capped atom, the atop site on the side atom, the short bridge site, and the long bridge site. Figure 2 shows the final adsorption structures. The calculated adsorption energies are shown in Table II and the structure parameters are shown in Table III.

From Table II we can conclude that the most favored site for ethylene adsorption is the short bridge site [Fig. 2(C)], and the adsorption energy is 1.78 eV. From our previous studies on ammonia and water adsorption,²⁷,²⁸ we know that the structure of the Si₅ cluster does not change significantly. However, things are very different from those in ethylene adsorption. The structure of the Si₅ cluster distorts significantly. To see it clearly, we show the structure changing process of Si₅ and ethylene in Fig. 3. From Fig. 3(C), we can find that the C₂H₄ reached the final structure after 1500 molecular-dynamics simulation iterations. But for Si₅ cluster, structure changes very slowly. Only after 4000 iterations or so, will it distort significantly. It can reach the final equilibrium structure after 6000 iterations.

After adsorption, the C–C bond stretches from 1.32 Å (double-bond length in ethylene) to 1.54 Å (Table III), which is almost the same with the single C–C bond length of 1.55 Å in diamond.²³ The C–H bond length remains unchanged, so we do not show this value in Table III (the same for Si₆ and Si₇). The H–C–C and H–C–H bond angles change significantly to 113.1° and 108.6°, which are around 109.5°. This indicates that the structure is close to tetrahedral conformation. Now we turn to the changes of the Si₅ cluster. Both the geometrical structure and the bond characters have changed significantly after the ethylene adsorption, i.e., Si₅ cluster was reconstructed. After adsorption, the cluster adopts a more packed structure, and it is also elongated in the direction of axis connecting capped atom 4 and capped atom 5 ($d_{4-5}$=3.66 Å, compared to the initial value: 2.96 Å). $d_{1-2}(=d_{1-3})$ changes from 3.04 Å to 2.34 Å (Table III), which is almost the same with the bulk Si–Si bond length of 2.35 Å. It indicates that the bond has formed. However, there is no bond between atom 2 and 3 ($d_{2-3}$=2.89 Å). As we know, the free Si₅ cluster adopts the three-center bond between the side atom 1–2–3,⁴⁵ which reduces the dangling bonds. After ethylene adsorption, the weak three-center bond breaks. Instead, the more strong bonds between side atoms are formed (bonds 1–2 and 1–3). The contour plot of total charge density for the plane of atom 1–2–3 are shown in Fig. 4, (B): Si₅+C₂H₄, (C): free Si₅, respectively. The charge accumulation in the regions between atom 1 and 2 (also for atom 1 and 3) indicates the formation of chemical bond, while no bond between atom 2 and 3 [Fig. 4(B)].

We also have tried other possible adsorption sites for Si₅+C₂H₄. The adsorption energy is only 0.37 eV (Table II). The structure changes of Si₅ and C₂H₄ are not significant. (For example, the $\theta_{\text{S-C-C}}$ is only 9.4°.) The other two possible adsorption sites are the atop site on the capped atom and the long bridge [Figs. 2(B), (D)], which have bigger adsorption energies, 1.15 and 1.20 eV, respectively. For these two adsorption sites, the changes of Si₅ cluster are not so significant, but the structure changes of ethylene molecule is similar to those for ethylene adsorption on the short bridge (Table III).

### B. $Si_{6}$

The possible sites for ethylene adsorption on $Si_{6}$ are shown in Fig. 5, and all the structure parameters are shown in Table IV. The most favored site is the atop site on the side atom [Fig. 5(A)], with the adsorption energy 0.85 eV (Table II). It is different from $Si_{5}$+$C_{2}H_{4}$. After adsorption, the changes of ethylene molecule are very similar to those of $Si_{5}$+$C_{2}H_{4}$, and one can refer to Table IV. The Si–C bond length now is 1.85 Å. It is also close to the Si–C bond length of 1.89 Å in $\beta$-SiC crystal, $^{21}$ so we can draw a conclusion that Si–C bond forms after adsorption.

The more interesting thing is the changes of the $Si_{6}$ cluster. As we already know, $Si_{6}$ has two lowest energy structures: tetragonal bipyramid and edge-capped trigonal bipyramid. $^{38-43}$ For tetragonal bipyramid, the $Si_{6}$ cluster adopts four-center bond among side atoms to reduce the dangling bond. $^{45}$ However, for edge-capped trigonal bipyramid [if atom 1 is assumed as the edge-capped atom, Fig. 1 (B)], instead of the four-center bond, all the side atoms form bonds with the nearby side atoms, but no bond between atom 1 and 5 or between atom 1 and 6. After ethylene adsorption on the atop site on the side atom 1, the bond length of $d_{2-4}(=d_{3-4})$ changes to 2.44 Å, which is close to the bulk Si–Si bond length of 2.35 Å. It indicates the formation of the bond. But the $d_{1-2}(=d_{1-3})$ is 2.99 Å, no bond. ### C. Si₇

The only possible ethylene adsorption site of Si₇ is the short bridge, with the adsorption energy 0.14 eV (Table II). The final structure is shown in Fig. 7, and the structure parameters are shown in Table V. The structure of the Si₇ cluster does not change very significantly, but it is elongated in the direction of axis of capped atom 6 and 7. $d_{6-7}$ changes from 2.49 to 2.88 Å. Compared with the bulk Si–Si bond length 2.35 Å, we can conclude that the bond between the capped atom 6 and 7 has broken. The Si–C bond length is 1.99 or 1.88 Å, and it also indicates the formation of bond between Si and C, just like Si₅+C₂H₄ and Si₆+C₂H₄. The changes of ethylene molecule are similar to those for Si₅+C₂H₄ and Si₆+C₂H₄. We do not repeat them here. However, Si₇ cluster cannot adsorb ethylene molecule on other sites according to our simulations.

### IV. SUMMARY
