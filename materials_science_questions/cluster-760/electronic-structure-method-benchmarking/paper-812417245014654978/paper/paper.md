# Adsorption and decomposition pathways for ICN on Si(1 0 0)-(2 × 1)

Evgueni B. Kadossov, P. Rajasekar, Nicholas F. Materer *

Department of Chemistry, 107 Physical Science, Oklahoma State University, Stillwater, OK 74078-3071, USA

Received 7 July 2002; in final form 13 January 2003

## Abstract

The adsorption and surface reactions of ICN on the Si(1 0 0)-(2 × 1) surface are studied using ab initio quantum calculations on model dimer clusters. ICN can physisorb via a dative bond through the N atom. This species can further react to form either a dissociated state through I-CN bond cleavage or a stable side-on intermediate by reacting across the dimer bond. There are two important differences between the ICN adsorption process and that of HCN. Unlike HNC, the INC dative bonded structure is not stable. Next, there is no transition state between the side-on adsorbed ICN and INC species.

## 1. Introduction

We have recently carried out a series of experiments investigating the adsorption and thermal chemistry of cyanogen iodide (ICN) on Si(1 0 0) surfaces by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and temperature programmed desorption (TPD) [1]. Briefly, after 0.2 L ICN exposure at low temperatures (100 K) the CN triple bond of ICN remains intact. XPS analysis of the C 1s photoelectron peak indicates that up to 25% of ICN is molecularly adsorbed. Upon annealing to room temperature, the CN bonds remain intact but all the IC bonds are completely broken on Si(1 0 0).

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN) is an analogous molecule that has been studied both experimentally [2] and theoretically by Lin and co-workers [3,4]. HCN dimerizes and possibly polymerizes on Si(1 0 0) surface at low temperatures [2]. Upon heating this surface up to 220 K, some of the HCN converts into an adsorbed CN species. However, an iminium (HC-NH) species, not found for ICN, also forms. The polymerization and the iminium species make the HCN surface chemistry more complex than that found for ICN.

To investigate the differences between ICN and HCN, the adsorption of ICN and its INC isomer is studied theoretically using the GAUSSIAN 98 package [5]. The Si(1 0 0) surface is represented primarily by a single-dimer Si₉H₁₂ cluster. This cluster has been utilized by Bacalzo-Gladden et al. [3,4] for HCN and in studies of other nitrogen containing compounds, such as ammonia and

## 2. Computational procedure

Energy calculations, geometry optimizations and frequency calculations are performed using the hybrid density functional method including Becke's 3-parameter nonlocal-exchange functional [10] with the correlation functional of Lee–Yang–Parr, B3LYP [11]. The 6-31G* basis set [12] is employed for all atoms except iodine, since no parameterization is included for this atom in **Gaussian 98**. In careful studies of ammonia adsorption, the 6-31G* results are found to be within 4 kJ/mol of the larger 6-311++G(2d,p) basis set [6]. An additional basis set is required for the I. Two basis sets are considered for I, the LanL2DZ and CEP-31G. The LanL2DZ+6-31G* basis set adds the LanL2DZ basis for I and the 6-31G* basis for Si, C and N. For I, LanL2DZ includes the D95 double-zeta basis set [13] combined with Hay and Wadt's [14] effective core potential. In the second basis set (CEP-31G+6-31G*), the Stevens–Basch–Krauss effective core potential split-valence basis set [15] CEP-31G is used for I with the 6-31G* basis for all other atoms. Except as noted in the text, all atomic positions are optimized without geometry constraints. Frequency calculations confirm that the stable geometries have real vibrational frequencies. The transition states, optimized using **Gaussian 98**, contain only one imaginary normal mode.

### 3.2. ICN adsorption on the Si(100) surface

Fig. 1 illustrates several possible structures for ICN adsorption on a Si cluster. Two possible models for ICN adsorption without dissociation are ICN1 and ICN2. Upon cleavage of the IC bond, two additional dissociated structures are possible, SiNC and SiCN. In these structures, the I atom is attached to one Si dimer atom while either the N or the C is bonded to the other dimer atom. Table 2 summarizes selected geometric parameters and energies relative to separated $\text{Si}_9\text{H}_{12}$ cluster and ICN molecule for these four configurations. The total electronic energy of the cluster and the ICN molecule are $-6\,859\,560$ and $-273\,568$ kJ/mol, respectively. INC1 and INC2 are two additional adsorption models, shown in Fig. 2, derived from adsorption of the INC isomer. The geometric parameters and energies relative to separated cluster and INC molecule are given in Table 3. For the $\text{Si}_9\text{H}_{12}$ cluster, the buckling angle is measured with respect to a plane that passes through the four atoms directly below the dimer atoms. The INC1 structure required a collinear constraint on the INC species, and is presented in order to contrast this adsorption mode with the analogous HNC system studied by Bacalzo-Gladden et al. [3,4]. The IN bond in INC1 is illustrated in Fig. 2 with a dashed length because the bond length significantly exceeds that calculated for molecular INC.

Fig. 3 shows the potential energy curves along with the relevant transition states for the various models. The solid line in Fig. 3 represents the primary pathways for the decomposition of ICN into absorbed atomic I and molecular CN. Table 2 contains selected geometric parameters for the relevant transition states. Although a stepwise decomposition path started from a molecular physisorbed state is shown, additional pathways which start from direct adsorption of gas-phase ICN into either a side-on geometry (ICN2) or a dissociated structure (SiNC) are possible. The dashed line in Fig. 3 illustrates an adsorption pathway starting with the formation of a side-on structure, INC2, from gas-phase INC. Geometrical parameters for transition state (TS5) between INC2 and SiNC are given in Table 3. Finally, the dotted lines in Fig. 3 illustrate pathways that are possible for HCN [3,4], but are not possible for ICN without additional structural constraints.

### 3.3. ICN adsorption and dissociation channel

The formation of the ICN end-on adsorption structure (ICN1) from gas-phase ICN and silicon single-dimer cluster is exothermic with total energy change $-54.2$ kJ/mol. The adsorption energy of the ICN1 species is close to the $-49.6$ kJ/mol energy change calculated for the analogous HCN structure. Since ICN1 contains a dative bond, cluster size effects are expected. For molecular HCN on a double-dimer silicon cluster, Bacalzo-Gladden et al. found an energy difference that includes zero-point corrections of 28 kJ/mol [4]. Utilizing a triple-dimer cluster for the less computationally demanding HCN analog, the adsorption energy is found to be 24 kJ/mol more negative than a single-dimer cluster. For ammonia, a 25 kJ/mol difference is found between a single and larger cluster [6]. Thus, the energy gained from the N dative bond by the increased delocalization on the larger cluster appears to depend more on the substrate than the adsorbate. A similar deviation is expected for ICN1.

For ICN1, the CN bond length is only $0.003$ Å greater than the gas-phase value indicating that the CN triple bond is intact. The nitrogen lone pair forms a dative bond between the nitrogen end of the ICN molecule and the electrophilic (buckled-down) Si generated by a $10.6^\circ$ buckling of the dimer. This buckling is also consistent with $13^\circ$ buckling found for ammonia [6-8]. Both the ICN and HCN absorption energies are less than the $-96.2$ kJ/mol value for ammonia, calculated using B3LYP/6-311++G(2df,2pd) and a single-dimer cluster [6], or the $-120$ to $-90$ kJ/mol range found for methyl, dimethyl and trimethylamines calculated using a single-dimer cluster and B3LYP with varying basis sets [9,20]. Thus, the $\text{sp}^3$ N in ammonia and the amines can donate more charge to a Si dimer atom than the sp N in the CN triple-bonded species.

Fig. 3 shows that the adsorption of ICN as atomic I and molecular CN (SiNC and SiCN) are energetically more stable than all other geometries. In both the SiNC and SiCN structures, the Si-Si dimer is practically unbuckled. The CN species retains its triple bond character with an average bond length of $1.17$ Å. The underlying reason for the stability of SiNC and SiCN structures is the creation of a strong SiC or SiN bond. The standard bond enthalpies of SiC and SiN are 435 and 439 kJ/mol [21], respectively, while the difference between the relatively strong CI bond of ICN [22] and the SiI bond [21] is approximately 20 kJ/mol. Since the energetic cost of breaking the CI bond is compensated by the formation of the SiI bond, the SiC and SiN bond energies determine the stability of these structures. The small difference between the SiN and SiC bond energies also results in the SiCN structure being only 32 kJ/mol more stable that SiNC. A similar small energy difference is also found for HCN on the silicon single-dimer [3,4].

UPS and XPS spectra show that room temperature adsorption of the ICN on Si(100) produces atomic I and molecular CN on the surface [1]. Assuming that the reaction pathway shown in Fig. 3 is the one followed experimentally, some tentative conclusions about the dynamics can be inferred. The dative-bonded end-on adsorption structure (ICN1) is expected to form via a barrierless transition state. Assuming that kinetic energy gained through adsorption can be utilized, this species can react, through transition state TS2, to form SiNC. The resulting kinetic energy gained from forming SiNC via ICN1 is sufficient to overcome the 88.9 kJ/mol barrier between SiNC and final reaction product, SiCN. The large 88.9 kJ/mol transition state barrier between SiNC and SiCN is a consequence of the partial breaking of the SiN bond before the SiC bond fully forms. An additional pathway to form SiCN involves the reaction of ICN1 across the silicon dimer to achieve an intermediate side-on configuration, ICN2. In ICN2, CN bond is significantly longer ($1.26$ Å) indicating a CN double-bond. Given the experimentally observed SiCN species formed at room temperature, the newly created ICN2 species must process sufficient energy to react further, first forming SiNC, followed by SiCN, the expected final reaction product.

### 3.4. INC adsorption and dissociation channel

The dashed potential energy curve in Fig. 3 represents the decomposition of gas-phase INC into adsorbed atomic I and molecular CN. Direct

adsorption from the gas-phase into the chemi- sorbed state, INC2, is possible. Upon adsorption, INC2 can further react to form the lowest energy dissociated structure SiCN through TS5. INC2 is structurally similar to ICN2. In INC2, the I atom is bound to the N atom, while for ICN2, the I is bound to the C atom. The buckling of the Si–Si dimer is $1.4^\circ$, slightly larger than $0.4^\circ$ found for ICN2, but much less than $10.6^\circ$ found for the ICN1. In ICN2, the atom directly connected to the iodine (C in ICN2 and N in INC2) bonds to the buckled down atom, while the carbon in INC2 bonds to the buckled up atom. However, the IN bond length is $28\%$ larger than that in gas-phase INC. This weak IN bond compared to the stronger IC in the ICN2 structure is possibly the reason for the significantly higher energy (158 kJ/mol) of this structure in comparison to ICN2.

The dotted potential energy curve in Fig. 3 il- lustrates these additional INC pathways based on those found for the adsorption of gas-phase HNC [3,4]. For HNC, the initial adsorption geometry is analogous to the INC1 structure, and consists of molecular HNC bonded to a Si dangling bond through the C atom. Calculations show that the HNC structure is stable without geometrical con- straints. In contrast, INC bond angle must be constrained at $180^\circ$ to achieve convergence. Sur- prisingly, the INC molecule is bound to the nu- cleophilic buckled-up Si atom. Examination of the geometrical parameters shows that the IN bond in the INC1 structure is abnormally long, $169\%$ of the INC bond length in the gas phase. It is pos- sible that the donation of charge to the INC molecule disrupts the weak bond (159 kJ/mol [21]) between two electronegative atoms, N and I, and necessitates the constraint required to keep INC stable.

### 3.5. Surface ICN to INC isomerization channel

For surface HCN to HNC isomerization, Bac- alzo-Gladden et al. [3,4] found a transition state between the HCN analogs to ICN2 and INC2. This pathway is illustrated by the dotted line in Fig. 3 between ICN2 and INC2 through TS6. Using both gas-phase isomerization and HCN as a model, the I atom is expected to be approximately midway between the C and N atoms in the tran- sition state. However, no similar transition state is found. Therefore, unlike HCN, the surface isom- erization can only occur through the unlikely pathway involving the SiCN and SiNC dissociated states.

## 4. Conclusions

Ab initio quantum calculations have been used to study the adsorption and surface reactions of ICN on the Si(100) surface represented by $Si_9H_{12}$ single-dimer cluster. ICN can adsorb as molecular species bond via a dative bond to the electrophilic (buckled-down) Si atom. The dative bonded species can react and directly form a dissociated state or react through a stable side-on adsorption intermediate. After dissociation, the CN is bound to the silicon surface through either the C or the N ends. UPS and XPS show that the adsorption of the ICN produces I and CN species on the surface [1]. Consistent with ex- perimental results, our calculations predict that the most stable product is adsorbed CN bond through the C. The calculated orbital energies for the $\pi$-bonded electron in the CN triple bond agree with the experimentally measured UPS spectra [1]. In addition, there are two important differences between the ICN adsorption process and that found for HCN. First, the INC dative bonded structure, INC1, is not stable. Thus, there is no reaction pathway between this species and the dissociated state SiCN. Secondly, there is no transition state between the side-on ICN2 and the INC2 structure. Direct surface isomerization, which is possible for HCN, cannot occur for ICN.
