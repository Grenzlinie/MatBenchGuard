# Is the intramolecular hydrogen bond energy valuable from internal rotation barriers? $^{\star}$

Giuseppe Buemi*, Felice Zuccarello

Dipartimento di Scienze Chimiche, Università di Catania, Viale A. Doria 6, 95125 Catania, Italy

Received 18 June 2001; revised 18 September 2001; accepted 26 September 2001

## Abstract
A method for evaluating the energy of intramolecular hydrogen bridges utilizing the rotation barriers of the donor and/or of the acceptor groups is proposed. The procedure has been tested successfully on various molecules containing $\mathrm{O-H\cdots O}$, $\mathrm{O-H\cdots Halogen}$, $\mathrm{O-H\cdots N}$, $\mathrm{N-H\cdots O}$, $\mathrm{N-H\cdots N}$ and $\mathrm{O-H\cdots S}$ bridges. Comparison between the obtained results and those coming from the classic method ($\Delta E$ between the *open* and *chelate* conformations) shows good agreement and reliability. Failure has been noted only for the $\mathrm{S-H\cdots O}$ and $\mathrm{S-H\cdots S}$ hydrogen bridges. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Hydrogen bonding; Hydrogen bridges energy; Rotation barriers; β-Diketones

## 1. Introduction
It is well known that a molecular conformation experiencing intramolecular hydrogen bond is generally stabilized with respect to other hydrogen bond free conformations. The strength of a $X-H\cdots Y$ hydrogen bridge depends on the nature of the $X$ and $Y$ atoms (electronegativity, net atomic charges, conjugation of the chelate ring and so on) and produces important modifications in the geometrical parameters of the molecule as well as in the related infrared (IR) and nuclear magnetic resonance spectra. Unfortunately, the energy associated with the hydrogen bond ($E_{\text{HB}}$) is not physically observable and its magnitude can be estimated only on theoretical ground. Being not possible to have absolute values, $E_{\text{HB}}$ is always a *relative energy*, i.e. a $\Delta E$ between the hydrogen-bonded energy and that of a reference, hydrogen bond free, conformation. The main problem is the choice or the definition of the *hydrogen bond free conformation* (representing the *zero point* in the $E_{\text{HB}}$ energy scale) which is inevitably different in *intermolecular* and *intramolecular* hydrogen-bonded systems. In fact, the strength of an intermolecular hydrogen bond between two molecules, A and B, is the difference between the energy of the $\mathrm{A\cdots B}$ adduct and the energy of A and B when situated at infinite distance from each other. Correction for the basis set superposition error (BSSE) is the main problem in this case, but the reference point is very well defined. This approach is not applicable when one must handle intramolecular hydrogen bonds as, say, those present in malondialdehyde (MDA, $\mathrm{R_1=R_2=R_2'=H}$), acetylacetone (ACAC, $\mathrm{R_1=H}$, $\mathrm{R_2=R_2'=CH_3}$) and their derivatives, for which a

In this case, $E_{\text{HB}}$, is commonly evaluated as the difference between the energy of the chelate conformation and the energy of the conformation having the $\text{O-H}$ group (donor group) outside rotated by $180^\circ$ (open conformation) or vice versa, if we prefer to handle positive values. In some cases, however, this choice is impracticable as, e.g. when $\text{NH}_2$ is the donor group (carbonylamine, formazan, ketohydrazone, etc.) because its rotation does not produce an open form. One could think to overcome the problem by rotating the acceptor group, but this choice implies the assumption of a different 'zero point' in the $E_{\text{HB}}$ scale, and it is important to bear in mind that comparison between hydrogen bond energies can be done only when they refer to the same reference conformation. In any case, whatever is the reference conformation, the earlier $E_{\text{HB}}$ definition implies the assumption that the strength of an intramolecular hydrogen bridge is the stabilization resulting from a balance between attractive and repulsive terms, conjugation and other interactions occurring in the two compared structures. $E_{\text{HB}}$ values are therefore to be handled with caution because they can be affected by non-negligible interactions extraneous to the hydrogen bond and whose magnitude cannot be easily predicted. One problem is whether the geometry of the open conformation is to be optimized or whether it is preferable to maintain the geometrical parameters of the chelate structure. Certainly, if optimization of the open form produces geometrical parameters too different from those of the chelate one, the resulting $E_{\text{HB}}$ could be misleading. Indeed, good $E_{\text{HB}}$ estimates are obtained for $\beta$-diketones when $\text{R}_1$, $\text{R}_2$ and $\text{R}'_2$ are small groups because the geometries of the chelate and open forms as well as their strains are not very different. On the contrary, if such substituents are very cumbersome or contain electronegative atoms ($\text{CF}_3$, $\text{CCl}_3$, $\text{NO}_2$, and so on), the chelate geometry could be significantly different from that of the open form, and in addition, the latter could be stabilized by formation of a new hydrogen bridge, as it occurs in hexafluoroacetylacetone (HFAA) [1]. In such situations, $E_{\text{HB}}$ is no longer attributable only (or mostly) to the $\text{O-H}\cdots\text{O}$ hydrogen bridge and the resulting $E_{\text{HB}}$ cannot be accepted as a reliable estimate of the hydrogen bond strength. Use of alternative reference conformations, as the following linear ones suggested for the enol and enethiol tautomers of thiomalondialdehyde [2,3] are also to be avoided for several reasons because the conjugated

![](./images/812710327656382467_4.jpg)

framework (trans double bonds) is too different from that of the chelate ring structure.

Problems also exist when empirical relations [4-6] between a physically observable (as the shortening of the distance between the two heteroatoms of the bridge or the shift of the frequency of the $X$-H stretching mode vibration) and the numerical value of the hydrogen bond energy because extrapolation for very strong hydrogen-bonded systems (e.g. H-centred structures) are not free from errors. In a previous study [7] on the evolution of the potential energy well of MDA along the proton transfer pathway, investigated at various $\text{O}\cdots\text{O}$ distances, it was concluded that for dramatically short $\text{O}\cdots\text{O}$ values (single minimum well) it is not possible to use corrections deduced for long distances where a double minimum well, different molecular symmetry and different degrees of anharmonicity exist. So it is highly desirable to explore other possibilities for referring always to the same energy scale.

One way could be the comparison of rotation barriers (RBs) calculated in the presence and in the absence of hydrogen bridges. In fact, excellent agreement with the classical method predictions were obtained when $E_{\text{HB}}$ of MDA [8] and ACAC were determined by comparing the $\text{O-H}$ RBs found in these molecules and the corresponding ones evaluated in hydrogen bond free reference compounds obtained by substituting the $\text{R-C=O}$ framework with an $\text{H}$ atom.

RBs were evaluated by scanning the potential energy surface with an increment size of $5^\circ$ (for each step the molecular geometry was optimized) until the maximum energy point is reached, which is generally associated with a rotation angle equal to $90^\circ$ or close to $90^\circ$. No correction for the zero point vibration energy was considered because it is not necessary to have so refined values for testing the validity of the proposed method and the imposition of frozen torsion angles during barrier calculations implies suppression of a freedom degree. Moreover, compounds and conformations were selected independently of their real existence or importance in the conformational equilibrium but only to compare $E_{HB}$ and $E_{HB1}$ estimates.

The GAUSSIAN98 and G98W [12] programs, installed on a SUN ULTRA1 workstation and on a PENTIUM III personal computer, respectively, were used for calculations.

On the contrary, the open forms of 9 suffer destabilizing non-bonded interactions between the hydroxyl hydrogen and the adjacent methyl group, which lead to predict $E_{\text{HB}}$ values for 9-trans and 9-II higher than that of the most stable conformation 9-cis and 9-II, respectively. $E_{\text{HB1}}$ values do not show such discrepancy and appear to be more reliable than the $E_{\text{HB}}$ ones. Of course, attention must also be paid to the interactions between the R₂ and/or R′₂ groups with R₁, when the latter is a bulk group. In particular, it is preferable that the spatial accommodation of such groups is the same in the examined and in the reference molecule.

In the open form of 13, there are stabilizing interactions between H₆ and the adjacent fluorine atom ($r_{\text{H···F}} \cong 2.18\ \mathring{\text{A}}$) which lower the total energy of this conformation. This extrastabilization should be roughly equal to 2.5 kJ/mol, which is the energy gain of the reference molecule, M, when the OH group is rotated by 180° (also in this form $r_{\text{H···F}}$ is about 2.18 Å). If $E_{\text{HB}}$ concerning the O–H···O bridge of 13 is renormalized by this amount, its gap with respect to $E_{\text{HB1}}$ is reduced to the acceptable value of about 4 kJ/mol.

Even if most of the results here reported come from B3LYP/6-31G<sup>**</sup> calculations, they show the same trend than the MP2/6-31G<sup>**</sup> ones, as it can be seen when both approaches were adopted. It should be noted that, inside the same family of compounds

### 4.3. $O-H\cdots F$ bridges

The intramolecular $O-H\cdots F$ bridges are weaker than the $O-H\cdots O$ ones. DFT calculations with different basis sets indicated an $E_{HB}$ limit value of 20 kJ/mol in 2-halophenols [13]. MP2/6-31 + G**//MP2/6-31G** + ZPVE calculation on 2-fluorophenol predicted $E_{HB}=12.2$ kJ/mol, which appears to be in some disagreement with the value of 6.8 kJ/mol deduced from torsional frequencies [14]. In 2-trifluoromethyl-vinylalcohol, the stability difference between open and chelate structures is 20.3 kJ/mol [15] at HF/6-31G level ($r_{O\cdots F}=2.73$ Å; $r_{H\cdots F}=1.96$ Å). In the present paper, we applied the RBM approach to 2-fluoro-vinylalcohol (2FVA) and 2-trifluoromethyl-vinylalcohol (2TFMVA), as well as to some conformations of the fluorinated compounds of Table 2, able to give rise to $O-H\cdots F$ bridges (Fig. 1), even if they are energetically unfavoured in the conformational equilibrium. Results are given in Table 4.

According to the classic procedure $E_{HB}$ is 17.5 kJ/mol in 2FVA and 24.0 kJ/mol in 2TFMVA (19.1 and 22.7 kJ/mol, respectively, at MP2 level), while the shift of OH stretching mode frequencies between open and chelate forms is $82\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ in both compounds. The best agreement between $E_{HB}$ and $E_{HB1}$ in 2FVA is obtained when the hydrogen bond free 2'TFVA is assumed as reference, while vinyl alcohol works well in the latter molecule. It seems that when F is directly attached to C of the chelate ring its elimination is not advisable.

Inspection of Table 4 reveals satisfactory agreement between classic and RBM approaches, although on a percent basis the difference is higher than in the stronger $O-H\cdots O$ bridges. In **6-I**, **7-I** and **10** no evaluation is possible according to the classic method, but the strength of the $O-H\cdots F$ hydrogen bond can be roughly identified with the difference between $E_{HB1}$ and $E_{HB}$ of the $O-H\cdots O$ bridges reported in Table 3. ## 5. Conclusion

From results available at this time, it can be affirmed that RBs and intramolecular hydrogen bond energy are strictly related to each other. It was shown here that the proposed RB method can be used with sufficient confidence for evaluating the energies of the hydrogen bridges in variously substituted $\beta$-diketones and *ortho*-phenol derivatives as an alternative approach to the classic one or as a complementary approach for obtaining further information on molecular interactions in particular cases. It is confirmed that $E_{\text{HB}}$ and $E_{\text{HB1}}$ are very close to each other when the chelate and open conformation do not suffer very different interactions. On the contrary, when the open conformations experience interactions affecting $E_{\text{HB}}$, evaluation of $E_{\text{HB1}}$ furnishes a more reliable value of the hydrogen bridge energy and provides an estimate of the additional interactions, as it was seen in HFAA and some of its derivatives ($\text{O-H}\cdots\text{F}$ bridge strength). It is noteworthy that use of donor or acceptor barriers

generally produces hydrogen bond energies closer to the classic $E_{\text{HB}}$ ones referred to the donor open confor- mation. B3LYP estimates are always higher than the MP2 ones that indicates an overestimation when correlation energy is evaluated through such a func- tional.

Obviously, the barrier approach is more expensive than the classic method since a higher number of calculations are required for determining the barrier values in the examined molecule and in the reference one. Care must be paid in selecting the reference compound, because it must be hydrogen bond free but, on the same time, it must preserve all the other interactions (or at least most of them) present in the molecule under study (see, e.g. the formazan results [8]). To be noted that increase of the internal RB indicates a strengthening of the hydrogen bond but $E_{\text{HB1}}$ does not increase proportionally, so that the reference compound cannot be eliminated. Certainly, deep analysis of the results here reported does not allow us to say that the proposed approach solves all our problems. However, the method can give an important help when conclusions coming from the classical method are unsatisfactory because the torsion barrier includes the stabilizing interactions present in the chelate structure but it is free from the spurious interactions of the open conformations.

The failure of the proposed method when applied to enethiol tautomers is a particular, but not a hopeless case. Further investigations (still in progress in our laboratory) and deeper analysis of the $C-S$ and $S-H$ bonds peculiarities are still necessary for understand- ing which are the problems causing the failure when the $S-H\cdots X$ bridges are taken into account.
