# Structures and Spectroscopy of protonated ammonia clusters at different Temperatures

Alhadji Malloum†, Jean Jules Fifen†, *, Zoubeida Dhaouadi‡, Serge Guy Nana Engo† and Nejm-Eddine Jaidane‡

† Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, The University of Ngaoundere, 454, Ngaoundere, Cameroon.
‡ Laboratoire de Spectroscopie Atomique Moléculaire et Applications, Faculté des Sciences de Tunis, Université de Tunis El Manar, Campus Universitaire, 1060, Tunis, Tunisia.

September 1, 2016

ABSTRACT: The accurate determination of solvation energies of the proton in ammonia is based on the precise knowledge of structures of neutral and protonated ammonia clusters. In this work, we have investigated all the possible and stable structures of protonated ammonia clusters $H^+(NH_3)_{n=2-9}$, along with their isomeric distribution at a specific temperature. New significant isomers are reported here for the first time and show that the structures of protonated ammonia clusters are not only branched linear as assumed by all previous authors. Branched linear structures are the only responsible of the population of protonated ammonia clusters for $n=4-6$ at any temperature. However, for larger cluster sizes, this type of structures compete with branched cyclic, double cyclic, branched double cyclic and triple cyclic structures depending on the temperature. In addition, we have shown that protonated ammonia clusters are all Eigen structures and the first solvation shell of the related ammonium ion core is saturated by four ammonia molecules. We have also carried out a study of the hydrogen bond network of protonated ammonia clusters establishing the stability rule governing the various isomers of each cluster from estimated energies of hydrogen bond types in $H^+(NH_3)_{n=2-9}$. With all these results, a route for the accurate determination of solvation energies of the proton in ammonia at a given temperature could be conceivable.

KEYWORDS: protonated ammonia clusters, interaction energies, relative stability, hydrogen bond network.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Solvation energies of the proton in a given medium is widely needed in chemistry for many purposes such as the evaluation of the antioxidant activity of some compounds, the evaluation of $pKa's$ and the determination of solvation energies of other ions in the concerned medium. $^{1-19}$ Thus, the accurate estimation of solvation energies of the proton is of fundamental importance. However, the accurate evaluation of solvation energies of the proton in ammonia solvent at a given temperature is subject to the determination of the most favored structures of various isomers of protonated $(H^+(NH_3)_n)$ and neutral $((NH_3)_n)$ ammonia clusters in gas phase and in ammonia at that temperature. $^{12,17,20-22}$ This accuracy must be achieved with a large number of explicit ammonia molecules solvating the proton in a clusters.

Recently, we pointed out the structures and relative stabilities of neutral ammonia clusters at high levels of computation, with a deep review on previous investigations. $^{23}$ Structures of protonated ammonia clusters have also been a great subject of interest in the cluster community. $^{24-40}$ Asmis et al. $^{38}$ showed that the recorded experimental infrared vibrational predissociation (IRVPD) spectrum of the protonated ammonia dimer corresponds to a structure with a $D_{3d}$ symmetry and, this structure is a transition state. Tono and co-workers. $^{36,37}$ carried out the infrared photodissociation spectroscopy of $NH_4^+(NH_3)_{3,4}$ and $NH_4^+(NH_3)_{5-8}$ in the range 1070 to $1680cm^{-1}$ and 1020 and $1210cm^{-1}$, respectively. They reported both experimental and theoretical IR spectra. They found that for each cluster type (from tetramer to nonamer), the branched linear structure is the most stable one, in agreement with their mid-infrared (MIR) photodissociation spectra. However, they found no evidence of coexistence of isomers. This conclusion is difficult to understand since neither all the possible structures of the concerned clusters have been investigated, nor the isomer distributions with the temperature were initiated. Thus, it would be more convincing to derive structures of protonated ammonia clusters by investigating all the possible structures conjointly with the temperature-dependent isomer distributions. Note also that recently, Katada et al. $^{40}$ reported IR spectra of protonated ammonia clusters $(H^+(NH_3)_n)$, $n=8-100$ in the NH stretching modes using the infrared photodissociation spectroscopy combined with a tandem type quadrupole mass spectrometer. These data could be used to complete or confirm information on the prefer structures of protonated ammonia clusters. Elsewhere, Searles and Kebarle $^{24}$ measured clustering free energies and enthalpies of protonated ammonia clusters up to $n=6$ using the proton-beam mass spectrometer. They observed that the first solvation shell is filled up to $n=5$ and give the pentameric structure $NH_4^+(NH_3)_4$; no more ammonia molecule can be bound to the ammonium ion core.

As far as the theoretical investigations of structures of protonated ammonia clusters are concerned, previous works have been limited to the completeness of the second solvation shell. Thus, they focused only on branched linear isomers, excluding therefore other types of isomers (cyclic, multicyclic and their branched counterpart). In fact, Hirao et al. $^{27}$ studied branched linear structures of protonated ammonia clusters $H^+(NH_3)_n$ at $ab$ initio level of theory up to $n=6$. The most stable isomers of all investi-

Wang et al. $^{33}$ used B3LYP/6-31+G(d) and MP2/6-31+G(d) levels of theory with basis set superposition error (BSSE) correction to investigate branched linear structures of ammonia clusters up to the hexamer. Few years later, Fouqueau and Meuwly, $^{35}$ reported structures and infrared spectra of branched linear protonated ammonia clusters from dimer to nonamer using B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) and semi-empirical DFT/molecular dynamics simulations. They found that for $n < 5$, the clusters are mostly stable up to 100 K, while the larger clusters ($n \geq 5$) isomerize. Thus, investigating all the various isomers (cyclic, branched cyclic, multi-cyclic, ...) of a given cluster and their relative stabilities is necessary for a full understanding of fingerprints of protonated ammonia clusters and, for accurate evaluation of pKa's and solvation energies of the proton in ammonia.

It is worth mentioning that the isomer distribution can change significantly with the temperature due primarily to an entropic effect. The major contributors to the entropy differences between isomers are the many soft vibrational modes that large clusters possess. Once the vibrational frequencies are obtained from an ab-initio computation, the temperature ($T$) dependent entropy term can be assessed in the so-called harmonic superposition approximation. $^{41}$ This has been done routinely for e.g., water clusters, $^{42}$ charged water clusters, $^{43}$ hydrated sodium clusters $^{44}$ and most extensively for protonated water $^{45-50}$ and methanol clusters. $^{17,51}$ For protonated ammonia clusters, no work has attempted to investigate entropy-driven isomers.

In the present work, we complete previous theoretical investigations by involving for the first time, cyclic, branched cyclic, multi-cyclic, ... structures of isomers of protonated ammonia $H^{+}(NH_{3})_{n=2-9}$. We also investigate the temperature effects in the isomer distributions of protonated ammonia clusters. The complete structures of isomers of $H^{+}(NH_{3})_{n=2-9}$ and their temperature-dependent distributions provide accurate information on the preferred structures of these isomers in an excellent agreement with the experimental IR spectra $^{36,37}$ and the experimental clustering free energies and enthalpies. $^{24}$

The availability of a large body of data on relative isomer stabilities generated herein, allow us to embark on a rather detailed investigation concerning the determinants of isomer stability as proposed for the first time in our previous work. $^{44}$ This, evidently, includes hydrogen bond (HB) energies of ammonia ligands in the first and second hydration shells of the ammonium core, which depend on the coordination number (CN) and the type of the HB. The problem is that binding energies are prone to basis set superposition errors (BSSE), $^{52}$ which we note are quite substantial in the first solvation shell. Computing all the isomers with, e.g. the counterpoise (CP) correction of Boys and Bernadi $^{53}$ is a huge task when so many isomers are involved. In our previous work, $^{44}$ we found that extensive BSSE corrections can be circumvented by having just a single HB energy that is corrected for BSSE. We summarized those findings as "stability rules" that allow, for the first time, to deduce the relative isomer energies and outline the factors determining them. The same procedure is then used for protonated ammonia isomers to derive their stability rules.

## METHODOLOGY

Initially, geometries were constructed intuitively. Most of geometries of cluster of size $n$, are obtained by exploring all possible arrangements of adding one ammonia molecule to each geometry of cluster of size $n-1$. Some few geometries (Cyclic, some perfect symmetric isomers, ...) of cluster of size $n$, cannot be built from geometries of cluster of size $n$. For these few geometries, we intuitively imagine the possibility of their hydrogen bound network. Most of these later geometries have not been reported previously. Next, all built geometries are optimized to the true local minima. In order to ensure the reliability of our intuitive search of structures, we have used the ABCluster code proposed by Zhang and Dolg $^{54,55}$. For all cluster sizes, ABCluster provides the same global minima as our intuitive search. Furthermore, almost all our geometries obtained from our intuitive search have been located by ABCluster code. However, the ABCluster code provides many geometries which are quite the same and supplementary intuitive search should be done to eliminate some guesses.

Geometry optimizations and frequencies calculations were performed at the M06-2X level of theory. We used the 6-31++g(d,p) basis set of Pople. $^{56-58}$ The M06-2X functional has already shown its performance in the description of clusters of protonated methanol, as compared to the commonly used B3LYP functional. $^{17}$ Besides, we have shown recently that the M06-2X could be more suitable than the MP2 in the description of hydrogen bond network in ammonia clusters. $^{23}$ Furthermore, the M06-2X is one of the few functionals that has been developed especially to handle van der Waals interactions and has demonstrated excellent results for these types of systems. $^{13,59,60}$ However, for each cluster size $n$, the most stable isomers reported at the M06-2X level of theory have been also optimized at the MP2 level of theory.

All calculations were carried out using Gaussian 09 suite of programs. $^{61}$ Thermodynamic parameters were evaluated at 298.15 K and 1 atm. For accurate convergence of the optimization we used tight option for which the convergence is reached when the root mean square force, the maximum force, the root mean square displacement and the maximum displacement are within respectively the threshold of 0.000010, 0.000015, 0.000040 and 0.000060 atomic units. For integrals, we used the ultrafine grid which requests a "pruned" (99,590) grid ("Pruned" grids are grids that have been optimized to use the minimal number of points required to achieve a given level of accuracy). The ultrafine grid is recommended for molecules containing lots of tetrahedral centers and for computing very low frequency modes of systems. This grid is also useful for optimizations of larger molecules with many soft modes such as methyl rotations, making such optimizations more reliable.

We used the revised version of our home made FORTRAN code named Tempo $^{51}$ for quickly accessing thermodynamic parameters of the various clusters investigated at any temperature and to derive the temperature effects on the relative stability (relative population) of the various isomers of a cluster of size $n$. For a given temperature, their relative stabilities are evaluated through

their canonical probabilities defined as,

$$
P_{n}^{(k)}(T)=\frac{\exp \left(-\beta G_{n}^{(k)}(T)\right)}{\sum_{k} \exp \left(-\beta G_{n}^{(k)}(T)\right)} \tag{1}
$$

where, $\beta=k_{B} T$, $k_{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant, $G_{n}^{(k)}(T)$ is the free energy of the $k$th isomer of size $n$ at the temperature $T$ as calculated by the program **Tempo**. It is worth reminding that the canonical assumption is not valid at low temperatures, since quantum effects become significant in these temperatures range. Moreover, on the basis of our previous work on the thermodynamics of light particles in gas phase, $^{62,63}$ the probability distribution of various isomers of protonated ammonia clusters could be strongly contaminated by quantum effects at cryogenic temperatures in regard of their masses, especially for $T<25 \mathrm{~K}$. Therefore, the temperature-dependent relative stability is discussed here for $T \geq 25 \mathrm{~K}$.

The IR harmonic spectra for the spectroscopic study of protonated ammonia clusters have been calculated at the M06-2X/6-31++g(d,p) level of computation. We used the gaussian profiles with an inhomogeneous bandwidth for the spectral absorption peaks calculated. Thus, The relative intensities of these spectral peaks were calculated as

$$
I(\omega)=\sum_{k} \frac{A_{k}}{\sigma_{k} \sqrt{\pi}} \exp \left[-\frac{\left(\omega-\omega_{k}\right)^{2}}{2 \sigma_{k}^{2}}\right] \tag{2}
$$

where the index $k$ refers to the $k$th vibrational peak in the spectrum. $\omega_{k}$ is the origin, $A_{k}$ is the integral intensity, $\sigma_{k}$ is the dispersion linked to the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) $\Gamma_{G}$ by the relation $\Gamma_{G}=2 \sigma \sqrt{2 \ln 2}$. $\omega$ is any frequency in or outside the absorption bands.

In order to match theoretical spectra with experimental ones, we calculated the scaling factor to be applied to harmonic frequencies $(\omega_{i}^{h})$. An optimal scaling factor, $\lambda$ can be found by using the least square minimizing the residual $(\Delta)$ which is defined as

$$
\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\lambda \omega_{i}^{h}-v_{i}^{e x p}\right)^{2} \tag{3}
$$

$v_{i}^{exp}$ stands for the $i$th experimental frequency corresponding to the harmonic calculated $\omega_{i}^{h}$ frequency. $N$ is the number of frequencies investigated.

The scaling factor that minimizes the aforementioned residual is then defined by

$$
\lambda=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_{i}^{h} v_{i}^{e x p}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\omega_{i}^{h}\right)^{2}} \tag{4}
$$

How well this factor improves the harmonic frequencies is assessed by evaluating the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the scaled frequency relative to the experimental one:

$$
R M S E=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\lambda \omega_{i}^{h}-v_{i}^{e x p}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{N}} \tag{5}
$$

The uncertainty, $\sigma(\lambda)$, associated with the scaling factor $\lambda$ is a critical measure of the confidence and applicability of the scaling scheme, as shown by Irikura and co-workers. $^{64-66}$ It is defined as

$$
\sigma(\lambda)=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\omega_{i}^{h}\right)^{2}}}. \tag{6}
$$

It is worth mentioning that anharmonicities may provoke line shifts and merging with increasing the temperature. $^{67-69}$ So, further investigations of IR spectra using ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations should be undertaken based on the present work as a starting point. Elsewhere, we compare our theoretical IR spectra with the IRVPD one. However, note that the IRVPD spectra may be different from our theoretical photoabsorption spectra, due to various laser fluence along the time, or incomplete intravibrational relaxation during the observation time. All energies used and reported in this work are zero point vibrational corrected.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

We start this section presenting and discussing structures and relative stability of all the various isomers of $H^{+}(NH_{3})_{n=2-9}$. We also studied the infrared spectroscopy of these clusters type. A similar approach as presented for hydrated sodium clusters $^{44}$ is discussed here for the first time in the case of protonated ammonia clusters. The section is closed by the calculated binding energies of the protonated ammonia clusters investigated.

The following denominations $\boldsymbol{Ln}$, $\boldsymbol{BLn}$, $\boldsymbol{Cn}$, $\boldsymbol{BCn}$, $\boldsymbol{DCn}$, $\boldsymbol{TCn}$, $\boldsymbol{BTCn}$, $\boldsymbol{BDCn}$ and $\boldsymbol{Can}$ are used for linear, branched linear, cyclic, branched cyclic, double cyclic, triple cyclic, branched triple cyclic, branched double cyclic and cage conformations of a cluster of size $n$. An integer subscript is added when several non degenerated isomers of the same type are reported. Eg. $\boldsymbol{BLn\_i}$, $i \in \{1,2,\cdots\}$.

### Structures and relative stability

Different isomers of protonated ammonia clusters from dimer to hexamer are reported on Figures 1-4. Relative electronic energies of these isomers are also reported on those figures as well as their corresponding point groups when different from $C_{1}$.

#### The Dimer.
Our calculations show that the protonated ammonia dimer presents a unique Eigen cation $^{70}$ (the excess proton is not equally shared by both ammonia molecule and it is bounded to one of the ammonia molecules to form the ammonium core) structure with a $C_{3v}$ symmetry. The same results were reported by Wang et al. $^{33}$, using B3LYP and MP2 levels with the basis set 6-31+G*. This result contrast with experimental works $^{28,38}$ which also reported a unique structure, but rather with a $D_{3d}$ symmetry where the excess proton is shared equally between two ammonia molecules: an Eigen structure $^{70}$ analogously to the protonated water structures. The explanation is clear. Asmis et al. $^{38}$ showed that the

### 3.1.2 The Trimer.
For the trimer, we found a linear structure **L3** with a $C_{2v}$ symmetry as the unique possible isomer with two ammonia molecules surrounding the ammonium ion core $NH_{4}^{+}$, in the first solvation shell, Fig. 1. All attempts for forming other isomers always converge to the **L3** structure. This result supports previous calculations $^{33,35}$ and experiments. $^{29,36,37}$ Note that Fouqueau and Meuwly, $^{35}$ reported that the structure of $H^{+}(NH_{3})_{3}$ with one ammonia molecule in the first solvation shell and one ammonia molecule in the second solvation shell is unstable. The same trend have been reported for the protonated water trimer $^{71-73}$ and the protonated methanol trimer, $^{17,74,75}$ where the proton prefers to be located on the central molecule.

### 3.1.3 The Tetramer.
**Structure.** For the tetramer, we found three possible isomers **BL4, L4** and **C4** with $C_{3v}$, $C_{s}$ and $C_{s}$ symmetries, respectively, Fig. 1. The most stable is **BL4**. This isomer has also been reported previously as the most stable one by Wang et al. $^{33}$ and Fouqueau and Meuwly $^{35}$, using B3LYP/6-31+G(d) and MP2/6-31+G(d) levels of theory with BSSE correction. In this isomer, the ammonium ion core is surrounded by three ammonia molecules, all in the first solvation shell. The second most stable isomer, **L4** lies by 4.2 kcal/mol above the most stable and belongs to $C_{s}$ point group. **L4** has two ammonia molecules in the first solvation shell and one $NH_{3}$ in the second solvation. Although **BL4** and **L4** have the same number of hydrogen bonds, those of **BL4** are ionic and shorter than those of **L4**. Furthermore, we noted that for small cluster size, the higher the number of ionic bonds in an isomer, the higher the stability of that isomer. This is because ionic bonds are more stronger than hydrogen bonds. The less stable isomer **C4** (Fig. 1) lies by 5.6 kcal/mol above **BL4** and is reported here for the first time. Elsewhere, our calculated canonical probabilities show that isomer **BL4** dominates exclusively the cluster population of $NH_{4}^{+}(NH_{3})_{3}$ by a 100 % probability at all temperatures.

**Spectroscopy.**
Three NH bands (PA4, PB4 and PC4) were observed in the $NH_{4}^{+}(NH_{3})_{3}$ mid-infrared (MIR) photodissociation spectrum of Tono et al. $^{37}$, see Fig.2 These bands are observed at 1157, 1491 and $1624 cm^{-1}$ and correspond to a symmetric bending of free ammonia molecule at the first solvation shell, a bending mode of ammonium core and an asymmetric bending of free ammonia molecule at the first solvation shell, respectively. These assignments are in excellent agreement with the calculated IR spectrum of the **BL4** isomer of $NH_{4}^{+}(NH_{3})_{3}$ as proposed by Tono et al. $^{37}$ who did not find any evidence of coexistence of isomers **BL4** and **L4**. Moreover, the IR spectra of the other isomers of the protonated ammonia tetramer present several peaks with no correspondence with the experimental peaks of the cluster of the protonated ammonia tetramer, see Fig.S1 in the supporting information (SI). This indicates that the **BL4** isomer dominates exclusively the population of the protonated ammonia pentamer. As our calculated canonical probabilities show that the **BL4** isomer is favored with a 100 % of probability at all temperatures, we can conclude that the experimental IR spectrum of Tono et al. $^{37}$ is under thermodynamic equilibrium.

### 3.1.4 The Pentamer.
**Structure.** As for the pentamer, there are eight different local minima on its PES. The most stable isomer of protonated ammonia pentamer is the branched linear, **BL5_1** isomer. In this isomer, four ammonia molecules surround the ammonium ion, and the first solvation shell is completed. In fact, Lee and coworkers $^{28,76}$ demonstrated from the IR spectroscopy that the first solvation shell of the ammonium ion core is completed at $n=5$ to form $NH_{4}^{+}(NH_{3})_{4}$. The **BL5_1** isomer belongs to $T_{d}$ point group. The second most stable isomer is a branched linear with an incomplete first solvation shell, **BL5_2**; which lies by 3.7 kcal/mol above the global minimum. We noted a jump in energy when the number of ammonia molecules surrounding the ammonium ion core changes. #### 3.1.5 The Hexamer.
Structure. For this cluster size, we reported two degenerates most stable structures, BL6_1 and BC6_1, see Fig.4. Both isomers belong to the $C_{s}$ point group symmetry. As for the case of pentamer, the second most stable isomer, BL6_2, exhibits a jump in energy due to the incompleteness of the first solvation shell; it lies by $3.1\mathrm{kcal}/\mathrm{mol}$ above the most stable and belongs also to the $C_{s}$ point group symmetry. Another jump in energy is observed from Cs6_2 to BC6_6, where the number of ammonia molecules in the first solvation shell decreases. Although Fouqueau and Meuwly $^{35}$ reported only branched linear isomers, their reported relative energies show the jump in energy when the number of ammonia molecules in the first solvation shell decreases. Note that Wang et al. $^{33}$, reported only the BL6_1 isomer. At the MP2 level of theory, we noted that BL6_1 is more stable than BC6_1 by about $0.2\mathrm{kcal}/\mathrm{mol}$. These isomers are degenerates. The difference in energy is so small that one or another could be predicted as the most stable at a given level of theory. This result shows that the isomers BL6_1 and BC6_1 are degenerates at both MP2 and M06-2X levels of theory.

Structures of protonated ammonia hexamer and larger size clusters do not exhibit the same trend like those of protonated water clusters. The presence of zundel and eigen types in the later clusters and the difference in coordination numbers provide different morphologies. ### 3.2 Hydrogen bond network of protonated ammonia

The stability of protonated ammonia clusters is highly related to its hydrogen bond network. The hydrogen bond network of a given protonated ammonia cluster is defined by the coordination type of ammonia molecules in that cluster. Several coordination types have been found in the protonated ammonia clusters: **A**, **D**, **AA**, **AD**, **AAD**, **AADD**, **AAA**, **ADD** (where **A** stands for proton acceptor while **D** stands for proton donor). First, we have to note that the coordination type of ammonia influences only the stability of isomers which have the same number of ammonia molecules in their first solvation shell. In fact, we stated in the previous section that the higher the number of ammonia molecules in the first solvation shell of a cluster, the higher its stability. This is because ionic bonds are stronger than hydrogen bonds. Thus, in this study, we consider $NH_4^+$ as an ionic core which cannot be a proton donor. With this in mind, all ammonia molecules in the first solvation shell can only be proton donors. So, this discussion concerns only ammonia molecules in the second and third solvation shell.

Consider, two ammonia molecules, $a_1$ and $a_2$, respectively in the first and second solvation shell. $a_1$ and $a_2$ are linked by a hydrogen bond. We note $\Delta E_{am}(p)$ the total energy (with ZPE) of the hydrogen bond between $a_1$ and $a_2$, when $a_1$ and $a_2$ are not involved in another hydrogen bond and $CN = p$. Besides, the binding energy due to a **T**-type ammonia molecule ($\mathbf{T \equiv AA}$, **AD, AAD, AADD**) in the second solvation shell is $\Delta E_T(p)$, when $CN = p$. For example, $\Delta E_{AA}(2)$ is the energy due to an AA-type ammonia molecule in a cluster with $CN=2$. Furthermore, we note $\Delta E_{p \to p-1}$, the energy necessary to break an ionic bond in a cluster when $CN = p$. Other ammonia hydrogen bond types described in this work are **AD** and **Ad**. The latter is used for the cyclic part of the cluster. We also noted by $\Delta E_A(p)$ the interaction energy of an acceptor ammonia molecule in the second solvation shell with its donor being at least a double donor and $CN = p$.

Using the aforementioned notations, we can establish a stability rule for relative energies of various isomers of protonated ammonia clusters. The rule should be predictable and the relative energies of various isomers for a given cluster could be derived without geometry optimization or frequencies calculations. Therefore, we use the relative energies of some isomers for small cluster sizes $(n \leq 6)$.

Isomer **L3** can be formed from **BL4** cleaving an ammonia molecule surrounding the ammonium ion core ($\Delta E_{3 \to 2}$). It can also be formed from isomer **L4**, cleaving the ammonia molecule in the second shell ($\Delta E_{am}(2)$). From isomer **C4**, it is formed cleaving the **AA** ammonia bond ($\Delta E_{AA}(2)$). Therefore, we can derive the following relations:

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta E_{L4} + \Delta E_{am}(2) &= \Delta E_{C4} + \Delta E_{AA}(2), \tag{7} \\
\Delta E_{L4} + \Delta E_{am}(2) &= \Delta E_{BL4} + \Delta E_{3 \to 2}. \tag{8}
\end{align}
$$

Where $\Delta E_{L4}$, $\Delta E_{C4}$ and $\Delta E_{BL4}$ are the relative energies of isomers **L4, C4** and **BL4** respectively as reported on figure 1. Thus we obtain, two equations with three parameters: $\Delta E_{am}(2)$, $\Delta E_{AA}(2)$ and $\Delta E_{3 \to 2}$. The $\Delta E_{3 \to 2}$ can be found using the energy difference between isomers **BL4, L3** and the ammonia monomer:

$$
\Delta E_{3 \to 2} = E(BL4) - E(L3) - E(NH_3). \tag{9}
$$

Solving equations (7), (8) and (9) yields: $\Delta E_{3 \to 2} = 14.8\mathrm{kcal/mol}$, $\Delta E_{am}(2) = 10.6\mathrm{kcal/mol}$ and $\Delta E_{AA}(2) = 9.2\mathrm{kcal/mol}$.

The other relations derived below are obtained similarly considering suitable isomers. All the equations are written using the fact that we can move from the isomer at the left of equations to the isomer at the right of equations (and *vice versa*) by breaking or constructing the appropriates bonds (That is by providing an appropriate energy to the isomer). However, we note that for two given isomers, we can break some appropriate bonds in each isomer to form a unique system. For example, the equation (7) means that when we provide $\Delta E_{am}(2)$ to the isomer **L4** and $\Delta E_{AA}(2)$ to the isomer **C4**, we have the same system.

Thus, we can write down the following equations using all the isomers of protonated ammonia pentamer, except **BC5_2**:

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta E_{BL5\_1} + \Delta E_{4 \to 3} &= \Delta E_{BL5\_2} + \Delta E_{am}(3) \tag{10} \\
\Delta E_{BL5\_2} + \Delta E_{am}(3) &= \Delta E_{BC5\_1} + \Delta E_{AA}(3) \tag{11} \\
\Delta E_{L5} + 2\Delta E_{am}(2) &= \Delta E_{BL5\_1} + \Delta E_{AA}(3) + \Delta E_{3 \to 2} \tag{12} \\
\Delta E_{L5} + 2\Delta E_{am}(2) &= \Delta E_{BC5\_3} + \Delta E_{AAD}(2) \tag{13} \\
\Delta E_{C5} + \Delta E_{Ad}(2) + \Delta E_{AA}(2) &= \Delta E_{L5} + 2\Delta E_{am}(2). \tag{14}
\end{align}
$$

### 3.3 Binding energies of protonated ammonia clusters

As we showed that clusters of protonated ammonia have an Eigen structure whatever the cluster size, the binding energy of $H^+(NH_3)_n$ is determined following the path:

$$
NH_4^+ + (n-1)NH_3 \longrightarrow H^+(NH_3)_n. \tag{25}
$$

Thus, if $E_n$ is the energy of the protonated ammonia cluster $(H^+(NH_3)_n)$ of size $n$, $E_1$ the energy of the ammonium ion core $(NH_4^+)$ and $E(NH_3)$ the energy of the ammonia monomer $(NH_3)$, the per ammonia binding energy of $H^+(NH_3)_n$ is determined as,

$$
\Delta E_n = \frac{E_n - (n-1)E(NH_3) - E_1}{n-1}. \tag{26}
$$

Aiming to evaluate binding energies of protonated ammonia clusters, we take their zero-point corrected global minimum energy for each cluster size at both M06-2X/6-31++g(d,p) and MP2/6-31++g(d,p) levels of theory. BSSE corrections are also taken into account. Results are reported in the Fig.16 and detailed values can be found in the SI. Our results show similar trends with those of previous authors when available. $^{33,35}$

799 Several new significant isomers of protonated ammonia clusters are proposed here for the first time. It is noteworthy that protonated ammonia clusters are all Eigen structures and the first solvation shell of the ammonium ion core is saturated by four ammonia molecules at least for cluster size $n=2-9$. Thus, there is no possible Zundel structure in the population of protonated ammonia clusters at least for $n=2-9$, contrary to the case of protonated water and protonated methanol clusters. The relative stability of isomers of protonated ammonia clusters depends on the number of ammonia molecules in the inner shells. The higher the number of ammonia molecules in the first solvation shells, the higher the stability of the isomer. Among isomers with the same number of ammonia molecules in their first solvation shell, the stability depends on the strength of bonds and the symmetry of the isomer.

Our investigations also show that the structures of protonated ammonia clusters are not only branched linear as assumed by all previous authors and entropic effects dictate the isomeric distribution. Finally, branched linear structures are the only responsible of the population of protonated ammonia clusters for $n=4-6$ at any temperature. However, for larger cluster sizes, this type of structures compete with branched cyclic, double cyclic, branched double cyclic and triple cyclic structures depending on the temperature.

Furthermore, our stability rule established can be extrapolated to other systems that contain a central ion and solvated by molecules forming a hydrogen bond network. Based on isomers of small cluster sizes, the stability rule can easily be established as presented in the text. Once the stability rule is established for small cluster sizes, it can be used to predict the relative stabilities of isomers of higher cluster sizes with a good accuracy, without any supplementary computations such as geometry optimizations or frequencies calculations.
