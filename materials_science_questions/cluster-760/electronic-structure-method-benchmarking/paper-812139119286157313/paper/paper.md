**Carbon monoxide adsorption on alkali and proton-exchanged chabazite: an *ab-initio* periodic study using the CRYSTAL code**

P. UGLIENGO*\dagger, C. BUSCO‡, B. CIVALLERI\dagger and C. M. ZICOVICH-WILSON§

\daggerDipartimento di Chimica IFM and NIS – Nanostructured Interfaces and Surfaces – Centre of Excellence, Università di Torino, Via P. Giuria, 7. 10125 Torino, Italy
‡Dipartimento DiSCAFF, Università del Piemonte Orientale, “A. Avogadro”, Via Bovio, 6. 28100 Novara, Italy

(Received 22 April 2004; accepted 31 August 2004)

*Ab initio* periodic calculations based on local Gaussian basis sets as coded in the CRYSTAL program have been performed to investigate the structure, the binding energy and the vibrational features of carbon monoxide adsorbed on $H^+$, $Li^+$, $Na^+$ and $K^+$-exchanged chabazite (Si/Al=11/1, i.e. one Al atom per unit cell). The hybrid B3LYP functional has been adopted for all calculations with a polarized double-zeta quality basis set. The B3LYP binding energies (BSSE corrected) are 16.0, 24.6, 20.4 and 5.1 kJ/mol for $H^+$, $Li^+$, $Na^+$ and $K^+$-exchanged chabazite, respectively. Corresponding CO hypsochromic stretching frequency shifts are 47, 68, 43 and $33\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, respectively. Comparison with the case of CO interacting with bare alkali cations and the available experimental data on a variety of zeolites has also been addressed.

### 1. Introduction

Chabazite is a natural aluminosilicate zeolite whose framework is made of hexagonal prisms connected by 4-membered ring units to give a 3-dimensional channel system. This produces pores made from 8-membered rings of $TO_4$ units (T=Si, Al). An all-silica chabazite has been recently synthesized [1] which has, however, little chemical relevance being unable to interact with molecules in a reactive fashion acting just as a molecular sieve [2]. Substitution of some Si atoms by Al may indeed transform an all-silica zeolite in a material which can engage chemical interactions with the guest molecules. This is because charge compensating ions have to be introduced in the structure in order to balance the negative charge due to the Al ions. In that respect, a search in the inorganic structural data base [3] reveals 55 structures based on the CHA framework, all containing Al atom as a substitute to the framework silicon and, as counterions, $H^+$ and the whole variety of alkali and alkaline-earth cations. When proton acts as a counterion, it imparts strong Brønsted acidity to the zeolite which becomes important as a material for the conversion of methanol to gasoline [4]. The alkali and alkaline-earth cations can effectively act as Lewis sites entrapping molecules travelling within the channels. This is the basic reason why cation-containing zeolites are useful in gas separation processes: for instance, the higher affinity of $CO_2$ for cations in comparison with $CH_4$, $N_2$ and $O_2$ allows its separation from the gas mixture [5]. Previous work [6] has dealt with cation selectivity in alkali-exchanged chabazite, using *ab initio* periodic methods based on local Gaussian basis sets as encoded in the CRYSTAL computer code [7]. The Hartree–Fock Hamiltonian has been adopted to predict the most stable location of the cations ($H^+$, $Li^+$, $Na^+$ and $K^+$) in a 11/1 Si/Al chabazite. Using those results as a starting point, the DFT hamiltonian with the hybrid B3LYP functional has been adopted here to study the interaction of carbon monoxide with the extraframework cations. Carbon monoxide, CO, is widely used as a molecular probe because its vibrational frequency is very sensitive to electric fields. Furthermore, CO is chemically inert ensuring that no complex reactions will ever occur as a consequence of adsorption. Experimental infrared spectra of CO adsorbed on a variety of proton and alkali-exchanged zeolites have been reported to date [8–15]. The main result is that, irrespective of the nature of the counterion, the CO frequency undergoes a hypsochromic shift, the amount being a function of the intensity of the electric field in

## 2. Computational details

### 2.1. Structural models

In the all-silica chabazite, only one crystallographic
independent $TO_4$ unit is present which generates the
twelve equivalent tetrahedral units. Following previous
work [6] a Si/Al=11/1 chabazite is generated by
replacing one of the $SiO_4$ unit by $AlO_4$, which
corresponds to one Al per unit cell and reduction of
the symmetry from $R\overline{3}m$ of the pure silica chabazite to
$P1$ symmetry. The location of the counterions is a
delicate problem, which has already been dealt with in
previous work [6] so that here the most stable structures,
as a function of the counterion, will be adopted. By
referring to figure 1 and following results and notation
from [6], the cation location has been set as follows:
both $Li^{+}$ and $Na^{+}$ sit at position SII, whereas position
SIII' is the one adopted by both $H^{+}$ and $K^{+}$ ions. For
$H^{+}$, position SIII' corresponds to protonation at the
oxygen framework O1. In the present work, we have
kept the unit cell parameters fixed at those optimized at
B3LYP level (with the same basis set adopted here)
computed for an all-silica chabazite [24] within the
rhombohedral lattice ($a=9.36$ and $\alpha=94.67$, space

### 2.2. Methods

For the present calculations, the most recent version of
the CRYSTAL program was used [7], in which the
forces on the atoms have been obtained by using the
recent implementation of the analytical energy gradients
at both HF and DFT levels [27, 28]; gradients have been
used to relax the starting atomic positions toward
the equilibrium structure, using a modified conjugate
gradient algorithm as proposed by Schlegel [29]. In the
present work, a unitary matrix has always been used as
the initial guess for the Hessian matrix, and geometry
optimization has been performed in cartesian coordi-
nates. Convergence is tested on the root-mean-square
(RMS) and on the absolute value of the largest
component of both the gradients and the estimated
nuclear displacements. The threshold for the maximum
and the RMS forces and the maximum and the RMS
atomic displacements on all the atoms have been set to
(in a.u.) 0.00045, 0.00030 and 0.00180, 0.00120, respec-
tively. The optimization is considered complete when
the four conditions are simultaneously satisfied.

The actual implementation of the automated geometry optimization in CRYSTAL has been recently described [30], showing that geometry optimization is now feasible for molecules, polymers, slabs, and crystals. The B3LYP, is the Becke's three-parameters hybrid exchange func- tional [31] in combination with the gradient-corrected correlation functional of Lee, Yang and Parr [32]. B3LYP has been shown to provide excellent results, when compared to other functionals, for atomization energies, equilibrium geometries and vibrational fre- quencies [33]. This level of calculation has been success- fully adopted by some of us to study the relative stability and the structural features of a set of dense [34] and microporous all-silica materials [35]. In the CRYSTAL code, the crystalline orbitals are expanded in term of a gaussian type basis set; for the present case, the 31G(p),6-21G(d), 6-31G(d), and 85-11G(d) contractions have been adopted for H, Si, O, C and Al, respectively; the exponents (in bohr $^{-2}$ units) of the most diffuse $s p$ shellsare 0.16 (H), 0.13 (Si), 0.27 (O), 0.17 (C) and 0.28 (Al); the exponent of the polarization functions ( $p$ on $H$ and d on Si, O, C and Al) are 1.1 (H), 0.5 (Si), 0.6 (O of theCHA framework), 0.8 (C and O of CO) and 0.47(Al); d shells are represented by five functions. For the alkali metal ions the following basis sets have been used: 6-1G for Li, 8-511G for Na and 86-511G for K [36]. The number of reciprocal lattice points ( $k$ -points) at which the Hamiltonian matrix has been diagonalized is 8, corresponding to a shrinking [37] factor $S=2$ . Default tolerances of (6,6,6,6,12) which control the Coulomb and exchange series have been adopted. For DFT, the exchange-correlation functional is integrated numerically on a grid of points. Integration over radial and angular coordinates is performed through Gauss-Legendre and Lebedev schemes, respectively.A pruned grid consisting of 55 radial points and 10 sub-intervals with (1,2,5,8,11,13,11,8,5,1) angularpoints has been used for all calculations, resulting in atotal number of integration points larger than 250000 per unit cell. Typical calculations consisted of more than550 basis functions so that they have all been run using the direct SCF technique for the two-electron integrals.Geometry optimizations (114 degrees of freedom) required from 5-7 days of CPU time on a single Linux box equipped with a 1.6 Ghz Athlon processor and512 Mb of memory.

In a periodic system and within the harmonic approximation the phonon frequencies at the $\Gamma$ pointare computed by diagonalizing the central zone $(k=0)$ mass weighted Hessian matrix:

$$
W_{i j}(k=0)=\sum_{G} \frac{H_{i j}^{0 G}}{\sqrt{M_{i} M_{j}}}
$$

where $H_{i j}^{0 G}$ is the second derivative of the electronic + nuclear repulsion energy $E$ evaluated at equilibrium $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ with respect to the displacement $u_{i}=x_{i}-x_{i}^{\ddagger}$ of atom $A$ in cell 0 and displacement $u_{j}=x_{j}-x_{j}^{\ddagger}$ of atom $B$ in cell $G$ from their equilibrium positions $x_{i}^{\ddagger}, x_{j}^{\ddagger}$ :

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{G} H_{i j}^{0 G}= & \sum_{G}\left[\frac{\partial^{2} E}{\partial u_{i}^{0} \partial u_{j}^{G}}\right]_{0} \quad i=1, \ldots, 3 N ; \\
& j=1, \ldots, 3 N.
\end{aligned}
$$

As the analytical energy first derivatives $\Phi_{j}$ of $E$ with respect to the atomic displacements are available[28, 38]:

$$
\Phi_{j}=\sum_{G} v_{j}^{G}=\sum_{G} \frac{\partial E}{\partial u_{j}^{G}} \quad j=1, \ldots, 3 N,
$$

the Hessian matrix is computed by the numerical differentiation of $\Phi_{j}$ using either the central-differenceformula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\left[\frac{\partial \Phi_{j}}{\partial u_{i}^{0}}\right]_{0} } & \approx \frac{\Phi_{j}\left(0, \ldots, u_{i}^{0}, \ldots, 0\right)-\Phi_{j}\left(0, \ldots,-u_{i}^{0}, \ldots, 0\right)}{2 u_{i}^{0}} \\
i & =1, \ldots, 3 N ; \quad j=1, \ldots, 3 N,
\end{aligned}
$$

or the less computationally demanding and less accuratedifference quotient formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\left[\frac{\partial \Phi_{j}}{\partial u_{i}^{0}}\right]_{0} } & \approx \frac{\Phi_{j}\left(0, \ldots, u_{i}^{0}, \ldots, 0\right)-\Phi_{j}(0, \ldots, 0, \ldots, 0)}{u_{i}^{0}} \\
i & =1, \ldots, 3 N ; \quad j=1, \ldots, 3 N.
\end{aligned}
$$

For a true stationary point $\Phi_{j}(0,..., 0,..., 0)=0$  j = 1,...,3N. However, in practical calculations, the minimum configuration is reached once $\Phi_{j}(0,..., 0,..., 0) ≤\varepsilon$ (usually $\varepsilon=0.00045$ atomic unit) so that, to increase the numerically stability of the difference quotient, this term is not disregarded.

In this paper a step $u_{i}=0.001 \AA$ has been adopted. More details on this topic can be found in reference [39].

The Hessian matrix eigenvalues provide the normal mode harmonic frequencies. As already anticipated in the introduction, building the full Hessian matrix can be very expensive when dealing with large unit cells, since N atoms in the unit cell implies performing 6N+1 SCF and gradient calculations (only $3 N+1$ when a difference quotient instead of the central-difference formula is adopted [39]). In the present case, however, we were only interested in calculating the OH (for the acidic

chabazite) and CO frequencies (for all cases), disregarding any other phonon modes. We assume, as a reasonable approximation, that both the OH and CO stretching modes are fully decoupled from the other framework normal modes, because the former vibrates at much higher frequency than any other phonon mode while the latter being only coupled with the chabazite modes by weak intermolecular forces. The result is a much smaller Hessian matrix, containing only a fragment of the whole structure which can be quickly built and diagonalized. This strategy, which has been proved recently [40] to work properly for other oxidic systems, is very useful when large and nonsymmetric unit cells are involved, and has been adopted also for the case of chabazite because of the limited computational facilities. Manipulation and visualization of structures have been dealt with the MOLDRAW program [25, 41] and molecular drawings have been rendered by the PovRay program using input files prepared by MOLDRAW.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Geometries of X-CHA and X-CHA|CO systems

Table 1 shows the total energies computed for all optimized zeolite structures and figure 2 the resulting structures. As already discussed in the structural models section, $H^{+}$ and $K^{+}$ both occupy position SIII' of the eight-membered ring whereas $Li^{+}$ and $Na^{+}$ position SII of the six-membered ring. Table 2 shows the geometrical features for all considered systems, mainly around the Al atom. For H-CHA, the interaction with CO brings the carbon atom at a distances of $1.952\mathring{A}$ from the acidic proton with a significative elongation of the O1–H bond length, in agreement with the hydrogen bond character of the interaction. The OC$\cdots$H angle is also close to $180^{\circ}$, as expected, while the CO molecular axis lyes outside the average eight-membered ring average plane by about 25 degrees (see figure 3 for details). Perturbations of the intraframework degrees of freedom around the Al atom are negligible. Inspection of a larger portion of the H–CHA/CO crystal structure shows no relevant intermolecular CO$\cdots$CO interaction (C$\cdots$C distance of about $9.4\mathring{A}$). It is interesting to compare K–CHA and K–CHA/CO with the H–CHA case. Here, the perturbation around the Al atom due to the $K^{+}$ ion is by far less important than that caused by the covalently bounded proton: the Si–O1 and Si–O8 bonds are more regular for the K–CHA and both Al–O1 and Al–O2 bonds are well below $1.8\mathring{A}$. For the H–CHA case, the Al–O1 bond (the one directly attached to the proton) is $1.9\mathring{A}$ long. All this is a clear indication of a fully ionic interaction which is also apparent by the K$\cdots$O1 distance of $2.8\mathring{A}$ and an average separation with the remaining eight-membered oxygen ring of about $3\mathring{A}$ (see table 2). As figure 2 shows, the $K^{+}$ ion is almost at the centre of the eight-membered ring, by virtue of its large ionic radius. The interaction with CO causes negligible changes in the zeolite framework and also the distances between $K^{+}$ and the oxygen of the eight-membered ring are only sligthly elongated (see table 2). At variance with the H–CHA/CO case, no room is left in the eight-membered ring to accommodate the CO molecule, which is forced to align its molecular axis in an almost perpendicular fashion with respect to the average eight-membered ring plane (see the values of C$\cdots$K–O1, C$\cdots$K–O2, C$\cdots$K–O3 and C$\cdots$K–O7 angles in table 2 and figure 3). Both $Li^{+}$ and $Na^{+}$ occupy position SII, with $Li^{+}$ being closer to the six-membered ring oxygens by about $0.5\mathring{A}$ than $Na^{+}$, as an average, in agreement with the smaller ionic radius of the former compared to the latter ($0.73\mathring{A}$ vs $1.13\mathring{A}$, respectively). Both cations are closer to O6 than to O2, with the former being the one directly bound to the Al ion and consequently more ionic. Due to their different ionic radius, $Li^{+}$ remains closer to the oxygen atoms nearby the Al atom whereas $Na^{+}$ is almost equidistant from O1 and O4, i.e. it is symmetrical solvated by the basic oxygen atoms of the zeolite framework. The structural differences between $Li^{+}$ and $Na^{+}$ exchanged chabazites are also evident in the adsorption of CO. For $Li^{+}$, the CO molecular axis is more bent towards the center of the six-membered ring than for the $Na^{+}$ chabazite (see C$\cdots$Na–O1 angles in table 2 and figure 3). The interaction with CO is attained without the need to desolvate the cations from their positions in the chabazite, as shown by the $Li^{+}\cdots O$ and $Na^{+}\cdots O$ distances which are almost unperturbed by the presence of the adsorbed CO (see table 2).

The adsorption of CO in cation exchanged chabazites is expected to be mainly of electrostatic nature. To verify if this is the case, a correlation between the sum of the carbon van der Waals radius plus the ionic radius of the cation X = Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, $R_{vdW}$(C) + $R_{ionic}$(X), and the B3LYP C$\cdots$X distance is reported in figure 5(a). For a pure electrostatic interaction, the B3LYP X$\cdots$C distance should be close to the $R_{vdW}$(C) + $R_{ionic}$(X) sum (no charge–charge penetration), resulting in points aligned on the unitary slope line, any deviation indicating the occurrence of some charge transfer. As shown in figure 5(a) and in agreement with simple chemical concepts, the deviations are largest for Li⁺, intermediate for Na⁺ and almost negligible for K⁺ showing that at least for Li⁺ some covalency is present in the bond.

A different way to address the same point is shown in figure 5(b). The B3LYP binding energies BE are correlated with those computed by means of the classical electrostatic expansion [42]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{BE}_{\mathrm{E}} &= \mu_{z}F + \frac{1}{2}\Theta_{zz}\nabla F + \frac{1}{6}\Omega_{zzz}\nabla^{2}F + \frac{1}{24}\Phi_{zzzz}\nabla^{3}F \\
&+ \cdots + \frac{1}{2}\alpha_{zz}F^{2} + \cdots
\end{aligned}
$$

in which $F$ is the electric field and its gradients, computed for the bare X-CHA (X = H⁺, Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) chabazite at the position of space which will be occupied by the CO centre of masses in the corresponding X-CHA/CO system, $\mu_{z}$, $\Theta_{zz}$, $\Omega_{zzz}$, $\Phi_{zzzz}$ being the electric multipoles and $\alpha_{zz}$ the polarizability of the free CO. The field $F$ and its derivatives has been computed by numerical differentiation of the electrostatic potential $V$ evaluated along the direction of the CO bond. ### 3.3. Comparison with vibrational and calorimetric experimental data

As already described in the introduction, no experi- mental data are available for the X-CHA/CO system. In the H-CHA/CO case, the H-bond between the Brønsted OH group and the CO molecule causes a definite batho- chromic shift of the OH stretching frequency which is one of the most common features of all H-bonded systems. Table 5 compares the computed B3LYP Δν(CO) and the Δν(OH) shifts of the H-CHA/CO system with the corresponding literature values mea- sured for a variety of acidic zeolites. It appears that both the computed Δν(CO) and Δν(OH) for H-CHA/CO are larger than the values of $32 \pm 2\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $303 \pm 18\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ resulting from averaging all data measured for different zeolites. A possible reason for that may be a too small basis set, which gives a BSSE so large (see table 3) to cause a general overestimation of the frequency shift because the system is overbound. Improving the flexibility of the basis set is not straightforward for a periodic calculation because: (i) diffuse functions may easily cause linear dependency in the basis set; (ii) larger basis set (triple zeta quality) may dramatically increase the cost of the calculation.

The MFI framework consists of straight channels (direction [010]) and intercrossing sinusoidal channels (direction [100]) [43]. In the orthorhombic form, there are 12 crystallographically distinct tetrahedral atom positions [44]. From previous studies based on shell-ion model potential calculations [45, 46] the most stable location of the Al atom is at the crystallographic position T7. Recent computational work based on the QM-Pot method [47] has also shown that the most stable location for the acidic proton is at oxygen O7 attached to the Al atom. Unfortunately there is neither experimental information about preferred protonation sites in H-MFI zeolite nor about preferred positions of aluminium atoms in the framework and, as a consequence, nothing is known about the configuration assumed by the adsorbed CO in H-MFI zeolite. A similar situation also occurs for the alkali-cation exchanged MFI zeolite. By measuring the heat of adsorption of CO in the all-silica MFI zeolite, Gorte and coworkers [48, 49] were able to estimate the dispersive contribution $\Delta\text{H}_{\text{a}}^{\text{disp}}$ to the heat of adsorption $\Delta\text{H}_{\text{a}}$ (see table 6). Because the adopted B3LYP Hamiltonian is unable to cope with the dispersion interaction [50, 51] our $-\text{BE}^{\text{C}}$ (see table 3) is compared with the $\Delta\text{H}_{\text{a}}^{\text{X/CO}}=\Delta\text{H}_{\text{a}}-\Delta\text{H}_{\text{a}}^{\text{disp}}$ value, representing the net contribution to the heat of adsorption purified by the framework contribution. Comparison with our computed data from table 3 shows a general agreement between the two dataset but for the K–CHA case, with the computed datum being definitely too low. It should be stressed, however, that our $-\text{BE}^{\text{C}}$ should also be corrected for zero point and thermal corrections to be properly compared with experimental data. Such corrections (not carried out here because we did not compute the whole set of frequencies for the X-CHA/CO systems) are expected from previous experience [52] to decrease the $\text{BE}^{\text{c}}$ values by about 25% bringing the computed $-\text{BE}^{\text{C}}$ and the measured $\Delta\text{H}_{\text{a}}^{\text{X/CO}}$ values in better agreement with each other. Data of table 6 concerning the $\Delta\nu(\text{CO})$ shift have been contrasted with the corresponding B3LYP $\Delta\nu(\text{CO})$ values computed for the X-CHA/CO systems (data from table 3). Figure 6 shows such a correlation. It results that all computed $\Delta\nu(\text{CO})$ shifts for X-CHA/CO are larger than those measured on the corresponding X–MFI/CO zeolites. We believe that the same reason illustrated for the H–CHA/CO system also holds here, i.e. a large BSSE which biases the computed vibrational data. One should however consider that the comparison is not so straightforward because the computed data are for CHA framework whereas the measured values are for the MFI framework.

## 4. Conclusions
In the present work the ab initio B3LYP approach combined with the use of a Gaussian basis set of double-zeta plus polarization quality has been used to study the X-CHA, $\text{X}=\text{H}^{+}$, $\text{Li}^{+}$, $\text{Na}^{+}$, $\text{K}^{+}$ system both free and when interacting with CO. The CRYSTAL code has been used to compute structures, CO adsorption energies, and the perturbation of the CO vibrational frequency caused by the adsorption in X-CHA. The main conclusions are: (i) The BSSE corrected CO binding energies are: 16 (H), 25 ($\text{Li}^{+}$), 20 ($\text{Na}^{+}$) and 5 ($\text{K}^{+}$) $\text{kJ/mol}$, respectively. (ii) BSSE is very large and due to non-bonded contacts of CO with the chabazite framework atoms. (iii) The CO adsorption is characterized by electrostatic forces as shown by the correlation with data derived from classical electrostatics and by the comparison with the case of CO adsorbed on bare cations. (iv) The B3LYP $\Delta\nu(\text{CO})$ shift is larger for CO adsorption on X-CHA with respect to data measured on the X–MFI zeolites as it is the $\Delta\nu(\text{OH})$ shift for the H–CHA/CO case when compared to experimental values measured on a variety of zeolites; the reason has been tracked down to some deficiencies of the basis set which, in turn, cause a large BSSE giving computed CO and OH frequency shifts which are too large on an absolute scale.
