## HIGHLIGHTS

- Probing the structure of bicyclo[2.2.2]octa-2,5-dione (BCOD).
- Electron momentum spectroscopy experiments and quantum chemistry.
- Insights into bicyclo[2.2.2]octa-2,5-dione by imaging molecular orbitals.
- $\pi/\sigma$ through-bond interactions of strained bridged cyclic diketones.

## ABSTRACT

Understanding the structure of strained hydrocarbons is important for understanding bonding and controlling reactivity and selectivity. Here we report an experimental and theoretical investigation into the valence electronic structure of bicyclo[2.2.2]octa-2,5-dione (BCOD). A high-resolution electron momentum spectroscopy (EMS) technique was employed to investigate the momentum dependence of specific ionization channels. These experiments were supplemented by an analysis of a measured photoelectron spectrum. Computational chemistry was used to explain the complex electronic structure of BCOD. We analyzed the influence of strain in bridged cyclic diketones by comparison of BCOD with corresponding results from structurally similar molecules to elucidate $\pi/\sigma$ through-bond interactions.

## 1. Introduction

Through-bond interactions [1], where separated $\pi$-bonds interact through a rigid $\sigma$-bonded frame, provide a mechanism for understanding long range electron and energy transfer between chromophores. It is known that the distance between the separated bonds, and the conformation and configuration of the spacer species, can influence the through-bond interaction, which in turn influences the effectiveness of the electron transfer process [2–4]. Introducing strain into the $\sigma$-bonded frame can enhance its $\pi$-bonding characteristics to further modulate these interactions [5].

Understanding the structure of strained hydrocarbons is of general interest for controlling chemical reactivity and selectivity. In particular the release of strain can promote reactions without the need of a catalyst [6], and they have been explored as biorthogonal labelling agents [7]. Furthermore, the release of strain may be rapid, an essential feature for trapping chemical intermediates such as short-lived cysteine sulfenic acids [8]. The enhanced reactivity of strained compounds can often facilitate non-specific labelling of compounds to reduce the sensitivity of chemical probes [9]. Improving our knowledge about the electronic structure of strained compounds may also assist in predicting reaction pathways of strained hydrocarbons, so that highly selective biomarkers can be identified.

Since its development, electron momentum spectroscopy (EMS) has become a powerful technique for gaining insights into the valence electronic structure of molecules through its ability to image molecular orbitals [10,11]. Further, the sensitivity of the technique in probing molecular structure has assisted in assessing the quality of different quantum chemical models and the approximations upon which they rely. The validation of quantum chemical models is important, for example, in ensuring that physical and chemical properties derived from such calculations are reliable. This is enhanced by the motivation for data to efficiently screen potential molecules for technological applications, such as pharmaceutical products or advanced materials.

kinematically-complete electron impact ionization reaction,
$$e_{0}^{-}\left(E_{0}, \boldsymbol{k}_{0}\right)+T_{B C O D} \rightarrow T_{B C O D}^{+}\left(\epsilon_{i}, \boldsymbol{q}\right)+e_{1}^{-}\left(E_{1}, \boldsymbol{k}_{1}\right)+e_{2}^{-}\left(E_{2}, \boldsymbol{k}_{2}\right). \tag{1}$$

Here an electron, $e_{0}^{-}$, with well-defined energy $(E_{0})$ and momentum $(\boldsymbol{k}_{0})$, ionizes the BCOD target molecule, $(T_{B C O D})$. The scattered and ejected electrons leaving the collision region have well-defined energies $(E_{1}, E_{2})$ and momenta $(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}, \boldsymbol{k}_{2})$, where the subscripts 1 and 2 refer to the scattered (fast) and ejected (slow) electrons, respectively. Conservation of energy in the ionization process dictates that:
$$E_{0}=E_{1}+E_{2}+\epsilon_{i}, \tag{2}$$
where $\epsilon_{i}$ is the energy required to remove an electron from the BCOD target to create a molecular cation, $T_{B C O D}^{+}$. Linear momentum is also conserved during the collision,
$$\boldsymbol{k}_{0}=\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+\boldsymbol{q} \tag{3}$$
and requires that the target molecular ion formed (assuming the target is originally at rest) recoils with momentum $\boldsymbol{q}$.

Under high momentum transfer conditions, where the ejected electrons momentum is also approximately equal in magnitude to the momentum transferred to the target, $|\boldsymbol{k}_{2}| \sim|\boldsymbol{k}_{0}-\boldsymbol{k}_{1}|$, the collision can be described through a binary-encounter approximation. In this binary encounter, the remaining (N-1) electrons of the target act as spectators during the collision, and we describe the collision as being between two free electrons. In this case, the target ions recoil momentum $(\boldsymbol{q})$ is equal and opposite to the momentum of the bound electron when it was ionized $(\boldsymbol{p})$,
$$\boldsymbol{p}=-\boldsymbol{q}. \tag{4}$$

In this way, electron momentum spectroscopy investigates the momentum dependence of the bound electron when it is ejected through a specific ionization channel.

For a randomly oriented molecule, in unresolved rotational and vibrational levels, the EMS cross section is given by:
$$\sigma\left(\epsilon_{i}, \boldsymbol{q}\right)=(2 \pi)^{4} \frac{k_{1} k_{2}}{k_{0}} f_{e e} \int d \Omega\left|\psi_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right|^{2}. \tag{5}$$

Here $f_{e e}$ is the free electron-electron collision factor, while $\psi_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})$ is the momentum space representation of the one-electron Dyson orbital describing the overlap between the neutral target and the residual ion state. Within the target Hartree-Fock or Kohn-Sham Approximation, where the ionization is rapid, and the remaining N-1 electrons behave

$$

$$

Note that the spectroscopic factors must satisfy the normalisation criterion,

$$
\sum_{j}\left(S_{i}^{(f)}\right)=1. \tag{7}

Hence, electron momentum spectroscopy can be considered a technique for imaging molecular orbitals.

The electron momentum spectroscopy experiments were performed using a high resolution experimental apparatus that has been previously described in detail [22]. A hemispherical electron monochromator, housed in its own differentially pumped chamber, produces a monochromatic $(E_{0}=1500$ eV $+\epsilon_{i})$ and well-collimated beam of electrons. Here the energy resolution of the beam is ~0.3 eV. This beam is crossed with an orthogonal target beam of BCOD. The molecular beam is formed from BCOD vapour entering the collision region through a capillary array, with the sample pressure being controlled through a variable leak valve. Here the high purity sample of BCOD (> 99%) was synthesized and provided by Prof. Rolf Gleiter [21,23], and was degassed prior to use. Unfortunately, the sample was exhausted before we could complete a more extensive series of measurements. Nonetheless, the experimental results presented later were all obtained with high statistical accuracy. Here the EMS experiment is performed under symmetric non-coplanar conditions. In this configuration, both the scattered and ejected electrons are detected such that their polar angles, made with respect to the incident electron beam's direction, are equal, $\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=45^{\circ}$, and the azimuthal angle, $\phi$, between the two electrons is varied. Within the symmetric energy sharing condition, the two outgoing electrons also have the same kinetic energies $(E_{1}=E_{2}=750$ eV). Binding energy spectra at specific azimuthal angles (recoil momenta) are obtained by recording the number of true coincidence counts while ramping the incident beam energy, with the data being collected in a "binning mode" of operation [10]. Momentum distributions are obtained by deconvoluting the binding energy spectrum at each azimuthal angle. Here we use Gaussian peaks and a least-squares fitting routine to identify the contribution of each energetically resolvable ionization channel to the measured binding energy spectrum. As the BCOD sample was limited in this study, it was only possible to obtain binding energy spectra at two discrete azimuthal angles, $\phi=0^{\circ}$ and $10^{\circ}$.

We performed quantum chemical calculations to aid interpretation of the experimental results. The optimal BCOD geometry was obtained using density functional theory (DFT) employing a Becke 3-parameter Lee, Yang and Parr exchange correlation functional and DGauss triple zeta valence polarised (B3LYP/TZVP) basis set [24,25]. Calculations were performed using the Gaussian [26,27] and General Atomic and Molecular Electronic Structure System (GAMESS) [28] quantum chemistry packages.

Theoretical cross sections were subsequently calculated within the plane-wave impulse approximation (PWIA) using molecular orbital information obtained from the quantum chemical calculations. These calculations were performed using the AMOLD code of McCarthy and Weigold [29] that explicitly folds in the instrumental angular, energy and momentum resolutions to facilitate a direct comparison between experimental and theoretical data. Here the experimental momentum resolution is $\sim$0.12 a.u. Because the experimental data is relative, a normalisation to the B3LYP/TZVP calculation was applied to place the experimental data on an absolute scale. Specifically, this was achieved by integrating the experimental flux between the 8–18 eV binding energy range for the binding energy spectra measured at $\phi=0^{\circ}$ and $10^{\circ}$, and normalising this quantity to the intensity obtained by summing over the corresponding B3LYP/TZVP cross sections.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Ionization spectra

In Fig. 2 we present the BCOD photoelectron spectrum and EMS binding energy spectrum that has been summed over the data at the different azimuthal angles ($\phi=0^{\circ}+10^{\circ}$). The PES was digitized from a spectrum obtained by Prof Rolf Gleiter, together with the synthesized BCOD sample [23]. The obtained PES and EMS spectral profiles were found to be in excellent qualitative agreement regarding the location of the various spectral features. We also note that the differences between observed intensities of individual features are consistent with the variations known to occur between the different experimental techniques [10]. In Fig. 3, the individual $\phi=0^{\circ}$ and $\phi=10^{\circ}$ binding energy spectra are also presented, along with the results from our deconvolution procedure. The momentum dependence of each ionization channel is apparent by observing how the intensity of a feature changes between these individual spectra at low ($\phi=0^{\circ}$) and intermediate ($\phi=10^{\circ}$) momenta, respectively. These azimuthal angle-resolved binding energy spectra are used to generate the momentum distributions (Figs. 4 and 5), discussed in detail below.

Calculated vertical ionization energies, obtained through either a Koopman's theory or a propagator interpretation of the quantum chemical calculations, are summarized in Table 1, along with previously reported values from a third-order algebraic construction method with a correlation-consistent polarized valence double-zeta basis set (ADC(3)/cc-pVDZ) [2]. Using the information provided by the structure calculations, we identified and assigned the photoelectron and EMS binding energy spectral features, also detailed in Table 1. The present OVGF ionization energies are in excellent agreement with the dominant ADC(3) poles, exhibiting variations $\Delta E \leq 0.2$ eV. This excellent agreement allows us to confidently use these data to assign the spectroscopic features observed in the experimental PES and EMS spectra. Ionization energies at the RHF/TZVP and B3LYP/TZVP level are in rather poor agreement but are included to detail the spectroscopic assignment to the experimental features. Finally, the OVGF and ADC(3) calculations agree that the single-particle ionization picture is appropriate here for the outer-most valence orbitals, which typically having pole strengths (PS) greater than 0.8.

Using the quantum chemical picture provided by our OVGF/TZVP calculations, we can assign 18 states to the 8-18 eV binding energy range covered by the EMS and PES experiments. The superior energy resolution of the PES experiment of ~20 meV (FWHM) allows 17 states to be uniquely assigned. Note that the PES spectrum was deconvoluted to obtain the natural widths of the spectral features. Combining these natural widths with the experimental EMS coincidence resolution (~0.5 eV, FWHM), enabled 11 spectral features to be uniquely identified. This procedure was also employed to deconvolute the binding energy spectra measured at specific azimuthal angles (see Fig. 3) to obtain the momentum distributions. The EMS and PES binding energy values are in excellent agreement with the corresponding theoretical values calculated using the OVGF and ADC(3) [2] methods.

### 3.2. Momentum distributions

Momentum distributions for the outer-valence molecular orbitals of BCOD are presented in Figs. 4 and 5. Using our analysis of the binding energy spectra, we can obtain two experimental points on the momentum distribution (MD) for each experimentally-resolved feature. Theoretical momentum distributions have been calculated using the RHF/TZVP and B3LYP/TZVP model chemistries and are also shown. Note that the experimental data has been put on the absolute scale through a single global normalisation factor derived using the theoretical B3LYP/TZVP data. Despite the somewhat limited experimental data available, we can observe good agreement in both the relative shape and magnitude between the experimental and theoretical MDs, confirming that the present spectral assignments are reasonable.

While the restricted experimental data limits a more in-depth quantitative assessment of the theoretical MDs over a broader angular (momentum) range, we do observe significantly better agreement when employing our B3LYP/TZVP model than that obtained using RHF/TZVP. As each orbital only experiences the average repulsion from the other electrons within the RHF model, this observation indicates the importance of dynamic electron correlation, included through the exchange correlation functional within the density functional theory framework, in describing the electronic structure of molecules. These correlation effects are particularly evident when considering the unresolved 13a + 12a and 7b orbital momentum distributions (see Figs. 4 and 5), where there is an enhancement of the low-momentum contribution through the inclusion of electron correlation and the MDs are consistent with the experimental observations. ### 3.3. Electronic structure of the cyclic diketones

To discuss the electronic structure of the cyclic diketones, in Fig. 6 we present a theoretical orbital correlation diagram that has been combined with the available photoelectron [19,23,30] and EMS binding energy spectra [19,20]. In creating this electronic picture, the OVGF/TZVP ionization energies for BCOD have been supplemented with data from BCHD [19] and stelladione [2] obtained through OVGF/cc-pVDZ and OVGF/cc-pVTZ calculations, respectively. For self-consistency in the analysis, we have also performed quantum chemical calculations and obtained electron momentum profiles for stella-2,6-dione [20] using the B3LYP/TZVP model chemistry employed in the present BCOD study and that used in the earlier BCHD investigation [19]. These MD values were found to be in excellent agreement with the previously reported results [20]. This orbital analysis provides orbital correlations across the cyclic ethers as indicated in the orbital correlation diagram. The correlations are assigned based on the dominant orbital contributions that arise from structural features that are common across the molecular series.

In looking at the ionization spectra across the cyclic diketones, we first consider the ionization of the two outermost molecular orbitals. In both BCOD and BCHD, we observe a single unresolved feature corresponding to ionization of both the highest and next-highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO and NHOMO, respectively). In the case of stelladione, however, these two orbitals split into energetically resolvable features. The spatial representation and momentum profiles for all these outermost valence orbitals are presented in Fig. 7. These orbitals correspond to the symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the oxygen electron lone pairs, with respect to rotations of the molecule about the carbon bridge(s) ($C_2$ axis). We observe that the orbitals relate to the O(2p) and its coupling to the CC-$\sigma$ frame. These features manifest as a double peak structure in the momentum profile, with the low and high momentum peaks corresponding to the CC-$\sigma$ and O(2p) structures, respectively. The O(2p) contributions to both the HOMO and NHOMO appears consistent for each molecule in the diketone series. Observed differences between the structures of the HOMO/NHOMO relate to the CC-$\sigma$ interactions. For the asymmetric orbitals [BCOD (13b), BCHD (12b) and stelladione ($7b_2$)], the calculated momentum profiles exhibit almost identical behaviour through the molecular series. Structural differences are observed for the symmetric orbitals [BCOD (14a), BCHD (12a) and stelladione ($6b_3$)], as the low momentum intensity from the CC-$\sigma$ bonds is reduced through an antibonding through-space interaction that decreases with increasing internal molecular strain. This is most pronounced in stelladione, where ionization energies of the HOMO and NHOMO are well separated. Nonetheless, we observe that the underlying bonding nature of the HOMO and NHOMO of these cyclic diketones is qualitatively similar across the molecular series.

The electronic structure of the cyclic diketones is quite congested in the 11 to 16 eV region. In particular, we observe that BCOD exhibits a distinct separation of its ionization spectrum into two components, over the 11–13 eV and 13–16 eV ranges, with a minimum at 13 eV. This is substantially different from BCHD, which has a single broad feature

over this entire region. The structural similarities of BCHD and BCOD suggest that this difference is due to different methylene and ethylene bridges. This observation is consistent with the behaviour observed in the PES spectra of bicyclo[2.2.2]octane (BCO) and bicyclo[2.2.1]hep- tane (norbornane, NBA) that have been reported by Bischof et al. [31]. They reported that NBA displayed a broad spectral feature over the 10-15 eV range, while BCO had two prominent features from 9.5 to 11 eV and 12 to 15 eV, and a minor peak at 11.2 eV. These observations highlight the different ethylene and methylene bridge interactions with the base cyclohexane ring. Again, the symmetry of the molecular orbital about the $C_{2}$ axis strongly influences whether the ethylene or methylene bridge can contribute to the orbital through symmetric/asymmetric atomic contributions. This symmetry allowed/forbidden interaction of the ethylene and methylene bridge strongly influences the orbital character, and consequently the energetic partitioning of the ionized states.

## 4. Conclusions

The electronic structure of BCOD has been investigated using the electron momentum spectroscopy technique. In addition, we undertook theoretical calculations to provide a quantitative interpretation of the previously unreported photoelectron spectrum and the EMS binding energy spectra. The characteristic structure of the molecular orbitals of BCOD have been elucidated through evaluation of the experimental and theoretical momentum profiles. Specifically, through comparison of RHF and B3LYP momentum profiles we could identify those orbitals for which electron correlation played an important role.

This study was also combined with results from previous in- vestigations on cyclic diketones, in order to improve our understanding of bonding mechanisms in strained compounds. Here it was observed that the underlying bonding mechanisms of the individual species were found to be quite similar, although symmetry influenced the ability for different atomic contributions to stabilise individual orbitals. Orbital symmetry also influenced the ability of the methylene and ethylene bridges to contribute to specific orbitals, which resulted in significant perturbations to the orbital ionisation energies in the considered cyclic species.
