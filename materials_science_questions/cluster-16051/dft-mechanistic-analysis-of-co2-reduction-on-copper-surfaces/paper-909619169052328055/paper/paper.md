### Abstract

Electrochemical CO₂ reduction to value-added chemicals or fuels offers a promising approach to reduce carbon emission and alleviate energy shortage. Cu-based electrocatalysts have been widely reported capable of reducing CO₂ to produce a variety of multicarbon products (e.g., ethylene and ethanol). In this work, we develop sulfur-doped Cu₂O electrocatalysts, which instead can electrochemically reduce CO₂ to almost exclusively formate. We prove that a dynamic equilibrium of S exists at the Cu₂O-electrolyte interface, and S-doped Cu₂O undergoes in-situ surface reconstruction to generate active S-adsorbed metallic Cu sites during CO₂ reduction reaction (CO₂RR). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations together with in-situ infrared absorption spectroscopy measurements show that the S-adsorbed metallic Cu surface can not only promote formation of *OCHO intermediate, but also greatly suppress *H and *COOH adsorption, thus facilitating CO₂-to-formate conversion during the electrochemical CO₂RR.

Keywords: CO₂ reduction reaction, electrocatalysis, formic acid, S modification, Cu-based electrocatalysts

### Introduction

Transforming CO₂ to value-added chemicals or fuels powered by renewable energy provides an attractive approach for alleviating global energy and environmental issues caused by burning fossil fuels. A large variety of products such as CO, HCOOH, CH₄, C₂H₄ and C₂H₅OH have been reported in the electrochemical CO₂ reduction reaction (CO₂RR).[¹] Among the various CO₂RR products, formic acid/formate possesses relatively higher economic value,[²] which has wide applications in leather,

pharmaceutical and rubber industries. In recent years, Sn-, $^{[3]}$ Bi-, $^{[4]}$ Pb-$^{[5]}$ and In-$^{[6]}$ based electrocatalysts have been widely investigated for $CO_2$ reduction to formic acid/formate owing to their capabilities of stabilizing the key *OCHO intermediate, among which, sulfide-derived Sn (S-Sn$^{[3a, 7]}$) and In (S-In$^{[6a, 8]}$) catalysts have been shown capable to further enhance the $CO_2$-to-formic acid/formate selectivity.

On the other hand, copper-based materials are the most promising heterogeneous electrocatalysts for the selective $CO_2$ reduction to produce $C_{2+}$ products, thanks to their moderate bindings to the key intermediates (e.g., *COOH, *CO, *CHO and *COH). However, the oxide-derived and sulfide-derived Cu-based electrocatalysts show very different products distributions in $CO_2$RR: oxide-derived Cu-based electrocatalysts can reduce $CO_2$ to multicarbon products such as $C_2H_4$, ethanol, acetate, n-propanol and so on.$^{[9]}$ In contrast, sulfide-derived Cu-based electrocatalysts reduce $CO_2$ mainly to formic acid/formate.$^{[10]}$ Furthermore, a shift in $CO_2$RR selectivity toward formate was also noticed on Cu catalyst if $SO_2$ impurity was introduced during the $CO_2$RR, which was attributed to the formation of $Cu_2S$.$^{[11]}$ However, how S can change the $CO_2$RR selectivity of Cu electrocatalyst is still unknown. S is unstable and prone to dissolve under cathodic potential$^{[6a, 7, 12]}$, which can cause changes in catalyst's composition and structure. Therefore, it is crucial to probe the real active species to understand how S on Cu-based catalysts influences the reaction pathway and product distribution in electrochemical $CO_2$RR.

In this work, sulfur-doped $Cu_2O$ catalysts were synthesized for electrochemical $CO_2$RR. Different from $Cu_2O$, the S-doped $Cu_2O$ catalysts mainly reduced $CO_2$ to formate. The 1.5%S-$Cu_2O$ catalyst with a sulfur content of 1.21 wt.% exhibited a

maximum formate Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 81.4% at -1.2 V vs. RHE in 0.1 M KHCO₃ electrolyte. During CO₂RR, the S-doped Cu₂O underwent *in-situ* surface reduction to form dynamically S-adsorbed metallic Cu, which acted as the real active species for CO₂RR. Electrochemical and *in-situ* infrared absorption spectroscopy measurements together with density functional theory (DFT) calculations demonstrate that the S-adsorbed metallic Cu surface can promote *OCHO intermediate formation while suppress *H and *COOH adsorption, greatly boosting CO₂ reduction to formate.

## Results and Discussion
Cu₂O and sulfur-doped Cu₂O (S-Cu₂O) were firstly synthesized based on a previously reported ligand-free method at room temperature (Figure 1a)¹³. Thioacetamide was used as a sulfur source, which could react with water to release H₂S. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images show that the as-prepared Cu₂O displays a dominant cubic shape with an average size of 70~80 nm, while the 1.5%S-Cu₂O exhibits smaller-sized cubes with rougher surfaces (Figure 1b and Figure S1). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images of Cu₂O and 1.5%S-Cu₂O (Figure 1c and Figure S2a) display lattice fringes of 0.213, 0.244, and 0.302 nm, corresponding to the (200), (111), and (110) crystal planes of Cu₂O, respectively. Compared to Cu₂O, the lattices of 1.5%S-Cu₂O are slightly distorted with noticeable defects (Figure 1c), which may be induced by the incorporation of S in 1.5%S-Cu₂O. The crystal structures of Cu₂O and 1.5%S-Cu₂O were examined by selected area electron diffraction (SAED) as shown in Figure S2. Figure 1d displays the HAADF-STEM image and the corresponding energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) elemental mappings for 1.5%S-Cu₂O, showing homogeneous distribution of Cu, O and S elements (S appearing to be more

homogeneous in the whole image may be due to the influence of electron beam during TEM observation).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of Cu₂O and n%S-Cu₂O (Figure 1e and S3) indicate phase-pure cubic Cu₂O (PDF#99-0041). The S content in different n%S-Cu₂O was determined by the CS elemental analysis method (Table S1). The weight percentage of S

in $0.1\%$S-Cu₂O, $0.6\%$S-Cu₂O, $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O, $3\%$S-Cu₂O, and $10\%$S-Cu₂O is $0.22$ wt%, $0.43$ wt%, $1.21$ wt%, $2.35$ wt%, and $7.26$ wt%, respectively. The surface composition and elemental valence states of Cu₂O and $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O were further investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As presented in Figure S4, the high-resolution Cu 2p XPS spectrum of both Cu₂O and $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O show two peaks at 932.5 eV (Cu $2p_{3/2}$) and 952.4 eV (Cu $2p_{1/2}$), corresponding to $Cu^{+}$ or $Cu^{0}$, with no observable satellite peak, suggesting absence of $Cu^{2+}$ in the samples.$^{[14]}$ To further distinguish $Cu^{+}$ from $Cu^{0}$, Cu LMM auger spectrum was recorded (Figure 1f). The binding energy of Cu LMM for both Cu₂O and $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O are located at 570.1 eV, indicating dominant $Cu^{+}$ in the samples, in good agreement with the XRD results.$^{[15]}$ The lattice oxygen at a binding energy of 530.4 eV was also observed in the two samples (Figure S5).$^{[15-16]}$ Furthermore, the high-resolution S 2p XPS spectrum for $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O as shown in Figure 1g shows two clear doublet peaks in contrast to that of Cu₂O. The S $2p_{3/2}$ peak at 161.7 eV can be assigned to the Cu-S-Cu bonds, matching well with the S $2p_{3/2}$ binding energy reported for Cu₂S.$^{[17]}$ The shoulder doublet at 162.9 eV can be assigned to a highly Cu-deficient nonstoichiometric sulfide.$^{[18]}$ The S 2p peak at 168.3 eV can be attributed to sulfate species ($SO_{4}^{2-}$), which may be formed due to oxidation of sulfide upon exposure to air.$^{[18b]}$ Moreover, XPS analysis with $Ar^{+}$ sputtering (20 nm) was performed on the $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O sample, which can completely remove the surface oxide layer formed due to air oxidation. The result indicates that S can be successfully incorporated into the lattice of Cu₂O in $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O (Figure S6).

The electrochemical CO₂RR performance of Cu₂O and $1.5\%$S-Cu₂O were examined in a typical H-type cell filled with 0.1 M CO₂-saturated KHCO₃ electrolyte in a three-

electrode configuration. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves of $Cu_2O$ and 1.5%S-$Cu_2O$ show comparable current densities, and the current densities can be well maintained after $CO_2RR$ (-1.2 V vs. RHE for 1 h) (Figure S7). But they display completely different product distributions in the cathodic potential range from -0.8 to -1.6 V vs. RHE (without iR correction) (Figure 2a). A variety of products including $H_2$, $CO$, $HCOOH$, and $C_2H_4$ could be produced over $Cu_2O$, similar to previous reports.$^{[9c, 19]}$ The other liquid products are expected to be alcohols,$^{[9c, 20]}$ which are difficult to be detected over the glassy carbon electrode in the H-type cell. The $Cu_2O$ achieved a $FE_{CO}$ of 36.8% at -0.8 V vs. RHE, and it gradually decreased with increasing the applied cathodic potential. Concomitantly, $C_2H_4$ FE increased and reached 33% at -1.6 V vs. RHE. In contrast, the main $CO_2RR$ product over 1.5%S-doped $Cu_2O$ is formate with a maximum formate FE of 81.4% at -1.2 V vs. RHE. As compared in Figure 2b, the formate partial current density over 1.5%S-$Cu_2O$ is significantly higher as compared to that over $Cu_2O$ across the entire examined potential window. The surface roughness for the two catalysts before and after $CO_2RR$ was further compared based on a series of cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements (Table S2). Formate partial current density normalized to roughness for 1.5%S-$Cu_2O$ is still significantly higher than that for $Cu_2O$, indicating that the large enhancement in formate production over 1.5%S-$Cu_2O$ is mainly attributed to the increased intrinsic catalytic activity of active sites towards formate production (Figure S8).

The relationship between S content in n%S-$Cu_2O$ and the catalytic performance was investigated at -1.6 V vs. RHE and -1.2 V vs. RHE (Figure 2c and Figure S9). As S content in n%S-$Cu_2O$ increased from 0 wt.% to 1.21 wt.%, both FE and partial current

density of formate apparently increased while those of $C_{2+}$ products decreased. At the S content higher than 1.2 wt.%, the $C_{2+}$ products almost disappeared. Commercial $Cu_2S$ also exhibited dominant formate production with a formate FE up to 69% towards $CO_2RR$ (Figure S10). These results indicate that introduction of S into Cu-based electrocatalysts can promote formate production while suppress $C_{2+}$ products formation in $CO_2RR$. Besides examining the catalytic activity and selectivity, long-term electrolysis was further carried out at -1.2 V vs. RHE to study the catalyst's stability. As shown in Figure 2d, the partial current density of formate maintains almost constant during 12 h of continuous electrolysis, indicating excellent catalytic stability of $1.5\% S-Cu_2O$. The increase in total current density is also consistent with a previous report related to $Cu_{2-x}S^{[21]}$, which may be due to continuous structural evolution during electroreduction.

To overcome mass transport limitation in H-type cell and investigate the catalytic performance of the as-prepared catalysts in an alkaline environment, we further performed the $CO_2RR$ in a flow cell using 1.0 M KOH as the electrolyte. As shown in Figure S11a, formate is still the major product over $1.5\% S-Cu_2O$, achieving a maximum FE of ~74% at a partial current density of $74\ mA\ cm^{-2}$ and an applied cathodic potential of -0.98 V vs. RHE (without iR correction) (Figure S11), demonstrating that formate is still the major product of $CO_2RR$ over $1.5\% S-Cu_2O$ in an alkaline environment. The $1.5\% S-Cu_2O$ shows superior formate production as compared to commercial $Cu_2S$ and is among the best Cu-based electrocatalysts reported in the literature that are able to catalyze electrochemical $CO_2$ reduction to formate (Table S3).

Cu₂O and S-Cu₂O would be reduced during CO₂RR.²² Recently, Yang and coworkers²³ have shown that a Cu@Cu₂O core-shell structure can run through a reduction/oxidation life cycle during and after the CO₂RR, and metallic Cu nanograins formed during electrolysis are the real active species for CO₂ electroreduction. To investigate the structural evolution during the CO₂RR over 1.5%S-Cu₂O, *ex-situ* XRD and *quasi in-situ* XPS measurements were performed. As shown in the *ex-situ* XRD patterns (Figure 2e), Cu⁰ peak appeared in the S-Cu₂O catalyst with obviously reduced Cu₂O intensity after the CO₂RR, indicating reduction of S-Cu₂O to metallic Cu. *Quasi in-situ* XPS was conducted under an inert atmosphere during the electrochemical measurements to prevent exposure of catalyst to air (0 min was taken before and after contact with electrolyte). Figure 2f displays the Cu LMM spectra of 1.5%S-Cu₂O, showing that after 10 min of electrolysis, a single Cu⁰ peak at 567.9 eV apparently appeared, and the peak ratio of Cu⁺/Cu⁰ significantly weakened as compared to 0 min spectrum, indicating that S-Cu₂O could be readily reduced to metallic Cu⁰. The XPS results of S-Cu₂O after CO₂RR also suggest formation of a thin oxide layer after removing the applied cathodic potential (Figure S12). The S 2p XPS spectrum indicates sulfur loss in S-Cu₂O catalyst during the CO₂RR (Figure 2g).

It has been reported that O atoms in Cu₂O can be rapidly replaced by S in S²⁻ containing solution via $\ce{Cu_{2}O + S^{2-} + H_{2}O -> Cu_{2}S + OH^{-}}$.²⁴ To study the effect of S in 1.5%S-Cu₂O on CO₂RR, Cu₂O was first immersed in 0.1 M KHCO₃ solution containing 0.25 mM K₂S for 30 min before CO₂RR (named as Cu₂O/CuₓS). After immersion, Cu-S bonds were formed on the Cu₂O surface as observed in the XPS spectrum (Figure 3a) and

the TEM image (Figure S13). XRD pattern indicates that $Cu_2O$ was still the dominant crystal phase (Figure S14). The Cu-S bonds on the $Cu_2O$ surface are unstable during $CO_2RR.^{[11,25]}$ To study leaching of S and the influence on $CO_2$ electroreduction, $CO_2RR$ was performed in $0.1~M~CO_2$-saturated $KHCO_3$ electrolyte with electrolyte refreshed every 30 min at $-1.2~V$ vs. RHE (Figure 3b). Formate is the major product with FE approaching $70\%$ in the first 30 min of reaction, and then the FEs of $C_2H_4$ and CO increased sharply with concomitant decrease in formate FE. S 2p XPS spectrum recorded after 2 h of $CO_2RR$ only displayed a peak of $SO_4^{2-}$, while the $S^{2-}$ peak in the inner part of the catalyst significantly decreased in intensity (Figure 3c), suggesting that S in $Cu_2O/Cu_xS$ could be leached out into the electrolyte after each electrolyte refreshing during $CO_2RR$. Cu LMM spectrum after $CO_2RR$ suggested that $Cu_2O/Cu_xS$ was reduced under the applied negative potential (Figure S15). Combined with the electrochemical results, the decrease of formate FE is closely related to the decrease of the surficial S concentration at the catalyst-electrolyte interface. We infer that there exists an equilibrium of S at the catalyst-electrolyte interface after S partially leaching out from the catalyst. But such equilibrium can be broken after each refreshing of the electrolyte and the S concentration at the catalyst-electrolyte interface would decrease after refreshing the electrolyte, leading to a decrease in formate FE. On the other hand, refreshing electrolyte during the $CO_2RR$ caused little changes in product FE over $1.5\%$S-$Cu_2O$ and commercial $Cu_2S$ (Figure S16).

$CO_2RR$ was further performed over $Cu_2O$ in $0.1~M~CO_2$-saturated $KHCO_3$ electrolyte with adding different concentrations of $K_2S$ (0.01 mM, 0.05 mM and 0.25 mM). During the $CO_2RR$, the CO and $C_2H_4$ FEs continuously decreased as electrolysis proceeded,

suggesting continuous change of the catalyst-electrolyte interface (Figure 3d and S17).

The FEs of CO and $C_{2+}$ products were reduced, while that of formate increased with increasing $S^{2-}$ concentration in the electrolyte (Figure 3e). The formate FE reached ~70% in the electrolyte containing 0.05 mM $K_2S$ over $Cu_2O$, which is about 7.7 times higher than that obtained in the electrolyte without $K_2S$. S 2p XPS spectrum without/with $Ar^{+}$ sputtering (20 nm) for $Cu_2O$ after $CO_2RR$ in 0.1 M $KHCO_3$ + 0.25 mM $K_2S$ electrolyte displayed peaks that could be assigned to $S\ 2p_{3/2}$ and $S\ 2p_{1/2}$ for $Cu_2S$ and Cu-deficient sulfide, indicating that S had been introduced into $Cu_2O$ during the $CO_2RR$ (Figure 3f). The $Cu_2O$ after experiencing 30 min of $CO_2RR$ in $K_2S$-containing electrolyte was then applied for another round of $CO_2RR$ in pure 0.1 M $KHCO_3$ solution. An increase in $C_2H_4$ FE and a decrease in formate FE were observed, similar to the $Cu_2O/Cu_xS$ catalyst, due to a decrease in S content caused by S leaching (Figure S18).

Based on the above results, a dynamic equilibrium of S at the $Cu_2O$-electrolyte interface was verified, which greatly influenced the $CO_2RR$ selectivity (Figure 3g). $S^{2-}$ in solution can react with $Cu_2O$ to form $Cu_xS$ at the surface, on the contrary, S in $S-Cu_2O$ or $Cu_xS$ is thermodynamically unstable under negative potential, which will be leached into solution. During $CO_2RR$, the surface $Cu_2O$ and $Cu_xS$ will be reduced to metallic Cu and sulfur-adsorbed Cu, respectively, which control the $CO_2RR$ pathway.

To understand the different CO₂RR selectivity over Cu₂O and S-Cu₂O, the CO₂RR intermediates were probed by attenuated total reflectance surface enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) (Figure 4). As shown in Figure 4a and b, the absorption peaks at 2967 and 2908 cm⁻¹ over Cu₂O and 1.5%S-Cu₂O can be assigned to the C-H stretching, which is the fingerprint for formate/formic acid formation.[²⁶] The intensity of C-H stretching over 1.5%S-Cu₂O is obviously stronger than that over Cu₂O, which increased with increasing cathodic potential, while that over Cu₂O remained almost unchanged. Besides, Cu₂O displayed another two obvious peaks at 1410 cm⁻¹ and 1276 cm⁻¹ (Figure 4c-d), which can be assigned to C-O and C-OH stretching in *COOH (the key intermediate for CO formation).[²⁷] The peak at 1392 cm⁻¹ can be attributed to CO₃²⁻ in solution, which may be formed through CO₂-saturated buffer equilibrium.[²⁷c, ²⁸] Over 1.5%S-Cu₂O, broader peaks are observed at the wavenumber range of 1370~1430 cm⁻¹ and 1550~1700 cm⁻¹ as compared to Cu₂O (Figure 4e-f). The peak at 1580 cm⁻¹ can be assigned to asymmetric O-C-O stretching in *OCHO intermediate or formate ions.[²⁶a, ²⁹] However, it is hard to distinguish the broad peak at 1370~1430 cm⁻¹ as there may exist a few vibration overlaps among *COOH, *OCHO, and (H)CO₃²⁻ (Table S4). To reduce the influence of CO₃²⁻ and HCO₃⁻, the ATR-SEIRAS experiments were further performed in a CO₂-saturated 0.1 M K₂SO₄ solution (Figure S19). The *OCHO intermediate/HCOO⁻ located at around 1368 cm⁻¹ (symmetric O-C-O stretching) could be clearly observed over S-Cu₂O. ⁴³, ⁵⁰⁻⁵² In contrast, obvious C-OH and C-O stretching (1290 cm⁻¹, 1410 cm⁻¹) in *COOH, and linear bonded *CO (CO_L) (2051 cm⁻¹) and bridge bonded CO (CO_B) (1942 cm⁻¹) intermediates appeared over Cu₂O.[²⁶a, ³⁰] These results demonstrate suppressed CO₂-to-CO pathway while enhanced CO₂-to-formate pathway over 1.5%S-Cu₂O,

matching perfectly with the electrochemical results.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further conducted to evaluate the reaction energetics of CO₂RR. Based on the experimental results, the active sites for CO₂RR over Cu₂O and S-doped Cu₂O are metallic Cu and S-adsorbed Cu, respectively. Accordingly, as shown in Figure 5a and S20, the CO₂RR intermediates on Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) (with 1/2 S coverage) surfaces were simulated. The DFT optimized geometries on S-adsorbed Cu(111) suggest that *COOH for the CO₂-to-CO pathway

binds onto the S atom via the C atom, while *OCHO for the CO₂-to-formic acid/formate pathway binds onto Cu atoms via two O atoms. S atoms with a high coverage on the Cu(111) surface will migrate when the *OCHO intermediate binds onto Cu atoms, while the arrangement of S atoms remains almost unchanged over S-adsorbed Cu(111) with *COOH adsorption. Therefore, the energy barrier of S atom migration was additionally considered for S-adsorbed Cu(111) in the *OCHO pathway (Figure 5a) and was calculated to be 0.48 eV. As seen in Figure S21, the distance between *CO₂ and S atom in the *OCHO pathway is longer than that in the *COOH pathway (3.855 Å vs. 3.560 Å), while the distance between *CO₂ and Cu atom gets closer in the *OCHO pathway (4.096 Å vs. 4.824 Å). For the *OCHO pathway, *CO₂ can approach Cu atoms after S migration, so that it can be hydrogenated and then bond to Cu atoms to form *OCHO intermediate in the next step. While in the *COOH pathway, *CO₂ directly approaches the surface of S atoms without S migration and hydrogenates to generate *COOH via bonding with S atoms.

The charge density difference of Cu(111) with *CO₂ (Figure S22) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) with *CO₂ (Figure 5b, c and Figure S23) indicate no obvious electron transfer between Cu(111)/S-adsorbed Cu(111) and *CO₂, instead, an intermolecular force is observed. Besides, in contrast to Cu(111), a dipole-induced dipole force is exhibited between adjacent S atoms and CO₂ over S-adsorbed Cu(111), which is induced by charge transfer between Cu atoms and S atoms over the S-adsorbed Cu(111) surface (electrons transferred from the outermost layer of Cu to the adsorbed S layer) (Figure S24). For the two configurations of S-adsorbed Cu(111) with *CO₂, the charge accumulation region in the middle of *CO₂ and S atoms of the *COOH pathway is only adjacent to S atoms,

while that of the *OCHO pathway is close to both S atoms and Cu atoms. The projected density of states (PDOS) of CO₂ molecule and Cu/S atoms on Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) with *CO₂ (Figure S25) also indicate no formation of chemical bonds.

Figure 5d and e compare the Gibbs free energies of the pathways for formate and CO formation over Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) surface. For the CO pathway, the potential-limiting step of both Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) is the *COOH formation. Compared to Cu(111), S-adsorbed Cu(111) displays a higher energy barrier for *COOH formation (0.65 eV). On the other hand, the potential-limiting step for the CO₂-to-formate pathway is distinct over Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111). The potential-limiting step on Cu(111) is *OCHO to HCOOH, whereas that on S-adsorbed Cu(111) is *CO₂ to *OCHO. Besides, the CO₂-to-formate reaction energy barrier over S-adsorbed Cu(111) (0.10 eV) is lower than that over Cu(111) (0.28 eV). As seen in the inset of Figure 5d and e, after adsorption of *COOH/*OCHO, charge transfer occurs on both Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) surfaces, forming bonds. More electrons can be transferred to *OCHO on S-adsorbed Cu(111) (0.89 e) than that on Cu(111) (0.63 e), in contrast, fewer electrons can be transferred to *COOH on S-adsorbed Cu(111) (0.31 e) than that on Cu(111) (0.41 e). Moreover, the projected density of states (PDOS) of *COOH and *OCHO intermediates on Cu(111) and S-adsorbed Cu(111) (before and after adsorption) are displayed in Figure 5f-g (energy at 0 eV corresponds to the Femi level). For S-adsorbed Cu(111), resonance existed for the coordinated S atom (S₆₉) and *COOH after *COOH adsorbed on S-adsorbed Cu(111), while the PDOS of the Cu atom (Cu₄₀, bound to the S atom) remained almost unchanged, indicating covalent bonds between *COOH and S₆₉. Besides, stronger resonance was observed for the coordinated Cu atoms (Cu₄₀ and Cu₆₀) and *OCHO after

*OCHO adsorbed on S-adsorbed Cu(111) than that on Cu(111). These calculation results indicate that S adsorbed on a metallic Cu surface can stabilize *OCHO species and inhibit *COOH adsorption as compared to a pure Cu surface, which can facilitate formate formation and suppress CO formation. Additionally, the $\Delta$G*H on S-adsorbed Cu(111) is 0.43 eV, higher than that on Cu(111) (-0.15 eV), which shall greatly suppress the competing HER over S-adsorbed Cu(111) during CO₂RR (Figure S26).

## Conclusion
In summary, Cu₂O and sulfur-doped Cu₂O electrocatalysts have been synthesized through a facile ligand-free method for CO₂ electroreduction. The as-prepared S-Cu₂O catalyst reaches a maximum formate FE of 81.4% at -1.2 V vs. RHE and exhibits a 7.0-fold enhancement in formate production as compared to Cu₂O. The S-Cu₂O catalyst can be reduced under cathodic potential to generate the active S-adsorbed Cu⁰ sites for CO₂RR to produce formate. Both experimental results and theoretical calculations reveal that S atoms adsorbed on metallic Cu surface derived from S-Cu₂O under cathodic potential can significantly suppress H₂ and CO pathways and at the same time promote formate formation.
