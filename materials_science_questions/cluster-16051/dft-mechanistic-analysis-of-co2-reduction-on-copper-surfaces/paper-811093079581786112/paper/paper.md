# Step Edge Assisted Direct Linear Alkane Coupling

Junjie Zhang, $^{a\ddagger}$ Chunran Chang$^{b\ddagger}$ Biao Yang, $^{a}$ Nan Cao, $^{a}$ Chencheng Peng, $^{a}$ Haiming Zhang, $^{a}$ Dan-Tam D. Tang, $^{c}$ Frank Glorius, $^{c}$ Gerhard Erker, $^{c}$ Harald Fuchs, $^{d}$ Qing Li$^{a,*}$ and Lifeng Chi$^{a,*}$

**Abstract:** Direct coupling of alkanes via C-H activation of terminal methyl groups has acquired tremendous interests both scientifically and technically. We herein present the results of linear alkane-coupling at the step edges of Cu surfaces at modulated temperatures. Combining the observations of scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) with density functional theory plus dispersion (DFT-D) calculations, we elucidate the mechanism of the reaction and demonstrate that the low activation barrier relies on heterogeneous catalysis at the upper step edges, where low-coordinated surface atoms are present. We further reveal the generality of the reaction, so that it can be applied on the step edges of different facets of surfaces.

## Introduction

Alkanes principally appear in petroleum and natural gas, and are thus considered as cheap feedstock by the chemical industry. However, the strong C-H bonds of alkanes make them chemically very unreactive, preventing the selective C-H activation and the subsequent C-C coupling of the alkanes. To date, most strategies for the C-H bond activation rely on transition metal catalysis, by the formation of metal-alkyl-bound intermediates, or the application of harsh conditions. $^{1,2}$ In addition, the products formed are normally a mixture of multiple components due to the poor reaction selectivity, whereas the latter approach is economically not favourable because of the high energetic expense.

On-surface chemistry, $^{3-24}$ wherein metal surfaces act as the catalyst, provides alternative strategies for C-H activation and subsequent C-C coupling. The strong catalytic abilities of surfaces arise from both the reduction of dimensionality and sufficient interactions between reactants and the support. Moreover, the considerable amount of defects on a surface, such as vacancies, step edges and kinks, are known to be of high catalytic activity. On this base, sp- and sp$^{2}$-C-H functionalizations have been successfully achieved on metal surfaces. $^{25-32}$ However, sp$^{3}$-C-H activation$^{33}$ is still considered as one of the most challenging topics for surface chemists. Until Recently, Zhong *et al.* reported a first-time linear alkane polymerization on a Au(110) surface under relatively mild conditions. $^{34}$ This reaction highly relies on the reconstructed $1 \times 3$-groove of the Au(110) surface, wherein the adsorbed molecules are confined. Nevertheless, a reconstructed Au(110) surface is only stable in ultrahigh vacuum (UHV), thus making a potential application under practical aspects rather difficult. Very recently, Cai *et al.* reported a dehydrogenative reaction of alkyl derivatives on an Cu(110) surface, on which various allotropes were formed, showing lower reaction selectivities. $^{35}$ Note, both of the two attempts were performed on a (110) surface, which is known to have strong catalitic ability. for catalysis on metal surfaces, *e.g.* using nanoparticles, or the method of chemical vapour deposition, (110) facets are unfavourable. It is therefore of great and general interest to explore alternative concepts for the alkyl chain homo-coupling with excellent reaction selectivity on a broad range of metals and facets (particularly the most applicable (111) facets).

Herein, we present a strategy to utilize step edges of copper surfaces as "active sites" for the heterogeneous catalysis of the alkane polymerization. $^{32,36}$ Aided by scanning tunnelling microscopy (STM), the linearly polymerized products were found to line up along the upper step edges. Unlike our previous work using Au(110) surfaces, $^{34}$ in which C-H activation took place at both the terminal methyl and penultimate methylene groups, almost all alkanes reacted highly selectively at the terminal methyl group by step edge assisted reaction. Furthermore, the activation barrier of this approach is lower than that on Au(110) so that the polymerization reaction can occur on a Cu(100) surface with more gentle thermal excitation ($\sim$100 °C). The reaction pathways were demonstrated *via* density functional theory plus dispersion (DFT-D) calculations, by which the low energy barrier and the excellent reaction selectivity were explained. Finally, since the step edge induced reaction is a result of the low coordination of surface atoms, these reactions are not limited to a specific terminated facet. Indeed, experimentally, we achieved the polymerization not only on the Cu(100) surface, but also on the most stable Cu(111) surface. Note this is a first example of achieve the direct alkane couplings other than the (110) metal facets. Thus we believe our studies not only enrich the methodologies for the surface catalytic reactions, but also open doors for the direct alkane polymerization with practical systems.

## Results and discussions
STM observations Experimentally, we chose $n$-dotriacontane ($C_{32}H_{66}$) as precursor. After deposition on a Cu(100) surface held at room temperature (0.05 ML), the $C_{32}H_{66}$-molecules self-assembled into ordered islands, as shown in Figure 1a. Each component is of identical length of ~4 nm and can be ascribed to $C_{32}H_{66}$-monomers. They aggregate at the lower step edge and align parallel to each other via van-der-Waals forces. Similar self-assembling behaviour of alkanes has been reported extensively by different groups. $^{37,38}$ A closer investigation revealed that the self-assembled islands have two apparent orientations (inset of Figure 1a): either parallel or perpendicular to the step edges. This phenomenon suggests that the step edges are not a perfect space confiner for the molecular orientations, which is different from the grooves of the reconstructed Au(110) surface. $^{34}$ A dramatic change takes place after annealing the substrate to 200 °C, as shown in Figure 1b: Only few objects (0.002 ML) can be found on the terrace due to the significant desorption of $C_{32}H_{66}$ at elevated temperature. However, it is conspicuous that the step edges are overlaid with bright linear features. High-resolution STM images (inset of Figure 1b) disclose that those objects are anchored and run along the upper step edges. They resemble $n$-dotriacontane except for the larger length. We thus hypothesize that these new features are oligomerized carbon chains formed via the direct coupling of monomeric $n$-alkane, similar to the previously reported alkane polymerization on Au(110). $^{34}$ It is worth noting that the step underneath the oligomer is virtually straight and runs along certain directions, whereas no oligomers could be observed along curvy randomly orientated edges (Figure 1b and inset), suggesting a considerable coupling between the products and the substrate (details are discussed vide infra). With incrementing precursor coverage (0.02 ML), almost all step edges are overlaid with oligomers/polymers. As a consequence, the steps are reshaped into two dominating orientations: [001] and [010] directions (Figure 1c). Figure 1d shows a representative high resolution reaction product. The length is measured about 25 nm, which can be ascribed to 6 monomeric units. The characteristic seamless zigzag alignment is consistent with the backbone of $n$-alkanes. Furthermore, the periodicity of the zigzag structure is clearly resolved and is about 0.24 nm (Figure 1e), which is in nice agreement with the carbon spacing of $n$-alkanes. $^{34,37}$ All these phenomenon provide strong evidence for the occurrence of a $n$-alkane coupling reaction along the steps of the Cu(100) surfaces.

To further evidence the step edge assisted direct $n$-alkane coupling, delicate STM tip manipulations experiments were carried out by increasing the tunnelling current to 2 nA and reducing the bias voltage to 50 mV. Figure 1f shows a segment of molecular chains that has been stripped from the edges laterally. The molecular chain remains unbroken after the manipulation, suggesting the formation of the alkane oligomers/polymers. Moreover, one can see from Figure 1f that the monomers are connected covalently not only along the step edges but also across the step kinks.

Though the steps remain unaltered during scanning at 77 K, it has been well known that surface adatoms evaporate from steps and kinks to the terrace via thermal activation. $^{39}$ The morphology of the surface then is not static at elevated temperatures, at which the reaction occurs. The considerable interactions between the adsorbed molecules and the surface, as described by the DFT calculations in below, stabilize the steps. As a consequence, the step edges underneath oligomers/polymers remain straight and run along [001] and [010] directions, whereas uncoated step edges do not have identical shapes, as shown in Figure 1b and 1c. Moreover, long steps are usually not stable, so that they transform into zigzag-shaped edges to reduce the free energy during cooling. This explains why the oligomers/polymers are synthesized across several kinks, as shown in Figure 1f.

Furthermore, we explored 5-icosylbenzo[$b$]thiophene (Figure 2a), which comprises a benzo[$b$]thiophene attached to the $C_{20}$-alkyl chain, as precursor. The nice thing of this precursor is that the benzo[$b$]thiophene terminals are ideal land markers, so that the reactions can be clearly resolved. After deposition on Cu(100) at room temperature, the precursors self-assembled into islands via van-der-Waals forces between the alkyl chains. Each monomer could be easily distinguished by a bright terminal

benzo[*b*]thiophene unit and a linear tail ($C_{20}$-alkyl chain), as shown in Figure 2b. Comparable changes took place after annealing at 200 °C, resulting in overlayed step edges. Close visualization reveals two kinds of objects at the upper step edges (Figure 2c). The shorter one (length of the linear tail: 2.48 nm), with a bright terminal unit, is a 5-icosylbenzo[*b*]thiophene monomer. The longer one instead, holds bright building blocks on both ends. Considering that the length of the longer object is exactly twice of that of the monomer, which strongly indicates the dimers to be formed by direct alkane coupling of precursors. This finding clearly suggests the occurrence of alkane polymerization at step edges. Please note that we did not check if the benzo[*b*]thiophene units themselves are covalently connected, as this is beyond the scope of this article.

The activation barrier is a very importance parameter for the chemical reactions. To determine the lowest temperature at which the alkane coupling takes place, systematic temperature dependent experiments were carried out for the $C_{32}H_{66}$ polymerizations on Cu(100) surfaces. Figure S1a-S1d display representative STM images after annealing of the $C_{32}H_{66}$ self-assembled surface at 100 °C, 130 °C, 160 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Distinct oligomers/polymers were observed at all given conditions. However, one could not find coupled structures if the annealing temperature is lower than 100 °C. We thus conclude that the step edge assisted C-H activation reaction could take place at as low as 100 °C, which is lower than the reaction on the Au(110) surface (140 °C) reported in our previous study.³⁴

**DFT calculations** To elucidate the reaction pathways and the mechanism of the *n*-alkane coupling with low activation barrier in the vicinity of the upper step edges, theoretical calculations were carried out (computational details can be found in the general methods part in the supplementary information). In order to identify the initial state of the reaction, we chose *n*-hexane as model precursor and placed it next to an upper step edge of a Cu(100) surface. Figure 3a depicts a model of a fully relaxed adsorption. The adsorption energy is -1.03 eV, indicating the binding with the upper step edge being thermodynamically favorable. To initiate the coupling reaction, one needs to remove a hydrogen atom at certain positions (marked as H(1), H(2) or H(3), as shown in Figure 3a) from the *n*-hexane model compound. Our calculations show that the activation barrier is 1.05 eV, 4.28 eV and 1.16 eV for the detachment of H(1), H(2) and H(3), respectively, as shown in Figure 3c. Note that since H(2) neither binds strongly to the step edge nor to the lower terrace, the dehydrogenation barrier is close to the thermal C-H bond dissociation energy. For the detachment of H(1), the step edge is able to stabilize the transition state by interacting with the semi-detached H(1) atom, which leads to a much lower activation barrier of 1.05 eV. The details of the reaction pathway of detachment of H(1) is shown in Figure 3b (enlarged figures can be found in Figure S2). Note, the barrier for H(1)-detachment (1.05 eV) is lower than for the previously reported alkane polymerization on Au(110) (1.33eV). This is in good agreement with the experimental observation that the reaction takes place at 100 °C along the edges of Cu(100) and 140 °C in the 1×3-groove of the Au(110) surface. Note that the activation barriers for the detachment of H(1)- and H(2)- atoms on a reconstructed Au(110) surface are very close to each other, which leads to the occurrence of C-H activation at both the terminal methyl and penultimate methylene groups with similar probability. On the upper step edges of the Cu(100) surface, however, the energy barrier for the detachment of H(2) is much higher than that for H(1). The huge energy difference results in a high reaction selectivity and almost all alkyl chains interconnect *via* the terminal methyl groups (experimentally, no nodes were detected among the reaction products). It is worth noting that although the barrier for removing H(3) is only slightly larger than that for removing H(1), steric effects hinder the coupling of the corresponding C(3)-atoms.

Since direct n-alkane coupling is ascribed to the low coordination number of the copper atoms at the step edges, such phenomenon should not be limited to (100)-facets. With nanoparticles or for chemical vapor deposition (CVD), (111)-facets are most stable and favorable. As a consequence, for practical applications, it was long-since demanded to apply reactions onto (111)-facets of metal crystals. Actually, decorated step edges are do observed as shown in Figure 4a, after annealing the $C_{32}H_{66}$ adsorbed Cu(111) surface at $200\ ^{\circ}C$ for 20 mins, indicating the presence of on-surface synthesized n-Alkane oligomers/polymers. Compared to the situation on the Cu(100) surface, no obvious adsorbates were visible on the terrace of Cu(111) after annealing, which could be explained by the stronger adsorption energy on the (100) surface. The relaxed model discloses that n-hexane anchors on the upper step edge with an adsorption energy of -0.92 eV (Figure 4b). Notably, the distance between the adsorbed monomer and the lower terrace on Cu(111) is much lower than the one on Cu(100), resulting in interactions of the monomer with atoms of both the upper and the lower terrace of the Cu(111) surface. As a consequence, the activation barrier for the detachment of H(2) is dramatically reduced from 4.28 eV on Cu(100) to 1.98 eV on Cu(111). In addition, calculations showed that the activation barrier for breaking the C-H(1) bond on the Cu(111) surface is 1.17 eV (Figure 4c), which is slightly larger than the barrier on the Cu(100) surface (1.05 eV). This is in nice agreement with the fact that the reaction triggering temperature on Cu(111) surfaces ($\sim120\ ^{\circ}C$) is higher than that on Cu(100) ($\sim100\ ^{\circ}C$). The activation barriers for the detachment of the different H-atoms are displayed in Figure 4d. Briefly speaking, the difference between the activation barrier on (111)- and (100)-facets is caused by the fact that atoms of (100)-facets are coordinatively more unsaturated than those of (111)-facets and they therefore are superior in stabilizing transition states.

Finally, we should note that the observed oligomers/polymers along the step edges are not formed at the terraces and then translate to the step edges. It is known that the movements of the reaction products are significantly suppressed due to their considerable mass. Actually, we have observed the reaction products at the step terrace upon higher temperature and relative high coverage (See Figure S3). It is quite clear that the oligomers cannot move freely and form self-assembly structure on the Cu(100) surfaces.

### Conclusions
In conclusion, we have successfully achieved the direct linear n-alkane coupling at the step edges of copper surfaces. Scanning tunnelling microscopy imaging reveals that such a reaction has a very low activation barrier and undergoes a high reaction selectivity. Combining with density functional theory plus dispersion calculations, we elucidate the reaction pathway and find that the low coordinated surface atoms play an essential role for the reaction. Moreover, we demonstrate that such a reaction generally takes place at step edges, so that it can also be applied on the Cu(111) surfaces. Our study may open doors to wide prospects for n-alkane polymerizations in practical systems.
