# Adsorption of the Amides *N*-Ethylacetamide and 2-Pyrrolidinone on $\gamma$-Alumina. Influence of the Stereochemistry of the Adsorbate

S. Affrossman,*,† D. R. Armstrong,† D. Robb,† and J. A. Treverton‡

Department of Pure and Applied Chemistry, University of Strathclyde, Glasgow G1 1XL, Scotland, and Alcan International, Banbury Laboratory, Banbury, England

Received December 12, 1994. In Final Form: March 16, 1995⊗

The adsorption isotherm of 2-pyrrolidinone on $\gamma$-alumina shows an initial rapid uptake of solute followed by a plateau. In contrast, $N$-ethylacetamide gives a gradual increase in adsorption. For both solutes, DRIFTS data indicate that the carbonyl and N-hydrogen functionalities are involved in bonding, and the most probable conformation of $N$-ethylacetamide on the surface is as the trans isomer. Theoretical calculations show that in the linear molecule the C−N linkage has a marked double bond character, which results in the molecule remaining in the trans form. The energies of interaction of the linear and cyclic molecules with one and two aluminium "sites" are calculated. The cyclic molecule can adsorb on a single surface "site" through both functionalities. Adsorption of the trans linear molecule on two "sites" gives a comparable interaction energy. Though both molecules adsorb sufficiently strongly from single solute solutions to survive washing, only 2-pyrrolidinone is adsorbed from a solution of the two amides. The differences in behavior are attributed to the steric restraints for adsorption on a real surface.

## Introduction

Amides are simple models for a significant class of biological materials, and an amide type structure also occurs in certain synthetic adhesives. A study of the adsorption of amides therefore provides a fundamental understanding of the interaction of those macromolecules with surfaces.

Amides adsorb on surfaces via van der Waals's, ion− dipole, or hydrogen-bonded interactions, depending on the nature of the surface. The adsorbate molecules are also subject to intermolecular van der Waal's and hydrogen-bonded latteral interactions, which may influence packing on the surface.

Törnkvist et al.¹ have used $N$-methylacetamide, as a simple model for peptides, to examine the effect of surface hydrophilicity on the interactions with the amide func- tionality. They drew attention to the interesting increase in stability of an overlayer of $N$-methylacetamide on a hydrophobic methylated silica surface compared to that on the hydrophilic hydroxylated silica and suggested that only very weakly bound hydrogen-bonded interactions occurred on the latter.

Previously, Garcia-Ramos and Carmona² investigated adsorption of $N$-methylacetamide on alkaline earth phos- phates and postulated that there is a relatively strong ion−dipole interaction between the carbonyl oxygen and a cation surface site. An additional $N$-hydrogen-bonded interaction was observed only at high coverages, and this was ascribed to intermolecular or molecule−surface bonding.

Aluminas are an important group of adsorbents and can exhibit a wide variety of surface sites. We report here an investigation of the bonding of two model amides on a $\gamma$-alumina surface by infrared spectroscopy and theoretical calculations. The models are chosen to probe the effect of altering the stereochemistry of the adsorbate on the adsorption properties. The adsorbates, $N$-ethy- lacetamide and 2-pyrrolidinone, have similar compositions but differ in the configuration of the molecule, being linear and cyclic respectively.

## Experimental Section

**Materials.** Aluminium oxide C (Degussa), a nonporous $\gamma$-alumina, was pretreated at 100 °C for 3 h prior to use. $N$-Ethylacetamide and 2-pyrrolidinone (Aldrich) were used without further purification. AnalaR grade toluene was dried with sodium.

**Infrared.** The sample was washed six times with solvent to remove physically trapped solute, mixed with KCl, and loaded into a diffuse reflectance cell. Spectra were obtained with a Nicolet 20SXB FTIR instrument at a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.

**Adsorption Isotherms. Single Solute.** The alumina and a known toluene soluton of the amide were equilibrated in stoppered flasks for 18 h at ambient temperature (24 °C) with agitation. The solid was then filtered off, and the solution concentration was determined by glc. Data were plotted as the amount of solute adsorbed per gram of alumina versus the equilibrium concentration.

**Double Solute.** A range of solutions were made containing both solutes in equal concentrations. The procedure was the same as above, but the data were plotted as the amount of each solute adsorbed versus the *initial* concentration.

**Surface Areas.** The surface area was obtained from a BET plot of adsorbed nitrogen, measured with a Perkin-Elmer Sorptometer. The specific area of the $\gamma$-alumina was 110 m² g⁻¹.

### Results

Adsorption. Adsorption from solution is controlled by the affinity of the surface for the adsorbate compared to the adsorbate-solvent interaction, i.e. the solubility of the adsorbate is an important factor, and by the relative affinity of the solvent molecules for the surface. Toluene, the solvent used here, will have only a very weak interaction with the alumina surface. Therefore the competition for surface sites between the solvent and the polar adsorbates should be negligible. Both compounds are readily soluble in toluene, which reduces the tendency for the solutes to deposit from solution on to the adsorbent.

The adsorption isotherms for $N$-ethylacetamide and 2-pyrrolidinone are shown in Figure 1. They are very different in shape, with the $N$-ethylacetamide giving a linear partition curve, while the isotherm for 2-pyrrolidinone has a steep initial rise followed by a gradual increase to a plateau region.

The isotherm for 2-pyrrolidinone is characteristic of an adsorbate which has a greater attraction for the surface sites than for the solvent molecules. The steepness of the initial part of the curve reflects the ease of adsorption in the early stages, and extrapolation of the data to low concentration show that ca. $1.2\times10^{-4}$ mol are adsorbed per gram alumina in the region of relatively "strong" adsorption.

The maximum number of possible adsorption sites has been calculated by Knözinger and Ratnasamy$^8$ to be 1.45 $\times10^{19}$ m$^{-2}$. From the BET area, the "strong" adsorption region of the 2-pyrrolidinone isotherm corresponds to 6.5 $\times10^{17}$ molecules m$^{-2}$ and the plateau region to $1.5\times10^{18}$ molecules m$^{-2}$. In a recent paper, Gervasini and Auroux$^9$ noted that, for several basic molecules larger than ammonia, the quantity adsorbed irreversibly from the gas phase was ca. $1.2\times10^{18}$ molecules m$^{-2}$. Considering that the alumina in Gervasini and Auroux's work was pretreated at 773 K, the similarly of the adsorbed amounts is suprising, given that on the heated alumina the adsorption site will be a Lewis acid, i.e. an aluminium ion, and our alumina surface is mainly hydroxyl groups.

The adsorption isotherm for $N$-ethylacetamide (Figure 1) is unusual. At first, sight, it may suggest that the adsorbate is very weakly bound and that, effectively, only the solution retained by the solid during the filtration step is being measured. However, direct measurement by DRIFTS of the adsorbate (see below) that remained on the solid after several washes showed that the $N$-ethylacetamide was present. A linear isotherm can also be interpreted as the solute creating new sites on adsorption,$^{10}$ e.g. by opening pores, and can therefore be a consequence of strong adsorption.

Competitive Adsorption. The relative strengths of interaction of the $N$-ethylacetamide and 2-pyrrolidinone may be obtained by investigating adsorption from a solution containing both adsorbates. The solute molecules then compete for surface sites, and the data may be evaluated by comparison with those from the single solute systems. The results for such a competitive adsorption are shown in Figure 2. The two-solute system is not readily displayed as a conventional adsorption isotherm because the equilibrium concentrations, from a given solution of the two solutes, are not equal. It should be noted that the data in Figure 2 are therefore plotted as adsorption against the initial concentration, where the initial concentrations of solute are equal. For comparison, the data for the adsorption of the single solutes, plotted in the same manner, are also shown. The curves, except for $N$-ethylacetamide, are approximately straight lines.

From Figure 2 it can be seen that the adsorption of 2-pyrrolidinone is little affected by the presence of the other component. On the other hand, the adsorption of the $N$-ethylacetamide is drastically reduced. The conclusion is that the cyclic compound adsorbs more readily than the linear one and blocks the adsorption sites.

Infrared Spectroscopy. The sensitivity of the DRIFTS technique is a function of the wavelength of the irradiation, which results in the signals of adsorbates being weak at high frequencies.

Amides are characterized mainly by the amide A, I, and II bands, corresponding to $\nu(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})$, $\nu(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O})$ and $\delta$ - $(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})+\nu(\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{N})$, respectively. The $\nu(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})$ band at ca. $3300 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ is weak, but the amide I and II bands give good signals. Figures 3 and 4 show the DRIFTS spectra for $N$-ethylacetamide and 2-pyrrolidinone, respectively, on alumina. The dominant signal is the $\nu(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O})$ band in both cases, at $1634 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ for $N$-ethylacetamide and $1668 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ for 2-pyrrolidinone.

Before examining the DRIFTS spectra in detail, it is instructive to discuss the data of Törnkvist et al., $^{1}$ who studied $N$-methylacetamide on surfaces of copper, gold, silicon, and related oxides. They considered that the $N$-methylacetamide adsorbed in essentially three modes: a non-hydrogen-bonded mode, a mode which is hydrogen bonded to the surface, and a mode with intramolecular hydrogen bonding. The extent to which these occurred depended on the surface, with hydrophobic surfaces favoring the non-hydrogen-bonded mode.

Comparing the amide I and II bands for $N$-methylac etamide on a thin $(126 \mathrm{~nm})$ silicon dioxide film with the matrix isolated spectrum and with the liquid spectrum, the following points are made. Free and hydrogen-bonded, adsorbed modes coexist on the silicon dioxide surface, and the stability of the surface layer varies with the substrate: Hydrogen bonding causes the $\nu(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O})$ band to decrease by ca. $50 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and the $\delta(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})+\nu(\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{N})$ band to increase by ca. $50 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$.

The bands for $N$-ethylacetamide are sufficiently similar to those of $N$-methylacetamide to be used for identification, though they are not identical because of substituent effects; e.g., $\nu(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O})$ is at $1715 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ for the ethyl and $1721 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ for the methyl derivatives in the vapor phase. $^{11}$

$N$-Ethylacetamide. In the DRIFTS spectrum of $N$ ethylacetamide adsorbed on alumina (Figure 3), the carbonyl band has a maximum intensity at $1634 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, corresponding to the hydrogen-bonded species. There is no trace of the free adsorbate molecule, as may be expected from the mode of preparation of the sample for DRIFTS, which involved washing the alumina after adsorption.

By comparison with the hydrogen-bonded liquid and adsorbate species for the methyl analogue, $^{1}$ the $\delta(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})$ $+\nu(\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{N})$ band for $N$-ethylacetamide should appear at ca. $1550 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, and measurements on liquid $N$-ethylac etamide gave a value of $1555 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The shoulder on the carbonyl peak, at $1564 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, is a probable candidate for this band.

Nyquist $^{12}$ reports the cis $\delta(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})$ deformation for cyclic lactams at $1450-1459 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ in the vapor. Hydrogen bonding would cause a shift in this frequency, which would be to higher wavenumbers, and Wang et al. $^{13}$ noted a value of $1496 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ for aqueous $\epsilon$-caprolactam from Raman spectroscopy. For cis $N$-methylacetamide in a quenched matrix, Ataka et al. $^{14}$ assigned a hydrogen-bonded $\delta(\mathrm{N}-$ H) deformation to the IR signal at $1485 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. There is no evidence of a peak corresponding to the cis $\delta(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H})$ deformation for $N$-ethylacetamide adsorbed on alumina (Figure 3). The data therefore favor the adsorbed species being in the trans state.

The prominent signal at $1443 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ corresponds to the $\delta_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{Et})+\delta_{\mathrm{as}}(\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{Et})$ band, which is seen in the liquid at ca. $1440 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The origin of the remaining feature at $1381 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ is controversial, but this region of the spectrum may contain information on the conformation of the adsorbate. $^{13}$

2-Pyrrolidinone. The spectrum of liquid 2-pyrrolidinone has a $\nu(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O})$ band at $1681 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The DRIFTS spectrum of 2-pyrrolidinone (Figure 4) shows a sharp $\nu(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O})$ signal at $1668 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, ca. $90 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ down from the vapor phase value, similar to adsorbed $N$-ethylacetamide. The mul- tiple band at $1495,1469,1445,1428 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ agrees well with the corresponding liquid values $(1493,1466,1438$, $1426 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ ), though there are changes in the relative intensities.

Competitive Adsorption of $N$-Ethylacetamide and 2-Pyr- rolidinone. The DRIFTS spectrum obtained from alumina exposed to an equimolar solution of the two solutes was almost identical to that of 2-pyrrolidinone, confirming the result of the adsorption measurements above, that 2-pyr- rolidinone is preferentially adsorbed.

## Discussion

Though the energy difference between the isomers is only $10\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, the trans isomer dominates the solution and vapor phases at ambient temperature because of the high activation energy for isomerization, calculated above. In the work of Ataka et al.,$^{14}$ the cis isomer was generated by heating to $770\ \text{K}$, which overcame the activation barrier. More recently, irradiation has been shown to achieve photoisomerization to the cis state in solution.$^{13}$

The literature data and the present DRIFTS results suggest that the linear molecule is adsorbed in the trans form, as would be expected. The questions which arise are, (a) why is the cyclic species highly preferentially adsorbed from a mixture of the linear and cyclic molecules, and (b) why does the plateau adsorption reach a similar value to systems where the alumina is heated to $773\ \text{K}$?

Consider first a solution of the linear molecule. The species in solution is predominently the trans conformer. This can adsorb via one or both functionalities, and the calculations show that for a single interaction the carbonyl interaction would be favored. Because of the NCO restricted rotation, the N-hydrogen of the trans $N$-ethylacetamide is then directed away from the surface and cannot interact, as may be seen from examination of the molecule in Figure 5b.

If both functionalities are involved, the molecule has to lie on the alumina in such a way that the carbonyl and amine groups are atomically close to the surface. The partial delocalization in the $\text{N-C-O}$ group will make the group planar, and a precursor adsorbed state will have

the N−C−O bond axes parallel to the surface. In this case, two suitable surface sites have to be within reach. The theoretical calculations suggest that the energies of interaction of the trans and cis molecules with two surface sites are very similar. However, those calculations adopt an optimum geometry for freely interacting species, *with no steric constraints*. The geometry of the hydrogen bonding to both functionalities on a real surface will differ for trans and cis configurations and, because of the awkward geometry, should be less favorable for the trans species.

The adsorption data show that a cis (ring) structure is much more readily adsorbed, though the DRIFTS data suggest that the linear molecule is adsorbed at both functionalities and provide some evidence for the adsorbate remaining in the trans form. Törnkvist et al.¹ suggest that, on adsorption, an increase in rotational freedom may occur on certain surfaces. Even though isomerization to the cis conformer would be favorable for adsorption, in this system the high activation energy for isomerization ensures that the adsorbed species remains in the trans form.

Turning now to the extents of adsorption, the linear isotherm observed for *N*-ethylacetamide may be the consequence of an activated process of adsorption. The aluminium surface is hydrated and there will be a high coverage of hydroxyl groups because of the low heat treatment (100 °C). The adsorption behavior suggests that there is a rearrangement or displacement at the surface which gives the trans molecule access to the binding sites.

The shape of the adsorption isotherm for the 2-pyrro- lidinone shows the entirely different behavior between the cyclic and linear molecules. The cyclic molecule approaches the surface with a favorable geometry for two- functionality adsorption. The adsorption evidently takes place readily, and the plateau value for adsorption is comparable to that observed for the several types of amine molecule on the dehydrated alumina.⁹ The theoretical calculations indicate that the 2-pyrrolidinone can adsorb at a single aluminium site with only 7 kJ less energy than at two adjacent sites, and this difference may be less in the case of a real surface because of the steric require- ments. Adsorption at a single site also means that only one oxide/hydroxide/water ligand shell has to be disturbed. Adsorption of 2-pyrrolidinone at a single aluminium site can therefore, in practice, be both energetically and sterically more favorable than for *N*-ethylacetamide at two surface sites.

The question of the maximum adsorption of 2-pyrro- lidinone may also be related to its ability to interact at one surface site. The interaction involves the bound hydroxyl groups, and the correspondence of adsorbate concentrations with and without heat treatment, noted above, suggests that the active hydrated surface sites are all potential acid sites of the dehydrated material.

If these active sites are not simply the lattice sites, the two-site-adsorption mechanism becomes critically de- pendent on the separation distance of the active sites. The ability of 2-pyrrolidinone to interact with both functionalities at a single site, in contrast to *N*-ethylac- etamide, then becomes even more advantageous.
