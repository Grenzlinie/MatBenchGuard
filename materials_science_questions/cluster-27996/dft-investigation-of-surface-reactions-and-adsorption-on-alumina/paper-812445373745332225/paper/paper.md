# Formation and reaction of allylic species on silver surfaces: bond-order conservation Morse-potential analysis

Evgeny Shustorovich

Corporate Research Laboratories, Eastman Kodak Company, Rochester, NY 14650-2001, USA

Received 6 July 1992; accepted for publication 1 September 1992

The reaction energetics, particularly the intrinsic activation barriers for possible reactions involving allylic species $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{X}$, $\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{OH}, \mathrm{O}, \mathrm{Cl}$ on clean and oxygen-preadsorbed $\mathrm{Ag}$ surfaces, have been calculated by using the bond-order conservation Morse-potential (BOC-MP) method. The calculations were made for low coverages of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{X}$ with qualitative corrections for higher coverages. On clean $\mathrm{Ag}$ surfaces, propylene $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{6}$ and allyl alcohol $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{OH}$ are projected to desorb without dissociation, in contrast to allyl chloride $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$, which is projected to desorb only at high coverages but to dissociate at low coverages forming a stable $\pi$-allyl (and atomic chlorine). It is found that the intrinsic activation barrier for dimerization of $\pi$-allyl into 1,5 -hexadiene is very small and the apparent barrier should be mainly of diffusional character. In the presence of preadsorbed hydroxyl $\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{s}}, \pi$-allyl is projected to undergo various transformations producing allyl alcohol, allyl alkoxide, acrolein, and propylene, when most recombination and disproportionation reactions have low intrinsic activation barriers. The BOC-MP model projections are in good agreement with experiment, particularly with the recent HREEL and TPD studies of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$.

## 1. Introduction

The striking difference between ethylene and propylene in their oxidation by oxygen on silver surfaces is that $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CH}_{2}$ can be selectively (at least, by $50 \%$ ) epoxidized to ethylene oxide
$$\mathrm{CH}_{2}-\mathrm{CH}_{2}$$
but $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CHCH}_{3}$ produces practically no propylene oxide
$$\mathrm{CH}_{2}-\mathrm{CHCH}_{3}$$
but undergoes combustion to $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}[1-4]$ or, on $\mathrm{Ag}+\mathrm{Au}$ alloys (rich in gold), selectively forms acrolein $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CHCHO}$ [4]. This difference is commonly explained [1] by the weakness of the allylic $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ bond in $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CH}-\mathrm{CH}_{3}$ compared to the olefinic $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ bond in $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CH}_{2}$, the energies of which are 86 [5] and 110 [6] kcal/mol, respectively. The formation of an allyl intermediate $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2}$ during oxidation of $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{3}$ on $\mathrm{Ag}+\mathrm{Au}$ alloys was inferred from high selectivity to $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCHO}$, and particularly from direct observation of diallyl $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{CH}_{2}$ $\mathrm{CH}=\mathrm{CH}_{2}$ (1,5-hexadiene) [4]. During propylene combustion, the allyl formation has never been corroborated, and its role remains elusive and even controversial. While Madix and coworkers propose that $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}$ is the reactive intermediate formed in the rate-limiting step [3], Carter and Goddard believe that combustion via $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}$ is energetically much less favorable than via an allyl alkoxy intermediate [7]. One can add that acrolein can be easily formed by oxidation of allyl alcohol $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}$ on $\mathrm{Ag}$ surfaces, say $\mathrm{Ag}(110)$ [8]. Here the reaction apparently proceeds via an allyl alkoxy intermediate, and no allyl formation was observed or appears to be necessary.

For the first time, a stable chemisorbed allyl species has recently been identified by Carter et al. during thermally activated dissociation of chemisorbed allyl chloride $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$ on $\mathrm{Ag}(110)$ at $180 \mathrm{~K}$ [9]. The $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}$ intermediate is stable to $\sim 280 \mathrm{~K}$ when it dimerizes to form diallyl (1,5-

hexadiene), which desorbs from the chlorinated surface near 310 K [9]. Most recently, the same authors have studied reactivity of allyl chloride chemisorbed on the oxygen-predosed Ag(110) and found a variety of products including allyl alcohol, acrolein, and propylene [10].

The controversial role of the allyl intermediate in propylene combustion and the richness of transformations of allylic species $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{X}(\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{H}$, Cl, and OH) on Ag surfaces beg for a theoretical understanding. The goal of the present work is a mechanistic analysis of these processes based on calculations of reaction enthalpies $\Delta H$ and activation barriers $\Delta E^{*}$ for elementary steps thought to be relevant to transformations of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{X}$. The calculations have been made by using the bond-order conservation Morse-potential (BOC-MP) method [11,12] which proved to be successful in similar analyses of heterogeneous catalytic processes such as CO hydrogenation to $\mathrm{CH}_{3} \mathrm{OH}$ [12,13], $\mathrm{CH}_{4}$ [12,14] and higher hydrocarbons (Fischer-Tropsch synthesis) [12,15], synthesis and decomposition of $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ [16], decomposition and reduction of NO [17], and oxygen-assisted X-H bond cleavage (X = O, N, C) [18].

## 2. Theoretical method

The analytic BOC-MP equations used to calculate $\Delta H$ and $\Delta E^{*}$ of the appropriate reactions are given in the appendix. Since the BOC-MP formalism has been extensively explained and published [11-18], there is no need to discuss it once more. Here, we will remind the reader only of the methodological principles of the BOC-MP method.

The BOC-MP method [11-18] is a phenomenological analytical approach to reaction energetics on transition metal surfaces. The experimental input consists of the gas-phase bond energies $D_{\mathrm{AB}}$ of $\mathrm{AB}$ molecules involved (reactants, intermediates, and products), which are thermodynamic constants, and the binding energies $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ of atoms A coordinated to a given surface (atomic chemisorption energies), which are basic parameters. The rest is straightforward algebra of maximization of total bond energies under constraints of bond-order conservation. The calculational output includes the heats of molecular chemisorption $Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$ [and, thus, the molecular enthalpy $\Delta H_{\mathrm{AB}}=-\left(D_{\mathrm{AB}}+Q_{\mathrm{AB}}\right)$] and the intrinsic activation barriers $\Delta E^{*}$ for elementary reactions of dissociation, recombination, and disproportionation. It should be stressed that the BOC-MP method can discern the preferred coordination mode of adsorbates (e.g., mono- versus dicoordination), although no knowledge of adsorbate geometries (bond lengths, valence angles, etc.) is needed to calculate the relevant values of $Q$ and $\Delta E^{*}$.

If the experimental values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ are known for the zero-coverage extreme $\theta_{\mathrm{A}} \approx 0$, the BOC-MP method allows one to calculate $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ for finite (medium and high) coverages, the values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ decreasing as $\theta_{\mathrm{A}}$ increases. Accordingly, the zero-coverage values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ are used to directly calculate the zero-coverage values of $Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$ and $\Delta E^{*}$ or to make approximate calculations for the finite coverage values of $Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$ and $\Delta E^{*}$.

The values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ are the only empirical parameters used from the realm of chemisorption, and their accuracy may be critical for the validity of the BOC-MP projections on surface reactions. Unfortunately, for many atoms on many surfaces, the experimental estimates of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ are not accurate enough or simply not available. The unknown atomic chemisorption energy $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ can be calculated from the experimentally known molecular chemisorption energy $Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$ (the reversed BOC-MP problem), although such a value of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ may not be very accurate (because the values of $Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$ are rather insensitive to $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$, cf. the appendix). Whenever there are uncertainties in $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$, it is recommended to perform calculations for a reasonable range of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ to see how sensitive are the model projections to the choice of this basic parameter. The BOC-MP values of $Q$ and $\Delta E^{*}$ are explicitly interrelated (cf. the appendix), the dissociation activation barrier $\Delta E_{\mathrm{AB}}^{*}$ being particularly dependent on the values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}, Q_{\mathrm{B}}$ of the dissociation products. For this reason, in cases when the calculated value of $Q$ differs greatly from its (reliable) experimental value, the latter may be used to make the calculated values of $\Delta E^{*}$ more accurate. Because the values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$

decrease with coverage $\theta_{\mathrm{A}}$, the BOC-MP formalism explicitly predicts that $\Delta E_{\mathrm{AB}}^{*}$ increases as $\theta_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{B}}$ increase.

Finally, one should comment on the BOC-MP analysis of surface reactivity. In the Arrhenius form of the reaction rate constant $r$,

$$
r=A \exp \left(-\Delta E^{*} / R T\right), \quad \text { (1) }
$$

$A$ is a preexponential factor and $\Delta E^{*}$ is the activation energy (barrier). Because of their exponential effect, the values of $\Delta E^{*}$ are usually critical, and the BOC-MP method deals with them. In the BOC-MP analysis, two reactions are compared by the values of $\Delta E^{*}$ only, ignoring differences in the values of $A$. The analysis is best justified for a given reaction on a given surface, say fcc (111), but of two different metals, say $\operatorname{Ag}(111)$ versus $\mathrm{Cu}(111)$. The next best case is two reactions of the same order on a given surface, particularly desorption versus dissociation of an adsorbed molecule $\mathrm{AB}_{\mathrm{s}}$. Here one has to compare two first-order reactions, desorption $\mathrm{AB}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{AB}_{\mathrm{g}}$ and dissociation $\mathrm{AB}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{s}}$ with the activation barriers (energies) $\Delta E_{\text {diss }}^{*}=\Delta E_{\mathrm{AB}, \mathrm{s}}^{*}$ and $\Delta E_{\text {des }}^{*}=Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$, respectively. Remember that the typical interrelation of the preexponentials is $A_{\text {des }} \gg A_{\text {diss }}$, when usually $A_{\text {des }} / A_{\text {diss }} \simeq 10^{3}-10^{4}$ $[19,20]$. Thus, if $\Delta E_{\mathrm{AB}, \mathrm{s}}^{*}>Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$, the $\mathrm{AB}_{\mathrm{s}}$ molecules will predominantly be desorbed intact. On the other hand, if $\Delta E_{\mathrm{AB}, \mathrm{s}}^{*}<Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$, both dissociation and desorption may occur.

Additional complications arise for secondorder reactions of recombination $\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{AB}_{\mathrm{s}, \mathrm{g}}$ and disproportionation $\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{s}}$. Here the BOC-MP value of $\Delta E^{*}$ (for example, eq. (A.9)) is the intrinsic barrier $\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*}$ for two species, $\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{s}}$ and $\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{s}}$, to couple provided they are in close contact. This close contact, however, can happen only through diffusion, so that the diffusion barrier $\Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}$ should also contribute to the apparent activation barrier $\Delta E_{\text {app }}^{*}\left(\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*}, \Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}\right)$. The theory of diffusion-influenced activation barriers is very complex and so far is very approximate [21]. In extreme cases, when $\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*} \gg \Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}$ (reaction controlled) or $\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*} \ll \Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}$ (diffusion controlled), one can assume that $\Delta E_{\text {app }}^{*} \simeq \Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*}$ or $\Delta E_{\text {app }}^{*} \simeq \Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}$, respectively. But in the general case of recombinative desorption, when $\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*}$ is comparable with $\Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}$, one can only say that $\Delta E_{\text {app }}^{*}$ lies between $\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*}$ and $\Delta E_{\text {dif }}^{*}$ [21b]. For diffusion-controlled reactions, one can expect non-Arrhenius behavior at higher temperatures even for atom-atom recombination reactions where no steric effects are present [21b]. In general, for recombinative desorption processes the activation energy is not necessarily identical to the desorption energy [21b]. Thus, the apparent activation barriers for dissociation (which is the reverse reaction of recombination) can also be affected by diffusion. Because of all these complications, the BOC-MP values of $\Delta E_{\text {int }}^{*}$ will be used below for comparison with the experimental values of $\Delta E_{\text {app }}^{*}$ with reserve and clarifications.

## 3. Results

As said above, the first step is selection of values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$, namely for $\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{O}, \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{Cl}$ on $\operatorname{Ag}(111)$, the surface not only convenient for model calculations but also representative for realistic (polycrystalline and supported) Ag catalysts. The experimental estimates have been reported only for $\mathrm{H}$ where $Q_{\mathrm{H}} \leq 52 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ [22a] and for $\mathrm{O}$ where $Q_{\mathrm{O}} \approx 80 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ for low oxygen coverage [1,22b]. For $\mathrm{Cl}$, by analogy with other transition metals one can assume that $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}$ $<Q_{\mathrm{O}}$. For example, for $\mathrm{Mo}(110), \mathrm{Pd}(111)$, and $\operatorname{Pt}(111), Q_{\mathrm{O}}=125,87$, and $85 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (cf. table 1 in ref. [12]) compared to $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}=95,59$, and 53 $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (cf. table 5 in ref. [11]), respectively. Making periodic extrapolation to $\operatorname{Ag}(111)$, one can estimate $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}<53 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (which indeed is much smaller than $Q_{\mathrm{O}} \approx 80 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ ). Given the uncertainties of this choice, in the present work we will explore a set of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}=50,55$, and 60 $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$. For $\mathrm{C}$, we have chosen the value of $Q_{\mathrm{C}}=133 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ from the BOC-MP calculations (via eq. (A.5)) to fit the experimental value of $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}}=9 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ on $\operatorname{Ag}(110)$ [23]. The low coverage values of $Q_{\mathrm{A}}$ used are given in table 1.

Coordination modes of the molecules in question and the relevant bond energies needed for calculations of $Q$ are shown in fig. 1. Remember that the BOC-MP method explicitly calculates the values of $Q$ for mono- and dicoordinated $\left(\eta^{1}\right.$

and $\eta^{2}$) species only. Accordingly, even large and complex molecules can be treated if they are effectively coordinated via one or two groups. Because within the BOC-MP formalism (see eq. (A.6)), the chelated $\eta^{2}$ coordination is always preferred over each of two possible $\eta^{1}$ coordina tions, we have considered for all allylic species $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CH}-\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{X}$ a chelated structure $(\eta^{2})$ coordinated via two groups, $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{X}$, where $\mathrm{X}$ is $\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{Cl}$, or $\mathrm{OH}$ (bound to $\mathrm{CH}_{2}$ ) and $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ (more precisely, $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CHR}$ ) is coordinated as $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=$ $\mathrm{CH}_{2}$, which is treated as a quasiatom ( $\eta^{1}$ coordinated) with $Q=9 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$. This coordination mode is consistent with experimental findings for $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}$ [8b] and $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{Cl}$ [9,10]. For $\sigma$-allyl chemisorption, the realistic coordination will be intermediate between two extremes, $\eta^{1}$ coordination via the terminal $\mathrm{C}$ atom only and $\eta^{2}$ coordination via both the terminal $\mathrm{C}$ atom and $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ bond (an analog of the chelated allylic species considered above). One can clarify that this model $\eta^{2}$ coordination corresponds to the conventional $\eta^{3}$ coordination (via three carbon atoms). Finally, the $\pi$-allyl chemisorption energy has been calculated for symmetric $\eta^{2}$ coordination via two terminal $\mathrm{C}$ atoms.

The calculated values of $Q_{\mathrm{AB}}$ (for details, see the appendix) are shown in table 1. Note that changes in $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}$ by $10 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ change $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}$ only by $2 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (from 13 to $15 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ ). Also, the energy difference between the two extremes of the $\sigma$-allyl coordination ( $\eta^{1}$ and $\eta^{2}$ ) is only $2 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, which is much less than the chemisorption energy of the (additional) $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ group $(9 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol})$. Indeed, from eq. (A.6), for the $\eta^{2}$ coordination we have $Q_{\eta^{2}}=31+9-(31$ $\times 9) /(31+9)=33 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, compared to $Q_{\eta^{1}}=$ $31 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$.

Table 1 summarizes the heats of chemisorption $(Q)$ and the total energies in the gas phase $(D)$ [24] and chemisorbed $(D+Q=-\Delta H)$ states for various molecular species representing reactants, intermediates, and products. The BOC-MP calculated values of $Q$ are compared with experiment [2,8-10,25] in table 2. The agreement is excellent, within $1 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, with the only exception of hydroxyl, which we will discuss later.

The data in table 1 elucidate the preferred coordination of allyl. Ab inito calculations [5] project that in the gas phase the symmetric $(\pi)$ $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}$ is more stable than the unsymmetric $(\sigma)$ $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}$ by $\Delta D=14 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$. For chemisorption energies $Q$, the BOC-MP calculations project the opposite relation when $\sigma$-allyl is bound stronger than $\pi$-allyl by $\Delta Q=6-8 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, where the range in $\Delta Q=2 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ reflects the lower and upper limit of $Q$ for $\sigma$-allyl chemisorption (see above). But even for the upper limit, the inequality $\Delta D>\Delta Q$ holds, so that the symmetric $\pi$-allyl coordination appears to remain preferred in the chemisorption state by $\Delta(D+Q)=8-6 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$.

The oxygen-assisted allylic C–H bond cleavage in propylene is projected to occur with the extremely low intrinsic activation barrier of $\Delta E_{\mathrm{CH}}^{*} \approx 2$ kcal/mol. Although the allylic C–H bond cleavage leads to the formation of $\pi-\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, \mathrm{~s}}$, the latter can hardly be observed because it will undergo oxidation to allyl alkoxide ($\Delta E^{*} \approx 0$) and further to acrolein ($\Delta E^{*}=8-9$ kcal/mol) or even directly to acrolein via the disproportionation $\pi$-$\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, \mathrm{~s}}+\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{4} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{s}}$ ($\Delta E^{*} \approx 0$). True, these intrinsic activation barriers $\Delta E^{*} \approx 0$ are just the lower limit of the real barriers where the diffusional contributions may be significant for strongly bound and/or large adsorbates. The stability of acrolein and its oxidation pathways as well as other routes for epoxidation and combustion of propylene are beyond the scope of the present work.

On a clean Ag(111), we find the desorption energy of allyl alcohol $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}$ (14 kcal/mol) to be smaller than the activation barriers for dissociation of its O–H and C–O bonds (16 kcal/mol for either cleavage). Assuming the pre-exponential for desorption to be not smaller (actually, it may be much larger [19,20]) than that for dissociation, we expect $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}$ to desorb intact under thermoactivation. By contrast, on the oxygen-predosed Ag(111), the O–H bond cleavage requires the small intrinsic activation barrier of $\Delta E_{\mathrm{OH}}^{*}=4$ kcal/mol, so that one expects $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}$ to convert mainly to allyl alkoxide $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}$. As already mentioned, once $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}$ is formed, it easily cleaves the alkoxy C–H bond producing acrolein $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCHO}_{\mathrm{s}}$. Note that the activation barrier for this cleavage practically does not change in the presence of (low coverage) $\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}$ or $\mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}$ (9, 8, and 7 kcal/mol, respectively), which is in sharp contrast to the allylic C–H bond cleavage, where oxygen assistance dramatically decreases the activation barrier (cf. table 3). If $\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{s}}$ is available, the rehydrogenation $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}$ may occur with a moderate barrier of 11 kcal/mol.

Now let us turn to allyl chloride. As mentioned, because of uncertainties in the value of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}$, we have tried a set of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}=50$, 55, and 60 kcal/mol leading to $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}=13$, 14, or 15 kcal/mol, respectively. Accordingly, the dissociation reaction $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \pi-\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, \mathrm{~s}}+\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathrm{s}}$ may be slightly endothermic, almost thermoneutral, or slightly exothermic ($\Delta H=5$, 1, or -3 kcal/mol, respectively). Regardless of the choice of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}$, however, the activation barriers $\Delta E_{\left(\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}\right) \mathrm{Cl}}^{*}$ for dissociation of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathrm{s}}$ are smaller than those for its desorption $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}$ (11–8 versus 13–15 kcal/mol, respectively). Thus, during thermoactivation of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathrm{s}}$ we expect both the desorption (at higher coverages) and dissociation (at lower coverages) to occur, unlike $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{3, \mathrm{~s}}$ and $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}$ where only desorption is expected (at all coverages). Further heating will result in the recombination $2 \pi-\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, \mathrm{~s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10, \mathrm{~s}}$ (1,5-hexadiene). This dimerization is hugely exothermic ($\Delta H=-26$ kcal/mol) and, by our estimates, has practically no intrinsic activation

barrier, although there should be a diffusional barrier to bring two allylic species in close proximity.

Consider now reaction of $C_3H_5Cl$ on oxygen-predosed Ag(111). Because in the presence of $O_s$ C-H bond cleavage leads to the formation of $OH_s$, one can assume both $O_s$ and $OH_s$ available. As shown above, the thermoactivated dissociation $C_3H_5Cl_s \rightarrow \pi-C_3H_{5,s} + Cl_s$ can readily occur. Once $\pi-C_3H_{5,s}$ is formed, it can interact with $OH_s$ to recombine into $CH_2CHCH_2OH_s$ with $\Delta E^*=2$ kcal/mol or disproportionate into $CH_2CHCH_{3,s}$ and $O_s$ with $\Delta E^*=8$ kcal/mol. Thus, while heating, one can expect first the formation of allyl alcohol, and then propylene. Of course, whenever $CH_2CHCH_2OH_s$ is present, it will interact with $O_s$ and/or $OH_s$ to form $CH_2CHCH_2O_s$ and $CH_2CHCHO_s$, the intrinsic activation barriers being persistently smaller for $OH_s$ than for $O_s$. If the dissociation $CH_2CHCH_2O_s \rightarrow CH_2CHCHO_s + H_s$ ($\Delta E_{CH}^*=9$ kcal/mol) occurs, the released $H_s$ can easily hydrogenate $\pi$-allyl to propylene ($\Delta E^* \approx 0$). Another less trivial and possibly more favorable energetic pathway to acrolein and propylene is the disproportionation $CH_2CHCH_2O_s + \pi-CH_2CHCH_{2,s} \rightarrow CH_2CHCHO_s + CH_2CHCH_{3,s}$ with $\Delta E^*=3$ kcal/mol. Summing up, the new features of thermal activation of $C_3H_5Cl_s$ should be the formation of allyl alcohol, allyl alkoxide, acrolein, and propylene.

## 4. Discussion

The experimental heats of chemisorption on various silver surfaces, particularly for Ag(111) and Ag(110), are very close, the heats typically being slightly higher for Ag(110) [1]. For example, the oxygen binding energy $Q_O$ is ~80 kcal/mol on Ag(111) but ~81 kcal/mol on Ag(110) [22]. Thus, although our BOC-MP analysis has been made for Ag(111), for all practical purposes the projections remain valid for other Ag surfaces, in particular, to Ag(110) for which most of the experimental data are available. (To be precise, because the values of $Q_A$ may be higher on Ag(110) than on Ag(111), for a given molecule, say $C_3H_5Cl$, one can expect dissociation to be relatively more pronounced on Ag(110) but nondissociative desorption on Ag(111).) The heats of chemisorption are compared with experiment in table 2. As already mentioned, the BOC-MP value of $Q_{OH}$ is strongly underestimated, and this appears to persist for other metals as well. For example, for low oxygen coverage on Pt(111), the kinetic experimental estimate is $Q_{OH} \approx 61$ kcal/mol [26a], and on a Pt wire, under oxygen-depleted conditions (low coverage $\theta_{OH}$) the experimental LIF estimate is $Q_{OH} \approx 60$ kcal/mol [26b], compared to the BOC-MP value of ~40 kcal/mol (for Pt(111) with $Q_O=85$ kcal/mol [12]). Similarly, for Cu(110), the thermodynamic experimental estimate is $Q_{OH} \approx 75$ kcal/mol [27], while the BOC-MP value would be ~60 kcal/mol (assuming the experimental value of $Q_O \approx 115$ kcal/mol [27]). It should be stressed, however, that from our extensive experience with BOC-MP calculations of $Q_{AB}$ [12-18], the failure is unique for OH (and we do not understand reasons for this uniqueness). As seen from table 2, for all other adspecies the calculated and experimental values of $Q_{AB}$ agree within 1 kcal/mol.

Now let us turn to reaction of allylic species summarized in tables 3 and 4. First of all, the oxygen-assisted C-H bond cleavage appears to be easily activated, which makes it a natural beginning of combustion of propylene, although the following steps are far from certain. Clearly, the allyl intermediate can hardly be isolated because it may react with oxygen and/or hydroxyl to form allyl alkoxy and acrolein with small activation.

We project that on clean Ag surfaces, allyl alcohol will desorb intact with $Q=14$ kcal/mol but on oxygen-predosed surfaces it will produce allyl alkoxide ($\Delta E^*=4$ kcal/mol) which, in turn, proceeds to acrolein ($\Delta E^*=8-9$ kcal/mol). Also, one can expect the rehydrogenation of $CH_2CHCH_2O_s$ to $CH_2CHCH_2OH_s$ ($\Delta E^*=11$ kcal/mol). Consistently, on the clean Ag(110) surface, $CH_2CHCH_2OH_s$ was found to desorb molecularly from states at 230 and 265 K corresponding to $Q=13.6$ and 15.8 kcal/mol (at the highest coverages studied) or at 255 K corresponding to $Q=15.1$ kcal/mol (at the lowest coverage studied) [8a]. When oxygen is pread-

sorbed, $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}_{s}$ reacts with $\mathrm{O}_{s}$ to form $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{s}$, which further dehydrogenates to $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCHO}_{s}$, when $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}$ (as well as $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$) is also observed to form [8a].

As far as allyl chloride is concerned, we predict that on clean $\mathrm{Ag}$ surfaces $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{Cl}_{s}$ will desorb with $Q=13-15 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ and dissociate into $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}_{s}$ with $\Delta E^{*}=8-11$ $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$. Since $Q>\Delta E^{*}$, we expect both dissociation and desorption of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$ to occur depending on coverage. If we use the common approximate interrelation [3,8-10,25]

$$
\Delta E^{*}(\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}) \approx 0.06 T(\mathrm{~K}), \tag{2}
$$

we expect $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{s}$ at low coverages to dissociate at $135-185 \mathrm{~K}$. Once $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s}$ is formed, it is projected to dimerize to 1,5-hexadiene with $\Delta H=$ $-26 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ and $\Delta E^{*} \approx 0$, provided that two allyl species are in close contact. Finally, we find that the $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s}$ species should have a $\pi$-allyl structure.

In summary, the BOC-MP calculations project the following sequence of surface reactions.

$$
\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{g} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{s}, \tag{3}
$$

$$
\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{s} \rightarrow \pi-\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s}+\mathrm{Cl}_{s}, \tag{4}
$$

$$
\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{s} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{g} \quad\left(\text { high } \theta_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}\right), \tag{5}
$$

$$
2 \pi-\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10, s}, \tag{6}
$$

$$
\mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10, s} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10, g}. \tag{7}
$$

The model projections are in good agreement with experimental findings about reactions of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}_{s}$ on $\mathrm{Ag}(110)[9,10]$. First of all, after exposure to $2 \mathrm{~L}\left(1 \mathrm{~L}=1 \times 10^{-6}\right.$ Torr $\left.\cdot \mathrm{s}\right)$ of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$ at $110 \mathrm{~K}$, the HREEL spectrum indicates molecular chemisorption via both the $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ bond and $\mathrm{Cl}$ lone electron pair. No molecular desorption of allyl chloride is observed for exposures of less than $3 \mathrm{~L}$. When the surface is annealed, dissociation into $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}_{s}$ is observed in the HREEL spectra. The dissociation begins around $140 \mathrm{~K}$ and is completed by $\sim 180$ $\mathrm{K}$, with the peak temperature of $\sim 160 \mathrm{~K}$ corresponding to $\Delta E_{\text {dis }}^{*} \approx 10 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$. For comparison, our calculations give $\Delta E_{\text {dis }}^{*}=8-11 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ $(T=135-185 \mathrm{~K})$ depending on the choice of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}$ $=50-60 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, respectively. The experimental estimate of $\Delta E_{\text {dis }}^{*}=10 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ corresponds to the BOC-MP value of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}=53 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, consistent with the periodic extrapolated value of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}} \leq 53 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (see above). Judging by the HREEL spectrum, the coordination of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s}$ has the symmetric $\pi$-allyl geometry, as we predicted. The dimerization $2 \pi-\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5, s} \rightarrow 1,5-$ hexadiene $\left(\mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10}\right)$ begins at $\sim 250 \mathrm{~K}\left(\Delta E^{*} \approx 15\right.$ $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ ) and is complete by $290 \mathrm{~K}$. $\mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10}$ desorbs at $310 \mathrm{~K}$ with $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{10}} \approx 19 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, which is twice as much as $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}}$ on oxygenated or chlorinated Ag surfaces [1,28]. Importantly, when a lower exposure of $0.5 \mathrm{~L}$ was used (resulting in a submonolayer coverage), the $\pi$-allyl species appeared to be mostly unreacted after annealing the surface to $310 \mathrm{~K}$, that is, the apparent activation barrier is higher than $19 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$. As the authors pointed out [10], this suggests that the dimerization reaction is limited by the diffusion of the $\pi$-allyl species rather than by the intrinsic reaction barrier for two of them to couple, in agreement with our projection.

When the $\mathrm{Ag}(110)$ surface is exposed to $3 \mathrm{~L}$ of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$, a desorption peak appears near $190 \mathrm{~K}$. With an exposure of $4 \mathrm{~L}$ or more, a desorption peak is observed at $170 \mathrm{~K}$. The relevant desorption energies $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}$ are 12 and $10 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, respectively, consistent with the zero-coverage BOC-MP value of $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}=14 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, which would correspond to the experimental estimate of $\Delta E_{\text {dis }}^{*}=10 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (cf. table 4).

One can mention that in the recent cluster-type quantum chemical calculations, the values of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}$ on $\mathrm{Ag}$ surfaces were estimated to be 73-91 $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ with the recommended average value of $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}} \approx 82 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ [7b]. If we use such high values, our conclusion that dissociation of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$ is preferred remains unchanged (even enhanced) but the quantitative agreement with experiment would become very poor. In particular, for $Q_{\mathrm{Cl}}=$ $80 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ the chemisorption energy of $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}$ would be $Q_{\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Cl}}=21 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, corresponding to the desorption temperature of $\sim 350 \mathrm{~K}$, and the dissociation activation barrier would be $\Delta E_{\left(\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5}\right) \mathrm{Cl}, s}^{*}=1 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, corresponding to the dissociation temperature of $\sim 20 \mathrm{~K}$.

CH_{2}CHCH_{3,s} \stackrel{10}{\rightarrow} CH_{2}CHCH_{3,g}, \tag{16}
$$

$$
CH_{2}CHCHO_{s} \stackrel{14}{\rightarrow} CH_{2}CHCHO_{g}. \tag{17}
$$

Now let us compare the reaction set (4)-(17) with results of the HREEL and TPD study [10]. There is no reaction between $C_3H_5Cl_s$ and $O_s$ at 110 K, but when the sample is annealed to 180 K, $C_3H_5Cl_s$ dissociates (reaction (4)) and partially combusts providing the initial source of $OH_s$. The authors suggest [10] that reaction (8) occurs at ~200 K, which corresponds to the activation barrier of ~12 kcal/mol. Our estimate for the intrinsic recombination barrier of reaction (8) is only 2 kcal/mol. The rest of 10 kcal/mol appears to be of diffusional origin, similarly to the dimerization $2C_3H_5\rightarrow C_6H_{10}$, where the diffusion barrier may be as high as 15-20 kcal/mol. The important point, however, is that of two recombination reactions, (8) and (9), involving the same reactants (diffusional conditions being equal), we project the formation of allyl alcohol (reaction (8)) with the smaller intrinsic barrier preferred over the formation of propylene (reaction (9)), in agreement with experiment. Subsequent annealing to 250 K reveals the formation of $CH_2CHCH_2O_s$ and $OH_s$ followed by desorption of $H_2O$ at 255 K and by desorption of a small amount of $CH_2CHCH_3$ at 260 K. The separate desorption of $H_2O$ and $CH_2CHCH_3$ occurring at close temperatures (255 and 260 K) is consistent with the BOC-MP projections that water and propylene are formed by different reactions but with practically equal activation barriers (reactions (10)-(13)). Again, two recombination reactions, (10) and (12), are projected to be intrinsically nonactivated with the apparent barrier (whatever it may be) of mainly diffusional character.

When the surface is annealed to 290 K, acrolein and propylene concurrently desorb [10]. This is consistent with the low-barrier disproportionation between allyl alkoxide and $\pi$-allyl (reaction (14)) when $CH_2CHCHO_s$ and $CH_2CHCH_{3,s}$ are formed at temperatures exceeding the desorption temperatures of both molecules (225 and 215 K, respectively [10]). Consistently, other (diffusional) conditions being equal, the consecutive reactions (eq. (15)) with the higher intrinsic barrier appear to be less favorable.

To the best of our knowledge, the only theoretical work dealing with (some aspects of) the formation and reaction of allylic species on Ag surfaces is quantum chemical calculations by Carter and Goddard (CG) [7]. Because most of the CG work was devoted to expoxidation and combustion of ethylene, but only marginally to reaction of propylene, a few CG results are ap-

propriate for comparison with ours. For allylic species, the values of $Q$ (actually, the heats of formation) were cited only for $C_{3} H_{6}$ and the allyl alkoxide $CH_{2} CHCH_{2} O$, namely 9 and 50 kcal/mol, very close to our values of 10 and 59 kcal/mol, respectively. Also, we and CG used practically the same values of $Q_{X}$ for $X=O$ and OH, namely 80 and 55 versus 78 and 55 kcal/mol, respectively (but not so for $Q_{Cl}$ , as discussed before). Not surprisingly, the enthalpies $\Delta H$ for reactions including $CH_{2} CHCH_{2} O$ were close in both calculations. In particular, for

$$\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{6, \mathrm{~s}}+2 \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}},\qquad(18)$$

$$\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCHO}_{\mathrm{g}}+\mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}},\qquad(19)$$

$$\mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mathrm{OH}_{\mathrm{s}}\qquad(20)$$

from table 1 we find $\Delta H=-40,-4$ , and -20kcal / mol, compared to the CG values of $\Delta H=$ -44, -9, and -29 kcal/mol, respectively. Ac- cordingly, the conclusion that allyl alkoxide may be an intermediate in oxidation of allyl alcohol to acrolein is common for both the CG and BOC-MP analyses. (True, there is no dispute about this mechanism which has been suggested by experi-ment [8].)

We differ from CG, however, in our conclu- sions on the role of allyl in oxidation of propy- lene. Unfortunately, CG did not calculate the value of $Q_{C_{3} H_{5}}$ to be compared with our estimate. Also, there is no point to compare the activation barriers $\Delta E^{*}$ because we directly calculate the barriers (cf. the appendix) but CG make only indirect estimates, mostly based on thermody- namic constraints [7]. In particular, CG estimatedΔE* ≈0 for eq. (18), which lead to the conclu- sion that allyl alkoxide should be the major inter- mediate in combustion of propylene at high oxy- gen coverage [7]. This conclusion was tested and rejected in the specially designed experiments [2].

## 5. Concluding remarks

The BOC-MP projections concerning reactivity of allylic species $C_{3} H_{5} X, X=H, OH, O, Cl$ , on clean and oxygen-preadsorbed Ag surfaces, con- sistently correlate with experimental observations revealing new and rich chemistry. Given the sim- plicity and rigor of the BOC-MP method com- bined with the very little use of empirical param- eters, this consistency is remarkable indeed. With the exception of hydroxyl, the calculated heats of chemisorption of molecular adsorbates quantita- tively agree with experiment. Accordingly, for desorption, where the BOC-MP intrinsic activa- tion barriers have the closest relevance to experi- ment, the agreement is very persistent and often quantitative. For dissociation-recombination and disproportionation reactions, where the experi- mental activation barriers should be affected by diffusion, the model projections could be made mostly at the qualitative and comparative level. Nevertheless, there were no BOC-MP model pro- jections that were inconsistent with experimental observations. Because the available experimental data on allylic systems $C_{3} H_{5} X$ are rather limited,the theoretical projections summarized in tables 3 and 4 may provide a framework for future experi- mental work.

### Note added in proof

Most recently, we have developed an analytic formalism to express the apparent activation bar- riers $\Delta E_{app }^{*}$ in terms of the appropriate intrinsic $\Delta E_{int }^{*}$ and diffusion $\Delta E_{dif }^{*}$ barriers for surface reactions of recombination $A_{s}+B_{s} \to AB_{s}$ andrecombinative desorption $A_{s}+B_{s} \rightleftharpoons AB_{s} \to AB_{g}$ [Surf. Sci., to be submitted]. We have shown that the endothermic recombinations should typically be reaction controlled but strongly exothermic recombinations may be diffusion controlled. The values of $\Delta E_{app }^{*}$ have been estimated for a variety of reactions, in good agreement with experiment. In particular, we have corroborated the sugges-tion made in the present work that on Ag(110)the recombinations $C_{3} H_{5, s}+C_{3} H_{5, s} \to C_{6} H_{10, s}$ (reaction (7)) and $C_{3} H_{5, s}+OH_{s} \to C_{3} H_{5} OH_{s}$ (reaction (8)) are diffusion controlled.

### Appendix

Given below are the BOC-MP equations used to calculate the values of $Q$ and $\Delta E^{*}$ at low coverages listed in tables 1-4. For derivation of these equations, the reader is referred to refs. [11a,12].

#### A.1. Heats of adsorption

For an adatom A in an $n$-fold site, $\mathrm{M}_{n}-\mathrm{A}$,
$$
Q_{\mathrm{A}}=Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}(2-1 / n), \tag{A.1}
$$
where $Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}$ is the two-center $\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{A}$ bond energy.

For a monocoordinated $(\eta^{1})$ admolecule $\mathrm{AB}$ with the bond energy $D_{\mathrm{AB}}$ in the $\mathrm{M}_{n}-\mathrm{AB}$ site where $\mathrm{AB}$ is normal to a surface with the A end down, there are three cases:

(A) Strong bonding
$$
Q_{\mathrm{AB}}=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}{Q_{\mathrm{A}}+D_{\mathrm{AB}}} \tag{A.2}
$$
for molecular radicals retaining localized unpaired electrons such as
$\dot{\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{H}_{2}, \dot{\mathrm{O}} \mathrm{H}$ or $\dot{\mathrm{O}} \mathrm{C} \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{R}$.

(B) Weak bonding
$$
Q_{\mathrm{AB}}=\frac{Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}^{2}}{Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}+D_{\mathrm{AB}}} \tag{A.3}
$$
for closed-shell molecules such as $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}, \mathrm{ROH}$, or $\mathrm{RCHO}$ coordinated in an on-top position ($n=1$) via O.

(C) Medium bonding
$$
Q_{\mathrm{AB}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Q_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}{Q_{\mathrm{A}}+D_{\mathrm{AB}}}+\frac{Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}^{2}}{\frac{1}{3} Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}+D_{\mathrm{AB}}}\right) \tag{A.4}
$$
for monovalent carbon radicals such as $\mathrm{CH}_{2} \dot{\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{H}$ and $\sigma-\dot{\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2}$.

For dicoordinated $(\eta^{2})$ admolecules, we will employ the following formulas.

(A) Homonuclear molecule $\mathrm{A}_{2}$
$$
Q_{\mathrm{A}_{2}}=\frac{\frac{9}{2} Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}^{2}}{3 Q_{0 \mathrm{~A}}+8 D_{\mathrm{A}_{2}}}. \tag{A.5}
$$

Polyatomic molecules coordinated via two A atoms with the A-A vector parallel to a surface can also be treated via eq. (A.5), where $D_{\mathrm{A}_{2}}$ stands for the total energy of all bonds formed by the A atom. For example, $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{C}=\mathrm{CH}_{2}\right)$ can be treated by eq. (A.5) with $D_{\mathrm{A}_{2}}=355 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ [29].

(B) Chelated molecule $\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{X}-\mathrm{B}$
$$
Q_{\mathrm{AB}(\mathrm{X})}=Q_{\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{X})}+Q_{\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{X})}-\frac{Q_{\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{X})} Q_{\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{X})}}{Q_{\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{X})}+Q_{\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{X})}}, \tag{A.6}
$$
where $Q_{\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{X})}$ and $Q_{\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{X})}$ are chemisorption energies for the monocoordination via A and B, respectively.

Examples are the allylic species $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CH}-$ $\mathrm{CH}_{2}-\mathrm{Y}, \mathrm{Y}=\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{OH}, \mathrm{Cl}, \dot{\mathrm{O}}$, coordinated via both $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CHR}$ and $\mathrm{Y}$ groups. We assume the $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CHR}$ chemisorption energy to be the same as for $\mathrm{CH}_{2}=\mathrm{CH}_{2}$ (eq. (A.5)). For the closed-shell molecules $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{Y}, \mathrm{Y}=\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{OH}, \mathrm{Cl}$, we calculate the chemisorption energy via $\mathrm{Y}$ within the weak-bonding formalism (see eq. (A.3)). For the radical $\mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{H}_{5} \dot{\mathrm{O}}$, where the monocoordination via $\dot{\mathrm{O}}$ is similar to that in $\dot{\mathrm{O}} \mathrm{H}$, we use the strong-bonding formalism (eq. (A.2)) but for $\sigma-\dot{\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{CHCH}_{2}$, monocoordinated similar to $\dot{\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{R}$, the medium-bonding formalism (eq. (A.4)) is proper. The the $\pi$-allyl coordination, $\left[\mathrm{CH}_{2} \cdots \mathrm{CH} \cdots \mathrm{CH}_{2}\right]$, where $\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{B}$, eq. (A.6) is reduced to
$$
Q_{\mathrm{A}_{2}(\mathrm{X})}=\frac{3}{2} Q_{\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{X})}, \tag{A.7}
$$
where $Q_{\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{X})}$ is calculated via eq. (A.2) for the quasiatom $\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{CH}_{2}$. Here $Q_{\mathrm{A}}=Q_{\mathrm{CH}_{2}}=56$ $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (via eq. (A.2) for $Q_{\mathrm{C}}=133 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$) and $D_{\mathrm{AB}}=130 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$ (cf. fig. 1).

#### A.2. Activation barriers for dissociation

$$
\begin{gathered}
A B_{s} \rightarrow A_{s}+B_{s} \\
\Delta E_{\mathrm{AB}, \mathrm{s}}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta H+\frac{Q_{\mathrm{A}} Q_{\mathrm{B}}}{Q_{\mathrm{A}}+Q_{\mathrm{B}}}\right), \tag{A.8}
\end{gathered}
$$

where
$$\Delta H=D_{\mathrm{AB}}+Q_{\mathrm{AB}}-Q_{\mathrm{A}}-Q_{\mathrm{B}}.$$

### A.3. Activation barrier for disproportionation
reactions $A_{s}+B C_{s} \rightarrow A B_{s}+C_{s}$

$$\Delta E_{(\mathrm{AB}) \mathrm{C}, s}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta H+\frac{Q_{\mathrm{AB}} Q_{\mathrm{C}}}{Q_{\mathrm{AB}}+Q_{\mathrm{C}}}\right), \tag{A.9}$$

where
$$\Delta H=D+Q_{\mathrm{A}}+Q_{\mathrm{BC}}-Q_{\mathrm{AB}}-Q_{\mathrm{C}},$$
$$D=D_{\mathrm{A}}+D_{\mathrm{BC}}-D_{\mathrm{AB}}-D_{\mathrm{C}}>0.$$
