# A Quantum Chemical Study on the Interaction of $BF_3$ and Hydroxyl Groups with Two Model Perfluoroethers©

WILFREDO MORALES (Member, STLE)
NASA Lewis Research Center
Cleveland, Ohio 44135

A quantum chemical computational study was performed on the interaction of $BF_3$ (a strong Lewis acid) and various OH groups, with perfluorodimethylether (PDME) and perfluorodiethylether (PDEE). The focus of this study was to gain insight on the decomposition of model perfluoro ethers on dehydroxylated alumina surfaces in order to understand the decomposition of perfluoropolyalkylether (PFPE) liquid lubricants under tribological conditions. The results indicated that surface Lewis acid sites were not responsible for perfluoroether decomposition and that surface OH groups could be involved.

## KEY WORDS
Additive Effects, Decomposition, Synthetic Lubricants

## INTRODUCTION

In the 1960s (1) a class of perfluoroethers was synthesized that led to their extensive use by industry. This class is the perfluoropolyalkylethers (PFPEs) and they can be considered to be low molecular weight liquid polymers. PFPEs have outstanding physical and chemical properties that have led to their use as lubricants (2).

A number of papers have addressed the surface catalytic decomposition of PFPEs on various solids. This is of particular interest to the author's company in terms of the use of PFPEs as space lubricants, as well as potential aerospace high temperature liquid lubricants.

Several authors have proposed the concept that PFPE decomposition was catalyzed by Lewis acid surface sites. Carré and Markowitz (3) reacted a PFPE with $FeF_3$ and concluded that $FeF_3$ acts as a Lewis acid catalyst resulting in the decomposition of the PFPE. Zehe and Faut (4) reacted a PFPE with $\alpha$-$Fe_2O_3$ and concluded that PFPE decomposition proceeded in two stages. In the first stage, a slow catalytic reaction occurred between the PFPE and the $\alpha$-$Fe_2O_3$ leading to the formation of $FeF_3$. In the second stage, the $FeF_3$ then served as a Lewis acid leading to the rapid catalytic decomposition of the PFPE. In a simialr study, Kasai et al. (5) decomposed several PFPEs with $\alpha$-$Al_2O_3$. They found that PFPE Z decomposed in the presence of $\alpha$-$Al_2O_3$ at 200°C, whereas PFPE D, K, and Y did not decompose. They concluded, as Jones et al. concluded earlier (6), that easier decomposition of PFPE Z occurred due to the presence of acetal groups (O-$CF_2$-O) in the molecule. They also concluded that the decomposition process proceeded in two stages, identical to those reported by Zehe and Faut: the slow formation of $AlF_3$ followed by the rapid Lewis acid catalytic decomposition of PFPE Z.

In 1989, a paper was published that suggested Lewis acid sites were not responsible for PFPE decomposition. Basu et al. (7) conducted an experiment where they adsorbed a partially fluorinated ether model compound, $(CF_2H)$-O-$(CF_2H)$, onto dehydroxylated gamma alumina ($\gamma$-$Al_2O_3$). The surface of dehydroxylated gamma alumina consists of coordinate unsaturated $Al^{+3}$ Lewis acid sites, oxide ion sites, distinct hydroxyl groups with different reactivities, and defect sites created during the dehydroxylation process (8).

In their studies, Basu et al. concluded that the ether molecules adsorbed on isolated surface OH groups by hydrogen bonding at $-123^\circ$C. They increased the temperature and observed that a majority of the ether molecules desorbed by $-23^\circ$C. At temperatures greater than $-23^\circ$C, a fraction of the adsorbed molecules decomposed to gaseous products and a surface formate, HCOO. They repeated their experiments after using pyridine to block the $Al^{+3}$ Lewis acid sites and found that the fluoro ether still decomposed to gaseous products and surface formate. They concluded that Lewis acid sites and hydroxyl groups were not responsible for the fluoroether decomposition, and postulated that an "oxygen center" was a possible site for the formate formation.

Two recent studies have also indicated that Lewis acid sites are not responsible for PFPE decomposition. Ball and Zehe (9) conducted infrared matrix-isolation spectroscopy studies on the interaction between $BF_3$ (a strong Lewis acid)

Ng et al. (10) conducted a study, similar to that of Basu et al., where they adsorbed PDEE onto dehydroxylated $\gamma$-alumina. They observed the formation of surface fluoroacetate and fluoroformate in the temperature range $-73^{\circ}C$ to $327^{\circ}C$. They also detected a trace of HF among the gas phase products. They used pyridine to block the Lewis acid sites and observed that the PDEE still decomposed. They then silylated the $\gamma$ alumina surface with $(CH_{3})_{3}$-SiCl via the following mechanism:

$$\mathrm{Al-OH\ +\ ClSi-(CH_{3})_{3}\ \rightarrow\ Al-O-Si(CH_{3})_{3}\ +\ HCl}$$

The process passivated the alumina surface by replacing the OH groups with the nonreactive trimethylsilyl groups. It was then found that the PDEE did not decompose. They concluded that surface hydroxyl groups were necessary for ether decomposition to occur.

This author (11) conducted a series of tests, using both a TGA and DSC unit, where commercial PFPE Z was decomposed on both dehydroxylated $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ alumina pellets. It was found that PFPE Z decomposition on $\alpha$ alumina was different from decomposition on $\gamma$ alumina. For the $\alpha$ alumina, the data showed that PFPE Z did not start to decompose until a critical temperature $(\approx 200^{\circ}C)$ was reached. At this temperature a small exothermic rection occurred followed by an endothermic reaction. For the $\gamma$ alumina, the data showed small decomposition of the PFPE Z from room temperature up to a critical temperature $(\approx 200^{\circ}C)$. At this temperature a large exothermic reaction occurred. It was also found that PFPE Z decomposed on pyridine-treated $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ aluminas whereas effective silylation of the alumina surfaces prevented decomposition. The experimental evidence indicated that Lewis acid surface sites were not responsible for PFPE Z decomposition, but that reductive surface sites were possibly responsible.

In order to gain a better understanding of the surface chemistry of perfluoroethers on alumina, preliminary quantum chemical calculations were performed with two model compounds. Although the surface of dehydroxylated $\gamma$ alumina has a number of different active sites, this study focused on the interaction of the two model compounds with $BF_{3}$, chosen to represent a Lewis acid site, and with OH groups, chosen to represent surface hydroxyl sites. Future work will address the oxide ion sites and the defect sites.

## MODEL COMPOUNDS AND COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS

The PDME molecule was chosen as one of the model compounds for two reasons: it is the simplest ether moiety of a PFPE molecular chain, and several papers (12), (13) have been published detailing computational chemical calculations about this molecule. The PDEE molecule was chosen as the other model compound in light of the experimental studies conducted by Ball and Zehe (9).

The following five chemical species were chosen to interact with the perfluoro ether molecules: $H^{+}, BF_{3}, OH^{+}, OH$, and $OH^{-}$.

The semiempirical method used in this study was the AM1 self-consistent field molecular orbital procedure developed by Dewar and co-workers. The computations employed in this study used the HyperChem version 4.0 of AM1 using the UHF option (the lowest spin multiplicity, one or two, was used) on a 486DX personal computer. Complete geometric optimization during energy minimization was performed for all single molecules and for all interacting pairs of molecules. Configuration interaction was not used. Computing times ranged from one hour to about eight hours.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Before computational runs were performed on the interaction between the perfluoroethers and the various chemical species, two preliminary tests of the AM1 program were made. The first test involved comparing the calculated proton affinity of the PDME molecule, using the AM1 program, with the proton affinity calculated using *ab initio* methods. The second test involved the analysis of the computational results between the interaction of $NH_{3}$ and $BF_{3}$.

### Second Test
A computational run was then performed on the interaction of $BF_3$ with $NH_3$. $NH_3$, like pyridine, is a molecule that interacts strongly with surface Lewis acid sites and is widely used as a poison to block the effect of these sites. Optimized geometric results are shown in Fig. 3 and total energy values are listed in Table 3. Henceforth, only pertinent distances and angles will be reported. The B-N distance, 1.782 Å, and the net atomic charge on the $BF_3$ group, $- 0.329$, indicated an adduct was formed between the $NH_3$ and the $BF_3$ molecules which is what one would expect experimentally. Also note that the structure of the $BF_3$ group was distorted from its normal planar trigonal geometry. The F-B-F angles were $113.2^\circ$, and the angle between $F_1$ (or $F_2$) and the B-F₃ line was $44.5^\circ$. The total energy of the $BF_3—NH_3$ system was 51.5 kJ/mol lower than the added total energy of the isolated molecules.

OH INTERACTIONS

The surfaces of ionic oxides at ambient conditions are usually covered with anion hydroxyl groups (14). The nature of these hydroxyl groups on γ alumina has been studied by many researchers (15), (16). According to Knozinger and Ratnasamy (8), five different types of OH groups may exist on the ideal surface layers of γ alumina, as Fig. 7 shows. Each type has a different coordination of the OH group and the Al⁺³ cation where γ-alumina has both tetrahedral and octahedral cations. In the Type I site, OH is coordinated to a single tetrahedral cation resulting in an OH group with a net charge of −0.25. In the Type II site, OH is a bridging group coordinated to both a tetrahedral and octahedral cation with the OH bearing a net charge of +0.25. In the Type III site, OH is coordinated to two octahedral cations where the OH has a net charge of zero. The Type IV site OH is coordinated to three cations in octahedral interstices where the OH has a +0.5 net charge. The Type V site OH is coordinated to a single octahedral cation where the OH bears a −0.5 net charge. Thus for γ-alumina one can expect a maximum of five different OH groups. α-alumina has only octahedral Al⁺³ cations and only one type of OH has been reported to exist on the surface (17).

In order to simulate the interaction of PDEE with alumina surface hydroxyls, calculations were performed on the PDME molecule interacting with the highly idealized, integrally charged groups $OH$, $OH^{+}$, and $OH^{-}$. The $H$ atom of the $OH$ and $OH^{+}$ hydroxyl groups has net positive charges, +.187 and +.363, respectively, whereas the oxygen atom and fluorine atoms of perfluorodimethyl ether have net negative charges, as Fig. 1 shows. Since the oxygen atom has the greater negative charge, this site was considered more likely to interact first with the $H$ atom. In general, the most likely interaction sites for two molecules will be those two atoms containing the largest, and opposite, net atomic charges.

The optimized structure and total energy for the interaction between the neutral $OH$ group and the oxygen atom of the ether molecule are shown in Fig. 8 and Table 3. The distance between the ether oxygen and the $H$ atom was $2.148\mathring{A}$. The net charge on the $OH$ group was - 0.004 and the charge on the ether group was + 0.004. The total energy for this system was 2.1 kJ/mol lower than the added total energy for the isolated molecules. These results indicated possible hydrogen bonding interaction between the $OH$ and the $O$ atom of the ether molecule. The next calculation involved the interaction between the $OH^{+}$ group and the oxygen atom of the ether molecule. The optimized structure and total energy are shown in Fig. 9 and Table 3. The distance between the $H$ atom and the $O$ atom of the ether molecule was $1.006\mathring{A}$. The distance between the $H$ atom and the hydroxyl $O$ atom was $1.940\mathring{A}$. The net charge on the hydroxyl $O$ atom was + 0.033, whereas the net charge on the ether-H group was + 0.967. The total energy for this system was 42.7 kJ/mol lower than the added total energy for the two isolated molecules. These results indicated protonation of the perfluoroether molecule followed by possible hydrogen bonding between the protonated ether and the hydroxyl $O$ atom.

The second calculation involved the interaction of the OH⁺ group with the F atom of the ether molecule. The distance between the F atom and the H atom was 0.835 Å, and the distance between the H atom and the hydroxyl O atom was 2.113 Å. The charge on the hydroxyl oxygen atom was 0.011, whereas the net charge on the ether molecule and the H atom was 0.989. The total energy for this system was 119.7 kJ/mol lower than the added total energy for the two isolated molecules. These results indicated protonation of the ether molecule occurred at the F atom followed by possible hydrogen bonding to the hydroxyl O atom.

The final calculation involved the interaction between the OH⁻ group and the F atom of the ether molecule. The distance between the F atom and the H atom was 2.104 Å, and the distance between the H atom and the hydroxyl O atom was 0.954 Å. The net atomic charge on the OH group was − 0.009, whereas the net charge on the ether molecule was − 0.992. The total energy for this system was 97.1 kJ/mol lower than the added total energy for the two isolated molecules. These results indicated an electron transfer from the OH⁻ group to the ether molecule occurred with no decomposition of the ether anion. The ether anion then hydrogen-bonded to the OH.

These computational results suggested that surface hydroxyl groups may be involved in perfluoro ether decomposition. The involvement of the OH groups seems to be limited to neutral OH and OH⁻ since Ng et al. (10) observed no infrared evidence for the existence of a protonated ether on the alumina surface. The neutral OH groups could serve to hydrogen bond the perfluoro-ether molecules to the alumina surface at low temperatures. At the higher temperatures, the hydrogen-bonded ether could then interact with a OH⁻ group at the ether C atom, resulting in decomposition of the ether molecule. This study suggests that PFPE decomposition under both static and tribological conditions may be mitigated by the use of an additive that can deactivate hydroxyl groups on solid surfaces. Future work will focus on the interaction of model perfluoroethers with other active surface sites of alumina, oxide anion sites and defect sites.

## CONCLUSIONS

The following conclusions were reached for this computational study:

1. The AM1 semiempirical quantum computational program provided reasonable proton affinity results when compared to ab initio results. The AM1 program also calculated the proper Lewis acid-base interaction between NH₃ and BF₃.
2. Computational runs showed interaction between DEE and BF₃, but no interaction between PDEE and BF₃. These results are in agreement with the experimental work of Ball and Zehe. These results suggested that Lewis acid sites were not responsible for perfluoroether decomposition.
3. Computational runs showed hydrogen bonding interaction between PDME and neutral OH. Interaction between the PDME carbon atom and OH⁻ resulted in ether decomposition. These results suggested that OH groups were actively involved in perfluoroether decomposition.

## DISCUSSION

JON L. HOWELL (Member, STLE)
E. I. DuPont de Nemours Co., Inc.
Jackson Laboratory
Deepwater, New Jersey 08023

It is good to observe the application of quantum chemical calculations applied to the interaction of perfluoro ethers with Lewis acids and hydroxyl groups as a model of the in- teractions between Lewis acids and perfluoropolyalkylethers (PFPAEs). As the use of semi-empirical techniques, coupled with powerful desktop computers, become available to more researchers, it provides another important tool for develop- ing the basic understanding of the interactions between lu- bricants and substrates.

It is common knowledge that PFPAEs can be decomposed by Lewis acids. The mechanism of this reaction is not under- stood, especially in the case of alumina when the decompo- sition can be blocked by altering some sites and not others. The observation of an induction period, followed by rapid decomposition, is also not clearly understood.

The author of this paper has clearly made the case that a free Lewis acid is not responsible for the decomposition of the model perfluorocarbon. The hypothesis, supported by the calculations that the hydroxyl compounds are responsible for the decomposition, fits well with the experimental evi- dence. Indeed, the case of hydroxyl anion interacting with the trifluoromethyl carbon was the only example which showed complete reaction.

Verification of the computation methodology with known experimental results prior to conducting the research by the author is commendable. Not only does it enhance the cred- ibility of the results, but it is frequently overlooked as a crit- ical part of the use of this technique. It is also important to note that the evaluation of the approach of the hydroxyl group to the different atoms in the model fluoro ether bal- anced the research effort.

It is hoped that the author will extend these calculations to other perfluoroethers whose structure more closely resem- ble commercial PFPAEs. One flaw in this paper is that PFPAEs are easily decomposed by pure $AlF_{3}$ or $FeF_{3}$. Because hydroxyl anion was shown to decompose perfluoromethyl ether, possibly fluoride ion could as easily do the same. If this is the case, then it might explain why the decomposition of the PFPAEs generally follows the acidity ranking in Lewis acids.

One other avenue of investigation might be to further ex- plore the induction period observed in the decomposition of commercial PFPAEs on alumina. One might speculate that low levels of $CHFCF_{3}$ in these PFPAEs might interact which can then accelerate the decomposition. This hypothesis could easily explain some of the author's experimental observations.

## AUTHOR'S CLOSURE

The author wishes to thank the reviewer for his remarks and insightful comments. The author agrees with the re- viewer that PFPEs are decomposed by $AlF_{3}$ or $FeF_{3}$ at elevated temperatures. The author's calculations, however, revealed no Lewis acid-base interaction between $AlF_{3}$ and perfluoro dimethyl ether. Although the presence of the metal atoms of a metal oxide or metal fluoride cannot be ruled out as a contributing factor in the high-temperature decomposition of PFPEs, this molecular orbital study did suggest that the formation of a classical Lewis acid-base adduct, like that of the $BF_{3}-NH_{3}$ system, which will not occur between PFPE molecules and surface Lewis acid sites.

The author agrees with the reviewer that fluoride anion interaction with model perfluoro ethers should be investi- gated. Preliminary calculations suggest interaction. The re- viewer also touched on a very important issue: the effect of small amounts of H atoms in PFPEs that may contribute to PFPE decomposition. This area should be pursued.
