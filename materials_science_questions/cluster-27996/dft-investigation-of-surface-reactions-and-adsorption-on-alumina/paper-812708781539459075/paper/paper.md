# Conditions for the self-assembling of cluster materials

## Abstract
Clusters with closed electronic shells are highly stable, and this is a robust property with far-reaching implications for cluster abundance, cluster shapes, dynamical response to electric fields and cluster dissociation. Clusters with closed shells are also good candidates for the synthesis of novel materials. The density functional formalism is currently used to investigate the self-assembling of metallic clusters to yield cluster solids. A high intrinsic stability is not the only requirement for expecting a successful assembling, and we investigate additional conditions by performing computer simulations for selected examples. The study of the assembling of hydrogen-doped icosahedral aluminium clusters shows that an optimized relative orientation of each cluster unit with respect to all its neighbour clusters becomes a favourable condition. The analysis of the clustering effects observed in crystalline alloys formed by Pb and alkali metals (tetrahedral Pb₄ clusters surrounded by the alkali cations form in those alloys) provides further insight: coating passivates the clusters and helps to stabilize the assembled material.

## 1. Introduction
The self-assembling of atomic nanostructures opens up the possibility of building materials with novel properties. A cluster-assembled material will be in general metastable. For the assembling to be successful, the clusters have to retain their character in the assembled material. This is what happens in the fullerite crystal. In metallic materials the interactions are more delocalized and the clusters may have a tendency to coalesce. The less reactive clusters are those with closed electronic shells and this point is reviewed first. Next we analyse the chemical bonding in a class of alloys known to display clustering effects. The analysis indicates that the stability of assembled materials becomes enhanced when the clusters are passivated by a coating layer. Finally we discuss computer simulations of the assembling of doped aluminium clusters. The simulations highlight the importance of an optimal relative cluster–cluster orientation.

## 2. Electronic shells in clusters
The population in cluster beams of the alkaline elements shows maxima at some particular *magic* sizes [1], and the ionization potential exhibits substantial drops between $N$ and $N+1$ at the same magic sizes $N = 2, 8, 18, 20, 34, 40, 58, 92\ldots$. These features reflect the existence of electronic shells for electrons confined in an effective spherical potential well with a radial shape intermediate between a square well and a harmonic potential: in this potential, that can be generated by the jellium background model [1,2], shells become occupied in the order $1s^2, 1p^6, 1d^{10}, 2s^2, 1f^{14}, 2p^6, \ldots$ with standard notation in which $n = 1, 2, \ldots$ is the principal quantum number (that is $n-1$ is equal to the number of nodes of the radial wavefunction), s ($l=0$), p ($l=1$), d ($l=2$), $\ldots$ indicate the orbital angular momentum and the superscripts give the degeneracy (orbital and spin) of the shell. These shells are separated by energy gaps, so clusters with closed shells are specially stable and have a large ionization potential. Density functional (DFT) calculations taking account of the granularity of the ionic skeleton [3] support the picture of electronic shells provided by the spherical well model. Experiments for clusters of the noble metals Cu, Ag and Au reveal the formation of similar electronic shells, and these features become explained by applying the shell model to the outer s electrons only.

The high stability associated with closed electronic shells is a robust feature. Shell closing is also imprinted in the

electric dipole polarizabilities of alkali clusters [4]. Similarly to the inert gas atoms, closed-shell clusters have a weakly polarizable electronic cloud. It is convenient to stress that the polarizabilities provide additional information on cluster structure. Calculations including the ionic skeleton concluded that a comparison of experimental and calculated static polarizabilities can be used to identify the ground state, between a possible set of calculated isomers with close energies [4-6]. In fact, for the typical temperatures at which polarizabilities are measured, there is a delicate interplay between the two ingredients, electronic shell effects and geometry [4,7]. Fedrigo and coworkers [8] have measured the optical spectrum of silver clusters. The mean absorption energy presents oscillations as a function of cluster size, with higher values for closed-shell clusters. In particular, maxima were found for $N=2,8,18,34$. The shell model also provides a useful framework to interpret the changes in magic numbers induced by the presence of some impurities in alkali cluster matrices [9].

A striking manifestation of the interplay between shell closing effects and cluster shape has been pointed out [10]: some clusters can be interpreted as supermolecules built from smaller magic clusters weakly bound to each other. For instance, the shape of $\mathrm{Na}_{4}$ is that of a supermolecule formed by two $\mathrm{Na}_{2}$ dimers with a weak separation energy of only $0.10 \mathrm{eV}$, and $\mathrm{Na}_{10}$ can be viewed as a dimer attached to $\mathrm{Na}_{8}$, with a separation energy of $0.15 \mathrm{eV}$. Soon after the discovery of the shell effects in clusters we predicted [11] that the most favourable fragmentation channel of a doubly charged cationic alkali cluster $X_{N}^{2+}$ involves the production of a singly charged magic fragment with two or eight electrons. Experiments confirmed this prediction. A doubly charged cluster is, in general, protected against fragmentation by an energy barrier. The barrier accounts for the deformation of the cluster from its ground-state shape to an elongated shape on its way to dissociating into two fragments. Calculations have shown that in many cases the magic fragments are already pre-formed in the ground state of the $X_{N}^{2+}$ cluster [12]. Then, the energy required to further deform the clusters following the path leading to a magic fragment is smaller than for other channels.

The concept of chemical hardness $(\eta)$ can be taken as a measure of the reactivity of a cluster. DFT allows us to identify $\eta=\frac{1}{2} \partial^{2} E / \partial n^{2}$, where the second derivative of the cluster energy $E$ with respect to the number of electrons $n$ has to be evaluated with the nuclei at fixed positions [13]. Using a finite-difference approximation for the derivative, the hardness becomes

$$
\eta=(I-A) / 2, \quad \text { (1) }
$$

where $I$ and $A$ are the ionization potential and the electron affinity, respectively. Clusters with closed shells have high ionization potentials and low electron affinities; that is, they are chemically hard. Their resistance to either donating or accepting an electron turns closed-shell clusters relatively inert. With some additional approximations, a rough estimate relates the hardness to the HOMO-LUMO gap

$$
\eta=\left(\epsilon_{L U M O}-\epsilon_{H O M O}\right) / 2. \quad \text { (2) }
$$

The HOMO and LUMO orbital energies are obtained from a DFT calculation for the neutral cluster. In exact DFT, $-\epsilon_{H O M O}$ is equal to the exact ionization potential, and it has been re- cently shown that the orbital energies of the other occupied lev- els can be interpreted as approximate, but rather accurate, re- laxed vertical ionization potentials [14]. This is not true, how- ever, for the approximate exchange-correlation energy func- tionals most often used. Since we are only interested in a quali- tative use of $\eta$ for a family of similar clusters, the rough approx imation given in equation (2) will be used in section 3 below.

3. Clustering in crystalline alloys of alkali metals and elements of the lead group

The equiatomic solid alloys of the heavy alkali metals Na, K, $\mathrm{Rb}$ and $\mathrm{Cs}$ with $\mathrm{Pb}$ contain tetrahedral $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}$ clusters, surrounded by the alkali atoms [15]. Similar clustering occurs for alloys of these alkalis with $\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}$ or $\mathrm{Sn}$, which belong to the same column of the periodic table as $\mathrm{Pb}$. Free $\mathrm{Pb}_{4} \mathrm{X}_{4}$ clusters, where $\mathrm{X}$ indicates an alkali element, have been investigated by DFT calculations [16] using the FHI96MD density functional code [17], with exchange-correlation effects approximated by the LDA [18]. This code uses a superlattice geometry, and the wavefunctions are expanded in plane waves. Core electrons are not explicitly considered, being replaced by non-local norm- conserving pseudopotentials [19]. Since we are interested in free clusters, the lattice constant of the superlattice is taken large enough to avoid interactions between clusters in different supercells. The calculated lowest-energy structure of all these clusters consists of an inner $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}$ tetrahedron surrounded by another, oppositely oriented, alkali tetrahedron. Neutral $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}$ is planar, and the transition to the three-dimensional structure on adding alkali atoms results from charge transfer from the alkali atoms to the $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}$ unit. With four electrons transferred to $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}$, this becomes a $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}^{4-}$ poly-anion with closed electronic shells. The four cations sit above the four faces of the tetrahedron and also play the role of stabilizing the charged poly-anions. The HOMO-LUMO gaps of this family of clusters, calculated from the Kohn-Sham orbital energies for the neutral species, are large: $2.54 \mathrm{eV}$ for $\mathrm{Pb}_{4} \mathrm{Li}_{4}, 1.92 \mathrm{eV}$ for $\mathrm{Pb}_{4} \mathrm{Na}_{4}$ and $1.36 \mathrm{eV}$ for $\mathrm{Pb}_{4} \mathrm{~K}_{4}$. However, the large gaps are not enough to explain clustering in the solid alloys, since $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}$ clusters form in the alloys when the alkali partners are $\mathrm{Na}, \mathrm{K}, \mathrm{Rb}$ or $\mathrm{Cs}$, but not when the partner is $\mathrm{Li}$ : the Li compound has the $\mathrm{CsCl}$ structure. The exception occurs, surprisingly, in the case of the largest HOMO-LUMO gap.

Although clusters with large gaps are less reactive than others, in metallic clusters the overlap between orbitals of neighbour clusters may lead to substantial interaction. For the case under study the interaction between the $\mathrm{Pb}_{4}^{4-}$ clusters is controlled by the size of the surrounding cations. In the structural map of figure 1, free clusters are represented in terms of two coordinates, the HOMO-LUMO gap and the diameter of the cation. In addition to $\mathrm{Pb}_{4} \mathrm{X}_{4}$ clusters, other closed-shell clusters of compositions $\mathrm{PbX}_{4}$ and $\mathrm{PbX}_{6}$ have been included (X again indicates an alkali element). The curve drawn in the figure separates those clusters related to alloy compositions displaying clustering effects from the rest. The conclusion is that a high HOMO-LUMO gap and a large cation size are both required for the occurrence of clustering in the solid.

In the PbNa crystal (also for X = K, Rb, Cs) the Pb₄ clusters form a body-centred tetragonal (bct) lattice with axis ratio $c/a=1.676$, and the simulations begin by placing the Pb₄Na₄ clusters in a bct lattice with the same $c/a$ ratio but with dimensions $c$ and $a$ expanded by a factor $f=2$. The factor $f$ was then reduced in steps, maintaining $c/a=1.676$, and at each step all the atoms were allowed to relax to minimize the energy. The structure obtained at the end of the simulations is the observed structure of the crystalline compound, and the solid can be viewed as formed by closed- shell $Pb_{4}^{4-}$ cluster anions surrounded by the alkali cations. The density of electronic states displays a narrow semiconducting gap. The binding energy gained by assembling the clusters is $\Delta E_{assembling}=4.09$ eV per cluster, to be compared with a value $\Delta E_{clustering}=11.19$ eV of the binding energy of the Pb₄Na₄ cluster with respect to the separated atoms. Results for the assembling of Pb₄K₄ clusters are similar. However, the simulation of the assembling of Pb₄Li₄ clusters gave qualitatively different results. As $f$ is reduced, the clusters interact strongly, and each Pb₄ tetrahedron opens up, forming a butterfly. The strong interaction leads to metallic character for the assembled solid, whose structure can be viewed as an interconnected network of broken Pb₄ clusters immersed in the background of $Li^{+}$ cations. The metastable assembled solid cannot be characterized as a solid containing clusters.

The conclusion of this section is that a closed-shell struc- ture and a large HOMO-LUMO gap are necessary conditions, but this is not enough for a successful cluster self-assembling. The reactivity of metallic clusters is large and an efficient way to passivate the clusters is by an appropriate coating.

## 4. Assembling of doped aluminium clusters

Some proposals for the assembling of doped aluminium clusters have been investigated by DFT simulations. Al₁₃ has a compact icosahedral structure and 39 valence elec- trons, short of just one electron to have a closed-shell elec- tronic configuration [21]. The extra electron can be pro- vided by an electropositive atom. For instance, Al₁₃K can be viewed as an ionic molecule, and the assembling of these super- molecules in CsCl and NaCl lattices has been modelled [22]. In these lattices the closed-shell macro-anions are surrounded by the $K^{+}$ cations, that separate the $Al_{13}^{-}$ clusters from each other. The simulations predicted metastable materials. Clus- ters Al₁₂C and Al₁₂Si have also been investigated. In this case the substitutional dopant atom has four valence electrons, so the cluster receives the electron required to close the outer shell. Gong [23] found a favourable arrangement that leads to a stable assembling. The arrangement is a simple cubic-like lattice where each cluster has six neighbour clusters.

We next discuss the assembling of Al₁₃H clusters. Al₁₃H also has 40 valence electrons. This cluster has a sizable HOMO-LUMO gap of 1.4 eV, measured by photoelectron spectroscopy of the negatively charged $(Al_{13}H)^{-}$ species [24]. We have first performed DFT calculations for free Al₁₃H clusters with the ADF code [25], using the local spin density approximation (LSDA) [26]. This is an all-electron code, but the Ne-like cores of the Al atoms were treated as frozen. The molecular orbitals of the valence electrons are obtained as optimized linear combinations of atomic orbitals. For each Al atom the basis set is a triple zeta with one polarization function (TZVP), and it contains three s, nine p and five d Slater orbitals. For hydrogen, three s and three p orbitals are considered. By optimization of the structure, without any symmetry restrictions, the equilibrium structure was obtained for the H atom above a triangular face of the icosahedron. The binding energy of the H atom to the cluster is 3.36 eV, and a small energy barrier of 0.08 eV separates equivalent sites on two adjacent faces, suggesting the easy mobility of H across the surface of the aggregate. The electronic structures and electronic densities of Al₁₃H and its isoelectronic cluster $Al_{13}^{-}$ are different. The presence of the H atom perturbs the jellium- like features of Al₁₃; nevertheless, in spite of the rearrangement of electronic levels, a sizable HOMO-LUMO gap occurs in Al₁₃H. The magnitude of this gap, calculated from corresponding orbital energies of the neutral cluster, is 1.77 eV. However, a better estimation of the gap can be obtained by subtracting the energies of the two highest occupied electronic levels of the anionic species $(Al_{13}H)^{-}$ , since this procedure deals exclusively with occupied levels, and in addition mimics what is measured in the photoelectron spectroscopy experiments. This estimation gives $\Delta \epsilon=1.63$ eV, which is in better agreement with the experimental result. The calculated adiabatic ionization potential of Al₁₃H is $I=7.05$ eV and this large value again reflects the high stability of the cluster. The calculated adiabatic electron affinity, $A=1.76$ eV, is consistent with the experimental value of 2.0 eV [24].

Simulations of the assembling of a material from $Al_{13}H$ clusters were performed with the FHI96MD density func- tional code [17], using norm-conserving pseudopotentials [19] for Al, and the LDA for exchange and correlation [18]. The cluster–cluster orientation in isomer B of $(Al_{13}H)_2$ suggests a favourable configuration such that neighbour clusters in the solid have edges in contact perpendicular to each other. This condition leads to a simple cubic lattice (six nearest-neighbour clusters). The energy of the assembled solid was calculated as a function of the lattice constant, first maintaining the structure of the clusters frozen. The curve obtained has a minimum for a lattice constant of 24.2 au and the solid is metallic. Al–Al dis- tances between atoms in neighbour clusters are comparable to intracluster Al–Al distances. The gain in binding energy by the assembling is 15 eV per cluster. This value is about one-third of the internal binding energy of the free $Al_{13}H$ cluster, and this fraction is similar to that found earlier for the assembling of the Pb–alkali clusters. The effect of a full relaxation of the atomic coordinates was investigated by performing constant- volume–constant-temperature dynamical simulations at 150 K. The time step was 5 fs and the simulation time was 3 ps. The results indicate that the assembled material is stable at this temperature. The icosahedral units retain their structure with only moderate distortions. The main effect of the temperature is to allow the migration of the H atoms to interstitial regions between the $Al_{13}$ clusters. In summary, the assembling leads to a clustered material that appears to be stable at least up to 150 K. The material has metallic character, but the density of states is very different from that of bulk aluminium [27].

Similar dynamical simulations were performed for the assembling of the clusters in an fcc-type lattice, where each cluster is surrounded by 12 neighbour clusters and the optimal relative orientation is lost. In this case the clusters do not retain their individual character and coalesce. These simulations add support to the idea that the relative orientation of the clusters is a favourable condition for a successful assembling. The stability barrier protecting the metastable simple cubic arrangement of $Al_{13}H$ clusters then becomes a magnitude of interest. Knowledge of this barrier would require a careful simulation of the gradual heating of the system to find the critical temperature for which thermal reorientation of the clusters leads to coalescence. In the meantime one can get some qualitative understanding of this process by returning to the alloys of alkali metals and elements of the Pb group. The ordered compound

SnNa has the same features as PbNa, that is, $Sn_4$ clusters surrounded by the Na atoms. Although this compound melts at a temperature of 854 K, another transition has been found at a lower temperature of 757 K [28]. This previous transition was interpreted as leading to a plastic phase of the compound, in which the $Sn_4$ clusters are able to execute rotational motions. The feature we notice here is that the temperature required to allow for the quasi-free reorientation of the clusters is not too far from the melting temperature of the alloy.

## 5. Conclusions

Assembling clusters of metallic elements to form clustered materials is not a simple task, since the clusters may coalesce. Theoretical simulations help to discover the requirements that the candidates have to fulfill. The most promising candidates are clusters with a large HOMO-LUMO gap. However, this is not sufficient. The study of the chemical bonding in equiatomic Pb-alkali crystalline alloys, that contain $Pb_4$ tetrahedral clusters, shows that the alkali atoms play an important coating role, stabilizing the $Pb_4$ tetrahedra. Even without a coating layer some clusters show promising features. The example studied in this paper is $Al_{13}$H. When oriented appropriately with respect to their neighbours, these clusters can be assembled into a solid while preserving their identity, so a additional favourable condition is an appropriate relative orientation of the clusters with respect to each other.
