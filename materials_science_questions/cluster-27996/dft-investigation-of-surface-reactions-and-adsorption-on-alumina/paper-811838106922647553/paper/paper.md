# Density Functional Studies of Ethanol Dehydrogenation on a $2 \mathrm{Rh} / \gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}(110)$ Surface

Shiuan-Yau Wu, $^{\dagger}$ Ying-Ren Lia, $^{\dagger}$ Jia-Jen Ho, $^{*, \dagger}$ and Horng-Ming Hsieh $^{\ddagger}$

Department of Chemistry, National Taiwan Normal University 88, Section 4, Tingchow Road, Taipei 116, Taiwan, and Institute of Nuclear Energy Research, No. 1000 Wenhua Road, Longtan, Taoyuan 325, Taiwan

Received: June 16, 2009; Revised Manuscript Received: July 23, 2009

We applied periodic density-functional theory to investigate the dehydrogenation of ethanol with and without $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ molecules on a $2 \mathrm{Rh} / \gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}(110)$ surface. In the absence of $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$, the adsorption energy of ethanol on the surface was calculated to be $-31.34 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$; ethanol might form a four- or five-membered-ring (oxametallacyclic) structure on the surface. Both rings are stable but can be dehydrogenated to form aldehyde and ethene, or the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}$ bond can break to form $\mathrm{CH}_{3(\mathrm{a})}+\mathrm{CO}_{(\mathrm{a})}$ with a dissociation barrier of $24.92 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$, eventually. When water molecules $\left(3 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)$ are present on the surface, the adsorption energy of ethanol is decreased to $-21.25 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$; ethanol can neither form a ring structure on the surface nor create a path to produce aldehyde or ethene, but instead undertakes uniquely the scission of the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}$ bond, forming $\mathrm{CH}_{3(\mathrm{a})}$ $+\mathrm{CO}_{(\mathrm{a})}$ with a barrier of $21.55 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mol}$.

## 1. Introduction

The scarcity of fossil fuels and increased environmental pollution problems make exploration of alternative resources a pressing issue in our daily life. $^{1}$ Being least polluting and reacting highly efficiently in a hydrogen/oxygen fuel cell to produce electricity, hydrogen is generally considered to be a desirable fuel. ${ }^{2,3}$ As there is no direct terrestrial source of hydrogen, its synthesis is essential. The toxicity of methanol is its main drawback although reforming it to produce $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ has been considered. $^{4}$ In contrast, ethanol is nontoxic and safely handled, and can be derived abundantly from sugar- or starch-containing crops. ${ }^{5,6}$ In addition, its use to produce hydrogen and energy can avoid a contribution to global warming, as biomass takes in carbon dioxide from the atmosphere for its growth. All these advantages inspired an intensive study of the production of hydrogen from ethanol via steam reforming or catalytic partial oxidation. ${ }^{7-9}$ A Rh-doped alumina-supported surface exhibits the best catalytic activity for dehydration of ethanol to ethene at low temperatures and for conversion of ethanol into $\mathrm{H}_{2}, \mathrm{CO}$, and $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ at high temperatures. ${ }^{10,11}$

Its great porosity and surface area make $\gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ one of the most widespread supports for heterogeneous catalysis in the refining and petrochemical industries. It allows the dispersion of active phases of various types and the modification of its acid-base surface properties. ${ }^{12}$ In their temperature-programmed desorption experiments on ethanol on a $\gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ surface, Clayborne et al. ${ }^{13}$ reported that ethene desorbed at $570-680 \mathrm{~K}$ and produced cokes, diminishing the activity of the catalyst. Cavallaro et al. ${ }^{14-16}$ mentioned that the added oxygen and water could decrease the formation of cokes.

To maintain the activity of a catalyst, it is essential to avoid the formation of ethene. The ratios of water/ethanol and oxygen/ ethanol and the proportion of rhodium are important. A theoretical calculation might furnish new insight into the experimental results. The $\gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}(110)$ surface is reported to dominate $(74 \%)$ the total area, followed by $(100)(16 \%)$ and then $(111)(10 \%) .{ }^{17}$ Using neutron-diffraction analysis, Beaufils and Barbaux estimated a similar result, namely $83 \%$ of (110) and $17 \%$ of $(100) .{ }^{18}$ In addition, the interaction of the support with the active phases plays an important role in the activity of catalytic surfaces. ${ }^{19,20}$ We have therefore performed DFT calculations simulating a Rh-doped $\gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}(110)$ surface to discuss the interaction mechanisms of ethanol with and without $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ molecules on these catalytic surfaces. Our results agree satisfactorily with experimental observations in the formation of acetaldehyde, ethene, $\mathrm{CH}_{4}$, and $\mathrm{CO} .{ }^{21,22}$ In addition, the presence of $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ molecules on the surface is essential to eliminate the formation of ethene that eventually forms coke.

## 2. Computational Method

For the slab calculation, we applied plane-wave, periodic, density-functional theoretical calculations implemented in the VASP code (Vienna ab initio simulation package). ${ }^{23}$ The Kohn-Sham equations, solved in a self-consistent manner under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) ${ }^{24}$ with the Perdew-Wang (PW91) exchange-correlation formulation, ${ }^{25}$ serve to calculate the total energy. The projector-augmentedwave method (PAW) ${ }^{26}$ implemented in VASP is applied. The Brillouin zone was sampled with the Monkhorst-Pack grid, and the calculations were performed with $(4 \times 4 \times 1)$ Monkhorst-Pack mesh $k$-points. ${ }^{27}$ To ensure an effective convergence of the energy, the truncation energy for all calculations is set at $400 \mathrm{eV}$. In the calculations we optimized the structures based on the conjugate gradient-minimization scheme taking into consideration the spin polarization.

The bulk structure of $\gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ taken from Digne et al. ${ }^{17}$ is monoclinic and contains $8 \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ units. The volume after structure optimization is $368.38 \AA^{3}\left(46.05 \AA^{3} / \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}\right.$ unit), which is slightly less $(0.78 \%)$ than the experimental value $\left(46.39 \AA^{3} /\right.$ $\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ unit). ${ }^{28}$ The lattice parameters after geometry optimization are $a_{\text {calcd }}=5.523 \AA, b_{\text {calcd }}=8.325 \AA, c_{\text {calcd }}=8.012 \AA$, and $\beta$ $=90.69^{\circ}$. The model surface of $\gamma-\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}(110)$ is shown in Figure 1. All calculations were performed on an 8-layer slab model containing $16 \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ units, with a vacuum region of thickness ca. $12 \AA$ to ensure no interaction between the surface adsorbates of one layer and the next slab. ## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Adsorption of Rhodium on a $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) Surface.
In general, the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface is replete with OH groups at normal temperature,³² but after thermal treatment at 1200 K the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) becomes completely dehydrated.³³,³⁴ Alumina-support catalysts loaded with rhodium to an extent of about 1% are active and selective toward hydrogen formation. To simulate the surface model with a small proportion of rhodium loaded on alumina-support catalysts at high temperature,¹⁴⁻¹⁶ we placed one and two Rh atoms adsorbed on the slab model for a dehydrated $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110)-(1×1) surface to discuss the possible mechanism of reaction of ethanol decomposition.

The structure of the dehydrated $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface is shown in Figure 1. The coordination numbers vary for all surface aluminum and oxygen atoms: Al<sub>III</sub>(1), Al<sub>IV</sub>(2, 3, 4, and 5), Al<sub>V</sub>(6), $\mu_2$O(D, E, and F), and $\mu_3$O(A, I, J, and K). Other atoms, such as Al<sub>V</sub>(7 and 8) and $\mu_3$O(B and C), are in the inward layer and less accessible to adsorbates. Those unsaturated aluminum atoms make a $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface highly reactive and increase the Lewis acidity.¹⁷ Similarly to Valero et al.,¹² we calculated the potential energy surfaces (PES) of Rh loaded on a dehydrated $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface with a regular surface grid (1.1 $\mathring{\text{A}}^2$ for each point). We fixed the coordinates of the adsorbate parallel to the surface plane $(x_o, y_o)$ at each grid point and relaxed the $z$ coordinate.

The computed PES of Rh adsorbed on the dehydrated $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface is shown in Figure 2, with its adsorption energies varying from −28 to −61 kcal/mol. A detailed analysis

### 3.2. Adsorption of Water and Ethanol. 3.2.1. Adsorption of H₂O.
Because of the strong Lewis acidity of unsaturated Al atoms on the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface, H₂O is readily adsorbed at various sites of Al through its O−H group. In Table 1, the adsorption energies of water at various Al sites, Al(1)−Al(3), of a clean $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface are −40.16, −32.96, and −17.29 kcal/mol, respectively. The dissociation energy barrier of H₂O into H + OH on the tricoordinated Al(1) site is only 5 kcal/mol together with a great exothermicity of 50.30 kcal/mol, in accord with the experimental measurement of 45.50 kcal/mol.³⁵ With the addition of one Rh on the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface, the H₂O adsorption energies at Al(1), Al(2), and Al(3) sites become −35.47, −20.35, and −13.57 kcal/mol, respectively, smaller than the previous counterparts, because Rh(1) occupies the basin between Al(1) and Al(2), causing the decreased average adsorption energy. On the 2Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface, the location of Rh(2) further affects the H₂O occupation sites of Al(1), which induces a more greatly decreased H₂O adsorption energy on the Al(1) site to −25.69 kcal/mol, indicating further decreased Lewis acidity of Al(1) on the addition of a second Rh atom. Nevertheless, the strong Rh−Rh metallic interaction leads to the decrease of the interaction between the Rh and other Al atoms (except for the Al(1) atom). For this reason, the acidity of Al(2) and Al(3) is slightly raised and the adsorption energies of H₂O on these Al sites will be larger than that of the one-Rh counterpart.

### 3.2.2. Adsorption of Ethanol.
Our calculations show that the most stable adsorption mode of ethanol on the dehydrated $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface is via its O−H group onto low-coordinated aluminum atoms Al(1)−Al(3). As seen in Table 1, adsorption at the tricoordinated Al(1) has the greatest adsorption energy, −42.58 kcal/mol, due to the strong Lewis acidity to adsorb an O−H group and the nearby surface oxygen atoms providing a weak hydrogen bond interaction with the H atom in O−H. On Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) and 2Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surfaces, in addition to considering the O−H group interacting with various aluminum atoms, rhodium atoms also have an effect on ethanol adsorption. In Figure 4-(1−3), A, B, and C are three major adsorption modes of ethanol on a 2Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface; similar adsorption modes appeared on Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110). The C_α−H bond (mode A) is attracted by Rh(1), with a C_α−H bond length 1.17 Å greater than its gas-phase value of 1.11 Å, in addition to the O−H adsorbed at Al(2). In mode B the C_β−H bond is attracted by Rh(1), also with an extended C_β−H bond, of length 1.17 Å. In mode C, although ethanol is also adsorbed via C_α−H to Rh(1), the O−H group is not directly adsorbed at Al(2); instead, the H atom of the O−H group is attracted to the O atom of the surface. Among these three modes, mode B has the largest adsorption energy, −34.50 kcal/mol (shown in Table 2). The addition of a Rh atom onto the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface has another influence on the adsorption energies of H₂O and ethanol. As shown in Table 1, the average adsorption energy of H₂O decreases on a one Rh atom surface (Rh/Al₂O₃(110)), whereas that of ethanol increases. We rationalize that the interaction of H₂O with Rh is insignificant; also, the added one Rh atom decreases the acidity of the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃ surface that decreases the H₂O adsorption energy. The C−H bond of ethanol interacts strongly with the added one-Rh atom in addition to its O−H group with the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃ surface, which increases the ethanol adsorption energy. Nevertheless, in a two Rh atoms case, 2Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110), although the second Rh atom occupies certain reactive sites of Al atoms, it unsettles the adsorbate at some Al sites; moreover, the newly formed Rh−Rh metallic interaction decreases the interaction between Rh atoms and the C−H bond of ethanol, and the adsorption energies in two adsorbates become more complicated and irregular.

In addition, we also calculated the relaxation energy to discuss how the adsorbates, including one Rh atom, two Rh atoms, water, and ethanol, affect the stabilization of the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface. Since this surface is very corrugated and it is highly possible that relaxation effects may affect the adsorption energies of the reactants. When one Rh atom is located on the surface, the calculated relaxation energy is 0.88 eV, which is smaller than two Rh atoms on the surface by 0.60 eV. This difference provides another explanation why adsorption energies of the water and ethanol in the Al(2) and Al(3) site of the 2Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) system are larger than those in 1Rh/$\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface. The relaxation energies of water and ethanol adsorbed on the $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface are 0.55 and 1.61 eV, respectively. This larger difference of relaxation energies ending up with similar adsorption energies of water and ethanol indicates that there would be a greater interaction energy between the ethanol and $\gamma$-Al₂O₃(110) surface.

### 3.3. Reaction Mechanism of Ethanol-$2\text{Rh}/\gamma$-$Al_2O_3$(110) Interactions.
To characterize the likely reaction path for the decomposition of ethanol on a $2\text{Rh}/\gamma$-$Al_2O_3$(110) surface, we discuss two major modes of ethanol adsorption, A and B, together with their dissociation intermediates and products along the paths. Their optimized structures are shown in Figure 4, and the potential energy diagram based on the NEB calculation is given in Figure 5.

#### 3.3.1. Decomposition of Ethanol via Mode A.
According to the optimized adsorption structure of mode A and the corresponding bond lengths, shown in Figure 4-(1) and Table 2, we know that the $\text{C}_{\alpha}$-$H$ and $\text{O-H}$ bonds are longer than those in the gas-phase molecule; breaking a $\text{C}_{\alpha}$-$H$ or $\text{O-H}$ bond might therefore become the first possible decomposition step. To break the $\text{O-H}$ bond, the calculated energy barrier (TS1) is 7.98 kcal/mol, forming intermediate LM1 ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{O}_{(\text{a})} + \text{H}_{(\text{a})}$, Figure 4-(4)), with the H atom attaching to $\mu_2\text{O(F)}$ and slightly exothermic by 0.11 kcal/mol. The length of the $\text{C-O}$ bond (LM1) is 1.31 Å, smaller than its initial value in mode A, 1.43 Å, indicating a stronger $\text{C-O}$ bond to have been formed. Alternatively, to break a $\text{C}_{\alpha}$-$H$ bond via TS2 forming a four-membered-ring intermediate ($\text{C}_{\alpha}$ binding to Rh(1), Figure 4-(5)), $\text{CH}_3\text{CHOH}_{(\text{a})} + \text{H}_{(\text{a})}$-(LM2), the energy barrier is 7.59 kcal/mol, slightly less than the former. The former path involves the formation of an acetaldehyde species, $CH_3CHO_{(a)} + 2H_{(a)}$ (LM3), with an energy barrier of 20.70 kcal/ mol and a slight endothermicity of 2.83 kcal/mol. The latter leads to formation of the LM4 intermediate, $CH_3COH_{(a)} + 2H_{(a)}$ (Figure 4-(7)), with an energy barrier of 15.37 kcal/mol and a slight exothermicity of 4.02 kcal/mol, indicating that LM2 → LM4 is favored over LM2 → LM3. Nevertheless, the next possible dehydrogenation step for both LM3 and LM4 intermediates leads in either case to the formation of LM5 $(CH_3CO_{(a)}$ + $3H_{(a)}$, Figure 4-(8)); the energy barrier for LM3 (LM3(scission of $C_{\alpha}-H$) → TS6 → LM5) is 8.47 kcal/mol with an exothermicity of 19.71 kcal/mol, and the barrier for LM4 (LM4(scission of $O-H$) → TS7 → LM5) is 14.50 kcal/mol with an exothermicity of 12.86 kcal/mol. As seen in Figure 5, the LM5 intermediate is the most stable with its four-membered-ring structure (Rh(1)−C−O−Al(2)), indicating that $C-C$ bond cleavage is impractical. There exists, however, a metastable state LM6 (Figure 4-(9), LM5 → LM6, endothermic by 21.20 kcal/ mol with no transition structure found) as a precursor for the next $C-C$ bond cleavage. In LM6, the four-membered-ring structure decomposes, with a shortened $C-O$ bond and extended $C-C$ bond, leading to the $C-C$ bond cleavage. Only 3.72 kcal/ mol is needed to cross the energy barrier for the $C-C$ bond cleavage from LM6 (but 24.92 kcal/mol from LM5), forming the product P1 $(CH_{3(a)} + CO_{(a)} + 3H_{(a)}$, Figure 4-(11)). Finally, the desorption to form $CH_{4(g)} + CO_{(g)} + H_{2(g)}$ is known to be driven by a large entropy term at elevated temperatures. Alternatively, the absorbed $CO_{(a)}$ might be converted to carbon dioxide through the water-gas shift (WGS) reaction, and $CH_{3(a)}$ might be further dehydrogenated to produce further hydrogen via steam reforming.

3.3.2. Decomposition of Ethanol from Adsorption Mode B.
Mode B is the most stable structure for ethanol adsorbed on a $2Rh/\gamma-Al_2O_3(110)$ surface with an adsorption energy of −34.50 kcal/mol. Our calculated barrier for $C_{\beta}-H$ bond scission is only 1.29 kcal/mol with a large exothermicity, 22.61 kcal/mol, forming the LM7 intermediate, $CH_2CH_2OH_{(a)}$, plus $H_{(a)}$ attached to Rh(1). The five-membered-ring structure (LM7) is a stable intermediate especially on Rh-containing metal surfaces. $^{36-39}$ With an extended $C-O$ bond (1.47 Å), LM7 is inclined to the cleavage of that $C-O$ bond, leaving the OH group on the Al atom, to form $CH_2CH_{2(a)} + OH_{(a)} + H_{(a)}$ (P2) (Figure 4-(12)). The adsorbed ethene on the Rh(1) atom forms a three- membered-ring structure with a large adsorption energy, in agreement with Valero et al. $^{40}$ Ethene is difficult to desorb from the surface due to its great adsorption energy. At a high temperature it would coke eventually, consistent with the experimental observation of ethanol steam reforming on a Rh/ $Al_2O_3$ surface for which ethene is a common byproduct at a small water/ethanol ratio. $^{14}$ According to the potential energy diagram in Figure 5, in the absence of $H_2O$, adsorption mode B of ethanol leading to ethene would be an important path, despite its large barrier, 34.0 kcal/mol, but with an exothermicity of −10.16 kcal/mol. We have calculated the addition of $H_2O$ molecules in the following system as a comparison for the ethanol decomposition paths.

The absence of these ring structures produces a simpler decomposition path, leading to the decrease of ethene and acetaldehyde formation while increasing the water/ethanol ratios in the experiment. $^{15,41-43}$

## 4. Conclusions
We have calculated the potential energy diagrams to describe the possible dehydrogenation paths of ethanol from its two most stable adsorption modes, and the effect of added $H_2O$ molecules on the reactions. Our results show that the presence of water molecules on the surface has little influence on the energy barrier in the cleavage of the C−C bond. $H_2O$ added to a surface such as $\gamma-Al_2O_3(110)$ alters the most stable adsorption structure of ethanol by saturating the surface aluminum atoms, which leads to a simple dissociation path and enhances the selectivity in forming products $CH_x$ and CO. Nevertheless, ethanol on a dehydrated $\gamma-Al_2O_3(110)$ supported surface tends to form ethene because its intermediate LM7 exhibits a five-membered-ring adsorption structure on the surface, which dissociates to ethene preferentially, in agreement with observations. $^{42,43}$
