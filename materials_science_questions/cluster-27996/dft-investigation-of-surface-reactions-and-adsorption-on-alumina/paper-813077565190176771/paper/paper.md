# Theoretical study of hydrogen dissociation on dialuminum oxide clusters and aluminum-silicon-oxygen clusters, as models of extraframework aluminum species and zeolite lattice

Povilas Lukinskas, Dan Fărcașiu*

Department of Chemical and Petroleum Engineering, University of Pittsburgh, 1249 Benedum Hall, Pittsburgh, PA 15261, USA

Received 9 May 2000; received in revised form 4 August 2000; accepted 5 August 2000

## Abstract

The reactivity of coordinatively unsaturated centers of aluminum oxide clusters, found for example in extraframework aluminum species of steamed zeolites, was examined theoretically on the example of the reaction of a hydrogen molecule with dialuminum hydroxide clusters $(HO)_{2}(H_{2}O)_{n}Al-O-Al(OH)_{2}(H_{2}O)$ (1). In the cluster, one aluminum atom is tricoordinated and the other is tetracoordinated $(2, n=0)$, or both aluminum atoms are tetracoordinated $(3, n=1)$. The system studied is also a model for transitional aluminas, in which two or three tetracoordinated aluminum centers can occur next to each other. Density functional theory calculations with electron correlation at the B3LYP/6-31G** level have identified a complex with physisorbed hydrogen and a complex with chemisorbed hydrogen in each case. Each of them was more stable than the corresponding complex formed by the corresponding one-aluminum cluster. The transition structures for chemisorption were identified. The reaction coordinate for chemisorption revealed that the reaction is a case of metal ion catalysis, rather than an acid-base reaction with heterolytic dissociation of hydrogen. The potential energy barrier (PEB) for hydrogen chemisorption was lower for the two-Al clusters than for the one-Al clusters. The chemisorption on a silicon-aluminum cluster, $(HO)_{2}(H_{2}O)Al-O-Si(OH)_{3}$ was also found to occur, but it had a higher PEB than for the corresponding two-aluminum cluster. Thus, zeolites can also exchange hydrogen, albeit less effectively than alumina, whereas the extraframework aluminum species in steamed zeolites should exchange hydrogen easier than the intact zeolite. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

**Keywords:** Hydrogen dissociation; Dialuminum oxide clusters; Aluminum-silicon-oxygen clusters

## 1. Introduction

The understanding of the reactivity of aluminum oxide is important for its use both as catalyst and as catalyst support. The dissociative chemisorption of hydrogen provides a good measure of catalytic activity of alumina [1]. This reaction has been studied by the examination of the hydrogen-deuterium isotope ex- change [2–7]. Elucidating its mechanism would help in designing catalytic systems, but experimental information that would clarify this matter is lacking. The reaction was discussed [8–11] in connection with the hydrogen "spillover" from a noble metal catalyst to the support [12–14] but the molecular mechanism of that reaction is also obscure. A case was reported where the phenomenon consisted of reduction of the support, rather than formation of hydrogen atoms adsorbed on it [15]. Attempts were made, therefore, to sort out the mechanism by theoretical (MO) calculations, both semiempirical [16] and ab initio [17,18].

The early computational studies examined the primitive clusters, ${\rm Al(OH)}_n$, where $n=3$ or 5. The ab initio calculations [19–21] were conducted with small basis sets and no electron correlation [17,18]. The tricoordinated cluster was found active [17,18], but no energy minimum with two hydrogens bonded was found for the cluster with $n=5$ [18]. The latter complex was not well described, but it must either have two negative charges or have an unnatural oxidation state for aluminum. It was found, however, that neglect of electron correlation leads in some cases to the misidentification of energy minima and transition states [22]. In addition, for the cluster found active, the reaction was forced on a reaction pathway predicating acid–base catalysis, involving the heterolytic cleavage of the hydrogen–hydrogen bond, synchronous with the bonding of the proton to the oxygen (basic site) and the hydride to aluminum (acid site) [17,18]. There was no reason, however, to postulate such a mechanism. Even though it is agreed that the reaction involves the coordinatively unsaturated aluminum atoms, which are also the Lewis acid centers [6,18,23,24] the nature of the reaction could be different from the standard Lewis acid–Lewis base interaction, because the noble metal atoms that dissociatively chemisorb hydrogen do not react as Lewis acids. Chlorinated alumina, which have stronger Lewis acid centers, gives the same level of hydrogen adsorption as the untreated oxides [11,25].

Another feature of the previous calculations was that tri- and pentacoordinated aluminum atoms were considered coordinatively unsaturated, but tetracoordinated atoms were not [16–18]. This assumption appeared theoretically unjustified to us. Moreover, the $^{15}$N NMR spectrum of pyridine-$^{15}$N adsorbed on $\gamma$-alumina had identified two types of Lewis acid sites [26]. As it was shown that the concentration of tricoordinated aluminum in that material is nil or vanishingly small [27], it was at least plausible that tetracoordinated sites possess Lewis acidity. Therefore, we reexamined the hydrogen chemisorption on tri-, tetra-, and pentacoordinated one-aluminum clusters, with geometry optimization and large basis sets [28]. The electron correlation was handled in traditional ab initio calculations [19] with the second order Møller–Plesset method (MP2) [21,29] and in density functional theory (DFT) calculations [30–32] with the B3LYP method [33,34]. The electrical neutrality of the tetra- and pentacoordinated aluminum clusters was achieved by using water as ligand. We found that two types of complexes were formed in all cases: with hydrogen physisorbed and with hydrogen chemisorbed. The former were weak, van der Waals, complexes, whereas the stability of the latter varied with the coordination level of the aluminum in the starting cluster, in the order: penta < tetra < tricoordinated. Good agreement was found between the MP2 and the B3LYP calculations [28].

The examination of the reaction coordinate without the imposition of a reaction pathway failed to substantiate the acid–base model postulated before [17]. Instead, the reaction was an example of metal ion catalysis, in which the hydrogen interacts with the aluminum atom until both hydrogen are within bonding distance and then one hydrogen migrates to the oxygen. The energy of activation, or, better said, the potential energy barrier (PEB) for the reaction varied in the order tri < tetra < pentacoordinated aluminum clusters. Based on the large difference in site concentrations in $\gamma$-alumina [27,35], it was concluded that tetracoordinated sites must be catalytically active in hydrogen chemisorption [28]. The same conclusion was reached at the same time by another group, for the CO chemisorption on alumina [36].

To investigate a model of extraframework aluminum species and to model better the interaction with alumina, we decided to investigate a larger cluster. In particular, the one-aluminum clusters contained only two kinds of oxygen atoms: in OH groups and in coordinated water. The bridging oxygens (Al–O–Al moieties), which exist in aluminum oxides, are not present in the small clusters. We examined, therefore, two-aluminum clusters and we report our findings here.

## 2. Computational method

The calculations were conducted with the programs Gaussian 94 [37] and Gaussian 98 [38], in the manner described previously [28]. The geometry optimizations were conducted with electron correlation with the DFT-B3LYP [30–34] method and the 6–31-G** basis set. The cluster containing one tricoordinated and one tetracoordinated aluminum atom was optimized with the terminal atoms frozen in the positions they had in the hydrated form and with the central part

allowed a free movement (Mode B in the previous paper [28]), as described below. The cluster containing two tetracoordinated aluminum atoms was optimized both in Mode B and in the unrestricted mode. The cluster containing one aluminum atom and one silicon atom was optimized without restrictions. The nature of the stationary points identified on the potential energy surface (minima or transition structures) was checked by frequency analyses and zero-point energy (ZPE) corrections were applied to all calculated energies. No corrections were made, however, for the basis set superposition error (BSSE) [39]. They would affect mostly the energies of the physisorbed complexes and would be, therefore, inconsequential. The transition structures were determined by standard Berny optimizations; a geometry close to that found for the corresponding one-aluminum cluster [28] was used as the first guess in each case.

The projections of the molecular geometry shown here were generated with the computer program Xmol [40]. Assignment of calculated frequencies to specific vibration modes was performed with the computer program MOLDEN [41].

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Cluster model selection

The agreement between the calculations conducted with the MP2 and B3LYP methods gave us confidence in expanding the calculations to larger clusters, which cannot be handled with a sufficiently large basis set at the MP2 level. In choosing the structures to be examined, it was important that the type of site included be present on the surface of $\gamma$-alumina in significant quantities. Thus, even if tricoordinated aluminum atoms are present in quantities catalytically relevant, the existence of two such sites adjacent to one another cannot be realistically expected. Normally observed are hexacoordinated and tetracoordinated aluminum atoms. Pentacoordinated sites, even when identified [27], were found in significantly smaller concentration. A typical composition was reported to consist of 70% octahedral aluminum atoms and 30% tetrahedral aluminum atoms [35], which means that two adjacent tetrahedral sites are likely to exist. Therefore, the active sites in the clusters considered in this work were tri- and tetracoordinated and the second atom was tetracoordinated in each case. As in the previous study [28], the electrical neutrality of the clusters was assured by using water as ligand. The clusters are, therefore, described by the common structure **1**, where $n$ is zero for the tri-plus tetracoordinated cluster (**2**) and one for the tetra-plus tetracoordinated cluster (**3**).

$$
\begin{align*}
n = 0 \tag{2} \\
\ce{(HO)2(H2O)_{n}Al-O-Al(OH)2(H2O)} \tag{1} \\
n = 1 \tag{3}
\end{align*}
$$

Clusters in which the second aluminum atom was hexacoordinated were also considered. The tri- plus hexacoordinated cluster rearranged to a tetra- plus pentacoordinated cluster by migration of a water molecule, whereas the tetra- plus hexacoordinated cluster lost a molecule of water during the geometry optimization and formed a tetra-plus pentacoordinated cluster. The silica-alumina cluster naturally consisted of a tetracoordinated aluminum connected to a tetracoordinated silicon.

We did not attempt to extrapolate from the clusters to models of the solid by the application of periodic boundary conditions, which presumes that the entire unit cell including the reactants is periodically repeated [42], because the sites that we wanted to model are defect sites. The active alumina surface is irregular. Moreover, embedding the structural moiety into the surface should have more of an effect on the energies of reaction and activation than on the mechanism of reaction and relative reactivity of sites of different structure, which were our goals. For the zeolite model, regular unit cells to be repeated do exist, but they are rather large: one of the smallest, in Chabazite, has 36 atoms, whereas that of ZSM-5 has 288 atoms [43]. It was important to conduct our calculations at a satisfactory level of theory, with a large basis set. Also, it was reported that the errors from neglect of long-range electrostatic effects are less important than the errors from inadequate optimization of structures [44]. A comparison of the results obtained for the double clusters with the results obtained for the one-aluminum clusters allows to establish the effect of the neighboring aluminum center on the chemisorption at the reactive aluminum center. The small clusters are, of course, appropriate for modeling the extraframework aluminum species present in steamed zeolites [45].

### 3.2. The reaction of a tricoordinated aluminum site in a cluster

It is now established [27,35] that the concentration of tricoordinated aluminum sites on the surface is much lower than once believed [46]. If they exist, however, a significant part of them should be adjacent to tetracoordinated sites [27].

We noted previously that the reactivity of tricoordinated aluminum centers strongly depends upon the degree of pyramidalization [28]. Adsorption on an $\text{Al(-O)}_3$ site constrained as a regular tetrahedron was highly exothermic, whereas planar $\text{Al(-O)}_3$ did not bind hydrogen at all. None of these geometries is necessarily adequate for the site on the surface. Transitional aluminas are formed by the calcination of highly hydrated aluminum oxides, such as boehmite, which was modeled at the primitive cluster level by $(\text{HO})_3\text{Al(OH}_2\text{)}_{x+1}$. One can consider that the bulk of the solid is already set to a significant extent at the boehmite stage and the structural reorganization upon calcination affects mostly the surface [1]. Therefore, geometry optimizations was conducted on the hydrated species, after which a molecule of water was removed to obtain the model of the active site on the surface after calcination. Then, the geometry of the dehydrated (reactive) cluster, $(\text{HO})_3\text{Al(OH}_2\text{)}_x$, and of products and transition structures for the reaction with hydrogen were optimized with constraints, in two modes [28]. In the first (A), the bond angles and dihedral angles around the aluminum atom were frozen as in the hydrated cluster and the other geometrical parameters were optimized.

The alternative mode, B, was more difficult to optimize, but was chemically more realistic. In it, the rigidity of the solid was simulated by freezing the end atoms of the cluster in the positions which they had in the hydrated form, then optimizing the geometry of the central part of the cluster. This allowed a "breathing" movement of the active site.

For the two-aluminum species, the hydrated cluster is $\boldsymbol{4}$ and dehydration to the reactive cluster $(\boldsymbol{2})$ is modeled by Eq. (1):

$$

Four terminal atoms were frozen in the optimization of $\boldsymbol{2}$ and of species formed from it, in Mode B: two terminal hydrogens in $\text{OH}$ groups at the active $\text{Al}$ site and two oxygen atoms in $\text{OH}$ groups bonded to the neighboring aluminum atom. Those frozen atoms are represented in bold letters in structure $\boldsymbol{2}$. With these constraints, the active site on the surface was allowed to optimize. Upon reoptimization of $\boldsymbol{2}$, the tricoordinated site flattened to a nearly planar structure (dihedral angle $\varphi(\text{O1-O2-Al-O}_\text{b}) = 178.7^\circ$), whereas $\text{Al-O}$ bonds were compressed to 1.69–1.72 Å, compared to about 1.74 Å in the neighboring tetracoordinated site.

The kinetics of chemisorption is difficult to assess. It was indicated that the reaction is slow (half-life of the order of hours at $500^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$), but the pressure and the volume of hydrogen used were not disclosed [11]. There are two reports of activation energies, both rather old, and none measured actual rates of exchange [5,7]. The observation of variation of the $E_{\mathrm{a}}$ with temperature indicated diffusion control [7].

Our result that a two-aluminum cluster is more reactive than a one-aluminum cluster is in line with the report based on rotational echo double resonance experiments (REDOR) that the Lewis acid sites on alumina consist of pairs of adjacent coordinatively unsaturated aluminum atoms [47].

The calculations shed light also on the nature of the reactivity of aluminosilicates, particularly zeolites. The interaction of hydrogen with these catalysts can in principle be centered either at Lewis sites (aluminum atoms) or at Brønsted sites (acidic hydroxyl groups). Examination of the hydrogen existing in a dehydrated silica-alumina failed to reveal any Brønsted acid sites, however. Only strongly electrophilic Lewis acid centers were observed [4,48]. It had also been established that an aluminosilicate calcined at $800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ was more active for hydrogen-deuterium exchange than the same material calcined at $400^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ [49]. For zeolites, the Brønsted sites are the acid sites. Even though all the aluminum atoms in an intact zeolite crystal are tetracoordinated (some pentacoordinated aluminum atoms have been identified in some materials) [50] they are not normally considered as Lewis acid sites. In fact, it is difficult to assess the Lewis acidity of those centers, as each is adjacent to a Brønsted site and the usual Lewis probe bases (pyridine, ammonia, water, methanol) are also Brønsted bases. The Brønsted sites are also considered as responsible for the activity in the reactions of methane, for example [51,52], although the acid strength of zeolites is far below the level found necessary for such reactions in solution $[53,54] .{ }^{2}$ For the chemisorption and exchange of hydrogen, this quandary has now been removed. Hydrogen does not react with the acidic protons and the reactivity of aluminum atoms is manifested toward it. We find, however, that an aluminosilicate is less reactive than alumina toward hydrogen. This result is in line with the experimental report on the hydrogen-deuterium exchange on alumina and alumina-silica [49]. We could not find any data about the reaction rate and activation parameters for hydrogen exchange on zeolites. ## 4. Conclusions

We found that the increase in size of aluminum hy- droxide clusters from one aluminum to two aluminum increases the stability of the product of hydrogen ad- dition and reduces the energy barrier for the dissocia- tive hydrogen chemisorption. We note that for a 30% content of tetracoordinated aluminum atoms [35], statistical distribution leads to a significant fraction of aluminum atoms being adjacent ("double clusters") and kinetically significant fractions being present in triple clusters or even in clusters of four. The exis- tence of clusters of coordinatively unsaturated alu- minum atoms might be the explanation of the effect of extraframework aluminum in steamed zeolites, which thus adds another mechanistic component to the Brønsted acidity of the framework [58]. The tetraco- ordinated aluminum in silica-alumina and zeolites re- tains its Lewis acidity and the ability to effect metal ion catalysis, but is less active than in the all-aluminum ox- ides. The synergistic effect for this type of reactivity of other metal cations present as impurities in the frame- work or in the cage of zeolites is worth considering.
