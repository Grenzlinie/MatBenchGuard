# Field-enhanced Si–Si bond-breakage mechanism for time-dependent dielectric breakdown in thin-film SiO₂ dielectrics

J. W. McPherson,⁽ᵃ⁾ V. K. Reddy, and H. C. Mogul
Texas Instruments, Inc., M/S 385, Dallas, Texas 75243

(Received 1 May 1997; accepted for publication 24 June 1997)

A field-enhanced Si–Si bond-breakage mechanism is presented which accurately describes the time-dependent dielectric breakdown behavior recently reported for thin-film SiO₂ dielectrics over a wide range of fields and temperatures. The breakdown kinetics (both the field and temperature dependence) are shown to be consistent with a field-dependent dipolar energy term associated with an oxygen vacancy which serves to reduce the activation energy required for Si–Si bond breakage.
© 1997 American Institute of Physics. [S0003-6951(97)02834-9]

The correct physical model for time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) in thin SiO₂ films is of great reliability interest in very large scale integrated circuit (VLSI) applications and has been the subject of much debate. McPherson and Baglee first proposed a thermochemical degradation model¹⁻³ for breakdown which is driven by a free energy difference between the stable dielectric and the broken-down dielectric. The authors showed that, by treating the dielectric as a collection of dipoles, the free energy of activation could be expressed as a series expansion of the electric field $E$ in the oxide. By keeping only the first term in the free energy of activation expansion, the time-to-failure equation was shown to obey an exponential dependence on the electric field. This model is commonly referred to as the ‘‘$E$-model.’’ Others,⁴·⁵ however, have suggested that the breakdown process is current driven and should show an exponential dependence with $1/E$, due to Fowler–Nordheim conduction, and is commonly referred to as the ‘‘$1/E$ model.’’ The $E$ versus $1/E$ controversy has continued for many years due to the fact that either model can fit TDDB rather well over limited field ranges. In order to clearly differentiate between the two models, TDDB data must be collected over a wide range of fields and, hopefully, even extending close to normal VLSI operating fields of approximately 5 MV/cm. Low-field TDDB data collection, however, requires very long test times.

Recently, however, there have been several long-term low-field TDDB studies published⁶⁻⁹ with each showing that the $E$ model fits the data better than does the $1/E$ model, with the most recent work covering a wide range of conditions (fields and temperatures) including those relatively close to expected device operating conditions. It is shown here that the very latest low-field TDDB data presented by Kimura is consistent with an assumed oxygen vacancy and its associated dipole moment interacting with the local electric field serving to reduce the activation energy required for Si–Si bond breakage. The molecular origins and the physics of low-field TDDB for intrinsic SiO₂ are just now starting to be properly understood.

The primary structural unit of all solid forms of SiO₂ is the SiO₄ tetrahedron shown in Fig. 1. The local tetrahedral atomic order, with bond angle $\phi = 109^\circ$ between O–Si–O, is maintained in all forms of SiO₂ whether it be crystalline, vitreous, or amorphous. Amorphous SiO₂ (which is of greater interest here) still shows the local tetrahedral coordination. The linking of these tetrahedra to form the solid is achieved through the bonding which occurs at the corners of the tetrahedra. The bonding angle $\Theta$ between the Si–O–Si atoms, at the corners of adjacent tetrahedra, is also shown in Fig. 1. This ever changing bond angle (generally varies from $120^\circ$ to $180^\circ$) procedures a ‘‘continuous random network model’’ for amorphous SiO₂.¹⁰

![](./images/812646378231562242_3.jpg)

FIG. 1. The local tetrahedral order is shown with the bond angle between O–Si–O given to be $\phi = 109^\circ$. In the continuous random network model for amorphous SiO₂, the bonding angle $\Theta$ for the Si–O–Si can vary from $120^\circ$ to $180^\circ$. Oxygen vacancies tend to form outside of this $\Theta$ range.

The bond strength of the Si–O–Si bond is severely weakened when the bond angle deviates from the mean bond angle of about $150^\circ$. Generally, bond angles greater than $180^\circ$ or less than $120^\circ$ are rarely observed¹⁰ and an oxygen vacancy tends to occur. These oxygen vacancies ($E'$ centers) can easily be detected with electron spin resonance (ESR) techniques.¹¹ The oxygen vacancy results in a Si–Si bond replacing the Si–O–Si bonding that would normally be expected in a stoichiometric network. It is this molecular defect (oxygen vacancy) in intrinsic SiO₂ films that is thought to dominate time-dependent breakdown.

$$
E_{\text{loc}}=\left(\frac{3+\chi}{3}\right)E_{\text{ox}} , \tag{1}

where $\chi$ is the electric susceptibility of the dielectric (2.9 for SiO₂) and $E_{\text{ox}}$ is the voltage dropped across the dielectric divided by the dielectric thickness. The local electric field is referred to as the Lorentz relation or Mossotti field.¹² Since the net polarization in SiO₂ must be induced, the permanent dipoles parallel to the electric field must grow (by localized lattice distortion) at the expense of the dipoles oriented antiparallel to the field, with the potential energy for the upper and lower parts of the $\mathrm{O}_{3} \equiv \mathrm{Si}-\mathrm{Si} \equiv \mathrm{O}_{3}$ molecule given by, respectively,

$$
U_{2}=U_{0}-p \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}}-\frac{1}{2} \cdot \alpha \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}, \tag{2}
$$

and

$$
U_{1}=U_{0}+p \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}}-\frac{1}{2} \cdot \alpha \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}, \tag{3}
$$

where $p$ is the permanent $\mathrm{Si} \equiv \mathrm{O}_{3}$ dipole moment component $[=3(z^{*}e)(1.2\ \mathring{A})]$ either parallel or antiparallel to the local electric field [see insert in Fig. 3] and $\alpha$ is molecular polarizability.

![](./images/812646378231562242_5.jpg)

FIG. 3. Comparison of the model with the activation energy reported by Kimura.

Note that the two silicon atoms [labeled 1 and 2 in Fig. 2(a)] are no longer at equivalent energy positions when an external field is applied. If the Si-Si bond were to be broken, and the bottom portion of the molecule collapses to a near planar $sp^{2}$ hybridization [as is shown in Fig. 2(b)], then the dipolar energy contribution $(p \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}})$ can be used to lower the activation energy required for bond breakage. Thus, if the enthalpy of activation for Si-Si bond breakage in the absence of field is $(\Delta H)_{0}$ then, in the presence of field, the activation energy is lowered by the amount,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\Delta H) &=(\Delta H)_{0}-p \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}} \\
&=(\Delta H)_{0}-3\left(z^{*} e\right)(1.2 \mathring{A})\left(\frac{3+\chi}{3}\right) E_{\mathrm{ox}}. \tag{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

The quadratic-field term has been ignored in Eq. (4) since the induced dipole moment energy term can be shown to be small compared to the permanent dipole energy term. The relative importance of the linear and quadratic field terms can be estimated using the Claussius-Mossotti relation for $\alpha,^{13}$

$$
\alpha=\frac{3\left(\epsilon_{r}-1\right) \epsilon_{0}}{\left(\epsilon_{r}+2\right) N_{v}}, \tag{5}
$$

where $\epsilon_{r}(=3.9)$ is the relative dielectric constant for SiO₂, $\epsilon_{0}(=5.52 \times 10^{5}\ \text{eV/V cm})$ is the permittivity of free space, and $N_{v}(=2.3 \times 10^{22}/\mathrm{cm}^{3})$ is the number of induced dipole moments per unit volume. This gives $\alpha=3.54$ ×10⁻¹⁷ e cm²/V and indicates that the quadratic term is much smaller than the linear term even at high fields. For example, at $E_{\text{ox}}=10$ MV/cm,

$$
\frac{\frac{1}{2} \alpha \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}}{p \cdot E_{\mathrm{loc}}}<0.01. \tag{6}
$$

According to the thermochemical model, proposed in previous work,³ if the Si-Si bond breakage is the elemental (molecular) unit of dielectric degradation then the time-to-failure equation becomes

$$
T F=A_{0} \exp \left[\frac{(\Delta H)_{0}-(7.2 e\ \mathring{A}) E_{\mathrm{ox}}}{k_{B} T}\right], \tag{7}
$$

where we have assumed that $z^{*} \sim 1$ since Si-O is very polar [with an electronegativity of 1.7] and becomes even more polar with significant bond distortion. Since the Si-Si bond is undoubtedly stretched at the site of an oxygen vacancy, the enthalpy of activation $(\Delta H)_{0}$ was estimated from the average Si-Si binding energy from bulk samples and determined to be 450 kJ/mole¹⁴ or 1.15 eV per bond. This is consistent with the value reported by Kimura.⁹

In Figs. 3 and 4, we show the comparison of the Kimura data and the model prediction. The agreement between the model prediction and the TDDB data is excellent, especially when considering the fact that no parameter optimization was undertaken. Not only are the qualitative features correct (prediction of a field-dependent activation energy and a temperature-dependent field acceleration factor) but the quantitative agreement is also good.

In conclusion, the excellent agreement between the thermochemical model and TDDB data suggests strongly that

the oxygen vacancy is the underlying molecular defect, in an otherwise intrinsic $SiO_{2}$ thin film, which is responsible for TDDB under low field and moderate temperature conditions. The local electric field serves to lower the activation energy required for $Si-Si$ bond breakage thereby accelerating the breakdown reaction rate. Eventual charge trapping at these broken bond sites and their wavefunction overlap leads to a conduction sub-band formation and severe Joule heating (thermal run away) conditions during the final stages of the breakdown process.
