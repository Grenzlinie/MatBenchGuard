# Defect scaling in non-crystalline floppy/under-constrained and rigid/over-constrained thin films: Applications to a-Se, a-Si, and a-Si(H)

Gerald Lucovsky $^{a, *}$ , Safa O. Kasap $^{b}$ , James C. Phillips $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Department of Physics, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC 27695-8202, USA
$^{b}$ Electrical Engineering Department, University of Saskatchewan, Saskatoon, Canada S7N 549
$^{c}$ Department of Physics, Rutgers University, Piscataway, NJ 08854, USA

Available online 20 February 2008

## Abstract

Bond constraint theory provides insights into glass and intermediate phase formation in covalently-bonded non-crystalline networks. Metrics are the average number of bonds/atom, $N_{av}$, and average number of bending and stretching constraints per atom, $C_{av}$. $C_{av}$ is a linear function of $N_{av}$:$C_{av}=2.5N_{av}-3$, with ideal, low defect density glasses and thin films having values of $N_{av}$ and $C_{av}$ equal to 2.4, and 3.0, respectively. In over-constrained films, it has been argued and demonstrated experimentally, that the density of intrinsic defects is proportional to $N_{av}-2.4$. In this paper we demonstrate that this scaling relationship, with the same empirical constants applies to floppy or under-constrained films with the example being a-Se where $N_{av}$ has been determined from EXAFS studies and is ~2.2. In addition we demonstrate that the density of defects in as deposited a-Si with a 6% density deficit, and broken bonding constraints, can be treated in the same way as spacing-filling over-constrained networks.

## 1. Introduction

Semi-empirical bond constraint theory, SE-BCT, has provided important insights into intermediate phase (IP) formation and defects in over-coordinated covalently- bonded non-crystalline space-filling networks in both bulk-quenched glasses and thin films [1,2]. This approach uses two mean-field metrics, the average number of bonds/atom, $N_{av}$ (also designated as $\langle r\rangle$ or $r_{c}$ ), and the average number of constraints per atom, $C_{av}$ (also designated as $n_{c}$ ). Constraint counting was initially performed using a valence force approach that included only 2-body bond-stretching and 3-body bond-bending valence forces [3]. For example, if $m$ is the atomic coordination, 4 for Si and Ge, 3 for As, and 2 for Se and Te, etc., then the number of bond-stretching constraints/atom, $C_{av}(st)=m/2$, and the number of bond-bending constraints $C_{av}(bnd)=2m-3$. These values have been used to establish a linear relationship between $C_{av}$ and $N_{av}$:$C_{av}=2.5N_{av}-3$. Easy glass formation, and low defect densities are predicted when $C_{av}$ equals the number of atomic degrees of freedom,3, for a spacing-filling continuous random network (CNR) with a dimensionality of 3. This gives values of $C_{av}^{*}=3$, and $N_{av}^{*}=2.4$, that have been used to mark the boundary between (i) under-constrained films, and (ii) over-constrained films.

Films with $C_{av}>C_{av}^{*}$, or equivalently $N_{av}>N_{av}^{*}$ have been designated as stressed-rigid with intrinsic bonding defects: e.g., dangling bond traps or recombination centers. Since 3-body bond- bending forces are smaller than 2-body bond-stretching forces [3], bond angle distortions, $\delta \theta$, increase with increas ing local strain which is proportional to $\sim N_{av}-N_{av}^{*}$, since strain energy scales as $(\delta \theta)^{2}$, the defect density, $D$, whichis correlated with strain energy, will scale as $D_{o}+$  $D_{1}[N_{av}-N_{av}^{*}]^{2}$ . $D_{o}$ and $D_{1}$ are empirical constants given by: $D_{o} \sim 1 ×10^{16} cm^{-3}$ and $D_{1} \sim 6 ×10^{17} cm^{-3}$ and have been obtained for stressed-rigid, space-filling materials, and interfaces [5]. To date this approach has been applied to films where $N_{av}>N_{av}^{*}$ , but not to materials in which bond-bending constraints are broken, and removed from the counting [2], or to materials where the valence force formulation applies, but where $N_{av}<N_{av}^{*}$ . This paper pre sents examples that address each of these types of thin film materials: (i) a-Si and a-Si(H) where bonding constraints are broken, and the films are generally inhomogeneous with respect to density deficits in a-Si, and clustered H in a-Si(H), and (ii) a-Se and a-Se alloys which are under-coor- dinated with $N_{av}<N_{av}^{*}$ .

This paper identifies two important force-field exten- sions that go beyond the original SE-BCT formulation. These have initially been used provide a basis for under- standing systematic differences in the widths of IP win- dows, and the values of $r_{c}(C_{av})$ that bracket these windows [6]. These modifications include (i) broken bond-bending constraints, which generally result from strain reducing chemical bonding self-organizations that minimize bond-strain at the expense of reductions in con- figurational entropy, and/or (ii) longer range many-body forces that are associated with repulsive interactions between electrons in lone pair orbitals on 2-and 3-fold coordinated alloy atoms, e.g., Se and As, as well as 1-fold coordinated terminal halogens such as I [6] (see Fig. 1). The focus here will be on the broken bending constraints.

The concept of broken bond-bending constraints for tet- rahedrally coordinated Si with two O and two Si bonding neighbors was first addressed by Lucovsky and Phillips in Ref. [7]. On the other hand, it is unlikely that the asym metric O-Si-Si bond angles will be constrained to the same degree because the local energy gaps are associated with O- Si and Si-Si bonds are different. If these gaps are suffi- ciently different the Pauling bonding resonance responsible for bond-bending forces is expected to be negligibly weak compared to the resonances responsible for the bond-bend- ing forces associated with the symmetric Si-Si-Si and O- Si-O bond angles, and the constraints for the asymmetric bond angles will be removed from constraint counting and characterized as broken constraints.

## 2. Experimental results and data reduction and analysis

### 2.1. a-Se

Shallow trapping, and shallow trap-controlled drift mobilities in a-Se have been studied extensively by the group at Xerox Corporation in the Webster Research Lab [9], by one of the co-authors (SOK) [10]. These studies have revealed two shallow traps, one at each band edge centered at energies $\sim 0.2-0.3$ eV removed from these edges. These traps determine the temperature dependence, and other properties of the shallow trap-controlled electron and hole drift mobilities. There are also deeper traps that *terminate the life* of the charged carriers that are not addressed in this article. The shallow traps have integrated densities of $\sim 1 \times 10^{16}-10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ (the exact value depends on the model and the density of states used to interpret the drift mobility data). These densities are approximately consistent with the difference between $N_{\mathrm{av}}$ for Se, 2.2, and 2.4, the value for an ideal 3-D CRN. Based on the ideal bonding in Se, $N_{\mathrm{av}}$, but many of the samples used in these studies were temperature stabilized with As and $\mathrm{Cl}$, and the resulting increase in $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ is consistent with an increase in the effective value of $N_{\mathrm{av}}$, i.e., an increase from 2 to 2.2. The value of 2.2 was obtained from EXAFS studies in Ref. [11].

The local bonding at these traps has been studied in Ref.[12]. They are associated with band edge non-bonding $3 \mathrm{p}-\pi$ states in parallel alignment, rather than at $\pm 90^{\circ}$ as in the bonding of ring and chain species in crystalline and amorphous Se [13]. One way to understand the origin of these defect states is to consider them being generated by chemical bonding self-organizations that contribute to a gain in configurational entropy at a modest cost in repulsive energy.

As already noted above, the experimental value of $N_{\mathrm{av}}$ for a-Se, as determined by EXAFS to be $\sim 2.2$ [12]. Based on the original formulation of SE-BCT that includes bond-stretching force constants, as well as un-broken bond-bending forces, the values of the constants, $D_{\mathrm{o}} \sim 1 \times$ $10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ and $D_{1} \sim 6 \times 10^{17} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$, that have used in the defect counting equation

$$
D \sim 1 \times 10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}+6 \times 10^{17} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}\left[N_{\mathrm{av}}-N_{\mathrm{av}}^{*}\right]^{2} \tag{1}
$$

have determined from over- rather than under-coordinated compositions. Substituting $N_{\mathrm{av}}=2.2$, and maintaining $N_{\mathrm{av}}^{*}=2.4$, yields a value for $D=2.3 \times 10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$. This is in excellent agreement with the experimentally determine values of $2 \pm 1 \times 10^{16} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$. This comparison between the empirical defect scaling and experiment establishes for the first time the generality of Eq. (1) with respect its application to materials in both the (i) floppy and under-constrained, and (ii) stressed-rigid and over-constrained regimes as well. The next subsection deals with defects in a-Si, where a model for the local bonding includes divacancy defects to explain a density deficit.

### 2.2. a-Si and a-Si(H)

The lowest levels of dangling bond defects in a-Si thin films have been addressed in Ref. [14], and are (i) $\sim 1 \times$ $10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ for a-Si deposited by magnetron sputtering at $300 \mathrm{~K}$, and (ii) $3 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$ in glow-discharge deposited a-Si(H) at $500 \mathrm{~K}$ [14]. To date, there have been no theories or models proposed to account for these dangling bond defect densities. They cannot be explained by SE-BCT in space-filling networks, because the resulting values of $N_{\mathrm{av}}$ are too large, 4 for a-Si, and 3.7 for a-Si(H). It is important to note broken bond-bending constraints have been taken into account in the determinations of $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ for a-Si(H).

The defect densities can be understood by taking into that a-Si and a-Ge, as well have significant density deficits, $\sim 6 \%$ on average [15,16]. If we assume this deficit is taken up in small voids [15,16], e.g., divacancies, then the density of defects in a-Si is readily accounted for (see Fig. 3).

removal of 2 Si atoms gives 6 Si atoms with 3-fold coordination pointing into the void space, as well producing broken bond-bending constraints on 18 other Si atoms, the three nearest neighbors the 6 Si atoms.

The sites with occupied dangling bonds are negatively charged, and those with empty dangling bond states are positively charged. Each of these configurations is effectively 3-fold coordinated with a value of $C_{\mathrm{av}}=4.5$. The 18 nearest neighbors are effectively in a distorted tetrahedral environment that breaks 1/2 of the bond-stretching constraints (the one that involves the dangling bond), and 1/2 of a each of the bending constraints associated with the two independent bond-angles that involve one back-bonded Si atom, and the Si atom with the dangling bond. This gives a value of $C_{\mathrm{av}}=5.5$, or an effective coordination of 3.4 for the Si atoms that are in the first two atomic shells of the divacancy. The remaining Si atoms are in fully constrained 4-fold coordinated sites. Proceeding in this way, $N_{\mathrm{av}} \sim 3.8$, and the calculated concentration of defects, $D=1.2 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$, in excellent agreement with the experimental value of $\sim 1 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The density deficit associated with divacancies then reduces strain in the density deficient network, but not to the same level of an ideal spacing-filling CRN with $<10^{17} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ defects.

The value of $3 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ in a-Si(H) cannot be explained by SE-BCT, even with the inclusion of a bonded H concentration of $10 \%$. Instead it is explained by noting that the density of dangling bonds at a $\mathrm{Si}-\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ interface [10] is essentially the same as in a-Si, equivalently, $\sim 2 \times$ $10^{12} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$ or equivalently $\sim 3 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$. Passivation by hydrogen atoms reduces this by a factor of about 500 to $\sim 3 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$ or equivalently, $5 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$. Assuming the same efficiency for dangling bond passivation by hydrogen atoms yields a value of $\sim 2.4 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$ for a-Si(H), again in excellent agreement with experiment.

## 3. Summary

The applications of SE-BCT to a-Se and a-Si have demonstrated: (i) defect densities in both floppy and over-constrained CRN amorphous thin films and glasses can be explained in the context of defect scaling as given by Eq. (1), and (ii) values of $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ are reduced by broken bond-bending constraints, so that $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ can no longer be obtained from the SE-BCT relationship between $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{av}}$ that applies in the limit of 2-body bond-stretching and 3-body bond-bending forces, i.e., $C_{\mathrm{av}}=2.5 N_{\mathrm{av}}-3$.

We have included calculated values of $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ for several Ge-Sb-Te alloys to indicate the effect of broken bond-bending constraints on $C_{\mathrm{av}}$, even though there are no measurements of defect densities to compare with estimates of defect densities based on Eq. (1). When broken bond-bending constraints are taken into account, higher values of $N_{\mathrm{av}}$ correspond to values of $C_{\mathrm{av}} \sim 3$; this is illustrated in Table 1 for the $\mathrm{Ge}_{2} \mathrm{Se}_{3}$ composition as well as several for Ge-Sb-Te alloys, including the $\mathrm{Ge}_{2} \mathrm{Se}_{2} \mathrm{Te}_{5}$ composition used in optical and electrical memory devices [2,17,18]. In each instance where broken bond-bending constraints apply in Table 1 because of either $\mathrm{Ge}-\mathrm{Ge}-\mathrm{Se}_{3 / 2}$ or $\mathrm{Ge}-\mathrm{Ge}-\mathrm{Te}_{3 / 2}$ arrangements, the values of $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ that take broken constraints into account are reduced with respect to the values of $C_{\mathrm{av}}$ that are based on the SE-BCT constraint counting that includes un-broken bond-bending constraints.
