# Surface Science Letters

## Stabilization mechanism of Si(1 1 1) $7\times7$ domain growth: important role of shared corner-holes

Wataru Shimada $^{a,*}$, Tomohiko Kato $^{b}$, Hiroshi Tochihara $^{c}$

$^{a}$ National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST), Tsukisamu-higashi, Sapporo 062-8517, Japan
$^{b}$ Fukuoka Institute of Technology, Wajiro, Fukuoka 811-0295, Japan
$^{c}$ Department of Molecular and Material Sciences, Kyushu University, Kasuga, Fukuoka 816-8580, Japan

Received 14 May 2001; accepted for publication 5 July 2001

### Abstract
We analyze the stabilization mechanism in the dimer-adatom-stacking-fault (DAS) structure formation on Si(1 1 1). A single faulted (F) half of the unit cell of the DAS structure grows into a small DAS domain, as previously observed by scanning tunneling microscopy. New F-halves are created from a corner-hole of an existing F-half at the edge of the DAS domain. Atomic-structure modeling of the formation process of a new F-half implies that a corner-hole shared by two F-halves reduces the number of dangling bonds. We calculated the surface free energy during the $7\times7$ DAS domain growth using an established cell model to deduce the critical size. The critical size of the DAS domain consists of two F-halves sharing one corner-hole at 653 K, which is in good agreement with our experimental results. We conclude that the shared corner-hole has a strong stabilizing effect on the DAS domain. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

**Keywords**: Scanning tunneling microscopy; Adatoms; Growth; Surface thermodynamics (including phase transitions); Surface energy

### 1. Introduction
The $7\times7$ structure is known to fit the dimer-adatom-stacking-fault (DAS) model [1,2], and thus is one of the most complicated surface structures. As a result, the formation process of the Si(1 1 1) $7\times7$ reconstruction has generated much interest. Several attempts to elucidate the formation process have been carried out. For instance, Ohdomari and coworkers carried out high-temperature (HT) scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) observations of unreconstructed regions (hereafter denoted '1 × 1') at 600-1000 K after quenching, and concluded that faulted (F) halves of the unit cell play a leading role in the DAS domain growth [3]. They also determined the formation and the decay rates of F-halves during the formation of $(2n+1)\times(2n+1)$ domains ($n=2$, 3,4), and claimed that a minimization of the periphery length stabilizes these domains [4-7]. Unfortunately, they analyzed only the formation or decay rates of F-halves, and did not take into account the atomic structures of the DAS domain.

Recently, we proposed a formation mechanism for single F-halves [8-11] and domain growth [12-14] of the DAS structure on Si(1 1 1). This was

[15] observed even-sized F-halves $(2n\times2n$-F) and odd-sized, irregular-type F-halves (denoted as $(2n+1)\times(2n+1)$-F$'$) in the formation of F-halves. We have demonstrated that during their formation and decay, these F-halves change their size sequentially as ‘$1\times1$’$\leftrightarrow5\times5$-F$'\leftrightarrow6\times6$-F$\leftrightarrow7\times7$-F$'\leftrightarrow8\times8$-F$\leftrightarrow9\times9$-F$'\leftrightarrow10\times10$-F$\leftrightarrow11\times11$-F$'\leftrightarrow12\times12$-F$\leftrightarrow13\times13$-F$'$ [11]. We have also analyzed the atomic structures of various types of corner-holes during domain growth [12–14].

On the other hand, a few theoretical studies were done to better understand the phase transition between the $7\times7\leftrightarrow$ ‘$1\times1$’ structures [16,17]. However, the models in these studies include assumptions that unrealistically represent atomic processes of the phase transition. Vanderbilt estimated the formation energy and stability of each element of the DAS structure [18]. Kato et al. then proposed a cell model [19] for the Si(1 1 1) surface using energy values estimated by Vanderbilt [18] irrespective of any other unknown parameters. In this model, the one-by-one formation processes are considered for formation of F-halves in DAS domain growth. Monte-Carlo simulation with this cell model reproduced the first-order phase transition between $7\times7\leftrightarrow$ ‘$1\times1$’ structures, and the obtained value of the transition temperature agreed with experiments. Using this model, Kato et al. also simulated growth patterns of the DAS domain [20], and calculated the probability distributions of the various sizes and types of F-halves [21].

The purpose of this paper is to describe the stabilization mechanism of the DAS structure during growth from a single F-half to a small domain. Based on in situ observations of DAS domain growth at 653 K by HT-STM, we will demonstrate both the characteristics and the atomic structures during DAS domain growth. Furthermore, we calculate the surface free energy during DAS domain growth using the cell model.

### 2. In situ STM observation of DAS domain growth

To construct a model, we first present the relevant experimental facts about domain growth of the DAS structure.

After flashing at 1500 K and cooling to 653 K within 1–2 s, we can produce small ‘$1\times1$’ regions. In these regions, atomic images are not achieved due to thermal fluctuation of adatoms, as seen in Fig. 1(a) [8]. In the ‘$1\times1$’ regions, there are some protrusions with higher brightness. However, these do not seriously influence the process of DAS domain growth [12].

We have successfully observed the growth of an isolated DAS domain at 653 K in the ‘$1\times1$’ region as shown in Fig. 1. When we started the measurements, the isolated F-half ($9\times9$-F$'$) denoted as “A” had already formed in the ‘$1\times1$’ region (Fig. 1(a)). The second F-half ($7\times7$-F$'$) “B” then formed and shared a corner-hole with “A” (Fig. 1(b)), and “A” changed to $9\times9$-F. By the time of Fig. 1(c), one $7\times7$-F “C” and one $9\times9$-F “D” had formed. The F-half “C” shares one cornerhole with “A” and “B”, whereas “D” shares a corner-hole with “A”. At 289 s (Fig. 1(d)), “B” had changed its size and type to $9\times9$-F, “C” had changed its size to $9\times9$-F$'$, and the $9\times9$-F “E” had formed and shared corner-holes with “A” and “B”. After this time, the three F-halves, “A”, “B”, and “E”, shared corner-holes with one another and did not change their size. In Fig. 1(e), “F” had formed and shared corner-holes with “B” and “C”. Then in Fig. 1(f), “G” had formed and shared corner-holes with “A”, “E”, and “D”. From the above observations, we determined that new F-halves are created from corner-holes of existing F-halves at the edges of the DAS domain.

Fig. 1(d) shows that three F-halves, “A”, “B”, and “E”, share corner-holes with one another, and that one unfaulted (U) half is automatically formed by the surrounding three F-halves. The U-halves have nothing to do with the domain growth. This is one of the distinguished features of DAS domain growth, which has been incorporated in the Monte-Carlo simulation of domain growth on terraces by Kato et al. according to the cell model [19].

In Fig. 2, "A", "B", "E", and "D" are regular-type 9 × 9-F, and "C" is irregular-type 9 × 9-F'. Now, we summarize the structural feature of various types of corner-holes. Each CH1 has one dangling bond, which are shown as arrowheads in Fig. 2. Each CH2 also has one dangling bond in its center, shown as a filled circles. Similarly, each complete corner-hole CH3 has one dangling bond in its center. In the case of an isolated odd-sized, regular-type F-half, there are three CH1s [10]. If two regular-type, odd-sized F-halves share one corner-hole, the two CH1s change to one CH2.

Therefore, this combination reduces one dangling bond. In the same way, a combination of one CH2 and one CH1 makes one complete corner-hole CH3 and reduces one dangling bond. Thus, the shared corner-holes combine to reduce the number of dangling bonds. Ohdomari and coworkers found a leading role for F-halves in the formation of the DAS domain with shared corner-holes, but their observation was phenomenological. Here we use high quality STM images and convincing structures of the DAS domain to clarify the formation process.

## 4. Surface free energy of DAS domain

Previously, Kato et al. proposed the cell model, which describes the state of the Si(1 1 1) surface by the states of half cells. This model succeeded in interpreting the $7 \times 7 \leftrightarrow$ ' $1 \times 1$ ' phase transition [19] and the characteristic features of the growth patterns of DAS domains on quenched surfaces [20]. We now briefly explain this model. We use the formation energy of each DAS structure element that was estimated by Vanderbilt [18]: energy $\varepsilon_{0}=-3.91$ eV for the stacking-fault and dimers in $7 \times 7$-F, and the energy $c=2.71$ eV for the complete corner-hole. Other elements belonging to a small DAS domain are the two types of incomplete corner-holes CH1 and CH2; their formation energies are set as $c_{1}=1.49$ eV and $c_{2}=1.84$ eV, respectively, so as to reproduce growth patterns that are presented in a previous paper [19]. The reduction of number of dangling bonds is included in these energy. We neglect both adatoms and dangling bonds because the difference in those densities between $7 \times 7$ and ' $1 \times 1$ ' phases is negligible. Thus, we can calculate the formation energy of the DAS domain $E_{\mathrm{DS}}$ as an integration of the above elements. Fig. 3 shows the surface structures of (a) after and (b) before the formation of DAS structure (single $7 \times 7$-F). In this case, we count $E_{\mathrm{DS}}=\varepsilon_{0}+3 c_{1}=0.56$ eV.

For the ' $1 \times 1$ ' structure at high temperature, we assume that the adatoms locate randomly on $\mathrm{T}_{4}$ sites with a density of 0.25 per $1 \times 1$ cell [22] with nearest-neighbor exclusion. Here, we neglect adsorption on $\mathrm{H}_{3}$ sites. Now we can estimate the entropy, $s_{0}$, per $1 \times 1$ cell units of Boltzman constant $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ for distribution of adatoms on the triangular lattice by following procedure: the number of random arrangements of $n$ adatoms with nearest-neighbor exclusion on a rhombic $L \times L$ triangular lattice, $W_{\mathrm{L}}(n)$, is exactly counted for $L=4-8$ and the corresponding entropy is calculated by $S_{\mathrm{L}}(n)=\ln \left[W_{\mathrm{L}}(n)\right]$. By interpolating the calculated values of $S_{\mathrm{L}}(n)$, the entropy corresponding to the density $0.25, S_{\mathrm{L}}^{*}$, is obtained. The $S_{\mathrm{L}}^{*}$ is expressed as

![](./images/813057085074833408_5.jpg)

Fig. 3. Atomic structure of (a) after and (b) before the formation of single $7 \times 7$-F. The area surrounded by dashed line correspond to (a) the prohibited area of random arrangements of adatoms by the formation of DAS structure and (b) the lost $\mathrm{T}_{4}$ sites.

$S_{\mathrm{L}}^{*}=s_{0} L^{2}+s_{1} L+s_{2}$ are determined by the method of least squares. The resulting value of $s_{0}$ which is the entropy per $1 \times 1$ cell for the infinite triangular lattice, is 0.249 [19]: we can count the entropy of random arrangement of adatoms. Therefore the free energy of the ' $1 \times 1$ ' structure is

$$
F_{1 \times 1}=-0.249 k_{\mathrm{B}} T n_{\mathrm{f}}, \quad(1)
$$

where $n_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the number of $\mathrm{T}_{4}$ sites in the ' $1 \times 1$ ' area that is replaced by the DAS structure. In the case of Fig. 3(a), random arrangements of adatoms are prohibited on the area surrounded by dashed line after the formation of DAS structure. In this area, adatoms located randomly on $\mathrm{T}_{4}$ sites before the formation of DAS structure. These lost $\mathrm{T}_{4}$ sites indicated in Fig. 3(b) as shaded circles. The sites on the dashed line count as half (on line) or one third (on corner), because a prohibition is caused by both sides. Therefore we calculate $n_{\mathrm{f}}=60.5$ and $F_{1 \times 1}=-15.06 k_{\mathrm{B}} T \mathrm{eV}$.

Based on above method, we could obtain the free energy of the DAS domain in reference to the ' $1 \times 1$ ' structure as

$$
F_{\mathrm{DAS}}=E_{\mathrm{DS}}-F_{1 \times 1}. \quad(2)
$$

Thus, we can calculate the surface free energy of the growing DAS domain structures in the ' $1 \times 1$ ' region.

Using this model, the transition temperature, $T_{\mathrm{c}}$, between ' $1 \times 1$ ' and $7 \times 7$ can be estimated: the free energy of $7 \times 7$ per unit cell in reference to the ' $1 \times 1$ ' becomes zero at $T_{\mathrm{c}}$, that is,

$$
F_{\mathrm{DAS}}=\left(\varepsilon_{0}+c\right)-\left(-0.249 k_{\mathrm{B}} T_{\mathrm{c}} \times 49\right)=0. \quad(3)
$$

This equation gives $T_{\mathrm{c}}=1140 \mathrm{~K}$. This result is in good agreement with the experimental result $\sim 1100 \mathrm{~K}$. This agreement is a strong evidence of the validity of the present model.

### 5. Critical size of DAS domain

Previously, Ohdomari and coworkers discussed the stability of the DAS domain of various sizes, and claimed that a minimization of the periphery length of a DAS domain stabilized the domain as mentioned above [4,5]. They treated the inside of the DAS domain as uniform. However, bounda- ries also existed inside the domain: the bounda- ries between F-halves and U-halves is the same as those between the ' $1 \times 1$ ' region and the DAS domain. For example, Fig. 2 shows dimer rows at the periphery boundary between the ' $1 \times 1$ ' region and the DAS domain, and also in the inside of the DAS domain. Therefore, Ohdomari and co- workers should have taken into account not only the periphery length but also the boundary length inside the DAS domain. Including both, the Gibbs free energy change $\Delta G$ for formation of a triangular $7 \times 7$ domain is

$$
\Delta G=-\frac{1}{2} \varepsilon m_{\mathrm{F}}+3 \gamma m_{\mathrm{F}}, \quad(4)
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the stabilization energy per half unit area due to the transition from the disordered ' $1 \times 1$ ' structure to the stacking-faulted structure, and $\gamma$ is the boundary strain energy per unit length between an F-half and a U-half. $m_{\mathrm{F}}$ is the number of F-halves consisting of a $7 \times 7$ domain. Ac- cordingly, in the model of Ohdomari and co- workers, there is no critical values in the Gibbs free energy for nucleation.

Here, we instead estimate the surface free energy based on the atomic structure during the domain growth of the $7 \times 7$ DAS structure. Fig. 4 shows the formation process of a $7 \times 7$ DAS domain, where the number of F-halves increases from 1 in (a) to 10 in (j). The sequential increase of the DAS domain size in Fig. 4(a)-(j) is idealized, but it is reasonable and appropriate to consider each step of the domain growth. For each configuration with a different number of F-halves, $E_{\mathrm{DS}}, F_{1 \times 1}$, and $F_{\mathrm{DAS}}$ are calculated as shown in Fig. 4. The formation energy of a DAS domain $E_{\mathrm{DS}}$ is negative except for (a), and decreases with domain growth. On the other hand, $F_{1 \times 1}$ decreases with domain growth. Then, we calculated $F_{\mathrm{DAS}}$ at $653 \mathrm{~K}$ for each step of the domain growth, and the results are plotted in Fig. 5. Therefore, $F_{\mathrm{DAS}}$ has a critical value. This curve shows that the critical size of the $7 \times 7$ DAS domain is $m_{\mathrm{F}}=2$ at $653 \mathrm{~K}$. These calculated sur- face free energies are based on the atomic struc- ture of the surface. We have estimated the stability of DAS domains of various sizes only at 653 K. Hoshino et al. obtained the critical sizes at higher temperatures by HT-STM [4]. For comparison, we calculated the critical sizes at higher temperatures using the above model: $m_{\text{F}} = 3$ at 900 K; $m_{\text{F}} = 6$ at 1000 K. However, Hoshino et al. have obtained the critical sizes experimentally: $m_{\text{F}} = 6$ at 873 K; $m_{\text{F}} = 36$ at 973 K [4]. Therefore our estimated critical sizes are much smaller than the experimental results. Our neglect of energy barriers to size-changes probably caused this discrepancy. We plan to perform a dynamical Monte-Carlo simulation including such energy barriers.

Finally, we discuss the primary cause of stabilization of the DAS structure during domain growth. As mentioned in Section 3, shared corner-holes decrease the number of dangling bonds. The formation energy of dangling bond is 1.45 eV [18]. Therefore, we conclude that shared corner-holes

contributes to the stabilization of DAS structures during domain growth.

## 6. Summary
Based on in situ HT-STM observations, we analyzed the stabilization mechanism of DAS domain growth on the Si(111) surface. From the analysis of atomic structure, we found that the shared corner-hole reduces the number of dangling bonds. We also calculated the surface free energy during $7 \times 7$ DAS domain growth based on the cell model. The critical size is two F-halves at 653 K, which is in good agreement with experimental results. We conclude that the reduced number of dangling bonds at shared corner-holes stabilized the DAS domain.
