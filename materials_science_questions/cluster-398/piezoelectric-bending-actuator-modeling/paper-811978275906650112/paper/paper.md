## SUMMARY

Ultrasonics is utilized widely for industrial application. An electrostrictive transducer is used widely for this purpose because of its high electromechanical conversion efficiency. In actual use, the transducer usually is operated under the mechanical vibration of large amplitude, and the mechanical loss of the vibrating body is responsible for the heat.

Since the transducer is also excited at high voltage, the dielectric loss in a ferroelectric body must be considered for the heat. These losses should be taken into account in the design. However, the temperature distribution and the influence of the temperature caused by the heat are not always clear. The heat design of the transducers depends on the experiments which at present, are made on a trial-and-error basis.

This paper describes the application of the finite element method to simulating a steady-state heat diffusion problem in electrostrictive vibrators. The simulation is made to evaluate the temperature distribution caused by the heat which is generated by the mechanical vibratory loss in the elastic body and the dielectric loss in the ferroelectric body, and then the influence of the temperature distribution on the transducer characteristic is examined. Simulation is an experiment on computer and could be used as a means of CAD. Among others, the capability of the visualization of the temperature distribution and the thermal stress distribution helps to design the ultrasonic transducers. A numerical demonstration is made whose results are compared with the experimental results. The technique is proved to be useful in solving problems of this kind.

## 1. Introduction

Ultrasonics has been used for industrial applications, for example, plastic bonding and semiconductor cleaning. Electrostrictive transducers are utilized widely for this application because of their high electromechanical conversion efficiency. As in actual use, a transducer is operated usually under the mechanical vibration of large amplitude at resonant frequency. The mechanical loss in the vibratory elastic body is responsible for the heat.

Since the transducer is also excited at high voltage, the dielectric loss in the ferroelectric body is also the cause of the heat. These losses should be taken into consideration in the design. However, the temperature distribution and the influence of the temperature caused by the heat which is generated by the mechanical and dielectric loss are not always clear. That is, the generation of the thermal stress and the change of the resonant frequency and the damped capacitance due to the temperature distribution are not clear. The internal heat generation in the ultrasonic transducer determines the operation limit. In spite of this fact, the heat design of the transducers has been made depending on experiment or on a trial-and-error basis at present.

Mori et al. [1] presented a study on the relation between the surface temperature of the transducer and velocity amplitude at the tip which provided only the measured data. In general, the thermal characteristics of the resonant frequency and the capacitance are provided by the transducer manufacturer. These are measured data at a certain condition [2].

Although Stulen et al. [3] investigated the temperature distribution of a Langevin-

The aim of the present work is to obtain useful information for the heat design through numerical simulation. We developed the finite element method for the analysis of axisymme- tric transducer systems and their steady- state temperature characteristics [4-9].

This paper is the natural extension of our previous work and includes the tempera- ture distribution in the ultrasonic trans- ducer, the thermal stress and the change of the resonant frequency and the capacitance associated with the temperature distribution. Cylindrical transducers, in particular, bolt-clamped Langevin-type transducers, are utilized for an ultrasonic level meter and an ultrasonic plastic weld- ing system, and also for the present numeri- cal examples. Once the computer program using the finite element method is developed, it can be applied to any transducers without constraints as to the shape, structures and components. The temperature distribution of the transducers and the associated thermal stress are calculated, and the change of the resonant frequency and the capacitance as the result is considered. Since the thermal characteristic of the transducers can be pre- dicted at an intense level, the simulation facilitates the design of the driving condi- tion of the transducers including the elec- tric circuit parameters of the power supply. Although we consider the model in which the electrostrictive vibration is independent of the heat, the thermal effect of the material components is taken into account.

For the boundary condition, both natural and forced cooling convections are considered. These numerical results are compared with the ex- perimental results whenever they are avail- able.

## 2. Finite Element Formulation. Axisymme- tric Vibrator as a Heat Diffusion Model

The internal heat generation associated with the ultrasonic power transducer deter- mines the operational limit. Internal loss results in high temperature and low efficien- cy. Depolarization may even occur when the temperature exceeds the Curie point.

High temperature not only changes the characteristics of the transducers, but also degrades the strength of the material. The heat design of the transducers must be made so that the temperature is within the safety range. In operation at an intense level, the thermal distribution must also be taken into account since it causes the thermal stresses.

In this paper, two kinds of heat sources are considered in the ultrasonic vibrators: that due to the mechanical loss caused by the viscous damping of the elastic material in vibration. Here, the loss is proportional to the square of the vibration strain $(q_{v}=\alpha_{v} E^{2} / 2)$ [10]; and that due to the dielectric loss which is proportional to the vibration fre- quency and to the square of impressed electric field $(q_{\delta}=\varepsilon_{0} \varepsilon \tan \delta \cdot V^{2} / 2)$ . Both are converted into heat [11].

Now we solve the heat diffusion problem with the sources as discussed. For instance,

In the analysis of the transducers vibration, needless to say, the finite element method which has already been reported [16] is employed. The procedure is as follows. The displacement distribution, strain distribution, electric potential dis- tribution, the resonant frequency and the capacitance at the electrical terminals are calculated first without considering the tem- perature rise. The distribution of the me- chanical vibratory loss is then calculated from the strain distribution, and the distri- bution of the dielectric loss from the elec- tric potential distribution in the electro- strictive part. Both the sources for the temperature rise and the temperature distri- bution in the transducer is calculated.

The temperature distribution influences the transducer characteristics as they change material constants and also the dimensions due to thermal expansion。Thus, the tempera-ture rise changes the resonant frequency $(f_{N})$  which is calculated and then compared with the frequency $(f_{N-1})$ calculated in the previ ous step. Until the ratio of the successive resonant frequencies converges or is within a specified value $(d)$ , the calculations for the thermal stress distribution and others are repeated. We solve first the equation of motion and then the heat diffusion equation. Here only the final discretized equations are shown. The reader interested in details ofthe formulations is referred to [9] and [16] for vibration, and to [12] for the heat dif- fusion. The equations of motion for the axi- symmetric electrostrictive transducers aregiven as follows:

$$\left(K+j R-\omega^{2} M\right) d+P \phi=F \quad(1)$$

$$P^{T} d-\left(G+j R^{\prime}\right) \phi=0 \quad(2)$$

where
- $K$ : stiffness matrix
- $R$ : damping matrix in elastic body
- $M$ : mass matrix
- $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ : nodal potential vector in elec- trostrictive body
- $P$ : electromechanical coupling matrix
- $d$ : nodal displacement vector
- $F$ : nodal driving force vector
- $G$ : capacitance matrix
- $\omega$ : angular frequency
- $R^{\prime}$ : damping matrix associated with dielectric loss

Equations (1) and (2) are coupled and can be solved as simultaneous equations for the mechanical driving force $F$ , nodal electric potential $\phi$ corresponding to the electrodes at given angular frequency $\omega$ . The displace ment distribution in the elastic body and the potential distribution in the electrostrictive body are obtained. Although we consider the model in which the electrostrictive vibration does not couple with the heat, the stiffness matrix $K$ , the capacitance matrix $G$ , and the coupling matrix $P$ are assumed to be functions of the temperature increase.

Strain is defined as a spatial derivative of displacement. Its distribution is given for each element from the displacement distri- bution. Since a triangular ring element with three nodes (first-order trial function is em- ployed there) is used, the strain is constant within the element. The mechanical vibratory loss will be caused by so-called structural damping in the elastic body, so that the damp- ing matrix is assumed simply as

$$R=\alpha K \quad(3)$$

where $\alpha$ is the damping coefficient. The di electric material with loss is expressed by complex figure $\varepsilon(1+j \beta)$ or $\varepsilon(1+j \tan \delta)$ , so that the damping matrix in the electro- strictive body is given as

$$R^{\prime}=\beta G \quad(4)$$

The resonant frequency is obtained from the frequency characteristic of the calculated input admittance. The capacitance is calcu- lated from the input admittance at the driving frequency of 1 kHz. The input admittance be- tween a pair of electrodes is given by

$$Y_{\text {in }}=j \omega Q_{s} / \phi_{s} \quad(5)$$

where $Q_{s}$ is the sum of electric charge at one of the electrodes, $\phi_{s}$ is the electric poten tial between the electrodes.

The discretized equation for the heatdiffusion is given as follows:

$$H \Phi+C \dot{\Phi}=q \quad(6)$$

where $H$ is the heat conduction matrix; $\Phi$ is the nodal temperature vector; $C$ is the heat capacity matrix; and $q$ is the heat flux vector.

The discretization in Eq. (6) is made in space with finite elements and the Crank-Ni- colson finite difference scheme is used in time. The second term on the left-hand side of Eq. (6) is disregarded in the case of the steady-state problems.

The sources of the heat, the vibratory loss $(q_{v})$ and the dielectric loss $(q_{\delta})$ , are calculated for each element. The vibratory loss $(q_{v})$ is given for a unit volume as
$$q_{v}=\frac{1}{2} \alpha E^{2}\qquad(7)$$
where $\alpha_{v}$ is the damping coefficient of the particular element and $E$ is the vibrational strain.

The dielectric loss $(q_{\delta})$ is given for a unit volume as
$$q_{\delta}=\frac{1}{2} \varepsilon_{0} \varepsilon \tan \delta \cdot V^{2}\qquad(8)$$
where $\varepsilon_{0}$ is the dielectric constant in vacuum, $\varepsilon$ is the relative dielectric constant, $\tan \delta$ is the dielectric dissipation factor, and $V$ is the electric field intensity.

As the boundary condition for Eq. (6), the heat transfer from the boundary surface to air must be considered. When $t$ is the temperature at the boundary surface, and $t_{c}$ is the temperature of the surrounding atmo- sphere, the heat flux at that boundary is given by $q=\tau(t-t_{c}) ; \tau$ is the heat-transfer coefficient, whose value depends on the con- dition, and free or forced cooling convection at the boundary, which is found in [11]. Be- sides the heat conduction, heat radiation must be considered. Since $t$ is the tempera ture at the boundary surface and $t_{r}$ is the temperature of the surrounding medium, the heat flux at the boundary surface is given by $q=\chi X(t^{4}-t_{r}^{4}) ; x$ is the Stefan-Boltzmann con stant and $X$ is a radiation shape factor whose value depends on the thermal emissivity of the material and its surface area. Again, the reader is referred to [13] for details. The temperature distribution causes the thermal stress and the dimensional change due to the thermal expansion. The equation of the ther- mal elasticity and constitution is given foran axisymmetrical body as follows:
$$\sigma=D \varepsilon\qquad(9)$$

$$K d=f\qquad(10)$$

$$f=2 \pi B D \varepsilon \gamma A_{e}\qquad(11)$$

\end{array}\right\}\qquad(12)$$
where

Equation (12) gives the thermal strain for the temperature increase and Eq. (9) gives the thermal stress. Equation (10) can be solved for the nodal point loading vector that is the result of the thermal strain. The displacement of each nodal point due to the thermal expansion is thus obtained.

The change of the resonant frequency and the capacitance with the temperature rise is due to the following reasons:

(1) change of the elastic modulus;

(2) dimensional change due to thermal expansion; and

(3) change of the mass density.

These reasons are taken into account for the frequency and the input admittance calcu- lation as explained in the previous section. In the case of item 1, the temperature coef- ficient of the elastic modulus is included so that the stiffness tensor is now temperature- dependent. For item 2, the element sizes change and these effects result in the change of the element stiffness matrix and the stiff- ness matrix in Eq. (10). For item 3, the element mass does not change after thermal expansion and the mass matrix is independent of the temperature change. Item 3 need not be taken into account with the finite element method.

7(a). In the figure, the arrows indicate the direction and the length of the relative mag- nitude. Also, the averaged vector of thermal stresses in two adjacent triangular elements are shown one after the other. The thermal stress is larger where the temperature changes steeply, and the tensile stress extends out- ward at the parts. The thermal stress dis- tribution of the Type B transducer is shown in Fig. 7(b). The stress extends in the end direction along the inner side, and on the other hand, it extends in the center direction along the outer side. Hence, the distribu- tion is not similar to that of the Type A transducer. These transducers are made of ceramic and are weak against tensile stress. In the case of the generation of the tensile stress at the outer side, a crack probably oc- curs as shown in Fig. 7(a) [14]. Thus, in comparison with the Type B transducer, the change of the displacement distribution is steeper and the vibratory stress is larger at the center of the Type A transducer. As a re- sult, the heat generation due to the vibratory loss and the dielectric loss is larger and hence the thermal stress is also larger. In addition, the tensile stress occurs at the outer side. Although these factors may not have a close relation with the crack, since the trans- ducers are made of sintered ceramics, the ten- sile stress together with the presence of foreign materials and/or the generation of cav- ities may cause the transducers to crack.

### 3.2 Bolt-clamped Langevin-type trans- ducers

A bolt-clamped Langevin-type transducer used for the ultrasonic plastic welding sys- tem is considered. In calculation and exami- nation, two types of boundary conditions-- free convection and forced cooling convection-- are considered along the surface. The thermal radiation is also taken into account for both conditions.

In plastic welding, the required output power range varies widely from ten to several

![](./images/811978275906650112_13.jpg)

kilowatts, and various transducers are manu- factured for these purposes. During one welding cycle, the energy consumed changes greatly in response to the change of the con- dition in the plastic article to be welded. Also, in such an operation process, since there is a change of the resonant frequency, the frequency follow-up control system which follows the change of the resonant frequency and the output control system and keeps the vibratory amplitude constant, is provided for the ultrasonic plastic welding system.

The frequency follow-up control is made by picking up the voltage which is propor- tional to the vibratory amplitude using vi- bration sensors attached to the transducers or with the electric motional bridge circuit. The voltage is used for the feedback signal [15]. The output power control is made by voltage-control, current-control or phase- control to keep the feedback signal constant.

The displacement distribution of the bolt-clamped Langevin-type transducer (here- after called BLT) is shown in Fig. 8. The numerical results are calculated using the procedure shown in Fig. 1. The calculated results are indicated by a solid line. The calculated maximum displacement is $18\ \mu m$ at the tip of the front plate. The measured displacement is indicated by the dots (●).

The measurement was made only at the working surface in the front plate by FOTONIC SENSOR. The measured displacement is 15.8 $\mu m$. BLT is a composite resonator and con- sists in five kinds of materials: iron for the bolt and nut, aluminum for the back plate, PZT for the electrostrictive wafers, beryl- lium-copper for the electrodes and, titanium for the front plate. The four PZT disks pro- viding four electrodes are sandwiched between the front and back plates which are clamped with the bolt and nut at the center. The resonant frequency of the BLT is 17.6 kHz.
The shape, the dimensions and the element di- vision of BLT are also shown in Fig. 8. The transducer vibrates entirely in the first lon- gitudinal mode for which the vibration node is almost established at the flange. The cal- culated resonant frequency is 17.51 kHz while the measured is 17.64 kHz, and the calculated capacitance is 14050 pF while the measured is 13200 pF.

To produce the foregoing displacement, the voltage of $1\ \mathrm{kV_{rms}}$ is required and the corresponding input power is 26 W without loading.

The calculated strain distribution of the BLT is shown in Fig. 9, and the vector of the averaged strain in two adjacent triangu- lar elements is illustrated one after the other. The strain in the clamping bolt near the PZT wafers is in the axial direction since the portion is located near the node of longi- tudinal vibration. The strain along the radi- al direction is excited in the electrodes, since the diameter of the BLT is relatively large in length, and the length/diameter ratio is about 3.

Figure 10 shows the calculated electric potential distribution in the electrostrictive elements of the BLT. The electric potential contour lines are of approximately equal in- tervals to each other, and hence the electric field is uniform.

For more detailed knowledge, finer element division is required. The electric potential of other portions which are not electrostric- tive material is not shown because it is natur- ally at zero potential. The electrostrictive elements are polarized in thickness direction, and they are arranged so that the polarity is altered sequentially. The four electrodes, which are thin beryllium-copper (BeCu), are inserted between the electrostrictive waters. The first and the third electrodes from the top are grounded to have potential while the second and the fourth are driven at $\phi_{s}$. The lead wires are attached to the electrodes in the actual transducers. However, they are discarded in the calculation.

Figure 11 shows the calculated and mea- sured temperature distribution along the sur- face of the BLT. The calculated results are indicated by the solid lines and the measured results by black dots (●). The transducer is composed of metal parts and electrostric- tive elements. The metal parts may have a rusted area, and thus the surface state is not uniform. Therefore, a black lusterless lacquer was painted on the surface to obtain uniform emissivity.

The convection heat transfer and the thermal radiation are considered for the boundary condition, which is same as that of the cylindrical vibrators described in the foregoing. The calculated temperature dis- tribution and that measured along the surface are shown in Fig. 11. The calculated results are indicated by solid lines and the measured results are indicated by black dots (●).
The calculated temperature is some 10 percent lower than that of the measured temperature as a whole.

Because an actual transducer is composed of several parts and the joint between bound- ary surfaces is not acoustically and thermally perfect, fraction losses occur at the boundary surfaces. However, in computation, it is dif- ficult to consider precisely the effects of the boundary surfaces. The measured tempera- ture at the fringe is lower. Twenty-four holes (radius 3 mm) appear at equivalent in- tervals in the fringe and prevent the coupling vibration from the radial vibration. The heat radiation effect may be due to these holes.
The numerical model is axisymmetric and cannot

deal with the effect of the holes. The calcu- lated equi-temperature contours are shown at the bottom of Fig. 11. The higher-temperature part does not always agree with the larger- strain part which is not similar to the fore- mentioned numerical examples of the cylindri- cal transducers.

In these examples, the temperature rise is distinct at the electrostrictive elements. The strain of the clamping bolt is larger but the heat generation is smaller since the vi- bratory loss in the bolt is small. Further- more, since the thermal conductivities of the nut, the back plate and the front plate are good, the heat in the bolt is conducted away and the bolt is cooled. On the other hand, since the thermal conductivity of the elec- trostrictive elements is poor, the heat can- not be conducted, and accordingly the tempera- ture at the elements rises.

Dielectric loss in a ferroelectric body is responsible somewhat for the heat. The calculated dimensional change due to the ther- mal expansion is shown in Fig. 12. In the calculation, the displacement along the z- axis is fixed at the working surface in the front plate. The displacement of the back plate made of aluminum is large. The temper- ature of this part is not higher than others, but the linear expansion coefficient is larger. On the other hand, the displacement caused by the thermal expansion at the electrostrictive elements is not recognized, since the tempera- ture of the elements is higher and their line- ar expansion coefficient is small. The maxi- mum displacement along the z-axis is $80.8\ \mu m$ as a whole. The influence of the deformation on the resonant frequency is less than 0.06 percent.

Figure 13 shows the calculated thermal stress distribution. The thermal stress is calculated together with the thermal expan- sion, and thus the displacement along the z- axis at the tip of the front plate is fixed as the mechanical boundary condition. The maximum thermal stress is $4.3\ \times\ 10^{8}\ kg/m^{2}$ in the bolt at the portion jointing with the nut. The stress toward the inner side is observed at the electrostrictive elements. The calcu- lated and measured temperature distribution along the surface under the boundary condition of the forced cooling convection are shown in Fig. 14.

The driving condition of the transducer is the same as that shown in Fig. 8. The forced cooling is made by an air blower (80 mm in diameter, $0.9\ m^{3}/min$ gas quantity) placed beside the transducer at a distance of 200 mm. In the calculation, the heat trans- fer coefficient is chosen to be $35J/(m^{2}\cdot sec\cdot$ °C). Except for this coefficient, the other thermal conditions are the same as those shown in Fig. 11. The calculated temperatures along the surface are indicated by solid lines and the measured temperatures with black dots. The tendency of the thermal distribution for which the measured results are higher than those of the calculated results is similar to that shown in Fig. 11.

The temperature distribution under the forced cooling convection is lower by some $30\ ^{\circ}C$ than that of the natural convention as a whole. Since the cooling effect is large at the high-temperature area, the temperature- difference is reduced in each of the other areas. The calculated temperature contours inside are shown at the bottom of the figure. The temperature gradient under the forced cooling convection is slightly higher than that of the natural convection.

Figure 15 shows the deformed shape caused by the thermal expansion due to the tempera- ture rise under the forced cooling convection. The tendency of the deformation is similar to that of Fig. 12 as a whole. Since the tempera- ture rise is suppressed, the displacement caused by the thermal expansion is suppressed, therefore the maximum displacement along the z-axis is $23.3\ \mu m$.

The influence of the thermal expansion on the resonant frequency is less than 0.018 percent. Figure 16 shows the thermal stress distribution in the BLT. In comparison with the stress distribution under the natural con- vection shown in Fig. 13, since the tempera- ture-difference is suppressed, and the maxi- mum value is reduced and is $1.4\ \times\ 10^{8}\ kg/m^{2}$, its ratio is 1/3 although the direction of the stress vectors is generally similar to that of Fig. 13.

Thus it is concluded that the forced cooling effect reduces not only the tempera- ture rise, but also the generation of the ther- mal stress. The motional bridge circuit for the frequency follow-up control system is employed for the high-frequency power supply to drive the BLT. The capacitance of the transducer and three capacitances form the motional bridge circuit, and the signal re- lated to the vibratory velocity of the trans- ducer is picked up by the electric circuit. Therefore, the change of the capacitance caused by the heat in the transducer degrades the signal/noise ratio.

Using the aforementioned simulation tech- niques, we can predict the capacitance of the transducer which is taken into account for the thermal effect in actual use. Thus we can select the optimum circuit constants. In other words, not only the design of the high- frequency power supply which provides the good signal/noise ratio, but also the sensitive fre- quency follow-up control system can be made using simulation techniques.

### 4. Conclusions

The finite element technique is applied to examine the heat diffusion in axisymmetric vibrators and its influence on their charac- teristics. The cylindrical electrostrictive transducers and the bolt-clamped Langevin- type transducers are selected for the numeri- cal examples. A comparison is made between the numerical results and the measured re- sults and good agreement is obtained.

For proper heat design of vibrators, a trial-and-error approach has been used. The choice of the proper transducer shape and the efficient driving condition is not only time- consuming and costly, but also requires ex- perience. Computer simulation is an experi- mental approach and could be used as a means of CAD. Among others, as demonstrated, the capability of visualizing the temperature and thermal stress distribution which determine the operational limit helps the design. A practical operational environment is in fact transient and repeating.

Understanding of the transient heat re- sponse such as the change of the temperature and the thermal stress distribution with time will be studied in the future.
