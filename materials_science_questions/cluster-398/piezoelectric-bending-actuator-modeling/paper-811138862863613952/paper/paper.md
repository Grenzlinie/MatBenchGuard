Dependencies of the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients for solid circular
cylindrical piezoelements (discs) of finite size are considered as a function of their height-to-diame-
ter aspect ratios, $h/2a$. At aspect ratios in the range of approximately $0.5 < h/2a < 1.5$ the piezoele-
ments are treated analytically, as a two degree-of-freedom coupled system by applying the coupled
mode theory approach [Aronov, Bachand, and Brown, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 126(6), 2983–2990
(2009)]. Results are presented as plots of resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients
vs aspect ratios. Experimental verification showed good agreement with the analytical predictions
and allowed sufficiently reliable characterization of the trends of parameters in the range of aspect
ratios, for which the analytical treatment could not be applied. The equivalent parameters that char-
acterize energy relations in the course of coupled vibration are determined, and the equivalent elec-
tromechanical circuit is introduced for calculating frequency response of the transducers.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Piezoelectric elements in the shape of thick discs or solid
circular cylinders having electrodes on their end surfaces and
polarized axially through their thickness undergo longitudinal
vibrations (Fig. 1). Such elements are used for many applica-
tions in a broad frequency range such as for depth sounders,
fish finders, and acoustic Doppler current profilers. The
height-to-diameter aspect ratio of these piezoelements, $h/2a$,
may change from very small [at $h/2a \ll 1$, Fig. 1(b)] to rather
large [at $h/2a \gg 1$, Fig. 1(c)]. In these limits of extreme
aspect ratios, the piezoelements are commonly called “discs”
and “rods,” respectively, and can be considered as one-
dimensional systems in terms of calculating their vibration in
the axial direction. In the intermediate cases in which the
height of the piezoelements is comparable with the diameter,
the piezoelements may be called “finite-height cylinders.”
This definition will be used further for all the geometries in
general so far as the dependence is concerned on their aspect
ratios.

There have been many results of investigations related
to the vibration of the circular discs having various height-
to-diameter aspect ratios published over the span of several
decades. They can be divided into two groups. The main
goal of publications of the first group¹⁻⁷ was in investigating
and classifying the axisymmetric vibrational modes of the
circular discs and their dependence on the height-to-diameter
aspect ratio, as elastic bodies *per se*. Piezoceramic discs
were used in these investigations with fully electroded end
surfaces as a convenient way to generate the axisymmetric
vibrations. Publications of the second group⁸⁻¹⁰ were
intended for considering the dependence of parameters of
the piezoceramic resonators in the shape of circular cylinders
of finite height from their aspect ratio by employing analysis
of the coupling between the axial and radial modes of
vibration.

The experimental¹⁻⁴ and theoretical⁵⁻⁷ works showed
complexity of vibrations especially for thin discs, and exis-
tence of axisymmetric modes of vibration other than the dila-
tational axial and radial. Authors of the referred works
performed very careful and detailed measurements of the
modal distributions of vibration over the discs surface in the
axial direction that proved to be essentially nonuniform in
most cases, which makes them virtually unusable for

This information is important, as it can be used for qualitatively predicting the ranges of aspect ratios that have to be avoided in practical designing of the piezoelements for electroacoustic transducers. Only a fraction of the theoretically predicted and experimentally revealed modes of vibration have sufficiently high effective coupling coefficient for a visible impact on a transducer performance. Based on the results of finite element analysis and experimentation, the authors of Ref. 4 introduced the "modal constant" that characterizes strength of excitation of a given vibration mode, and showed that contribution of the modes other than dilatational axial and radial can be practically neglected. Putting aside these modes of vibration, the fol- lowing qualitative description can be made from the point of view of the coupled vibration concept of how parameters of piezoelements change as the aspect ratio progresses from extremely small to larger values (or as the resonance fre- quency of axial mode of vibration, $f_{ax}$ , changes from high to lower values as the diameter of the discs remains constant).

When the resonance frequency of the axial vibration approaches the resonance frequencies of the radial modes of vibration, $f_{rad i}$ , the effect of coupling between these modes takes place that influences the values of the resonance fre- quencies and effective coupling coefficients, which results in nonuniform distribution of axial displacement over the surface due to contribution of the radial modes. So far as the aspect ratio remains small, the radial modes are of high order (about $i>5$ ), their effective coupling coefficients are small, and contribution of these modes to the axial displacement can be practically neglected. In this range of aspect ratios the axial (thickness) vibration of a disk can be approximately considered as one-dimensional.

As the aspect ratio increases, the radial modes having higher effective coupling coefficients come into action and the effects of their coupling with the axial mode become more pronounced, including raising nonuniformity of the distribution of axial vibration that may result in corrupting the directional factor of a corresponding transducer. These effects are especially significant in the ranges of aspect ratios around values that correspond to the condition $f_{ax} \approx f_{rad i}$ , at which the coupling between modes is strongest. After the aspect ratio reaches the values at which interaction has to be taken into account with the first (the lowest, at $f_{rad 1}$ ) mode of radial vibration only, the cylinder can be considered as a two degree-of-freedom system, and the coupled vibration tech- nique can be applied to calculating its parameters. With a further increase of the aspect ratio the longitudinal vibration of a relatively tall cylinder can be considered isolated and one-dimensional theory of calculating the corresponding transducers is applicable.

The goal of this paper is to analyze the dependence of the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients of the finite-size cylindrical piezoelements for the range of aspect ratios, for which coupled vibration technique can be applied, to provide means for calculating the operational characteristics of the transducers that employ vibration of such piezoelements, and to determine approximate value of aspect ratio, after which the longitudinal vibration of the cyl- inder can be considered one-dimensional. For this purpose the coupled vibration analysis technique is used, which is described in Ref. 8. Another goal of the paper is to show, experimentally, how the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients of the finite-size cylinders change in the range of aspect ratios, for which the coupling analysis approach cannot be applied.

Different approaches to analyzing the coupled vibra- tions in the finite-size cylindrical piezoelements were dem- onstrated in Refs. 9-11. In Refs. 9 and 10 coefficients of mechanical coupling between stresses in a vibrating body were introduced that depend on the state of vibration of the body, and elastic constants of material are formally pre- sented as functions of these coefficients. This makes them unknown prior to solving the vibration problem, and depen- dent on the mechanical (and acoustical) loading of the piezo- elements. The possibility of practical implementation of this approach is not illustrated in Refs. 9 and 10, as no analytical or graphic representations were presented of the final results of calculating the resonance frequencies or the effective cou- pling coefficients vs aspect ratios of the related piezoele- ments. The authors of Ref. 11 did not take into consideration the multimode complexity of the problem. Their final results regarding the behavior of effective coupling coefficients vs aspect ratio do not comply with intensive experimental data presented in the former works. $^{1-4}$ The same mistake in inter preting the results of coupling was made in Ref. 11, as was admitted by the same authors on the analogous issue in Ref. 12. Detailed explanation of this misinterpretation was presented in Ref. 13.

The content of the paper is organized as follows. First, the range of aspect ratios is discussed in Sec. II, for which the vibration problem for the finite-size cylinder can be con- sidered as two-dimensional. The coupled vibration theory is used for analysis of the problem in Sec. III, which resulted in calculating dependencies of the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients vs aspect ratios for their values in this range. In Sec. IV, the forced vibrations of the finite-size cylinder as a coupled system are considered, and the equivalent electromechanical circuit is presented, which allows calculating parameters of a transducer as an electromechanical device employing such piezoelements. Recommendations on calculating the velocity distribution over its radiating surface are made in Sec. IV, which is a prerequisite for solving the acoustic radiation part of the transducer designing problem and is out of the scope of this paper. In Sec. V, an experimental investigation is described that was performed with the goals to verify results of theo- retical predictions and to present experimental data for the range of aspect ratio values, for which the coupled theory analysis could not be applied. An overview of results obtained and recommendations based on the results appear in Sec. VI.

## II. A FINITE-SIZE ELASTIC CYLINDER AS TWO DEGREE-OF-FREEDOM SYSTEM

In order to be qualified as a two degree-of-freedom cou- pled system a real body has to meet certain requirements. In general, the energy state of a two degree-of-freedom system

---

Boris S. Aronov and Corey L. Bachand 2163

can be represented as for a combination of two partial systems undergoing coupled vibrations, and it can be characterized by the set of equations¹⁴

$$
W_{\mathrm{kin}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(M_{1} \dot{\xi}_{1}^{2}+2 M_{12} \dot{\xi}_{1} \dot{\xi}_{2}+M_{2} \dot{\xi}_{2}^{2}\right), \tag{1}
$$

$$
W_{\mathrm{pot}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{1} \xi_{1}^{2}+2 K_{12} \xi_{1} \xi_{2}+K_{2} \xi_{2}^{2}\right), \tag{2}
$$

where $K_{i}$ and $M_{i}$ are the equivalent rigidities and masses, respectively, of the partial systems that remain if one of the generalized coordinates (magnitude of displacement $\xi_{i}$) is set equal to zero; $K_{12}$ and $M_{12}$ are the parameters that characterize the elastic and inertial coupling mechanisms between the partial systems; the time dependent factor $\exp(j \omega t)$ for the generalized coordinates is omitted, and $\dot{\xi}$ is the generalized velocity. Formulas for the resonance frequencies of the partial systems follow from these expressions as

$$
f_{i}=(1 / 2 \pi) \sqrt{K_{i} / M_{i}}, \quad i=1,3,..., \tag{3}
$$

and for the coefficients of elastic and inertia coupling between the partial systems as

$$
\gamma_{k}=K_{12} / \sqrt{K_{1} K_{2}} \quad \text{and} \quad \gamma_{m}=M_{12} / \sqrt{M_{1} M_{2}}. \tag{4}
$$

The strongest coupling effect takes place at the aspect ratios (the height-to-diameter aspect ratio of a cylinder in our case) for which the partial resonance frequencies are equal. If the ratio of the partial resonance frequencies is denoted as $\beta$, then this condition corresponds to

$$
\beta=f_{1} / f_{2}=1. \tag{5}
$$

The condition for applicability of the coupled technique approach is that the partial systems should each have a single degree-of-freedom in all the intended range of changing the aspect ratio of the body. The main problem in the technique application is in appropriately determining the partial systems. Giebe and Blechschmidt suggested¹⁴ to assume that for passive isotropic elastic bodies of certain configurations (rectangular plates and rods, and solid and hollow cylinders, in particular) distribution of displacements in the partial systems is approximately the same, as in one-dimensional bodies, to which the real body approaches at the extreme values of its aspect ratio. (The suggestion is known in literature as "hypothesis of Giebe and Blechschmidt.") In our case the subscripts 1 and 2 that correspond to the partial systems have to be specified as $a$ and $h$. Thus, $\xi_{a}$ and $\xi_{h}$ are the displacements in the radial and axial directions, respectively. The one-dimensional bodies at the extreme values of height-to-diameter aspect ratio $h / 2 a$ can be: the radial vibrating thin disk and axial vibrating thin bar at $h \to 0$ and at $a \to 0$, respectively; or the radial vibrating infinitely long cylinder $(h \to \infty)$ and the thickness vibrating disk of a large diameter $(a \to \infty)$. The pair of the partial systems that represents radially vibrating infinitely long cylinder (in limit $h \to \infty$, $\xi_{h} \to 0$) and the thickness vibrating disk of a large diameter (in limit $a \to \infty$, $\xi_{a} \to 0$) comply with Eqs. (1) and (2), because at $\xi_{h}=0$ the remaining equations characterize the radially vibrating infinitely long cylinder, and at $\xi_{a}=0$ they characterize the axially vibrating disk of infinite radius. This pair of the partial systems will be used for calculations. The first set of the extreme cases can be readily used for the experimental verification of results of approximate solution obtained.

If to consider the axial vibration of a finite-size cylinder around its fundamental resonance frequency only, which will be the case in our treatment, then distribution of displacement in the axial direction is

$$
\xi(z)=\xi_{h} \sin (\pi z / h), \tag{6}
$$

and it remains the same in all the range of aspect ratios.

The radial vibrating thin disk or long cylinder cannot be considered as one degree-of-freedom systems in the entire frequency range of interest because they have multiple resonances within this range. The axisymmetric distribution of displacement in the radial direction is known as

$$
\xi(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \xi_{i} J_{1}\left(k_{i} r\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \xi_{a i} J_{1}\left(k_{i} r\right) / J_{1}\left(k_{i} a\right) \tag{7}
$$

for both the cases. Here $J_{1}(k_{i} r)$ is the Bessel function of the first order, $\xi_{a i}$ is the displacement at $r=a$, $k_{i}=\lambda_{i} / a$, and $\lambda_{i}$ are the eigenvalues for the vibration problem (note that they are different for the thin discs and tall cylinders). In order to reveal the range of aspect ratios, in which application of the two-dimensional coupling technique is possible, the distribution of displacement in the radial vibrating piezoelements and their electromechanical properties have to be considered in some detail. Due to qualitative similarity of the radial vibration problem for a thin disk and tall cylinder, this will be done for the thin disk for simplicity, as the solution for the effective coupling coefficients for this case was considered previously¹⁵ and experimental results regarding the modal distributions of displacement over the disk surface are available.¹

## A. Axisymmetric radial vibrations of a thin piezoceramic disk

The modal equivalent electromechanical parameters of a thin axisymmetric piezoceramic disk with free boundary [Fig. 1(b)] were considered in Ref. 15 based on application of the energy method.¹⁶ The parameters were calculated starting from the solution for distribution of displacements (7) in radial vibrating isotropic elastic disk with free boundary. The eigenvalues for the vibration problem have consequent values at the Poisson's ratio $\sigma=0.3$,

$$
k_{i} a=\lambda_{i}=2.05,5.38,8.5,..., i \pi-0.9 \tag{8}
$$

(the expression $\lambda_{i}=i \pi-0.9$ is practically valid for $i \geq 2$). The resonance frequencies that correspond to the modes of vibration are

$$
f_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2 \pi a} \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right)}}, \tag{9}
$$

where $Y$ and $\rho$ are the Young's modulus and density of a disk material, respectively. In the case of the piezoceramic discs $Y$ and $\sigma$ have to be replaced by $Y_{1}^{E}=1/s_{11}^{E}$ and $\sigma_{1}^{E}=s_{12}^{E}/s_{11}^{E}$. Although for different piezoceramic materials $\sigma_{1}^{E}\neq0.3$, it can be shown from the equation for determining the eigenvalues

$$
J_{0}(k_{i}a)=(1-\sigma)\frac{J_{1}(k_{i}a)}{k_{i}a} \tag{10}
$$

that within limits of values of $\sigma_{1}^{E}$ for the most usable PZT compositions (approximately between $\sigma_{1}^{E}=0.27$ and $\sigma_{1}^{E}=0.35$) the values of $\lambda_{i}$ change by less than 1% from their values at $\sigma=0.3$. Thus, formula (9) for the resonance frequencies remains valid for piezoceramic discs within this accuracy. The spectrum of the resonance frequencies of a thin disk is illustrated with Fig. 2(a), in which the moduli of the input admittances of a disk having aspect ratio $h/2a=0.05$ are depicted. It is qualitatively clear from Fig. 2(a) that the modal coupling coefficients reduce significantly, as the number of the mode increases, and become virtually negligible. A quantitative estimation of the modal coupling coefficients was made in Ref. 15. Their values determined for a thin disk made of PZT-4 ceramic are presented in Table I together with those measured with the disk having aspect ratio 0.05 by resonance-antiresonance method$^{17}$ from the admittance plots presented in Fig. 2(a) by formula $k_{\text{eff}}^{2}=1-(f_{r}/f_{\text{ar}})^{2}$, where $f_{r}$ and $f_{\text{ar}}$ are the resonance and the antiresonance frequencies of a transducer, respectively.

With an increase of the aspect ratio (increase the height with $2a$ constant) above approximately $h/2a\approx1.5$, the third mode of the longitudinal vibration cannot be ignored in terms of applicability of the coupled vibration technique due to their coupling with the first radial mode. (The strongest coupling between the third mode of longitudinal vibration and first radial mode of a tall cylinder takes place at $h/2a\approx1.8$.) But the range of aspect ratios above this value does not present problems in terms of transducer designing, because at these aspect ratios the longitudinal vibration of the cylinder can be treated as one-dimensional, as will be shown in the current paper, and the radial modes of vibration do not present a practical interest.

Analysis of Fig. 2(a) and Table I allow the following conclusions. The entire range of aspect ratios can be divided conditionally into several regions that may differ qualitatively in terms of contribution of radial vibration of a disk into its thickness vibration and in terms of applicability of treating the vibration as a coupled two-dimensional problem.

In region I, at aspect ratios $h/2a<0.1$, the contribution of effects of vibration in the radial direction can be practically neglected due to negligible values of the modal coupling coefficients. The thickness vibration can be considered as the extreme case of one-dimensional vibration of a plate with infinitely large lateral dimensions.

Region II, with aspect ratios approximately in the interval $0.5>h/2a>0.1$, is characterized by coupling the thickness (axial) mode of vibration with multiple radial modes having relatively small separation between the resonance frequencies. This makes the vibration problem rather complicated, and results in distributions of displacements on the

### III. COUPLED VIBRATION ANALYSIS OF THE FINITE-SIZE PIEZOCERAMIC CYLINDER

Following the above discussion the assumed distributions of displacements will be accepted according to expression (6) along the axial axis and according to the first term of the series (7), i.e.,
$$
\xi(r)=\xi_{a} J_{1}\left(k_{1} r\right) / J_{1}\left(k_{1} a\right). \tag{13}
$$

Thus, the strain in the body of a cylinder will be
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{1} & =\frac{\partial \xi_{r}}{\partial r}=\xi_{a} \frac{1}{J_{1}\left(k_{1} a\right)} \frac{\partial J_{1}\left(k_{1} r\right)}{\partial r} \\
& =\xi_{a} \frac{k_{1}}{J_{1}\left(k_{1} a\right)}\left[J_{0}\left(k_{1} r\right)-\frac{J_{1}\left(k_{1} r\right)}{k_{1} r}\right],
\end{aligned} \tag{14}
$$

$$
S_{2}=\frac{\xi_{r}}{r}=\xi_{a} \frac{k_{1}}{J_{1}\left(k_{1} a\right)} \frac{J_{1}\left(k_{1} r\right)}{k_{1} r}, \tag{15}
$$

$$
S_{3}=\frac{\partial \xi_{z}}{\partial z}=\xi_{h} \frac{\pi}{h} \cos \frac{\pi z}{h}. \tag{16}
$$

In calculating parameters of the cylindrical piezoelement expressions for the potential, $W_{\text{pot}}^{E}$, kinetic, $W_{\text{kin}}^{E}$, and electromechanical, $W_{\text{em}}$, energies are involved. $^{16}$ The general expressions for the energies are as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{\text{pot}}^{E} & =\frac{1}{2} 2 \pi \int_{-h / 2}^{h / 2} \int_{0}^{a}\left(S_{1} T_{1}^{E}+S_{2} T_{2}^{E}+S_{3} T_{3}^{E}\right) r d r d z \\
& =\frac{1}{2}\left(K_{a}^{E} \xi_{a}^{2}+2 K_{a h}^{E} \xi_{a} \xi_{h}+K_{h}^{E} \xi_{h}^{2}\right)
\end{aligned} \tag{17}
$$
(superscripts $E$ are due to the fact that parameters of piezoceramic and stresses should be taken at electric field $E=0$),
$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{\text{kin}} & =\frac{1}{2} 2 \pi \int_{-h / 2}^{h / 2} \int_{0}^{a} \rho\left[\dot{\xi}_{r}^{2}+\dot{\xi}_{z}^{2}\right] r d r d z \\
& =\frac{1}{2}\left(M_{a}^{E} \dot{\xi}_{a}^{2}+2 M_{a h}^{E} \dot{\xi}_{a} \dot{\xi}_{h}+M_{h}^{E} \dot{\xi}_{h}^{2}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{18}
$$

In the expressions (17) and (18) $K_{a}^{E}, K_{h}^{E}$, and $M_{a}^{E}, M_{h}^{E}$ are the equivalent rigidities and masses, respectively, that belong to the partial systems (their "self" parameters), and $K_{a h}^{E}, M_{a h}^{E}$ are the "mutual" parameters that characterize coupling between the partial systems.

The electromechanical energy is $^{18}$
$$
W_{\mathrm{em}}=\frac{1}{2} 2 \pi \int_{-h / 2}^{h / 2} \int_{0}^{a} D_{3}^{E} E_{3} r d r d z=\frac{1}{2} V\left(n_{a} \xi_{a}+n_{h} \xi_{h}\right). \quad(19)
$$

Here $V$ is the voltage applied to the piezoelement, and $n_{a}, n_{h}$ are the electromechanical transformation coefficients.

As the expressions for the strain are known, it is convenient to use the piezoelectric equations in the form $^{19}$ for getting expressions for the stress $T_{i}^{E}$ and for the charge density $D_{3}^{E}$ at $E_{3}=0$,
$$
T_{1}=c_{11}^{E} S_{1}+c_{12}^{E} S_{2}+c_{13}^{E} S_{3}-e_{31} E_{3}, \tag{20}
$$

$$
T_{2}=c_{12}^{E} S_{1}+c_{11}^{E} S_{2}+c_{13}^{E} S_{3}-e_{31} E_{3}, \tag{21}
$$

$$
T_{3}=c_{13}^{E} S_{1}+c_{13}^{E} S_{2}+c_{33}^{E} S_{3}-e_{33} E_{3}, \tag{22}
$$

$$
D_{3}=e_{13}\left(S_{1}+S_{2}\right)+e_{33} S_{3}+e_{33}^{S} E_{3}. \tag{23}
$$

(The standard notations of the piezoelectric constants of ceramic are used, as they are defined in Ref. 19.)

Thus, the stress and charge density that are involved in expressions (17) and (19) for the potential and electromechanical energies may be readily expressed through the known expressions for strain $S_{i}$. Calculating all of the energies listed above becomes available after this is done. Two notes have to be made in regard to these calculations.

(a) In the case that a tall cylinder (at $S_{3}=0$) is taken as one of the partial systems the equivalent value of the Poisson's ratio in Eq. (10) for determining eigenvalues of the radial modes is $\sigma_{r d}^{E}=c_{12}^{E} / c_{11}^{E}$ (the subscript "rd" stands for "rod" in distinction from $\sigma_{1}^{E}=-s_{12}^{E} / s_{11}^{E}$ for a thin disk). For the piezoceramic compositions PZT-4, -5, and -8 the values of $\sigma_{\text{rd}}^{E}$ are 0.56, 0.62, and 0.54, respectively. The eigenvalues obtained from Eq. (10) at these values of $\sigma_{\text{rd}}^{E}$ are
$$
k_{i} a=\lambda_{i}=2.2,5.4,8.6, \ldots \tag{24}
$$
with deviation within less than 1% for different compositions. Thus, $k_{1} a=2.2$ has to be taken for calculating strains $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$.

(b) Using the stress $T_{3}^{E}$ at $E_{3}=0$ in expression (17) for the potential energy corresponds to the case in which the piezoelement is segmented in the direction of polarization (i.e., in the axial direction) and the segments in number of about six or more are connected electrically in parallel. Under this condition the electric field can be considered as constant throughout the length of the piezoelement. $^{18}$ If the piezoelement is solid, or segmented with the segments connected in series, the value of potential energy in this case differs from those determined at $E_{3}=0$ by the additional term $\Delta W$ and

becomes $W_{\mathrm{pot}}=W_{\mathrm{pot}}^{E}+\Delta W$. This is equivalent, finally, to changing value of rigidity $K_{h}^{E}$ related to the axial vibration at $E_{3}=0$, which will become

$$
K_{h}^{\prime}=K_{h}^{E}\left[1+\left(1-\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\right) \frac{k_{33}^{2}}{\left(1-k_{33}^{2}\right)}\right],
\tag{25}
$$

as it is shown in Ref. 18. Here $k_{33}$ is the coupling coefficient of piezoceramic, as defined in Ref. 19.

Omitting the straightforward, though cumbersome, calculations and equating their results to the general expressions (17)-(19) for the potential and kinetic energies we arrive at the following expressions for the equivalent parameters of the finite-size cylindrical piezoelement:

$$
M_{a}=0.86 M, \quad M_{h}=0.5 M, \quad M_{a h}=0,
\tag{26}
$$

where $M=\pi a^{2} h \rho$;

$$
\begin{aligned}
& K_{a}^{E}=13.4 h c_{11}^{E}, \quad K_{a h}=4.2 \pi a c_{13}^{E}, \\
& K_{h}^{E}=\frac{\pi^{3} a^{2}}{2 h} c_{33}^{E} \quad \text { at } E_{3}=0 ;
\end{aligned}
\tag{27}
$$

$$
n_{a}=2 \pi a e_{31}, \quad n_{h}=\left(2 \pi a^{2} / h\right) e_{33}.
\tag{28}
$$

With the addition of expression for the capacitance of the clamped piezoelement

$$
C_{e l}^{S}=\left(\pi a^{2} / h\right) \varepsilon_{33}^{S},
\tag{29}
$$

the full set of the equivalent parameters that are needed for calculating the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients of a cylindrical finite-size piezoelement is available.

Further, we will assume that the cylindrical piezoelements under consideration are solid or made from segments connected in series. The reason behind this is that for the most interesting range of aspect ratios around the point of strongest coupling ($h/2a$ is about unity) this is usually the case, and the piezoelements of this kind are more readily available for experimental verification of results of calculations.

The coefficients of coupling by formulas (5) are

$$
\gamma_{k}=\left(K_{a h} / \sqrt{K_{a}^{E} K_{h}^{\prime}}\right) \approx \frac{c_{13}^{E}}{\sqrt{c_{11}^{E} c_{33}^{E}}}, \quad \gamma_{m}=0.
\tag{30}
$$

(The $\approx$ sign is due to 2% difference between values of this expression for PZT-4 and -8.) Thus, for piezoelements made of ceramics PZT-4 and -8 it can be taken

$$
\gamma=0.58
\tag{31}
$$

(the subscript $k$ will be further omitted).

The partial frequencies are

$$
f_{a}=\frac{1}{2 \pi} \sqrt{\frac{K_{a}^{E}}{M_{a}}}, \quad f_{h}=\frac{1}{2 \pi} \sqrt{\frac{K_{h}^{\prime}}{M_{h}}}.
\tag{32}
$$

Note that $f_{h}$ coincides with the resonance frequency of a tall cylinder for the extreme case of the aspect ratio $(h/2a) \to \infty$.

For the ratio of the partial resonance frequencies (6) we arrive at

$$
\beta=\frac{f_{a}}{f_{h}} \approx 1.4 \frac{h}{2 a},
\tag{33}
$$

where the factor 1.4 differs for PZT-4 and PZT-8 by 2%.

After all the parameters in expressions for energies (17)-(19) associated with vibration of the cylindrical piezoelement are determined (the energies of the electrical and mechanical losses are not included for simplicity because they are not essential at this phase of the treatment), the Lagrange's equations that govern operation of the piezoelement as electromechanical transducer can be represented in the following form: $^{18}$

$$
\left(j \omega M_{a}+K_{a}^{E} / j \omega\right) \dot{\xi}_{a}+\left(K_{a h} / j \omega\right) \dot{\xi}_{h}=n_{a} V,
\tag{34}
$$

$$
\left(K_{a h} / j \omega\right) \dot{\xi}_{a}+\left(j \omega M_{h}+K_{h}^{\prime} / j \omega\right) \dot{\xi}_{h}=n_{h} V,
\tag{35}
$$

$$
I=j \omega C_{e l}^{S} V+n_{a} \dot{\xi}_{a}+n_{h} \dot{\xi}_{h}.
\tag{36}
$$

Here $I$ is the input current, and the time dependent factor $\exp(j \omega t)$ is omitted.

In order to determine the resonance frequencies and the resonance modes of vibration of the piezoelement, consider the free vibration equations obtained from Eqs. (34) and (35) at $V=0$ as presented in the form

$$
\left(f_{a}^{2}-f^{2}\right) \dot{\xi}_{a}+\left(K_{a h} / K_{a}\right) f_{a}^{2} \dot{\xi}_{h}=0,
\tag{37}
$$

$$
\left(K_{a h} / K_{h}^{\prime}\right) f_{h}^{2} \dot{\xi}_{a}+\left(f_{h}^{2}-f^{2}\right) \dot{\xi}_{h}=0.
\tag{38}
$$

The frequency equation that follows from Eqs. (37) and (38) is

$$
\left(f_{a}^{2}-f^{2}\right)\left(f_{h}^{2}-f^{2}\right)-\gamma^{2} f_{a}^{2} f_{h}^{2}=0.
\tag{39}
$$

After denoting

$$
\Omega=f^{2} / f_{a}^{2}
\tag{40}
$$

as the non-dimensional frequency factor and introducing the coefficient $\beta=f_{a}/f_{h}$, the frequency equation becomes

$$
\Omega^{2}-\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\right) \Omega+\left(1-\gamma^{2}\right) \frac{1}{\beta^{2}}=0.
\tag{41}
$$

Two branches of the non-dimensional resonance frequency factor, $\Omega_{1}(\beta)$ and $\Omega_{2}(\beta)$, follow from Eq. (41). These dependencies are presented in Fig. 3. They are calculated for piezoelement made from PZT-4 and PZT-8 [as $\gamma=0.58$ in both cases according to expression (30)] under the assumption that $2a$ remains constant and $h$ is changing.

$$
m s_{i}=\left.\left(\xi_{a} / \xi_{h}\right)\right|_{a t \quad \Omega=\Omega_{i}}, \quad i=1,2
\tag{42}
$$

may be found from either of Eqs. (37) and (38). Thus, for example, from Eq. (37),

$$

$$

## IV. FORCED VIBRATION OF THE FINITE-SIZE PIEZOELEMENTS AND THE EQUIVALENT ELECTROMECHANICAL CIRCUIT OF THE RELATED ELECTROMECHANICAL TRANSDUCERS

The operational electromechanical properties of a transducer that employs the finite-size cylindrical piezoelement can be calculated using the set of Eqs. (34)-(36) or from the electromechanical circuit presented in Fig. 6, which is equivalent to this set of equations (note that $C_h^E$ and $C_a^E$ are the equivalent compliances that are related to the equivalent rigidities as $C_h^E = 1/K_h^E$ and $C_a^E = 1/K_a^E$). We assume that the side surface of the cylinder is free of a load, and the mechanical (acoustical) load, $Z_m$, that is applied to one end surface (it is included in the equivalent circuit by observations, as well as the resistances of the electrical and mechanical losses, $R_{eL}$ and $r_{mL}$) has magnitude small enough as not to change distribution of displacement in the axial direction, which is usually the case for electroacoustical transducers. The impedances $Z_{ha} = z_{ha}(U_a/U_h)$ and $Z_{ah} = z_{ah}(U_h/U_a)$, where $z_{ah} = z_{ha} = K_{ah}/j\omega$, are introduced impedances that characterize coupling between contours that correspond to the partial vibrating systems; $z_{ah}$ is the mutual impedance between the partial systems. Velocities $U_h$ and $U_a$ result from the combined effect of the "self" velocities of the partial systems (i.e., without effect of coupling) and their mutual contributions due to coupling between the systems. Thus, $U_h$ can be represented as
$$
U_{h}=\dot{\xi}_{h}+\dot{\xi}_{a h}, \tag{49}
$$
where $\dot{\xi}_h$ is the self-partial axial velocity and $\dot{\xi}_{ha}$ is the additional axial velocity due to coupling with the radial vibrating partial system.

Likewise,
$$
U_{a}=\dot{\xi}_{a}+\dot{\xi}_{a h}, \tag{50}
$$
where $\dot{\xi}_a$ is the self-partial radial velocity and $\dot{\xi}_{ah}$ is the additional radial velocity due to coupling with the axial vibrating partial system. The velocities $U_h$ and $U_a$ have to be calculated from the equivalent circuit in Fig. 6. The self-partial velocities $\dot{\xi}_a$ and $\dot{\xi}_h$ have to be determined from the equivalent circuit considering the contours as independent. ## V. RESULTS OF THE EXPERIMENTAL INVESTIGATION

The experimental investigation was fulfilled with piezoelements in the shape of finite-size circular cylinders having different height-to-diameter aspect ratios. The main body of experiments was done with samples made from PZT-8 ceramics. The diameter of the samples, $2a=25.8$ mm, was kept constant, and the height was changed so as to form cylinders with aspect ratios ranging from 0.05 up to 2.1 by gluing discs of different thickness. All the discs were electrically connected in series. They were glued with Devcon 5-min epoxy mixed with 10% nickel powder to ensure electrical contact between the electrodes. Two more sets of samples were made of PZT-4 ceramics. One of them covered a range of aspect ratios from 0.07 up to 0.28. Its basic piezoelement had diameter $2a=29.9$ mm and thickness (height) $h=2.08$ mm. Another set was made of discs having diameter 38.1 mm and aspect ratio 0.5. This set covered the range of aspect ratios from 0.5 up to 2.5. Results obtained with all the sets of samples were reduced to the same diameter and electromechanical parameters as the samples made of PZT-4 with diameter 38.1 mm, and thus they could be included into the common summary of the results. The main difficulty of experimenting originated from the fact that results were influenced by quality of gluing the piezoelements of small height. Therefore, multiple regluing and excluding evidently false results was performed in order to obtain the reliable results.

The resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients of the samples were measured by standard resonance/antiresonance method $^{17}$ using an HP 4194A Impedance Analyzer. The results of testing are depicted in Figs. 3 and 5 by circles. The sequences of resonances that belong to the radial and extensional modes were obtained from the experimental data presented in Refs. 1–4.

It is noteworthy that the results of measuring the coupling coefficients cannot be considered rigorous for range II of aspect ratios that have multiple radial resonance modes, because the axial modes of vibration cannot be considered isolated, strictly speaking. But the main goal of experimenting for this region was rather in revealing a trend in behavior of the coupling coefficients vs aspect ratio than in providing their exact values for the transducer calculating purposes. In order to increase reliability of the experimental data they were measured with small increments of the aspect ratios. In the vicinity of the aspect ratio $h/2a=1$ the effective coupling coefficient for the mode of vibration that corresponds to the high frequency branch (branch 2) drops to zero. The reason for this is that vibration in the axial and radial directions has the same sign in this mode according to Fig. 4. The piezoelectric constant for deformations in radial $(e_{31})$ and axial $(e_{33})$ directions have different signs and effect of electromechanical conversion canceling takes place. In opposite some rise of $k_{\text{eff}}$ occurs for the mode of vibration that corresponds to the low frequency branch (branch 1), for which vibrations in the axial and radial directions have different signs. The analogous effect takes place at aspect ratios around $h/2a=0.4$–0.5 due to coupling between the axial and second radial modes of vibration. The effect is less pronounced because the second radial mode has lesser effective coupling coefficient (see Table I).

The theoretical and experimental results were both normalized to their extreme values at $(h/2a)\rightarrow\infty$. Namely, to the effective coupling coefficient of a tall end-electroded bar with comprising segments connected in series ($k_{n}=0.59$ for PZT-4 and $k_{n}=0.57$ for PZT-8), and to the resonance frequency of radial vibration of a tall cylinder $[f=(2.2/2\pi a)(c_{11}^{E}/\rho)^{1/2}]$. The results obtained show a fairly good agreement with theoretically predicted behavior of the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients for the intended range of aspect ratios $1.5>h/2a>0.5$, especially, if to take into consideration that parameters of different piezoceramic samples could differ from those taken into calculation according to specifications.

At the aspect ratios $h/2a>1.5$ results of calculations for radial mode related part of branch 2 (both in terms of resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients) may become corrupted due to coupling of the radial mode with the third mode of axial vibration. (The point of strongest coupling of these modes should be around the value of aspect ratio 1.8.) Deviations of the experimental results from calculations for branch 2 at aspect ratios larger than 1.5, both for resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients in Figs. 3 and 5, reflect the effect of this coupling. The experimental data related to the third mode of axial vibration are presented in Fig. 3 for the resonance frequencies and in Fig. 5 for the effective coupling coefficients as additional branches marked by triangles. Although both the radial mode and third mode of axial vibration at aspect ratios of 1.5–2.5 do not present a practical interest, these data may be useful for identifying results of experimenting with finite-size cylinders in the related range of the aspect ratios.

Such a conclusion was made previously on the basis of highly nonuniform distributions of displacements on the surface of a transducer (see Ref. 1) that take place by the same reason of coupling between the axial and radial modes. The coupled effects can be eliminated by slicing the circular discs into rods and thus achieving uniform distribution of displacements and getting a high effective coupling coefficient. The greatest positive effect in terms of the effective coupling coefficient may be obtained in this case, if the height to average diameter of the rods aspect ratio is about unity or more, as the theoretical and experimental results presented in Fig. 5 show. The same effect of decoupling vibrations is achieved in the composite piezoelement designs. Plot of the modulus of admittance for such a circular disk piezoelement having aspect ratio $h/2a=0.13$ is depicted in Fig. 2. It shows that vibrations of the disk are one-dimensional in all the range of aspect ratios with effective coupling coefficient having maximum possible value for a particular piezoceramic composition. Thus, the composite piezoelement design can be recommended as the best choice for thickness vibrating transducers with aspect ratios in region II.

## VI. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

The dependencies of the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients for circular cylindrical piezoelements of finite size was considered as a function of their height-to-diameter aspect ratios, $h/2a$, by using the method of coupled vibrations in axial and radial directions of the piezoelements. All the range of the aspect ratios is divided conditionally in three regions, in which effects of the coupling between vibrations differ significantly. In region I (at $h/2a<0.1$) axial (thickness) vibration can be considered as independent of radial one-dimensional vibration for a disk with large lateral dimensions. In region II $(0.1<h/2a<0.5)$ a strong coupling between the axial and multiple radial modes of vibration takes place that results in highly nonuniform distribution of displacements in axial direction and in significant reduction of the effective coupling coefficient. This assertion is confirmed by previous works $^{1-4}$ in terms of the displacement distributions and by the experimental results presented in Table I and Fig. 5. Avoiding this range of aspect ratios can be recommended for designing transducers with solid circular cylindrical piezoelements. Much better characteristics can be obtained with the piezoelements having the same overall dimensions that are sliced into rods or made by using the composite technology. Results for Region III $(h/2a>0.5)$ are presented in Figs. 3 and 5 in the form of plots normalized to their values for the extreme case of the aspect ratio $h/2a\gg1$, and thus can be applied to piezoelements made from different PZT ceramics. The conclusion can be made based on results presented in these figures that the axial vibrations of the cylinder can be considered as one-dimensional at aspect ratios larger than 1.5, because values of the resonance frequencies and effective coupling coefficients already practically reach their values for the extreme case. This conclusion is supported by the result of measuring the mode shape of vibration in the axial direction of a cylinder at $h/2a=1.5$ that proved to be uniform, as shown in Fig. 7(b).

The procedure to determine the distribution of displacement over the surface of a finite-size cylinder was also described. This characteristic is important for calculating the acoustic radiation parameters of a transducer. The procedure involves straightforward calculations that have to be made
