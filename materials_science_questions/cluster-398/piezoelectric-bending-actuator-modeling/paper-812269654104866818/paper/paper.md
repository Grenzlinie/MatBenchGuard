# Auger Recombination in an Intense Piezoelectric Noise Field

Doan Nhat QUANG

Institute of Theoretical Physics, Academy of Sciences of Vietnam,
P. O. Box 429 Bo Ho, Hanoi 10000, Vietnam

(Received January 8, 1990)

A theory is developed of the Auger recombination occurring in a piezoelectric semiconductor propagated by an intense acoustic noise flux. The calculation of the Auger coefficient is carried out within the classical approach to the piezoelectric noise field and for the case of nondegenerate carrier statistics. Then a simple analytic expression of the Auger coefficient is derived, which exhibits a linearly exponential function of the flux intensity. The screening effect of charge carriers is shown to be of great importance. The Auger recombination is found to be strongly enhanced as compared to that in the appropriate noiseless sample up to several orders of magnitude at large flux intensity, low temperature and small carrier concentration. Thus this gives another example illustrating the earlier statement that the Auger transition might still be one of the leading recombination mechanisms for disordered systems.

## §1. Introduction

Auger transition has seemed to be the most important nonradiative recombination mechanism of non-equilibrium carriers in many semiconductor devices. $^{1-4)}$ In fact, electric fields are often present in semiconductor experiments, not only external fields but also internal ones (e.g. surface fields, p-n junctions). As usual, they are approximated by a uniform field for theoretical treatment. $^{5-7)}$ The result was that the Auger recombination is shown to be greatly enhanced by such a field.

It is well known $^{8)}$ that acoustic noise in a piezoelectric material can be amplified when the drift velocity of electrons due to an applied dc electric field exceeds the velocity of sound. Then charge carriers in the sample are strongly affected by the acoustic waves over piezoelectric interaction. Obviously, the piezoelectric noise field is not a uniform one as treated previously and, in addition, assumes randomness. Hence, the charge carriers in an intense piezoelectric noise field make up in essence a non-crystalline system whose disorder is associated with a random field.

It was pointed out $^{9,10)}$ that owing to the random field-induced disorder, the electronic energy spectrum of a semiconductor is considerably changed, e.g., there exist density-of-state tails deep in the band gap of the appropriate crystalline material. This modification of the electronic band structure causes in turn relevant changes in the observable properties of the sample, in particular, in the optical ones connected with radiative transitions. $^{9,10)}$ The piezoelectric noise field is suggested to exert a remarkable influence also on nonradiative processes.

The purpose of the present paper is to investigate the Auger recombination occurring in piezoelectric semiconductors through which an intense acoustic noise flux propagates. This problem is of some practical interest in view of opening a new possibility to estimate the characteristic of the acoustic noise flux (its intensity) directly from Auger lifetime measurements. In $\S 2$ we collect the formulae to be used for calculating the Auger lifetime of excess carriers in materials subjected to some random field. The random piezoelectric field accompanying an acoustic noise flux is treated in $\S 3$, taking account of piezoelectric and elastic anisotropies. Evaluation of the influence of this field on the Auger coefficient proceeds in $\S 4$ for the case of nondegenerate carrier statistics. Plots illustrating the order of magnitude of the effect and conclusions will be presented in $\S 5$.


## §2. Basic Formulation

In a lot of systems of physical interest the disorder is uaually given rise to by some random field $U_{l}(\boldsymbol{R})$, which is only statistically described by a probability functional. In what follows, the discussion will be restricted to the case where $U_{l}(\boldsymbol{R})$ is a Gaussian field so that it may be completely characterized by the binary correlation function $^{10)}$
$$
\Psi_{l}\left(\boldsymbol{R}, \boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)=\left\langle U_{l}(\boldsymbol{R}) U_{l}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle. \quad (1)
$$

Here the angular brackets stand for averaging over all configurations of the random field, the band indices $l=\mathrm{c}, \mathrm{v}$ refer to the conduction and valence bands, respectively.

As usual, we shall suppose the random potential to be normalized by the condition
$$
\left\langle U_{l}\right\rangle=0, \quad (l=\mathrm{c}, \mathrm{v}). \quad (2)
$$

Hereafter, the field under consideration is assumed to obey the following inequality
$$
\frac{\hbar^{2} \psi_{2 l}}{4 m_{l} \psi_{1 l}^{3 / 2}} \ll 1, \quad (l=\mathrm{c}, \mathrm{v}), \quad (3)
$$
where $m_{l}$ denotes the effective mass of the $l$-th band, $\psi_{1 l}$ and $2 \psi_{2 l}$ are the mean squares of the random potential fluctuation and the field strength, respectively
$$
\psi_{1 l}=\Psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{R}, \boldsymbol{R})=\left\langle U_{l}^{2}\right\rangle, \quad (4)
$$

$$
\psi_{2 l}=\left.\frac{1}{2} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{R}} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}} \Psi_{l}\left(\boldsymbol{R}, \boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)\right|_{\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(\nabla U_{l}\right)^{2}\right\rangle. \quad (5)
$$

Furthermore, the root-mean square of the random potential fluctuation is supposed to be small when compared with the band gap of the sample in no-field conditions:
$$
\psi_{1 l}^{1 / 2} \ll \varepsilon_{g}, \quad (l=\mathrm{c}, \mathrm{v}), \quad (6)
$$
which permits neglecting interband scattering due to the field.

It has been pointed out $^{9,10)}$ that if the inequality (3) is fulfilled the field $U_{l}(\boldsymbol{R})$ varies slowly in space so that a semiclassical approach to it is applicable. Then, by developing a Green's function formalism it has been proved $^{11)}$ that in the purely classical approximation the reciprocal lifetime of Auger recombination of non-equilibrium carriers in the conduction band (e-e-h) process (Fig. 1(a)) and the valence band (h-h-e) one (Fig. 1(b)) may be represented in the form of the volume and configuration average of a local one:
$$
\frac{1}{\tau}=\left\langle\frac{1}{V} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{R} \frac{1}{\tau(\boldsymbol{R})}\right\rangle, \quad (7)
$$
with $V$ as the sample volume. Here $\tau(\boldsymbol{R})$ is the life-time of a local Auger transition $12 \to 1^{\prime} 2^{\prime}$ taking place at the point $\boldsymbol{R}$ and is given by

![](./images/812269654104866818_1.jpg)

Fig. 1. Auger recombination via (a) the conduction band process due to e-e collision and (b) the valence band one due to h-h collision. The shaded regions indicate the density-of-state tails for electrons and holes, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}$ being the edges of the conduction and valence bands, respectively.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\tau(\boldsymbol{R})}= & \frac{V^{2}}{(2 \pi)^{8} \hbar \delta n} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{k}_{1} \mathrm{~d} \boldsymbol{k}_{2} \mathrm{~d} \boldsymbol{k}_{1^{\prime}} \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{k}_{2^{\prime}}|M|^{2} \Theta_{i f}(\boldsymbol{R}) \\
& \times \delta\left(E_{1}(\boldsymbol{R})+E_{2}(\boldsymbol{R})-E_{1^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R})-E_{2^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R})\right) \delta\left(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}-\boldsymbol{k}_{1^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2^{\prime}}\right),
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

where $\delta n$ means the excess-pair concentration, $M$ is the matrix element of the electron-electron interaction. The local statistical factor is defined as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Theta_{i f}(\boldsymbol{R})= & {\left[1-\exp \left(-\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}-\zeta_{\mathrm{v}}}{T}\right)\right] f\left(E_{1}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{1}\right) f\left(E_{2}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{2}\right) } \\
& \times\left[1-f\left(E_{1^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{1^{\prime}}\right)\right]\left[1-f\left(E_{2^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{2^{\prime}}\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

with $T$ being the sample temperature in units of energy. This term accounts for the occupation probabilities of the different states, which are described by the Fermi distribution functions $f\left(E_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{l}\right)$ and depend on space coordinates $\boldsymbol{R}$ because of field-induced fluctuations of the band edges

$$
E_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{R})=\varepsilon_{l}(\boldsymbol{k})+U_{l}(\boldsymbol{R}), \tag{10}
$$

$\varepsilon_{l}(\boldsymbol{k})$ being the dispersion relation for an electron in the Bloch state $\lambda=|l \boldsymbol{k}\rangle$ of the relevant fluctuationless problem. $\zeta_{l}(l=\mathrm{c}, \mathrm{v})$ are the quasi-Fermi levels for the electrons and holes in separate quasi-equilibria in the corresponding bands.*

It should be remarked that in the adopted classical approximation where all forces arising from the random field are omitted, the influence of it is reduced simply to smooth bending of the band edges of the conduction and valence bands as indicated in eq. (10). It is well known $^{14)}$ that owing to the band edge modulation non-equilibrium carriers are to separate spatially, which inhibits their recombination. For amorphous semiconductors described by the Fritzsche model, $^{15)}$ the band edge fluctuations are assumed to be of just the order of the band gap of the relevant crystalline material so that the spatial electron-hole separation was shown to play an important part, e.g., giving rise to the memory effect. $^{14)}$ When calculating the recombination rate with the aid of eq. (7), the spatial electron-hole separation was neglected, resulting in an overestimation of the observable rate. However, eq. (7) may be referred to as a good approximation. Indeed, for Gaussian fields quoted above, the band edge fluctuations are substantially of the order of the root-mean square of the potential fluctuation, $\psi_{1 l}^{1 / 2}$. Those are, under the assumption (6), small as compared to the band gap. Consequently, the memory effect in the case in question is thought to become of far less importance. Moreover, eq. (7) was found to be taken as the basis for study of the Auger recombination in those non-crystalline semiconductors that can be described by the smooth random field model. $^{16-18)}$

## §3. Random Piezoelectric Field Due to an Acoustic Noise Flux

In this paper we will be concerned with cubic crystals with zincblende symmetry (point group $\overline{4} 3 \mathrm{~m}$ ) which lack an inversion centre as typified by the III-V compounds and the cubic II-VI ones. Let an intense acoustic noise flux travel through a crystalline sample, having the form of a wave packet with a small angular divergence and a long central wavelength $\lambda_{0}: \lambda_{0} \gg a_{0}, a_{0}$ being the lattice constant. Then the displacement of the lattice from its equilibrium position associated with the flux can be written as:

$$
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{R})=\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{q, v} u_{q}^{v} \boldsymbol{e}^{v} \cos \left(\boldsymbol{q} \boldsymbol{R}+\varphi_{q}^{v}\right). \tag{11}
$$

Here $N$ denotes the number of unit cells in the fundamental volume, $u_{q}^{v}$ and $\varphi_{q}^{v}$ are respectively the real amplitude and the initial phase of the normal mode $v$ with propagation vector $\boldsymbol{q}$ and unit polarization vector $\boldsymbol{e}^{v}$. In accordance with the noise nature of the acoustic flux, the phases are supposed to be random, statistically independent for different $v$ and

* It is interesting to note that when the random field being switched off, $U_{l}(\boldsymbol{R})=0$, we reproduce the wellknown expression for the lifetime of band-to-band Auger recombination in crystalline systems. $^{12,13)}$

$q$, and uniformly distributed in the interval $(0, 2\pi)$.

An acoustic flux travelling in the sample is, as usual, accompanied by an energy flux. Taking an average of the energy flux density over the oscilation period, we obtain the flux intensity of the $\nu$-mode in the form:

$$
P_{\nu}=\frac{2 \rho v_{\nu}^{3}}{N} \sum_{q} q^{2}\left(u_{q}^{\nu}\right)^{2}, \tag{12}
$$

where $\rho$ means the mass density of the sample, $v_{\nu}$ is the phase velocity of sound depending upon the direction of propagation and the mode in question: $v_{\nu}=(c / \rho)^{1 / 2}$, with $c$ representing an appropriate combination of the non-vanishing components of the elastic stiffness tensor of the medium $c_{11}, c_{12}$ and $c_{44}$. It is well known that the acoustic flux is also accompanied by a longitudinal electric field, affecting electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence one via piezoelectric interaction. This field is of a long-range character and is then screened by free charge carriers as follows $^{19,20}$

$$
U(\boldsymbol{R})=-\frac{4 e e_{14}}{\varepsilon \sqrt{N}} \sum_{q, v} \frac{1}{1+\left(\lambda / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}}\left(e_{1}^{\nu} e_{2} e_{3}+e_{2}^{\nu} e_{3} e_{1}+e_{3}^{\nu} e_{1} e_{2}\right) u_{q}^{\nu} \cos \left(\boldsymbol{q} \boldsymbol{R}+\varphi_{q}^{\nu}\right). \tag{13}
$$

Here $e$ denotes the electron charge, $\varepsilon=\kappa \varepsilon_{0}$ means the dielectric constant (we shall employ SI units), $r_{0}$ is the screening length and, as before, $\boldsymbol{e}^{\nu}$ is the unit polarization vector, $\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{q} / q$ the unit vector along the propagation direction, and $\lambda=2 \pi / q$ the wavelength of the mode in question. In cubic crystals with zincblende structure there is one nonvanishing component of the piezoelectric stress tensor referred to the crystallographic axes: $e_{123}=e_{132}=e_{231}=e_{213}=e_{312}=e_{321} \equiv e_{14}$.

In accordance with the chaotic nature of the phases $\varphi_{q}^{\nu}$ entering eq. (13), the piezoelectric field accompanying an acoustic noise flux is a random one. Moreover, the expression (13) can be easily shown to satisfy the Bogoliubov theorem. $^{10,21)}$ Hence, the piezoelectric noise field obeys the Gaussian distribution. As seen from eq. (13), this field is independent of the band index so that the band gap still remains as constant over the whole sample despite of the random fluctuations of the band edges of the conduction and valence bands. (This is also the case of the random impuriry field in a heavily doped semiconductor $^{17)}$ ). The random field of this kind seems to be of some interest in regard to optical transitions. $^{21,22)}$ Correspondingly, the band index will be hereafter dropped.

Now, upon making use of the usual procedure $^{10)}$ we may find the binary correlation function in the form:

$$
\Psi\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4 e e_{14}}{\varepsilon \sqrt{N}}\right)^{2} \sum_{q, v} \frac{1}{\left[1+\left(\lambda / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}}\left(e_{1}^{\nu} e_{2} e_{3}+e_{2}^{\nu} e_{3} e_{1}+e_{3}^{\nu} e_{1} e_{2}\right)^{2}\left(u_{q}^{\nu}\right)^{2} \cos \boldsymbol{q}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right). \quad(14)
$$

It should be remarked that the random field under consideration is anisotropic, depending strongly on the direction of propagation and that of polarization of the acoustic noise flux as well.

First, we are dealing with the case when the acoustic noise travels in the direction $\boldsymbol{e}=[110]$. Then only the transverse mode with displacement in the direction $\boldsymbol{e}^{T}=[001]$ turns out to be piezolelectrically active, yielding

$$
\Psi\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2 e e_{14}}{\varepsilon \sqrt{N}}\right)^{2} \sum_{q} \frac{1}{\left[1+\left(\lambda / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}}\left(u_{q}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2} \cos \boldsymbol{q}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right). \tag{15}
$$

Now, inserting the function (15) into eqs. (4) and (5) we are able with the aid of eq. (12) to represent approximately the mean squares of the potential fluctuation and the strength of the piezoelectric noise field in terms of the flux intensity $P_{\mathrm{T}}$ of the shear mode in question:

$$
\psi_{1}=\left(\frac{e e_{14}}{2 \pi \varepsilon}\right)^{2} \frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{\left[1+\left(\lambda_{0} / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}} \frac{P_{\mathrm{T}}}{\rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}, \tag{16}
$$

and


$$
\psi_{2}=\left(\frac{e e_{14}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{\left[1+\left(\lambda_{0} / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}} \frac{P_{\mathrm{T}}}{2 \rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}, \quad(17)
$$
where $v_{\mathrm{T}}$ being the sound velocity of the [110]
shear mode
$$
v_{\mathrm{T}}=\left(c_{44} / \rho\right)^{1 / 2}. \quad(18)
$$

Next, we return to the general case (14). As quoted above, the piezoelectricity of the cubic crystals having zinc-blende structure, and to a lesser extent, their elasticity are strongly anisotropic. This leads, as usual, to the re- quirement of some averaging. For an approx- imate calculation, it seems to be suitable to adopt the method that was used for calculating the piezoelectric mobility of those materials. $^{19,20,23)}$ We shall then carry out spherical averaging of the piezoelectric stress constant separately for the longitudinal and transverse modes of the acoustic wave by means of the following substitution procedures $^{20,23)}$

$$
e_{14}^{2}\left(e_{1}^{\mathrm{L}} e_{2} e_{3}+e_{2}^{\mathrm{L}} e_{3} e_{1}+e_{3}^{\mathrm{L}} e_{1} e_{2}\right)^{2} \to \frac{3}{35} e_{14}^{2}, \quad(19)
$$

$$
e_{14}^{2}\left(e_{1}^{\mathrm{T}} e_{2} e_{3}+e_{2}^{\mathrm{T}} e_{3} e_{1}+e_{3}^{\mathrm{T}} e_{1} e_{2}\right)^{2} \to \frac{4}{35} e_{14}^{2}. \quad(20)
$$

As a result, we get

$$
\bar{\Psi}\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{2}{N}\left(\frac{e e_{14}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2} \sum_{q} \frac{1}{\left[1+\left(\lambda / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}}\left\{\frac{12}{35}\left(u_{q}^{\mathrm{L}}\right)^{2}+\frac{16}{35}\left(u_{q}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}\right\} \cos q\left(\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}\right),\quad (21)
$$

where the overbar being the spherical averaging over all propagation directions of the acoustic flux.

Again, putting the binary correlation function just obtained into eqs. (4) and (5) gives

$$
\bar{\psi}_{1}=\left(\frac{e e_{14}}{2 \pi \varepsilon}\right)^{2} \frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{\rho\left[1+\left(\lambda_{0} / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}}\left(\frac{12}{35} \frac{\bar{P}_{\mathrm{L}}}{\bar{v}_{\mathrm{L}}^{3}}+\frac{16}{35} \frac{\bar{P}_{\mathrm{T}}}{\bar{v}_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}\right),\quad (22)
$$

and

$$
\bar{\psi}_{2}=\left(\frac{e e_{14}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{2 \rho\left[1+\left(\lambda_{0} / 2 \pi r_{0}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}}\left(\frac{12}{35} \frac{\bar{P}_{\mathrm{L}}}{\bar{v}_{\mathrm{L}}^{3}}+\frac{16}{35} \frac{\bar{P}_{\mathrm{T}}}{\bar{v}_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}\right).\quad (23)
$$

Here we have introduced the spherical averages of the flux intensities $\bar{P}_{\mathrm{L}}$ and $\bar{P}_{\mathrm{T}}$, and those of the sound velocities of the longitudinal and transverse modes: $:^{20,23)}$

$$
\bar{v}_{v}=\left(c_{v} / \rho\right)^{1 / 2}, \quad(v=\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{T}) \quad(24)
$$

with

$$
c_{\mathrm{L}}=\frac{1}{5}\left(3 c_{11}+2 c_{12}+4 c_{44}\right), \quad(25)
$$

$$
c_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{1}{5}\left(c_{11}-c_{12}+3 c_{44}\right). \quad(26)
$$

## §4. Influence of the Piezoelectric Noise Field on the Auger Recombination

Now, we apply the theory developed in the preceding sections to study Auger processes oc- curring in a piezoelectric crystal travelled by an acoustic noise flux. As mentioned above, the piezoelectric field accompanying the flux is independent of the band index, so it drops out of the energy conserving $\delta$-function in eq. (8). Therefore, its effect on the Auger lifetime is only via the local statistical factor, $\Theta_{l f}(\boldsymbol{R})$, over which the configuration averaging must now be performed. In what follows, the discus- sion will be confined to the case of a nondegenerate semiconductor whose electrons and holes may be described by the Boltzmann statistics

$$
f\left(E_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}\right)=\exp \left[-\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{k})+U(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}}{T}\right],
$$

$$
1-f\left(E_{\mathrm{v}}(\boldsymbol{R})-\zeta_{\mathrm{v}}\right)=\exp \left[-\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{v}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}(\boldsymbol{k})-U(\boldsymbol{R})}{T}\right].
$$

The calculation proceeds in the same way sketched in refs. 11 and 16. Inserting eqs. (27) and (28) into eq. (9), and then taking the con-

figuration average of the resulting expression with the help of the well-known standard formula¹⁰)

$$
\langle\exp [\alpha U(\boldsymbol{R})]\rangle=\exp \left(\frac{1}{2} \alpha^{2} \psi_{1}\right), \quad(29)
$$

it holds for both the conduction and valence band processes depicted in Fig. 1:

$$
\left\langle\Theta_{l f}(\boldsymbol{R})\right\rangle=\Theta_{l f} \exp \left(\frac{\psi_{1}}{2 T^{2}}\right), \quad(30)
$$

$\Theta_{l f}$ being the statistical factor without the random field.

Next, putting eq. (30) into eqs. (7) and (8), and presenting the result in terms of the Auger coefficient defined through

$$
\frac{1}{\tau}=C n^{2}, \quad(31)
$$

with $n$ being the majority-carrier concentration, we finally find the following interesting relationship

$$
\frac{C}{C_{0}}=\exp \left(\frac{\psi_{1}}{2 T^{2}}\right), \quad(32)
$$

$C_{0}$ being the Auger coefficient in the absence of the noise field.

Thus, the Auger coefficient in a nondegenerate piezoelectric semiconductor travelled by an acoustic noise flux depends exponentially on the random parameter, $\psi_{1}$. Therefore, according to eq. (16) or (22) the Auger coefficient is found to be a linearly exponential function of the flux intensity. On the other hand, the parameter $\psi_{1}$ as a function of the central wavelength possesses a rather sharp maximum at the point

$$
\lambda_{0}=2 \pi r_{0}. \quad(33)
$$

Because of the particular sensitivity of the Auger coefficient to $\psi_{1}$, this peak is obviously of practical interest. So, we shall, hereafter, restrict the discussion to that value of $\lambda_{0}$.

First, we are dealing with the case of the acoustic noise of [110] shear mode. By replacing $\lambda_{0}$ entering eqs. (16) and (17) with the value (33), we have

$$
\psi_{1}=\left(\frac{e e_{14} r_{0}}{2 \varepsilon}\right)^{2} \frac{P_{\mathrm{T}}}{\rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}, \quad(34)
$$

and

$$
\psi_{2}=\left(\frac{e e_{14}}{2 \varepsilon}\right)^{2} \frac{P_{\mathrm{T}}}{2 \rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}. \quad(35)
$$

It is worth noting that according to eqs. (32) and (16) or (22) (see also (34)) the Auger coefficient depends strongly upon the screening length, $r_{0}$. Thus, the screening effect of charge carriers assumes vital importance, reducing considerably the influence of the noise field on the Auger transition. For nondegenerate carrier statistics, $r_{0}$ is given as the Debye-Hücked screening length.²⁰)

$$
r_{0}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon T}{e^{2} n}\right)^{1 / 2}. \quad(36)
$$

It results

$$
\psi_{1}=\frac{e_{14}^{2} T P_{\mathrm{T}}}{4 \varepsilon n \rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}. \quad(37)
$$

Inserting eq. (37) into eq. (32), we obtain for the ratio $C / C_{0}$ of the Auger coefficients for a piezoelectric semiconductor with and without the noise field accompanying an acoustic flux of [110] shear mode:

$$
\frac{C}{C_{0}}=\exp \left(\frac{e_{14}^{2} P_{\mathrm{T}}}{8 \varepsilon n T \rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}\right). \quad(38)
$$

Now, we return to the semiclassical condition (3). With the use of eqs. (35) and (37) it may be in the case under consideration rewritten as follows

$$
\frac{e^{2} \hbar^{2}}{4 e_{14} m_{l}}\left(\frac{n^{3} \rho v_{\mathrm{T}}^{3}}{\varepsilon T^{3} P_{\mathrm{T}}}\right)^{1 / 2} \ll 1, \quad(l=\mathrm{c}, \mathrm{v}). \quad(39)
$$

This inequality indicates that the piezoelectric noise field in a given sample will behave semiclassically at low carrier concentration, high temperature and large flux intensity.

Lastly, it should be remarked that since the longitudinal modes are usually stiffer than the transverse ones $(c_{\mathrm{L}}>c_{\mathrm{T}})$, at equal flux intensities the latter is, as seen from eqs. (22) and (24), predominant over the former in contribution to the influence of a noise field on the Auger transition.

We have carried out numerical calculations of the Auger coefficient ratio $C/C_{0}$ given by eq. (38) for the case when an intense acoustic noise flux propagates in the [110] direction. This ratio was plotted as a measure of the effect due to the random piezoelectric field in dependence of the flux intensity in a range of $10^{4}$ to $10^{8} \, \text{W/m}^2$ for different temperatures (10, 77 and 300 K) and various carrier concentrations ($10^{20}$, $10^{21}$ and $10^{22} \, \text{m}^{-3}$). From the results given in Fig. 2, the noise field is found to be able to raise the Auger recombination rate up to several orders of magnitude, in particular, at low temperatures (10 and 77 K) and small carrier concentrations ($10^{20}$ and $10^{21} \, \text{m}^{-3}$). With larger carrier concentration ($10^{22} \, \text{m}^{-3}$) the disorder effect is considerably diminished owing to increase of screening, especially, it may be almost omitted when the temperature being higher (300 K). Nevertheless, it is to be noticed that according to a theoretical estimate$^{21)}$ using experimental data on the absorption edge modulation caused by acoustoelectric domains, $^{22)}$ the flux intensity could amount to more than $10^{10} \, \text{W/m}^2$, so the noise field effect might be pronounced even with large carrier concentration ($10^{24} \, \text{m}^{-3}$) and at higher temperature (300 K).

Thus, we can again come to the conclusion, which has already been drawn based on examining other physical realizations of the smooth random field model, $^{16-18)}$ that the Auger transition might still be one of the most effective recombination mechanisms for noncrystalline semiconductors.

It should also be emphasized that strong enhancement of the Auger recombination in such systems is intimately connected both with nondegeneracy of the carrier statistics and essential modification of the electronic energy spectrum in regions where the densities of states are small, namely, near the band edges and in the band gap of the appropriate crystalline sample, see Fig. 1. Indeed, in the purely classical approach to the noise field adopted above, the momentum conservation requirement is not relaxed, giving rise to a non-zero threshold energy for the Auger process. It is well known that the Auger recombination rate is a rapidly decreasing function of the threshold energy. The appearance of density-of-state tails in the band gap seems to lead to an effective remarkable reduction of the threshold energy, enhancing strongly the Auger process.
