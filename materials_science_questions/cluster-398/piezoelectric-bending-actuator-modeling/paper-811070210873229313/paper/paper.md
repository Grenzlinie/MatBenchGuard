# ULTRASONIC POWER BALANCES—EFFECT OF A COUPLING WINDOW ON THE POWER MEASURED FROM PHYSIOTHERAPY ULTRASOUND UNITS

M. J. LUNT* and F. A. DUCK†

*Medical Physics Department, Dorset County Hospital, Dorchester, UK; and †Medical Physics Department, Royal United Hospital, Bath, UK

(Received 22 December 2000; in final form 25 April 2001)

Abstract—The output from physiotherapy ultrasound (US) probes is usually measured using a radiation force balance. Some balances are sealed and incorporate a membrane coupling window. The probes are highly resonant, and the output may be affected by changes in acoustic loading caused by the membrane. A model has been developed to examine this effect, and predicts that a polythene membrane 100-$\mu$m thick can change the output power by up to 17%. ## INTRODUCTION

Ultrasonic power balances are used to measure the power output from physiotherapy ultrasound (US) units. There have been different designs of balances (Wells 1977), all of them containing a force-measuring device immersed in fluid, usually water. When the incident US is reflected or absorbed by the device, there is a force proportional to the incident power, total absorption causing a force of $6.7 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}\ \text{W}^{-1}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$ (Wells 1977). Balances can be calibrated to read the incident power. In many balances, the force-measuring device moves and the amount of the deflection is measured. More recent designs (Perkins 1989) incorporate an electromagnetic device to provide a restoring force, and the current required to maintain equilibrium is measured. This can provide increased sensitivity. The design and accuracy of radiation force balances is discussed in European Standard EN 61161 (1994).

The coupling of the probe of a physiotherapy US unit to the fluid surrounding the balance can be achieved either by immersing the probe in the fluid or by pressing the probe against a thin membrane that separates the probe from the fluid and provides a coupling window. Coupling gel is used to ensure good ultrasonic trans- mission. The second method has the advantage that the balance can be a sealed unit, but the probe is highly resonant with the output tuned to the resonance fre- quency, so it is possible that the membrane impedance will alter the acoustic load on the probe and, therefore, alter the emitted power. This is recognized in the European Standard EN 61161 (1994), which states “For precision measurements, the ultrasonic trans- ducer should be coupled directly to the measurement liquid to avoid an impedance transformation by an additional coupling foil. Avoiding the impedance transformation caused by the addition of a coupling foil is particularly important in measurements on high-Q ultrasonic transducers.” Many hospitals con- tinue to use balances with membranes because of ease of transportation and it is, therefore, important to know the effect of a membrane.

This paper describes a model that has been devel- oped to predict the power emitted from the probe of a physiotherapy US unit, and uses the model to quantify the effect of a coupling membrane. The model was developed following anecdotal reports of systematic dif- ferences of about 10%, maximum 30%, when the power from a physiotherapy unit was measured using two dif- ferent calibrated balances (P. ### Model of a piezoelectric transmitter
The ultrasonic waves generated by a piezoelectric transmitter can be described using the longitudinal displacement from equilibrium $(\xi)$ caused by the ultrasonic wave. The simplest model (Fig. 1) is a thin disk of polycrystalline piezoelectric material transmitting into two media, one each side of the disk. The longitudinal displacements in medium 1 $(\xi_{1})$, the circular disk $(\xi_{c})$, and medium 2 $(\xi_{2})$, are assumed to be of the form

$$
\xi_{1}=A_{1} \exp (i \omega t+i k_{1} x) \tag{1}
$$

$$
\xi_{c}=B \exp (i \omega t-i k x)+C \exp (i \omega t+i k x) \tag{2}
$$

$$
\xi_{2}=A_{2} \exp (i \omega t-i k_{2} x), \tag{3}
$$

where $k=2 \pi / \lambda$.

The ultrasonic waves are generated by applying a sinusoidal voltage across the thickness of the disk. The piezoelectric properties of the disk are described by various constants, in particular $\alpha$ and $h$, where the tensile stress $T$ in the disk is given by:

$$
T=\alpha \partial \xi_{c} / \partial x-h D \exp (i \omega t) \tag{4}
$$

where $\alpha$ and $h$ are piezoelectric constants of the disk and $D$ is the electrical displacement in the disk.

The excess pressure $P$ in each of the media is given by

$$
P=-\rho_{n} c_{n}{ }^{2} \partial \xi / \partial x \tag{5}
$$

where $\rho_{n}$ is the density of medium $n$ and $c_{n}$ is the speed of US in the medium $n$.

The model assumes that the longitudinal displacement and the pressure are continuous across the boundaries between the disk and the media. Hence, equating displacement gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text { At } x=0, \quad A_{1} \exp (i \omega t)=B \exp (i \omega t)+C \exp (i \omega t)
\tag{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text { At } x=d, \quad B \exp (i \omega t-i k_{c} d) &+C \exp (i \omega t+i k_{c} d) \\
&=A_{2} \exp (i \omega t-i k_{2} d). \tag{7}
\end{aligned}
$$

Equating pressures in the media to $-T$ in the disk gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text { At } x=0,-\rho_{1} c_{1}{ }^{2} A_{1} i k_{1} \exp (i \omega t) &=-T \\
&=\alpha B i k_{c} \exp (i \omega t) \\
&-\alpha C i k_{c} \exp (i \omega t)+h D \exp (i \omega t) \quad(8)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text { At } x=d, k_{2} i \rho_{2} c_{2}{ }^{2} A_{2} \exp (i \omega t-i k_{2} d) & \\
&=\alpha i B k_{c} \exp (i \omega t-i k_{c} d) \\
&-\alpha i C k_{c} \exp (i \omega t+i k_{c} d)+h D \exp (i \omega t). \quad(9)
\end{aligned}
$$

The voltage $V$ across the disk and the current $I$ through the disk are given by:

$$
V \exp (i \omega t)=-h\left[\xi_{c}\right]_{0}^{d}+d D \exp (i \omega t) / K \tag{10}
$$

$$
I \exp (i \omega t)=S i \omega D \exp (i \omega t), \tag{11}
$$

where $K$ is the permittivity at constant strain, $S$ is the disk area, and $[\xi_{c}]_{0}^{d}$ is the displacement $(\xi_{c})$ at $x=d$ minus the displacement $(\xi_{c})$ at $x=0$.

From eqns (6) to (11), the coefficients $A_{1}, B, C$ and $A_{2}$ can be eliminated and the electrical impedance $Z$ of the disk is given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z=V / I=d /(i \omega S K) &+h^{2}(2 \cos \beta-2 \\
+ & \left(m_{1}+m_{2}\right) i \sin \beta) /(\alpha \omega \operatorname{SK}((m_{1}+m_{2}) \cos \beta \\
& \left.+\left(1+m_{1} m_{2}\right) i \sin \beta\right) \quad(12)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\beta=k d, m_{1}=\rho_{1} c_{1}{ }^{2} k_{1} / \alpha k$, and $m_{2}=\rho_{2} c_{2}{ }^{2} k_{2} / \alpha k$.

The first term is the impedance of a parallel plate capacitor of area $S$ and thickness $d$, and can be represented as $1 / i \omega C$. The remaining terms describe the resonance of the disk, which occurs when the disk thickness $(d)$ is equal to half a wavelength. At resonance, $\beta=\pi$, and the resonance frequency $\omega_{0}$ is given by:

$$
\omega_{0}=c \pi / d. \tag{13}
$$

### Validation of the simple model
This simple model was validated by comparing the predicted impedance with that measured on a disk of PC4, a lead zirconate titanate originally supplied by Unilator Technical Ceramics, Wrexham, UK. The disk was held loosely in a clamp on the surface of a water tank, so that one face radiated into water and the other into air (Fig. 3). The impedance was measured by connecting a resistance in series with the disk, and measuring the amplitudes and relative phase of the voltages across the disk and the resistance at different frequencies (Fig. 4). Real and imaginary components were extracted, and $I(Y)$ plotted against $R(Y)$ (Fig. 5). The points lie close to a circle as predicted by the theory. The deviation from a circle occurring at $I(Y) = 0.15$ and $R(Y) = 0.15$ is caused by an additional resonance within the disk, possibly a harmonic of a lower frequency radial mode.

The effect of membranes on power balances

The model was then used to investigate the effect of a polythene membrane on the power emitted from a physiotherapy US unit. To calculate the power emitted, a sinusoidal drive voltage of amplitude $V$ volts and fre- quency $\omega$ is assumed. The ultrasonic power emitted $(P)$ can be expressed in terms of the applied voltage $(V)$ and the probe impedance $(Z)$.

$$
P = V^2R(Z)/(2|Z|^2) \tag{14}
$$

where $R(Z)$ is the real part of $Z$ and $|Z|$ is the modulus of $Z$.

The dimensions and resonance frequency of the Therasonic probe were used, and the disk was assumed to be air-backed and made of PC4. The amplitude of the driving voltage was assumed constant at 1 volt. The results show that the presence of a membrane can have a significant effect, increasing or decreasing the power, depending on whether the driving frequency is below or above the resonance frequency.

The effect of the membrane can be quantified by calculating the percentage change in output at each frequency. The largest change occurs at 1.0820 MHz and corresponds to a 17% increase. The largest reduction is −14%, occurring at 1.0995 MHz. This demonstrates that the polythene membrane of the balance can have a significant effect on the power output.

The membrane thickness will be different on different balances, but is usually between 50 and 100 μm. The model was used to examine the effect of membrane thickness, and showed that the predicted changes in output are nearly linear with membrane thickness from 0 μm to 140 μm.

### Experimental studies

The probe from the Therasonic physiotherapy US unit was investigated experimentally to determine changes in output. Output power was measured using an ultrasonic power balance (Perkins 1989). The front face of the probe was either immersed in the water of the balance or was separated by a 61-μm thick polythene membrane. It proved difficult to obtain consistent results, especially with the membrane present, because a thin layer of coupling medium was needed between the stainless-steel front face of the probe and the membrane. Because the balance was sealed, it was not possible to push the membrane flat against the front of the probe, and pushing the probe against the membrane caused the membrane to bow, trapping a variable thickness of gel between the probe and the membrane. However, the results showed that the membrane reduced the output power above resonance and possibly increased power slightly below resonance, consistent with the theoretical predictions (Fig. 10).

Losses will damp any resonances and, therefore, the power output curves would be flatter. This, in turn, is likely to reduce the effect of a membrane on the output power.

The type of piezoelectric disk will also affect the predictions. The modeling above assumed the disk to be PC4. Table 2 lists properties of other transducer materials and gives the predicted maximum percentage change in power output when a 100-$\mu$m membrane is inserted in front of a probe with a disk of each material. The effect is similar with all the materials, giving a maximum increase of 16% to 18% and a maximum decrease of 14% to 15%. This suggests that the effect of the membrane is insensitive to the properties of these piezoelectric materials.

The electrical circuit used to drive the probe will also have an effect. The model assumes that the probe is driven at a constant voltage and that this is applied directly to the probe. However, the impedance of the probe near to resonance has a capacitative component due to the parallel plate capacitance of the disk. Higher output for a given voltage can be obtained by using an inductance in series with the probe to cancel the capacitance and leave a purely resistive impedance at resonance. The effect of tuning the probe in this way was investigated using the PC4 probe model. There was a slight increase in maximum output, but the effect of the membrane was unchanged.

## CONCLUSIONS

This analysis has demonstrated that a thin polythene membrane can significantly alter the output from a probe of the type used on physiotherapy US units. For a probe with no losses, the theoretical maximum difference caused by a 100-$\mu$m membrane is typically 17%. Comparison with actual probes is difficult without information on the type of piezoelectric material used, the dimensions of the different elements of the probe and the electrical driving circuit used. The model suggests that the maximum percentage difference is insensitive to the piezoelectric material and to the driving circuit, but losses in the probe are likely to reduce the effect of a membrane. The membranes used on ultrasonic balances are typically 60- to 100-$\mu$m thick, so the effect is likely to be significant.
