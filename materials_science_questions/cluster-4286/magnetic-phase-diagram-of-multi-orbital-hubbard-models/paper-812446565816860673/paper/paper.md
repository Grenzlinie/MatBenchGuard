# INTERACTING VIRTUAL STATE APPROACH TO THE MAGNETISM OF TRANSITION METALS-Cr and FeHr-

Y. TERAOKA and J. KANAMORI*
College of General Education, University of Osaka Prefecture, Sakai 591 and *Department of Physics, Osaka University, Toyonaka 560, Japan

A new theory based on the Anderson model of 3d virtual states is developed to discuss the magnetism of transition metals from the point of view unifying the concept of a localized moment with that of itinerant electrons. It explains quantitatively various features of experimental data on the spin density wave in Cr, and leads us to a new view of the mechanism of the formation of the SDW. It can elucidate also the mechanism underlying the transition from the antiferromagnetic state to the ferromagnetic one in FeRh.

## 1. Introduction

We propose here a localized moment treat- ment of the magnetically ordered states of a transition metal which corresponds to an ex- tension of the Alexander-Anderson-Moriya theory of the exchange interaction between two transition element magnetic atoms [1]. We treat the inter-atomic electron transfer as a pertur- bation on the 3d virtual bound state of each atom in the one-electron approximation; we determine the 3d levels in the Hartree-Fock scheme to minimize the total energy including the perturbation energy of the electron transfer. The magnitude of the magnetic moments and the effective exchange integral of their in- teraction thus calculated depend on the relative orientation of the moments, reflecting the itinerant character of d electrons.

After discussing the formalism in section 2, we present an application to the spin density wave (SDW) in Cr in section 3. It is generally believed that the SDW is generated by the nes- ting of the Fermi surfaces around the $\Gamma$ and H points in reciprocal space. The commonly used model which considers only the states near the nesting Fermi surfaces explicitly, however, seems to be inadequate for discussing the SDW quantitatively, since the calculated SDW am- plitude is too small compared with the observed value unless one adds a contribution from a not well defined "electron reservoir" [2]. This fact is consistent with Windsor's calculation of the un- enhanced $q$-dependent susceptibility to which the nesting states prove to contribute only an eighth at maximum [3]. Our approach, on the other hand, yields good quantitative agreement with the data on the SDW first and third har- monics [4], the charge density wave (CDW) [5], the magnetic moment of the antiferromagnetic(AF) state at the SDW-to-AF transition in Cr- Mn alloys [6], the volume change at the same transition [7] and the strain wave [8]; we shall not discuss the last two subjects in this paper. We can explain also the first order transition at $T_{N}$. A new interpretation of the mechanism un- derlying the SDW formation in Cr will be given as a conclusion.

In section 4 we discuss the FeRh ordered alloy of the CsCl type which undergoes a tran- sition from the AF to the ferromagnetic (F) state with increasing temperature. The Rh atom has no magnetic moment in the AF and acquires about $1\ \mu_{B}$ in the F, while the Fe atom has about $3\ \mu_{B}$ in both phases [9]. We present a micro- scopic theory of the AF-to-F transition and also the magnetization process of the AF state under an external field at low temperatures, calculating the electronic structure by use of the present approach. In section 5 we discuss briefly various elaborations of the approach, since we assume the simplest version of it in the preceding sec- tions.

## 2. Formalism

In this paper we assume the single orbital Anderson model with a constant virtual level half-width $\Delta$ to describe the unperturbed state. The perturbation expansion of the one-electron Green function to the second order of the inter- atomic electron transfer yields the local state density of electrons with spin $\sigma$ at the $j$th atom given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{j \sigma}(\epsilon)=\rho_{j \sigma}^{0}(\epsilon)+\sum_{l} V_{l}^{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{j+l \sigma}^{0}(\epsilon)-\rho_{j \sigma}^{0}(\epsilon)}{\left(E_{j+l \sigma}-E_{j \sigma}\right)^{2}}\right. & \\
& \left.+\frac{\partial \rho_{j \sigma}^{0}(\epsilon) / \partial \epsilon}{E_{j+l \sigma}-E_{j \sigma}}\right), \quad(1)
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\rho_{j \sigma}^{0}$ representing the lorentzian centered at the Hartree-Fock atomic level $E_{j \sigma}$.
$$
\rho_{j \sigma}^{0}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=(\Delta / \pi) /\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-E_{j \sigma}\right)^{2}+\Delta^{2}\right\}.\qquad(2)
$$

Here $\epsilon$ is the energy variable, $V_{l}$ is the average transfer integral to the neighbors specified by $l$ and $E_{j \sigma}$ is to be determined self-consistently to satisfy
$$
E_{j \sigma}=E_{j}+U n_{j \quad \sigma}.\qquad(3)
$$

In this $E_{j}$ represents the energy level in the absence of the Coulomb interaction between electrons; $n_{j \sigma}$ is the average number of electrons with spin $-\sigma$ which is obtained by integrating $\rho_{j \sigma}(\epsilon)$ given by eq. (1) up to the Fermi energy at absolute zero.

In the following calculation we take account of the transfer to the nearest and next nearest neighbors only, specifying the corresponding transfer integrals by $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$, respectively. We assume $V_{1} / \Delta=0.32$ and $V_{2} / \Delta=0.12$ unless otherwise specified; the ratio $V_{2} / V_{1}$ is in good agreement with that obtained from the averages of Pettifor's parameters of the tight binding calculation of the bcc d band [10]. We assume that the expansion to second order in the $V$ values is quantitatively meaningful in a bcc metal in the calculation of those quantities which do not depend on the details of the energy spectrum. In fact the state density of the nonmagnetic (NM) state calculated by use of eq.(1) reproduces well the two peak structure ofthe d band in a bcc metal, as is shown in fig. $1.^{*}$

Integrating eq. (1) to the Fermi energy $\epsilon_{F}$ , weobtain self-consistent equations for the $n_{j \sigma}$  values. $\epsilon_{F}$ is determined by the condition that the average number of electrons per atom cal- culated from the $n_{j \sigma}$ values should agree with a given value, $N_{d}$ . The self-consistent equations are solved numerically by an iterative method. The above mentioned $N_{d}$ and the Coulomb in tegral parameter $U / \Delta \dagger$ are varied as parameters to find the most appropriate situation for the problem under consideration.

## 3. The spin density wave in $Cr$ 
We assume in the calculation of the SDW that the period in units of the lattice constant is an integer $p$ with the wave vector pointing in the[001] direction. Furthermore, the nodes of the envelope of atomic moments are assumed to be halfway between two adjacent (001) atomic planes, as is illustrated in fig. $2 \ddagger$ . Since atoms on the same (001) plane are in the same state, wedetermine in the self-consistent calculation $n_{j \sigma}$  values of atoms for one-quarter of the period specified, making use of the symmetry of the solution shown in fig. 2 with respect to the loop of the envelope. The equations are solved by iteration with arbitrarily chosen initial values of the $n_{j \sigma}$ ; various choices of the initial values are adopted to see that they converge to the same final solution after iteration. The calculation of the AF, F and nonmagnetic (NM) states can be carried out in a much sim- pler way. Assuming different quantization axes of spin for atoms and modifying eq. (1) ap- propriately, we can obtain the helical (H) solu- tion as well. When $U$ is not too large, we can obtain the condition for the appearance of a magnetic solution of a given $p$ with infinitesimal magnetic amplitude by expanding the self-con- sistent equations in powers of deviation of each $n_{j\sigma}$ from its value in the NM state, $N_{d}/10$. We confine ourselves here to the case $N_{d}\geq5$, be- cause this case yields the observed phase rela- tion between the SDW and the charge density wave (CDW) [8]. Then we find that the magnetic solution is lower in energy than the NM for $N_{d}$ larger than a critical value $N_{c}$; $N_{c}$ turns out to decrease with increasing $p$, being smallest for the AF. $N_{c}$ values for the SDW and H for the same $p$ coincide with each other. $N_{c}$ decreases with increasing $U$. When $U$ is sufficiently large, the magnetic solution with finite amplitude be- comes energetically equal to the NM at a critical value of $N_{d}$ when $N_{d}$ is increased from $N_{d}=5$. This critical value which we call again $N_{c}$ can be determined by energy comparison only. Thus the range of $N_{c}$ is divided into the second order regime corresponding to larger $N_{c}$ for smaller $U$ and the first order regime corresponding to $N_{c}$ close to 5 for larger $U$. As is discussed below, the SDW can be of lowest energy for such a choice of $U$. We believe that the first order nature of the transition at the Néel tem- perature in Cr is related to this first order ap- pearance of the magnetic solution; we shall re- port a calculation at finite temperatures in the future. The first order appearance is due to the presence of the deep valley at the middle of the state density of the NM shown in fig. 1. As is well known, we can expect the first order tran- sition when the Fermi level is near a minimum of the state density.

Fig. 3 shows also the energies of the SDW, AF and H states plotted against $N_{d}$, from which we can see that the SDW is of lowest energy in a limited range of $N_{d}$ with the amplitude around $0.4\ \mu_{\text{B}}$ per atom. By varying $U$, we find that the SDW can be the ground state for $U/\Delta=4.3\sim$ 4.5; $N_{c}$ values of the SDW and AF are around the boundary of the first order regime for such choices of $U/\Delta$. The energy of the H state, on the other hand, remains always higher than that of the AF state. It is difficult within the adopted accuracy of numerical solution to determine precisely the period $p$ of the SDW of lowest energy, though the $p$ value of lowest energy seems to be around 20 with the tendency to decrease first and increase after passing a mini- mum with increasing $N_{d}$. An example of the envelope of the SDW whose energy is lower than the AF state is shown in fig. 4. When $N_{d}$ or $U$ is increased beyond the range where the SDW is of lowest energy, the envelope becomes more rectangular, or in other words, approaches the antiferromagnetic state with periodic anti- phase boundaries.

The numerical solution obtained above is con- sistent with various experimental data. The magnitude of the magnetic moment in the AF state is shown to be almost equal to the am- plitude of the first harmonic of the SDW for a given $N_{d}$ except for $N_{d}$ near $N_{c}$ (see fig. 3). This result is in good agreement with the ex- perimental data on Cr-Mn alloys which show the SDW-to-AF transition [6], while the nesting model seems to fail to explain it [2]. The mechanism which lowers the energy of the SDW relative to the AF is a compromise between the two antiferromagnetic couplings of the nearest and next nearest neighbors which is achieved with the help of the CDW formation. Noting that the coupling of the next nearest neighbors is sacrificed in the AF, we may expect that the spatial modulation of the atomic magnetic moment can lower the SDW energy. In fact we find that by varying $V_{2}$ the range of $N_{d}$ where the SDW is lower in energy than the AF tends to disappear for smaller $V_{2}$. On the other hand, the H state where one might expect a similar compromise without CDW cannot be lower in energy than the AF in the present calculation, which is consistent with the well-known fact in the case of the Heisenberg model of the bcc lattice. In the SDW the associated CDW decreases the number of electrons at nearly nonmagnetic atoms around the node and increases that at atoms around the loop. The fact that we find the SDW ground state at $N_{d}$ near the boundary of the first order appearance regime means that a slight decrease of the electron number around the node can reduce the energy increase caused by the presence of nearly nonmagnetic atoms, since a smaller number of electrons favors the NM. Also the fact that the SDW spatial modulation can contain the higher harmonics appropriately contributes to the energy lowering. Altogether we can understand qualitatively the reason why we find the SDW ground state in a rather limited range of the choices of parameters. If we are well inside the first order regime with large $U$, for example, the spatial modulation becomes difficult because of a well developed atomic magnetic moment in the AF.

When we take note of the volume change at the SDW-to-AF transition, we should consider the redistribution of electrons between the d states and the s band caused by a change of the Fermi energy. We can show, however, that it does not make an appreciable contribution to the energy difference between the SDW and the AF discussed above. We shall not go into the discussion of this subject and also that of the relation between the present theory and the nesting model theory. We do not intend at present to deny the possibility that the nesting of the Fermi surface plays an important role in determining the period of the SDW. Our theory, on the other hand, yields a quantitatively good description of the SDW and a reasonable explanation for the fact that we find the SDW in Cr only among the transition metals.

### 4. The AF-to-F transition in ordered FeRh alloy

We assume here the CsCl type ordering of Fe and Rh atoms with equal concentration, deferring the case of nonstoichiometry to future publication. Our calculation shows within reasonable choices of parameters that Rh atoms have no localized moment in the AF state, while Fe atoms have a moment of about $3\ \mu_{B}$. It shows that the number of electrons of Fe atoms is decreased in FeRh to the extent that the interaction between Fe atoms is antiferromagnetic. The interaction between Fe and Rh atoms, on the other hand, favors the ferromagnetism. We discuss the magnetization process of the AF state at absolute zero, in order to elucidate the situation.

Assuming that the AF state is the ground state, we apply an external field $H$ to it. Then the sublattice magnetizations of Fe atoms and the Rh magnetization take the configuration shown in fig. 5 in the absence of anisotropy. We denote Fe atoms on the sublattice 1 by Fe1.

Taking the spin quantization axis for each sublattice in the direction of its magnetization, we add the Zeeman energy $-\sigma H \mu_{\mathrm{B}} \cos \theta$ and $-\sigma H \mu_{\mathrm{B}}$ with $\sigma= \pm 1$ to $E_{i \sigma}$ given by eq. (3) for $\mathrm{Fe}$ and $\mathrm{Rh}$ atoms, respectively; $\theta$ is defined in fig. 5. $n_{i \sigma}$ in eq. (3) is now the number of electrons with spin $\sigma$ referred to the abovementioned quantization axis; we may assume $n_{\mathrm{FeI} \sigma}=n_{\mathrm{FeII} \sigma}$ and hence $E_{\mathrm{FeI} \sigma}=E_{\mathrm{FeII} \sigma}$. We note that the electron transfer between $\sigma$ spin states and $-\sigma$ ones is allowed between FeI and FeII and also between $\mathrm{Fe}$ and $\mathrm{Rh}$. Calculating the total energy to the second order of the electron transfer, we determine the angle $\theta$ by the torque balance condition, $\mathrm{d} E / \mathrm{d} \theta=0$, given by

$$
E_{z} \sin \theta+E_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Rh}} \frac{1}{2}(\sin \theta)+E_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Fe}} \sin 2 \theta=0, \quad(4)
$$

$$
E_{z}=-\left(n_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}-n_{\mathrm{Fe}-\sigma}\right) \mu_{\mathrm{B}} H, \quad(5)
$$

$$
E_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Rh}}=8 V_{1}^{2} \sum_{\sigma}\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{Rh} \sigma}^{0}-n_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}^{0}}{E_{\mathrm{Rh} \sigma}-E_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}}-\frac{n_{\mathrm{Rh}-\sigma}^{0}-n_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}^{0}}{E_{\mathrm{Rh}-\sigma}-E_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}}\right),
$$

$$
E_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Fe}}=3 V_{2}^{2} \sum_{\sigma}\left(-\rho_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}^{0}\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\right)-\frac{n_{\mathrm{Fe}-\sigma}^{0}-n_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}^{0}}{E_{\mathrm{Fe}-\sigma}-E_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}}\right), \quad(7)
$$

where $n_{\mathrm{Fe} \sigma}^{0}$ is the quantity obtained by integrating $\rho_{\mathrm{Fe}}^{0}$ to the Fermi energy. The first term in eq. (4) is the torque due to the external field; $E_{\mathrm{ex}}$ for the second and third terms may be called the "exchange energies" for Fe-Rh and Fe-Fe. The above expressions for $E_{\mathrm{ex}}$ agree with those derived by Moriya [1], though our $E_{\mathrm{ex}}$ varies with the change of the magnetic configuration with increasing $H^{*}$. We note that the variation of $E_{\mathrm{ex}}$ represents the deviation from the Heisenberg model in which it is a constant. We can easily extend the above discussion to finite temperatures within the one-electron approximation. ## 5. Concluding remarks
We have presented two applications of our "interacting virtual states" approach in its simplest version. An obvious elaboration of the calculation is to distinguish between the $d \epsilon$ and d y orbitals together with the inclusion of the inter-orbital exchange interaction in the Anderson model. We have to introduce then three kinds of transfer integrals, $dd \sigma, dd \pi$ and $dd \pi^{\prime}$. We can take into account also the higher order terms in the perturbation expansion of the transfer, which we carry out on the basis of the pseudo-Greenian formalism [12]. The presence of the s band, or in other words the free electron branch, must be taken into account in some problems, as was mentioned at the end of section 3.

Apart from these elaborations we can apply the present theory to several problems where we need to unify the concept of localized moment with that of itinerant electrons. Among them the easiest one is the calculation of the spin wave stiffness in a ferromagnetic metal. We have made also an application to the surface magnetism of transition metals. We report on these problems as well as the details of the present study in future publication.
