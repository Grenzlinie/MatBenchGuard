# The stability and thermodynamic behaviour of spin-density waves
## I. Hartree-Fock theory

M. J. MORRIS† and J. F. CORNWELL‡

Mathematics Department, University of Leeds
MS. received 8th February 1968, in revised form 11th March 1968

Abstract. A detailed investigation has been made of the application of the Hartree-Fock approximation to the stability and thermodynamic behaviour of spin-density waves in a model with a single electronic energy band and a delta-function-type interaction between electrons. It is shown that for a half-filled band the ground state at zero temperature is always a spin-density wave, provided only that the width of the band is sufficiently small. For other bands the conditions for the relative stability of the spin-density wave, ferromagnetic and paramagnetic states are more complicated, and are studied in detail for a special band form. For a half-filled band the energy per atom and specific heat have been calculated as functions of temperature for the spin-density wave, ferromagnetic and paramagnetic states. The temperature dependence of the amplitude for the spin-density wave and ferromagnetic states, and of the entropy for the spin-density wave and paramagnetic states have also been calculated.

The formulation of the problem has been made using a Green function method in order to allow the development in a later paper of a higher-order approximation to the correlation problem.

## 1. Introduction

Ever since the original paper by Overhauser (1960) suggesting the existence of static spin-density waves there has been much debate on their stability, some of which will be referred to later. All of these discussions have been within the framework of the Hartree-Fock approximation. The principal aim of the present investigation has been to see to what extent the conclusions reached must be modified in a higher-order approximation of the correlation problem.

The higher-order approximation that has been studied is a generalization of that considered for ferromagnetism by Hubbard (1963). The ferromagnetic state may be regarded as a limiting case of a spin-density wave with zero wave factor, and in this limiting case the present approximation reduces to that of Hubbard. In order to carry the analysis through, it has been necessary to consider a particular electronic energy band structure, which differs from any considered previously in this context. To compare the Hartree-Fock approximation with the higher-order approximation, both were studied in detail for this band structure. This paper is devoted to the study of the Hartree-Fock approximation, the results of which appear to be of considerable interest in their own right, while the higher approximation is analysed in the following paper (Morris and Cornwell 1968, to be referred to as II).

A somewhat similar comparison of a higher-order correlation theory with a Hartree-Fock theory has been carried out by Hewson (1965, 1966) in the localized magnetic states problem, the Hartree-Fock theory in question being that of Anderson (1961).

Although most of the detailed results refer specifically to the special band structure, the formulation of the problem in $\S \S 2$ and 3 and the theorem proved in $\S 4$ apply for any single electronic energy band. The formulation of $\S \S 2$ and 3 involves the application of Green functions, but it is of course possible to derive the Hartree-Fock equations without introducing Green functions. The advantage of the Green function derivation is that it can be generalized to higher-order approximations, as in II.

## 2. Basic theory

The model of an antiferromagnetic metal that will be used here is the same as that model of a ferromagnetic metal which has recently been closely studied, particularly by Hubbard (1963). The Hamiltonian operator $H$ is assumed to be

$$
H = \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \sum_{\sigma} E(\mathbf{k}) a_{\mathbf{k} \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} a_{\mathbf{k} \sigma}+I \sum_{j} b_{j+}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j+} b_{j-}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j-}.
$$

Here $a_{\mathbf{k} \sigma}{ }^{\dagger}$, $a_{\mathbf{k} \sigma}$ are creation and annihilation operators for a Bloch state with quasi-momentum $\mathbf{k}$ and spin $\sigma$ ($\sigma = +$ or $-$), whose single-particle energy is $E(\mathbf{k})$, and $b_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger}$, $b_{j \sigma}$ are the corresponding operators for the Wannier state centred on lattice vector $\mathbf{R}_{j}$. $I$ is the interaction integral corresponding to the Coulomb interaction between electrons in Wannier states centred on the same lattice site. The neglect of all other interaction integrals corresponds to the replacement of the Coulomb interaction by a delta-function interaction.

The quantum-mechanical statistical average $\langle A \rangle$, the retarded anticommutator Green function $\ll A(t_{1}); B(t_{2}) \gg$, its equation of motion, its Fourier transform $G(E)$ and the relationship between $G(E)$ and $\langle BA \rangle$ are given in equations (2.2), (2.1b), (2.17), (3.10b) and (3.28) respectively of the review article of Zubarev (1960). The only difference in notation here is that the chemical potential will be denoted by $p$.

The components $S_{j}^{x}$, $S_{j}^{y}$, $S_{j}^{z}$ of the spin operator $\mathbf{S}_{j}$ for an electron in a Wannier state centred on the lattice site $\mathbf{R}_{j}$ are given (in units where $\frac{1}{2}\hbar = 1$) by

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
S_{j}^{x} &= b_{j+}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j-}+b_{j-}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j+} \\
S_{j}^{y} &= -\mathrm{i}(b_{j+}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j-}-b_{j-}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j+}) \\
S_{j}^{z} &= b_{j+}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j+}-b_{j-}{ }^{\dagger} b_{j-}
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
$$

The helical spin configuration that will be considered in this work is such that

$$
\langle \mathbf{S}_{j} \rangle = \mu(\cos 2\theta_{j}, \sin 2\theta_{j}, 0)
$$

where

$$
\theta_{j} = \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{j}.
$$

Here $\mu$ denotes the amplitude and $2\mathbf{q}$ the wave vector of the spin-density wave. The special case $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{0}$ corresponds to ferromagnetism (but with the spins aligned along the $x$ axis instead of the conventional $z$ axis).

A set of operators $\alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger}$, $\alpha_{j \sigma}$ may be defined by

$$
\alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} = 2^{-1/2}\{b_{j+}{ }^{\dagger} \exp(-\mathrm{i}\theta_{j})+\sigma b_{j-}{ }^{\dagger} \exp(\mathrm{i}\theta_{j})\}
$$

and its adjoint. Here again $\sigma = +$ or $-$. These operators satisfy the anticommutation relations $[\alpha_{i \sigma}, \alpha_{j \sigma'}{ }^{\dagger}] = \delta_{ij}\delta_{\sigma \sigma'}$, and the spin configuration (2) corresponds simply to

$$
\mu = \sum_{\sigma} \sigma \langle \alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j \sigma} \rangle
$$

together with

$$
\langle \alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j \sigma'} \rangle = 0 \quad \text{for} \quad \sigma \neq \sigma'.
$$

It may be assumed that $\langle \alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j \sigma} \rangle$ are independent of $\mathbf{R}_{j}$, so that the number of electrons in the band per atom, $n$, defined by

$$
n = N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \sum_{\sigma} \langle a_{\mathbf{k} \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} a_{\mathbf{k} \sigma} \rangle
$$

is then also given by the simple expression

$$
n = \sum_{\sigma} \langle \alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j \sigma} \rangle.
$$

In terms of these new operators the Hamiltonian (1) becomes
$$
H=\sum_{i, j, \sigma}\left\{\nu_{i j} \alpha_{i \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j \sigma}+\lambda_{i j} \alpha_{i \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j-\sigma}\right\}+I \sum_{i} \alpha_{i+}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{i+} \alpha_{i-}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{i-}
\tag{6}
$$
where
$$
\nu_{i j}=N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \nu_{\mathbf{k}} \exp \left\{\mathrm{i} \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right)\right\}
$$

$$
\lambda_{i j}=N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \lambda_{\mathbf{k}} \exp \left\{\mathrm{i} \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right)\right\}
$$
and
$$
\nu_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\{E(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})+E(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\}
$$

$$
\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\{E(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})-E(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\}.
$$

For brevity let $A^{\sigma}=\left\langle\alpha_{j \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{j \sigma}\right\rangle$. Let us define the Green functions
$$
\left.\begin{array}{l}
X_{i j}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(t)=\left\langle\left\langle\alpha_{i \sigma}\left(t_{1}\right) ; \alpha_{j \sigma^{\prime}}{ }^{\dagger}\left(t_{2}\right)\right\rangle\right. \\
Y_{i j}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(t)=\left\langle\left\langle\alpha_{i \sigma}{ }^{\dagger}\left(t_{1}\right) \alpha_{i \sigma}\left(t_{1}\right) \alpha_{i-\sigma}\left(t_{1}\right) ; \alpha_{j \sigma^{\prime}}{ }^{\dagger}\left(t_{2}\right)\right\rangle\right.
\end{array}\right\}
\tag{7}
$$
where $t=t_{1}-t_{2}$. The Fourier transforms $X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)$ may be defined by
$$
X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)=\frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}} X_{i j}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(t) \exp \left\{\mathrm{i} E t+\mathrm{i} \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right)\right\} \mathrm{d} t
$$
with a similar definition holding for $Y_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)$. The equations of motion for the $X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)$ are then simply
$$
\left(E-\nu_{\mathbf{k}}+p\right) X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)=-\frac{1}{2 \pi} \delta_{\sigma \sigma^{\prime}}+\lambda_{\mathbf{k}} X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{-\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}+I Y_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{-\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}
\tag{8}
$$
where the zero of energy has been chosen so that
$$
\sum_{\mathbf{k}} E(\mathbf{k})=0.
\tag{9}
$$

The two required averages $A^{\sigma}$ follow from the two Green functions $X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma}(E)$, but their equations of motion involve all eight Green functions of (7).

### 3. The Hartree-Fock approximation

In terms of Green functions the Hartree-Fock approximation corresponds to the assumption that
$$
Y_{i j}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(t) \equiv\left\langle\alpha_{i \sigma}{ }^{\dagger}\left(t_{1}\right) \alpha_{i \sigma}\left(t_{1}\right) \alpha_{i-\sigma}\left(t_{1}\right) ; \alpha_{j \sigma^{\prime}}{ }^{\dagger}\left(t_{2}\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha_{i \sigma}{ }^{\dagger} \alpha_{i \sigma}\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle\alpha_{i-\sigma}\left(t_{1}\right) ; \alpha_{j \sigma^{\prime}}{ }^{\dagger}\left(t_{2}\right)\right\rangle\right.
$$
so that $Y_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)=A^{\sigma} X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{-\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)$. Equations (8) are then in closed form and may be solved to give $X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma^{\prime}}(E)$. From $X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma, \sigma}(E)$ it follows that
$$
A^{\sigma}=N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{+}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{-}\right)^{-1} \sum_{\sigma^{\prime}} \sigma^{\prime}\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma^{\prime}}-\nu_{\mathbf{k}}-I A^{-\sigma}\right) f\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma^{\prime}}\right)
\tag{10}
$$
where
$$
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma}=\nu_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{1}{2} I n+\sigma\left\{\left(\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)^{2}+\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{2}\right\}^{1 / 2}, \quad \sigma=+\text { or }-
\tag{11}
$$
and $f(\epsilon)$ is the Fermi-Dirac function defined by
$$
f(\epsilon)=[1+\exp \{\beta(\epsilon-p)\}]^{-1}.
$$

Thus from (3), (5) and (10), one obtains the two basic Hartree-Fock conditions for a spin-density wave, namely for $\mu \neq 0$
$$
-1=I N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \sum_{\sigma}\left\{(I \mu)^{2}+\left(2 \lambda_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-1 / 2} \sigma f\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma}\right)
\tag{12}
$$

and
$$
n=N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \sum_{\sigma} f\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma}\right). \tag{13}
$$

From $X_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma,-\sigma}(E)$ the initial assumptions (4) are regained.

The total energy per atom, obtained using (12), is
$$
N^{-1}\langle H\rangle=N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} \sum_{\sigma} \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma} f\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma}\right)-\frac{1}{4} I\left(n^{2}-\mu^{2}\right). \tag{14}
$$

Equations (12), (13) and (14) form the basis for the analysis of this paper. They are, of course, modified in the correlation theory of II.

In the limiting case of zero temperature that will be considered in $\S \S 4$ and 5, $f(\epsilon)=\theta(p-\epsilon), \theta(\epsilon)$ being the unit function.

### 4. Spin-density-wave stability for $n=1$

In this section it will be proved that in the Hartree-Fock approximation the ground state at zero temperature for a half-filled band gives always a spin-density wave, provided only that the width of the band is sufficiently small. The precise condition is that $4 \Delta<I$, where $\Delta$ is defined as the maximum value of $|E(\mathbf{k})|$, it being assumed as before that the zero of energy is such that (9) holds.

To prove this result it is sufficient to consider spin-density waves with small $|\mathbf{q}|$. Indeed, given any positive number $\epsilon$, a $\mathbf{q}$ can always be chosen so that $\epsilon>2\left|\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}\right| / I$ is satisfied for all $\mathbf{k}$ vectors. The equations of $\S 3$ may then be solved by neglecting terms of order $\epsilon^{4}$, or higher, to obtain the desired conclusion.

It follows from (11) that $\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{+}>\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}{ }^{-}$for all pairs of $\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{k}^{\prime}$, provided $2 \Delta \leqslant I \mu$. The chemical potential $p$ is then consistent with taking $\theta\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{+}\right)=0$ and $\theta\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{-}\right)=1$ for all $\mathbf{k}$, as then (13) is satisfied. Substitution for $\theta\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}{ }^{\sigma}\right)$ in (12) gives
$$
1=N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left\{\mu^{2}+\left(\frac{2 \lambda_{\mathbf{k}}}{I}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-1 / 2}
$$
as $\mu \neq 0$. Expansion of the right-hand side gives
$$
\mu=1-2 N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}}{I}\right)^{2}+\mathrm{O}\left(\epsilon^{4}\right) \tag{15}
$$
so that by (14) the Hartree-Fock energy per atom of the spin-density-wave state is
$$
E_{\mathrm{SDW}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}=-I N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}}{I}\right)^{2}+\mathrm{O}\left(\epsilon^{4}\right). \tag{16}
$$

Equations (15) and (16) are only useful for values of $\epsilon$ that are small relative to $\Delta / I$. For such values (15) gives $\mu \sim 1$, and it is evident that the assumption $2 \Delta \leqslant I \mu$ is then satisfied if $4 \Delta<I$.

The energy $E_{\mathrm{SDW}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$ must now be compared with $E_{\mathrm{F}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$, the Hartree-Fock energies of the ferromagnetic and paramagnetic states with $n=1$ respectively.

$E_{\mathrm{F}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$ is simply obtained from (16) by taking the limit $\mathbf{q} \rightarrow 0$, giving
$$
E_{\mathrm{F}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}=0
$$
as $\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}=0$ for all $\mathbf{k}$. Thus $E_{\mathrm{F}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}>E_{\mathrm{SDW}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$, so that the ferromagnetic state is unstable relative to a spin-density wave with small $\mathbf{q}$.

$E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$ can be obtained from (14) by putting $\mu=0$ and $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$, giving
$$
E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}=2 N^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} E(\mathbf{k}) \theta(E(\mathbf{k}))+\frac{1}{4} I
$$
so that $E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}} \geqslant-\Delta+\frac{1}{4} I$. Thus $E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}>E_{\mathrm{F}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}>E_{\mathrm{SDW}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$ provided $4 \Delta<I$.

An extension to cover the case $4\Delta \geqslant I$ is complicated by the fact that one can no longer confine attention to spin-density waves with small $|\mathbf{q}|$, but instead one must compare $E_{\mathrm{p}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$ with the energy of the most stable spin-density wave. The condition $4\Delta < I$ is therefore merely a sufficient condition for stability.

Unfortunately the result proved above implies nothing about the case $n \neq 1$. The spin-density-wave stability considered in this section arises both from exchange and Umklapp effects. The work of Yoshimori (1961) demonstrates that exchange effects are not sufficient alone to induce spin-density-wave stability. Moreover, the correlation effects that result from corrections to the Hartree-Fock theory tend to reduce the exchange effects. This suggests that stable spin-density waves are most likely to be found in nearly half-filled bands, where Umklapp effects are dominant.

### 5. Spin-density-wave stability for $n \neq 1$

In order to investigate spin-density-wave stability for $n \neq 1$ it is necessary to be specific about the band structure, and it is essential to include Umklapp effects.

One possible model band structure is that corresponding to free electrons, in which $E(\mathbf{k})=\hbar^{2} \mathbf{k}^{2} / 2 m$ for $\mathbf{k}$ lying within the first Brillouin zone. For $\mathbf{k}$ outside the first Brillouin zone including Umklapp effects implies evaluating $E(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{K})$ at an equivalent point inside the zone. This model in the Hartree-Fock approximation has been investigated by Yoshimori (1961) (with Umklapp neglected), and by Tachiki and Nagamiya (1963), Nagamiya et al. (1965), Fukuda et al. (1965) and Penn and Cohen (1967). However, because of the difficulty of dealing with Umklapp processes with this structure, particularly in higher approximations than the Hartree-Fock theory, it is desirable to work with a simpler structure. One such possibility is that for which
$$
E(\mathbf{k})=-\Delta \cos \left(a k_{x}\right) \cos \left(a k_{y}\right) \cos \left(a k_{z}\right)\tag{17}
$$
when Umklapp effects are taken automatically into account. However, the three-dimensional integrals involving functions of $E(\mathbf{k})$ can then only be evaluated by very difficult numerical calculation.

To avoid this latter difficulty the following idealized band, which incorporates the desirable features of (17), was investigated. For this band
$$
E(\mathbf{k})=\Delta g\left(\epsilon_{1}, \frac{2 k_{x} a}{\pi}\right) g\left(\epsilon_{2}, \frac{2 k_{y} a}{\pi}\right) g\left(\epsilon_{3}, \frac{2 k_{z} a}{\pi}\right)\tag{18}
$$
for $-\pi / 2 a < k_{x}, k_{y}, k_{z} < \pi / 2 a$, where
$$
g(\epsilon, z)=\left\{\begin{aligned}
-1+\epsilon(4 z-1), & & |z| < \frac{1}{2} \\
1+\epsilon(4 z-3), & & \frac{1}{2} < |z| < 1.
\end{aligned}\right.
$$

This band has been constructed to satisfy (9) and provides a crude approximation to the form of (17).

Hartree-Fock solutions for the limiting case $\epsilon_{i} \rightarrow 0$ ($i=1,2,3$) in (18), but for arbitrary values of $\mathbf{q}$ and $n$, will now be obtained. Because of the electron-hole symmetry of the equations of $\S 3$, the discussion can be limited to the values $n \leqslant 1$.

The $\mathbf{k}$ vectors in the first Brillouin zone can be uniquely assigned to one of three classes A, B and C by the prescription
$$
\left.\begin{array}{lllll}
\mathbf{k} \in \mathrm{A} & \text { if } & \lim v_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta & \text { and } & \lim \lambda_{\mathbf{k}}=0 \\
\mathbf{k} \in \mathrm{B} & \text { if } & \lim v_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Delta & \text { and } & \lim \lambda_{\mathbf{k}}=0 \\
\mathbf{k} \in \mathrm{C} & \text { if } & \lim v_{\mathbf{k}}=0 & \text { and } & \lim \left|\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}\right|=\Delta
\end{array}\right\}
$$
where lim denotes the limit as $\epsilon_{i} \rightarrow 0, i=1,2,3$. The populations of these classes will be denoted by $N_{\mathrm{A}}, N_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{C}}$. They do of course depend on $\mathbf{q}$. It follows immediately that $N_{\mathrm{A}}+N_{\mathrm{B}}+N_{\mathrm{C}}=N$ and $N_{\mathrm{A}}=N_{\mathrm{B}}$. To calculate these numbers explicitly, let $\lambda_{i}=2 a q_{i} / \pi$ ($i=1,2,3$) where $\mathbf{q}=\left(q_{1}, q_{2}, q_{3}\right)$ may by symmetry be restricted to the

positive octant of the first Brillouin zone, which is assumed to be cubic. Then $0 \leqslant \lambda_{i} \leqslant 1$, and
$$
N_{\mathrm{A}}=N_{\mathrm{B}}=\frac{1}{2} N(1-\lambda), \quad N_{\mathrm{C}}=N \lambda
$$
where
$$
\lambda \equiv \lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}-2 \lambda_{1} \lambda_{2}-2 \lambda_{2} \lambda_{3}-2 \lambda_{3} \lambda_{1}+3 \lambda_{1} \lambda_{2} \lambda_{3}
$$
so that $0 \leqslant \lambda \leqslant 1$. The value $\lambda=0$ corresponds to the ferromagnetic state with $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$, while $\lambda=1$ corresponds to the state in which $\left\langle\mathbf{S}_{j}\right\rangle=\mu(-1)^{n_{1}}(1,0,0)$, where $\mathbf{R}_{j}=a\left(n_{1}, n_{2}, n_{3}\right)$, that is to an antiferromagnetic state in which atoms in adjacent lattice planes normal to the $O x$ axis have oppositely directed spins.

Let
$$
\epsilon_{Y}^{\sigma}=\lim \epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma}
$$
for $\mathbf{k} \in Y$, where $Y$ denotes one of the classes A, B or C, and lim is as defined above. Then, from (11),
$$
\left.\begin{array}{l}
\epsilon_{\mathrm{A}}^{+}=-\epsilon_{\mathrm{B}}^{-}=\Delta+\frac{1}{2} I(\mu+n) \\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{B}}^{+}=-\epsilon_{\mathrm{A}}^{-}=-\Delta+\frac{1}{2} I(\mu+n) \\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{C}}^{+}=-\epsilon_{\mathrm{C}}^{-}=\left\{\left(\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)^{2}+\Delta^{2}\right\}^{1 / 2}+\frac{1}{2} I n
\end{array}\right\}.
$$

The Hartree-Fock conditions (12) and (13) may then be recast in terms of the quantities $g_{Y}^{\sigma}(\lambda)$, defined for $0<\lambda<1$ by
$$
g_{Y}^{\sigma}(\lambda)=\lim N_{Y}^{-1} \sum_{\mathbf{k} \in Y} \theta\left(p-\epsilon_{Y}^{\sigma}\right)
$$
lim being defined as above, while, for $\lambda=0,1$,
$$
g_{Y}^{\sigma}(0)= \begin{cases}0, & Y=\mathrm{C} \\ \lim _{\lambda \rightarrow 0+} g_{Y}^{\sigma}(\lambda), & Y=\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}\end{cases}
$$

$$
g_{Y}^{\sigma}(1)= \begin{cases}\lim _{\lambda \rightarrow 1-} g_{Y}^{\sigma}(\lambda), & Y=\mathrm{C} \\ 0, & Y=\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}.\end{cases}
$$

Equations (12) and (13) then become, for $\Delta \neq 0$ and $\mu \neq 0$ and zero temperature,
$$
-1=\sum_{\sigma} \sigma\left[(1-\lambda) \frac{g_{\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}(\lambda)+g_{\mathrm{B}}^{\sigma}(\lambda)}{2 \mu}+\lambda g_{\mathrm{C}}^{\sigma}(\lambda)\left\{\mu^{2}+\left(\frac{2 \Delta}{I}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-1 / 2}\right]
$$
and
$$
n=N^{-1} \sum_{\sigma} \sum_{Y} N_{Y} g_{Y}^{\sigma}(\lambda)
$$
respectively, while the Hartree-Fock energy per atom of the spin-density-wave state, as given by equation (14), becomes
$$
\begin{aligned}
E(\lambda) \equiv E_{\mathrm{SDW}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}= & \sum_{\sigma}\left[\frac{1}{2} \Delta(1-\lambda)\left\{g_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{\sigma}(\lambda)-g_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{\sigma}(\lambda)\right\}\right. \\
& \left.+2 \Delta^{2} \lambda I^{-1}\left\{\mu^{2}+\left(\frac{2 \Delta}{I}\right)^{2}\right\}^{-1 / 2} \sigma g_{\mathrm{C}}{ }^{\sigma}(\lambda)\right]+\frac{1}{4} I\left(n^{2}-\mu^{2}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Equations (19), (20) and (21) constitute a basis for the Hartree-Fock theory of the band (18) in the limiting case $\epsilon_{i} \rightarrow 0$.

The Hartree-Fock energy per atom of the paramagnetic state, $E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{\mathrm{HF}}$, is similarly given by
$$
E_{\mathrm{P}}{ }^{H F}=-\Delta n+\frac{1}{4} I n^{2}.
$$

A very detailed investigation of the behaviour of $\mu(\lambda)$ and $E(\lambda)$ as functions of $n$ and $\Delta/I$ has been made by Morris (1967), who has shown that the Hartree-Fock ground state of the system is a spin-density wave if $\frac{1}{2} < n < 1$ or if $n = 1$ and $\Delta > \frac{1}{2}I$, that it is a paramagnetic state if $n = 1$ and $\Delta \geqslant \frac{1}{2}I$ and that it is a ferromagnetic state if $n = \frac{1}{2}$. For $n < \frac{1}{2}$ the state of lowest energy is degenerate, but may be any one of the states given by $\mu = n$ and $0 \leqslant \lambda \leqslant 2n-1$. Here the situation is rather delicate and non-zero values for $\epsilon_i$ would have to be taken to break the degeneracy and decide the ground state. (These results are consistent with the result obtained in $\S 4$, namely that for $n = 1$ and $\Delta < \frac{1}{4}I$ the spin-density-wave state is stable.)

## 6. Finite-temperature theory

For $n = 1$ and the energy band (18) the Hartree-Fock equations (12), (13) and (14) can be considerably simplified. Indeed (13) is satisfied by taking $p = \frac{1}{2}I$, and (12) and (14) then become, for $\mu \neq 0$,

$$
1=(1-\lambda) \frac{\tanh \left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta\left(\Delta+\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)\right\}-\tanh \left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)\right\}}{2 \mu}+\frac{I \lambda}{2 d} \tanh \left(\frac{1}{2} \beta d\right) \tag{23}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(\lambda, \beta) \equiv N^{-1}\langle H\rangle= & -\frac{1}{2} \Delta(1-\lambda)\left[\tanh \left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta\left(\Delta+\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)\right\}+\tanh \left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)\right\}\right] \\
& -\frac{\lambda \Delta^{2}}{d} \tanh \left(\frac{1}{2} \beta d\right)+\frac{1}{4} I\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)
\end{aligned} \tag{24}
$$

respectively, where

$$
d=\left\{\Delta^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2} I \mu\right)^{2}\right\}^{1 / 2}.
$$

The dependence of the energy per atom, $E$, and the amplitude $\mu$ on $\beta=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ and $\lambda$ is indicated by displaying these variables explicitly.

The energy per atom of the paramagnetic state $E_{\mathrm{P}}(\beta)$, obtained as the limit as $\mu \to 0$ of (24), is

$$
E_{\mathrm{P}}(\beta)=-\Delta \tanh \left(\frac{1}{2} \beta \Delta\right)+\frac{1}{4} I. \tag{25}
$$

A solution to the problem involves minimizing $E(\lambda, \beta)$ with respect to $\lambda$ and subject to the constraint (23). If the minimum corresponds to $\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta)$, then $E_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta) \equiv E(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta), \beta)$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta) \equiv \mu(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta), \beta)$ give the most stable state of the system at any temperature.

An explicit minimization process using Lagrange's multipliers can be avoided by using the following argument. In $\S 4$ it was shown that for $n = 1$ the zero-temperature ground state is a spin-density wave with $\lambda = 1$ if $\Delta < \frac{1}{2}I$ and is paramagnetic if $\Delta \geqslant \frac{1}{2}I$. Thus, if $\Delta < \frac{1}{2}I$,

$$
E_{\mathrm{s}}(\infty)=-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{I}, \quad \mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\infty)=\left\{1-\left(\frac{2 \Delta}{I}\right)^{2}\right\}^{1 / 2}, \quad \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}(\infty)=1
$$

while, if $\Delta \geqslant \frac{1}{2}I$,

$$
E_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta)=E_{\mathrm{P}}(\beta), \quad \mu_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta)=0
$$

these latter results being valid for all $\beta$. Thus only the case $\Delta < \frac{1}{2}I$ need be considered further.

The critical temperature $T_{0}=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}\beta_{0}(\lambda)$ at which the amplitude vanishes is such that $\mu(\lambda, \beta_{0}(\lambda))=0$ and $E(\lambda, \beta_{0}(\lambda))=E_{\mathrm{P}}(\beta_{0}(\lambda))$. On taking the limit $\mu \to 0$ of (23) it follows that the critical temperature is a root of the equation

$$
\lambda\left[\frac{I}{2 \Delta} \tanh \left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta_{0}(\lambda) \Delta\right\}-\frac{1}{4} I \beta_{0}(\lambda) \operatorname{sech}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta_{0}(\lambda) \Delta\right\}\right]=1-\frac{1}{4} I \beta_{0}(\lambda) \operatorname{sech}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta_{0}(\lambda) \Delta\right\}. \tag{26}
$$

It can be shown that, provided $\Delta < \frac{1}{2}I$, there is a unique solution $\beta_{0}(1)$ of (26) corresponding to $\lambda = 1$, and $\beta_{0}(1) < \beta_{0}(\lambda)$ whenever $\beta_{0}(\lambda)$ exists and $0 \leqslant \lambda < 1$. Thus $\lambda =1$

is given by

$$
S_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta)=
\begin{cases}
k_{\mathrm{B}}\left\{2 \lg 2-\left(1+\frac{2 d}{I}\right) \lg \left(1+\frac{2 d}{I}\right)-\left(1-\frac{2 d}{I}\right) \lg \left(1-\frac{2 d}{I}\right)\right\}, & \beta>\beta_{0}(1) \\
k_{\mathrm{B}}[2 \lg 2-\Delta \beta \tanh (\frac{1}{2} \beta \Delta)+2 \lg \{\cosh (\frac{1}{2} \beta \Delta)\}], & \beta \leqslant \beta_{0}(1)
\end{cases}
$$

from which it follows that $S_{\mathrm{s}}(\beta) \rightarrow 0$ as $\beta \rightarrow \infty$ and the entropy is continuous at the transition point.

Had the possibility of spin-density waves not been considered, the most stable state for the present case of $n=1$ and $\Delta<\frac{1}{2} I$ would have been the ferromagnetic state. It is interesting to compare the spin-density-wave results with the thermodynamic behaviour of this ferromagnetic state. The Curie temperature $T_{\mathrm{C}}$ is given by $T_{\mathrm{C}}=1 / k_{\mathrm{B}} \beta_{0}(0)$, where by (26) with $\lambda=0$

$$
\beta_{0}(0)=\frac{4}{I} \cosh ^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{2} \Delta \beta_{0}(0)\right\}.
$$

This equation has two solutions for $\Delta<\frac{1}{2} I$, and $\beta_{0}(0)$ is chosen as the largest of these. We denote by a suffix F quantities referring to the ferromagnetic state and then equations (23) and (24) give for $\beta>\beta_{0}(0)$

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{F}}(\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tanh \left[\frac{1}{2} \beta\left\{\Delta+\frac{1}{2} I \mu_{\mathrm{F}}(\beta)\right\}\right]-\tanh \left[\frac{1}{2} \beta\left\{\Delta-\frac{1}{2} I \mu_{\mathrm{F}}(\beta)\right\}\right]\right) \tag{30}
$$

and

$$
E_{\mathrm{F}}(\beta)=-\frac{\Delta \mu_{\mathrm{F}}(\beta) \sinh (\beta \Delta)}{\sinh \left\{\frac{1}{2} \beta I \mu_{\mathrm{F}}(\beta)\right\}}+\frac{1}{4} I\left\{1-\mu_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}(\beta)\right\}.
$$

For $\beta \leqslant \beta_{0}(0)$ the system would be paramagnetic. The energy is continuous at the transition point but the specific heat has a discontinuity.

## 7. Conclusions
A detailed investigation has been made of the application of the Hartree-Fock approximation to the stability and thermodynamic behaviour of spin-density waves. The formulation of the problem in $\S\S 2$ and 3 and the theorem on the stability of spin-density waves at zero temperature for $n = 1$ in $\S 4$ apply for any single electronic band structure. The subsequent analysis is based on a particular electronic band structure defined in (18). The conditions for the stability of spin-density wave, paramagnetic and ferromagnetic states are summarized at the end of $\S 4$. The complicated behaviour of the total energy and the amplitude of the spin-density wave as functions of $n$ and $\Delta/I$ even for the idealized band structure (18) shows both the sort of features that must be expected of a more realistic band structure and also how complex such an analysis would be.

The conclusions reached here on stability of spin-density waves will be compared in II with the results of an analysis based on a higher approximation of the correlation problem.
