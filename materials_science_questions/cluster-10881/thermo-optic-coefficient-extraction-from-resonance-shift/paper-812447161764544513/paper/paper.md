Received 26 February 1992; revised manuscript received 30 July 1992

The self-frequency doubling crystal NYAB has been investigated for the thermal properties of second harmonic generation of 1.064 µm. The temperature dependence of the phase-matching angle $\theta_{\mathrm{m}}$ for type I frequency doubling has been measured in the range from $23\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $133\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. The phase-matching angle varies with the temperature at a rate of $\mathrm{d}\theta_{\mathrm{m}}/\mathrm{d}T=(2.60\pm0.16)\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/^{\circ}C}$. This quantity can also be calculated from the ratio of the angular acceptance width to the temperature acceptance width. The measured angular and temperature widths are $\Delta\theta L=0.64\ \mathrm{mrad\ cm}$ and $\Delta TL=26\ ^{\circ}\text{C cm}$, respectively. The ratio of these two widths is $2.46\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/^{\circ}C}$ which agrees with the measured $\mathrm{d}\theta_{\mathrm{m}}/\mathrm{d}T$ value.

## 1. Introduction

Neodymium yttrium aluminium borate $[\mathrm{Nd}_{x}\mathrm{Y}_{1-x}\mathrm{Al}_{3}(\mathrm{BO}_{3})_{4}]$ referred to as NYAB, is a self-frequency-doubling crystal that has attracted much attention in recent years because of its potential as a compact diode-pumped green laser [1]. A self-frequency doubling crystal is one that combines the active and nonlinear optical properties so that the laser emission generated by the crystal is frequency-doubled by the crystal itself. NYAB is negative uniaxial and belongs to space group R32.

Because of its good laser and nonlinear optical properties, NYAB can be used in a compact diode-pumped laser system to produce coherent radiation in the green region. The NYAB green laser operates on the $^{4}\mathrm{F}_{3/2}$-$^{4}\mathrm{I}_{11/2}$ transition of the $\mathrm{Nd}^{3+}$ ion at 1.062 µm which is frequency-doubled by itself to generate radiation at 531 nm. With the advances of semiconductor technology, diode lasers with a few watt output power are available as the pumping source for a NYAB green laser. The crystal heats up substantially when a large fraction of the pump power is absorbed. Since the refractive index of the nonlinear crystal changes with temperature, the phase-matching angle changes as well.

The temperature dependence of the phase-matching angle has been studied in other nonlinear crystals such as $\mathrm{LIO}_{3}$ [2], KDP [3], and BBO [4]. In this paper, we measured the temperature dependence of the phase-matching angle in the NYAB crystal for type I second harmonic generation (SHG) of 1.064 µm. We also measured the angular and temperature acceptance widths. The relationship between the measured values were found to be in good agreement with theoretical expectations.

## 2. Temperature dependence of phase matching angle

The second harmonic output power $P_{\mathrm{SHG}}$ in the

constant pump approximation has the $\text{sinc}^2$ function form [5]:

$$
P_{\text{SHG}} \propto \frac{\sin^2(\Delta k\ L/2)}{(\Delta k\ L/2)^2} = \text{sinc}^2(\Delta k\ L/2) , \tag{1}
$$

where $L$ is the length of the crystal and $\Delta k$ is the wave vector mismatch defined by

$$
\Delta k=2k_1 -k_2=\frac{2\omega_1}{c}\ (n_1 -n_2) , \tag{2}
$$

where $n$ is the index of refraction, $k$ is the wave vector, $\omega$ is the angular frequency, and $c$ is the speed of light in free space. The subscripts 1 and 2 refer to the fundamental and the second harmonic beams, respectively.

The SHG output is maximum when the phase-matching condition $\Delta k=0$ is achieved. Since the index of refraction depends on the direction of propagation and the temperature of the crystal, the wave vector mismatch is likewise a function of both the angle $\theta$ and the temperature $T$. The phase-matching angle $\theta_{\text{m0}}$ at some certain temperature $T_0$ can be found by $\Delta k(\theta_{\text{m0}},\ T_0)=0$. The mismatch $\Delta k$ can be expanded as a series of two variables $\theta$ and $T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta k&=(\partial \Delta k/\partial \theta)_T(\theta-\theta_{\text{m0}})+(\partial \Delta k/\partial T)_{\theta}(T-T_0)+.... \\
&=\beta_{\theta}(\theta-\theta_{\text{m0}})+\beta_{T}(T-T_0)+.... \tag{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

For a temperature near the reference temperature $T_0$ and propagation direction close to the phase-matching angle $\theta_{\text{m0}}$ for the temperature $T_0$, we can neglect the higher order terms. Equation (3) becomes

$$
\Delta k=\beta_{\theta}(\theta_{\text{m}}-\theta_{\text{m0}})+\beta_{T}(T-T_0)=0 . \tag{4}
$$

The angular sensitivity parameter $\beta_{\theta}$ so defined by the above equation can be determined experimentally by measuring the SHG output variation with the propagation direction $\theta$ while keeping the crystal at constant temperature $T_0$. Fitting the result to eq. (1) gives the $\beta_{\theta}$ value. The temperature sensitivity parameter $\beta_{T}$ can be obtained in a similar method by varying the crystal temperature while keeping the crystal angle fixed at the phase-matching angle $\theta_{\text{m0}}$ for $T_0$.

The sensitivity parameters are related to the commonly used acceptance widths $\Delta TL$ and $\Delta \theta L$ by

$$
|\beta_{\theta}|=5.56/\Delta \theta L , \tag{5}
$$

$$
|\beta_{T}|=5.56/\Delta TL . \tag{6}
$$

The quantities $\Delta T$ and $\Delta \theta$ are the tolerances allowed on $T$ and $\theta$ before the second harmonic output drops to half of its maximum (fwhm).

Another quantity that can be determined experimentally is the variation of the phase-matching angle with temperature $\text{d}\theta_{\text{m}}/\text{d}T$. Since the phase-matching angle $\theta_{\text{m}}$ is defined by eq. (3) for a temperature near the reference temperature $T_0$ and a propagation direction close to $\theta_{\text{m0}}$, the variation of $\theta_{\text{m}}$ with temperature can be written as

$$
\text{d}\theta_{\text{m0}}/\text{d}T=-\beta_{T}/\beta_{\theta} . \tag{7}
$$

That is, the phase-matching angle $\theta_{\text{m}}$ varies linearly with temperature $T$ for a temperature near the reference temperature $T_0$. Using eqs. (4) and (5), eq. (7) can be rewritten in terms of the fwhm widths as

$$
|\text{d}\theta_{m}/\text{d}T|=\Delta \theta/\Delta T . \tag{8}
$$

The absolute value of $\text{d}\theta_{\text{m}}/\text{d}T$ can be evaluated if both the temperature and the angular acceptance widths are known. The sign of $\text{d}\theta_{\text{m}}/\text{d}T$ is determined by observing the direction of the phase-matching angle movement and compare it with the phase-matching angle for wavelengths other than 1064 nm.

### 3. Acceptance widths

The expression for the fwhm angular acceptance width for type I phase-matching can be derived by equating $(\partial \Delta k/\partial \theta)$ in eq. (3) to $0.886\pi/\Delta \theta L$. Substituting the appropriate refractive index expression into eq. (2), the angular acceptance width can be expressed as

$$
\Delta \theta L=\frac{0.886\lambda_1}{n_{10}^3(n_{2\text{e}}^{-2}-n_{2\text{o}}^{-2})\sin2\theta_{\text{m}}} . \tag{9}
$$

Using the published values of refractive indices and the phase-matching angle [4], the acceptance width for SHG of 1.064 $\mu$m is obtained as 0.67 mrad cm.

The fwhm temperature acceptance width can be obtained in a similar way as

$$
\Delta TL=\frac{0.443\lambda_1}{\partial(n_2 -n_1)/\partial T} , \tag{10}
$$

where, for type I phase-matching, $n_1$ is equal to $n_{10}$ and $n_2$ is $n_{2e}(\theta_{\mathrm{m}})$.

## 4. Experiment
In this experiment, we used an NYAB crystal with dimension of $3 \times 3 \times 4 \mathrm{~mm}^3$ and an Nd concentration of $x=4 \%$. Shorter crystals would have provided larger acceptance widths and hence better reading accuracy, however, the tuning range of the oven would not have provided enough variation in the SHG output to determine the peak position and the temperature width. The end faces were polished but uncoated and had been cut at the type I phase-matching angle for SHG of $1064 \mathrm{~nm}\left(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}=32.9^{\circ}\right.$ (6]). A $Q$-switched Nd:YAG laser at $1064 \mathrm{~nm}$ was used with a transverse mode of $\mathrm{TEM}_{00}$, pulse width of $10 \mathrm{~ns}$ and repetition rate of $10 \mathrm{~Hz}$. The energy per pulse was $5 \mathrm{~mJ}$. At this energy level, the heating of the crystal due to absorption was measured to be $0.3^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$.

The experimental setup is shown in fig. 1a. After being reflected by two folding mirrors, the beam was focused into the NYAB crystal. A screen placed in front of the crystal was used to record the position of the beam reflected by the entrance surface of the crystal. The beam propagation direction in the crystal was then calculated easily by using simple geometry and Snell's law. The NYAB crystal was placed in an oven which could be stabilized within $\pm 0.1{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. A thermocouple was placed at the interface between the crystal and the heater to monitor the sample temperature to $\pm 0.1{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The distance from the screen to the crystal was $1.2 \mathrm{~m}$. The error in reading the reflection position on the screen was estimated to be less than $1 \mathrm{~mm}$ which corresponds to $240 \mu \mathrm{rad}$ $\left(0.014^{\circ}\right)$. The error in centering the laser beam with the screen origin was estimated to be within $0.5 \mathrm{~mm}$ which corresponds to a systematic error of $120 \mu \mathrm{rad}$. The systematic error did not contribute to the error in determining the change of the phase-matching angle width temperature or the acceptance widths of the SHG process, but did cause an error in determining the absolute cut angle of the crystal, which was of minimal importance.

The NYAB crystal used for the measurement was cut at the angle for type I SHG of $1064 \mathrm{~nm}$. But the $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ axis direction of the crystal was not known. It was necessary to determine the $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ direction for two reasons. Firstly, the crystal had to be rotated about the $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ axis of the crystal in order to tune the $\theta$ angle. Secondly, the polarization direction of the input fundamental beam needed to be parallel to the $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ axis to achieve efficient frequency conversion. A diagram of the relation between the polarization direction and the crystal index axes is shown in fig. 1b. The polarization direction of the input beam was fixed in our experiment. The crystal was mounted on a rotational stage so that it could be rotated about the laser beam to ensure that the crystal $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ axis was parallel to the polarization direction of the input. The following method was used to ensure the parallelism. The phase-matching condition was dependent on an angle $\theta$ equal to the phase-matching value, and was independent of angle $\phi$. Hence, the loci of the reflection on the screen when phase-matching was achieved ( $\phi$ varied, $\theta$ fixed) was a line parallel to the $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ axis. This line rotated with the crystal when the crystal was rotated about the laser beam. We rotated the crystal till this line became parallel to the polar-

ization direction of the input beam. This brought the $n_{\mathrm{o}}$ axis to be parallel to the input polarization within $1^{\circ}$. This unparallelism introduced an error of about $0.02 \%$ in measuring the change of the phase-matching angle.

## 5. Analysis

To find the position in temperature or $\theta$ angle at which the SHG signal was maximum and to determine the temperature and angular widths, the least-squares-fit method was used to process the experimental data. Since the SHG intensity was expected to have the form of a $\operatorname{sinc}^{2}$ function as in eq. (1), the experimental data were fitted to the following form:

$$
y=A+B \operatorname{sinc}^{2}[C(x-D)], \tag{11}
$$

where $y$ represents the SHG intensity and $x$ represents either the angle $\theta$ or the temperature $T$. Equation (1) predicted that the second harmonic intensity would vanish when $\Delta k L / 2$ is equal to $\pm \pi, \pm 2 \pi$, .... However, due to factors such as scattering caused by imperfection of the crystal or the beam divergence of the focused beam, the second harmonic intensity did not vanish at these expected zeros. These factors are represented in eq. (11) by the fitting parameter $A$, whereas parameter $B$ represents the amplitude of the $\operatorname{sinc}^{2}$ function or the peak harmonic intensity at the phase-matching condition. To find the values of either the angular or temperature sensitivity parameter, $C$ was calculated. Parameter $D$ was calculated to obtain the angle or temperature at which perfect phase-matching occurred.

The measured SHG intensity variation versus angle at five different temperatures is shown in fig. 2. Also shown in the figure are the fitted curves. The abscissa is the angle relative to the crystal face normal. The phase-matching angles at various temperatures extracted from the fitting to eq. (11) and the regression line of these values are shown in fig. 3. The values of these angles and the residual differences from the regression line are listed in table 1. The rms value of the residuals listed in table 1 is only $44 \mu \mathrm{rad}$, which is smaller than the estimated uncertainty in reading the angle. The curve fitting yielded a slope of $(2.60 \pm 0.16) \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{rad} /{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ at $90 \%$ confidence level. The sign of $\mathrm{d} \theta_{\mathrm{m}} / \mathrm{d} T$ was determined by observing the direction of phase-matching angle movement as the temperature changed and comparing this with the phase-matching angle at $1.32 \mu \mathrm{m}$. The value of the phase-matching angle at $1.32 \mu \mathrm{m}$ was smaller than that of $1.06 \mu \mathrm{m}$.

The variation of the SHG signal with temperature was also measured. The angle of the crystal was fixed in this measurement. In order to determine the temperature acceptance width, the angle of the crystal was slightly tilted so that the SHG peak occurred at a temperature above the room temperature. The measured data and the fitted curve was shown in fig. 5. The data fit nicely to the $\text{sinc}^2$ function although the minimum was not obvious. The fwhm width was calculated to be $66\ ^\circ\text{C}$ which yielded $\Delta TL=26\ ^\circ\text{C}$ cm. With both $\Delta TL$ and $\Delta\theta L$ values obtained, eq. (8) was used to get $\text{d}\theta_{\text{m}}/\text{d}T=2.46\times10^{-5}\ \text{rad}/^\circ\text{C}$ which agrees with that in fig. 3 to within 6%. For comparison, this value is about 2.5 times larger than that of BBO while the temperature width is only about half of BBO [4]. Hence, NYAB is more sensitive to the temperature variation than BBO.

The $\Delta TL$ value was used in eq. (10) to evaluate the difference in the temperature variation of the refractive indices $\partial(n_2-n_1)/\partial T$. This yielded $\partial(n_2-n_1)/\partial T=1.8\times10^{-6}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$.

### 6. Conclusion

In conclusion, we obtained experimentally the

value $\mathrm{d}\theta_{\mathrm{m}}/\mathrm{d}T=(2.60\pm0.20)\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/^\circ C}$ for the change of the phase-matching angle with respect to the temperature for type I SHG at $1.064\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ for an NYAB crystal in the range of $23$-$133\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. The angular and temperature acceptance widths were determined to be $\Delta\theta L=0.64\ \mathrm{mrad\ cm}$ and $\Delta TL=26\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ cm. These independently measured values were found to agree very well with the relation $|\mathrm{d}\theta_{\mathrm{m}}/\mathrm{d}T|=\Delta\theta/\Delta T$. The small value of the NYAB acceptance width and the large temperature dependence of the phase-matching angle $(\mathrm{d}\theta_{\mathrm{m}}/\mathrm{d}T)$ mandates a very critical alignment when this crystal is used.
