# Optical Constants of Far Infrared Materials.
## 2: Crystalline Solids

Ernest V. Loewenstein, Donald R. Smith, and Robert L. Morgan

The far infrared optical constants of four crystalline materials at room temperature and at 1.5 K are reported. The materials are crystal quartz, sapphire, germanium, and silicon. The first two of these are birefringent (uniaxial) and both sets of optical constants are reported. The measurements extend over the range from $30\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ to $350\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.

## I. Introduction

In a previous paper¹ we discussed a method of determining optical constants of far infrared materials by the application of Fourier spectroscopy to the analysis of channeled spectra. This publication reports the results of the application of the technique to a number of crystalline materials. A forthcoming paper will report optical constants of plastic materials. Some of the materials reported here have been the subject of previous investigations²⁻⁴ but our data cover a broader spectral range and we report measurements at liquid helium temperature as well as room temperature. The materials reported herein are crystalline quartz, sapphire, silicon, and germanium.

## II. Experimental Procedure

The measurements were made with a far infrared Michelson Fourier spectrometer utilizing Mylar beam splitters of two different thicknesses: $6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and $25\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. Radiation from an 85-W GE mercury arc lamp (whose glass envelope has been removed) is collimated, passed through the interferometer, and brought to a focus at the end of a brass light pipe 1.1 cm in diameter. The light pipe passes the radiation down into a liquid helium dewar to the carbon bolometer detector, which is in an integrating chamber at the end of a 3:1 reducing cone. The detector temperature is about 1.5 K. The total length of light pipe is about 1 m, and there is one $90^\circ$ corner.

There are two sample positions, one at the top end of the light pipe, in the interferometer chamber, the other in the helium dewar just before the condensing cone. The latter is illustrated in Fig. 1. The light pipe has a vacuum seal at the entrance end to maintain the vacuum in the interferometer chamber. It is cut near the detector and a six-position sample wheel is interposed. The sample wheel is immersed in the liquid helium and can be turned from outside the dewar. The light pipe is not sealed at the sample position, and the infrared radiation is allowed to pass through the liquid helium, which appears to be entirely transparent throughout the region we observed ($30$-$300\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$). A few small holes are drilled in the light pipe to prevent any acoustical oscillations. The detector will not work if it is immersed in liquid helium, and consequently must be sealed. This is accomplished by attaching a sapphire disk with epoxy in the opening of the integrating cavity. The cavity is not pumped prior to cooling, for the liquid helium condenses the small amount of air trapped in it without any observable effect on the operation of the detector.

To measure the two sets of optical constants of birefringent materials, x-cut samples were used and the radiation was polarized by grid type polarizers.⁶ For the room temperature measurements the polarizer was kept in one orientation and the sample was turned, because the interferometer introduces a partial polarization of the radiation⁷ and it was naturally desirable to maximize the energy reaching the detector. For the cold measurements, the polarizer was mounted on the sample in the liquid helium bath. The orientation of the polarizer with respect to the incoming radiation was irrelevant, as a light pipe is a fairly efficient depolarizer.

Filtering is accomplished by means of the two vacuum windows. One is the sapphire disk that seals the detector chamber with a cutoff at about $350\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and the other is a loaded polyethylene sheet, described by Yamada et al.,⁵ that forms the room-temperature window between the vacuum tank and the light pipe. ### III. Thickness Measurements

The thickness of the samples must be known as accurately as possible, since this quantity enters directly into the determination of the refractive index. We measured our samples by comparing with gauge blocks traceable to NBS, using a Brown and Sharpe type 950 electronic comparator. The comparator was first calibrated using the gauge blocks; then the samples were measured and the calibration was repeated.

Our samples were quite thin: ½-mm and 1-mm thick disks were used. They were not as parallel as we might have wished and our knowledge of the thickness was limited by this fact rather than by the comparator, which permits measurement to better than ¼ μm. The samples were all slightly wedged.

For the cold samples the thermal expansion coefficient must be integrated from room temperature to 1.5 K to yield the change in thickness. Germanium and sapphire expansion coefficients have been published in detail from 300 K to 40 K at 10-degree intervals.⁸ Crystal quartz⁹ and sapphire¹⁰ expansion coefficients are not published in such complete detail. The integral, however, is not strongly dependent upon the detailed behavior of the expansion coefficient and, assuming that the coefficient goes to zero as $T \to 0$, we can arrive at a reasonable estimate of the thickness change. The changes in thickness for all the samples used are tabulated in Table I.

### IV. Results

#### A. Crystal Quartz

We have measured both pairs of optical constants of crystal quartz at room temperature over the 20-$200\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ range and at 1.5 K over the $20$-$330\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ range (Table V). In both the ordinary and the extraordinary ray the refractive index was slightly smaller at 1.5 K than at room temperature (about 1 part in 500), but the absorption coefficient decreased substantially upon cooling. The two well-known absorption lines in the ordinary ray, which are observed at about $128\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ and $263\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ at room temperature, both move toward higher wavenumbers ($131\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ and $264\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, respectively) upon cooling.

The absorption coefficient of the extraordinary ray shows two broad features at 1.5 K that are not seen at room temperature. These features are suspiciously close to the strong ordinary ray absorption lines at $131\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $264\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, and at first sight we suspected an error. A careful evaluation of the experimental procedure and setup together with the observation that the features in the extraordinary ray are about four times as broad as those in the ordinary leads us to conclude that they are indeed real, and not experimental artifacts.

The discontinuity in the refractive index at 131 $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ (Fig. 4) illustrates a problem that was discussed in Ref. 1. The order number $m$ of the zero crossings of the channeled spectrum does not have to increase monotonically with increasing wavenumber, and a dispersion feature such as that at $131\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ is a place where $m$ decreases in going from one zero crossing to the next. In this case, it was easy to determine what we should do with the value of $m$ because (1) we knew that there was an absorption line at this position, and (2) without the correction, the index curve had a rather strange shape. Furthermore, making the change of 2 in $m$ gave a reasonable-looking curve. The situation is more difficult at the $264-\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ line, where a much larger discontinuity occurs in the value of $m$. Here we had to determine the value of the discontinuity in a different way. We assumed that the index difference between 1.5 K and 300 K would be the same at wavenumbers above the absorption line as it is below. Applying this difference to one position on our data determines the entire curve, for the dispersion is fixed by the spacing of the zero crossings that are known. There remains the possibility of a small systematic error in the refractive index between $265\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $330\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.

### 1. Discussion

We have found an unexplainable discrepancy between our work and that of Russell and Bell in Ref. 2: our room-temperature values of refractive index match theirs for the extraordinary ray, but not for the ordinary ray. We have carefully re-examined our procedure for any possible difference in handling of the two cases and found none. We have, in fact, taken data on the ordinary ray with three different samples: two $Z$-cut and one $X$-cut, and all match each other to within better than 1 part in 2000. The discrepancy from Russell and Bell is 1 part in 500; our refractive index values are higher than theirs. This discrepancy is not repeated in our sapphire data, where the match is better than 1 part in 2000 for both rays.

The shift of the two ordinary ray resonances at 128 $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $263\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ toward higher wavenumbers upon cooling is consonant with data taken at elevated temperatures. Saksena$^{11}$ found that heating a quartz sample to 800 K caused the resonance at 128 $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ to move to $98\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, and the $263-\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ line similarly moved to a lower wavenumber.

The small shoulder in the ordinary ray absorption at $325\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ does not correspond to any previously reported feature of quartz, and may be an artifact of our measurement. The index curve is climbing so steeply in this region that a small feature would be difficult to discern for verification.

### 2. Dispersion Fit.

We have calculated dispersion constants for the two lowest frequency resonances of the ordinary ray of crystal quartz at 1.5 K. The computational procedure starts from a reflectance curve, which we calculate from our optical constants using the equation

$$R = [(n - 1)^2 + k^2]/[(n + 1)^2 + k^2],$$

where $k = \alpha/4\pi\sigma$. The results did not depend measurably upon $k$ in the range of our calculation, since in all cases we found $k \ll n$.

The high wavenumber limit of our refractive index measurements for the cold samples was set not by the transparency of the sample but by the dispersion of the material. The dispersion has the effect of spreading out the signatures in the interferogram (see Ref. 1). With sufficient dispersion the first sig- nature (the one used in all cases for this work) can spread out far enough to run into the second. That situation actually occurred for the sapphire data, where we were using samples ½ mm thick. The re- fractive index curve leveled off at the high wavenum- ber end rather than continuing to rise as it should (the first lattice vibration occurs at $385\ \text{cm}^{-1}$). We increased the length of interferogram used to the maximum extent possible and terminated our curves at the point where the slope of the index curve began to decrease. The dc values of the refractive index, obtained by plotting $n$ against $\sigma^2$ and extrapolating to $\sigma=0$, are given in Table IV.

We ob- served no difference between its optical constants and those of sapphire, to within our experimental ac- curacies. A chemical analysis of the crystal has re- vealed no impurities present in concentrations ex-

The initial values we used were the room-temperature dispersion constants given by Russell and Bell,² which were close enough to our final results that satisfactory results were ob- tained with fewer than fifty iterations. Our results are tabulated in Table IV, along with the room-tem- perature results of Russell and Bell. The match be- tween the calculated and observed reflectance in our computed fit is better than 2% at all but a few points very close to the line centers, where it was somewhat worse. Repeated efforts to improve the fit by increasing the number of iterations or changing one of the dispersion parameters did not yield any substantial improvement.

### C. Germanium and Silicon

The resonances are too weak to show up in the refractive index, except for a small effect at 280 cm⁻¹, which disappears upon cooling.

The optical constants of silicon are shown in Fig. 10. The 1% reduction in refractive index upon cool- ing is again ascribed to the phonon contribution, al- though this time there is no resonance observable in the absorption curve. This results from the fact that a phonon resonance is a combination effect that oc- curs near the lattice fundamental. In the case of sil- icon the nearest lattice resonance is far outside the region of our observations (above 600 cm⁻¹), whereas in germanium the main resonance is at about 370 cm⁻¹.

The slight decline at the low wavenumber (long wavelength) end in the room-temperature refractive index appears at first sight to be a free carrier effect, but a calculation shows this not to be the case. The optical constants can be calculated from the equa- tion for the complex dielectric constant:

$$
\epsilon(\omega)=\epsilon_{\mathrm{a}}\left[\left(1-\omega_{p}^{2}\right) / \omega(\omega-i \nu)\right]. \tag{1}
$$

In this equation $\omega = 2\pi f$, where $f$ is the frequency at which the dielectric constant is calculated, and $\omega_{p}$ and $\nu$ are quantities related to the free carrier con- centration and mobility. The optical constants are related to the dielectric constant by the equations:

$$
n^{2}+k^{2}=R e(\epsilon), \tag{2a}
$$

$$
2 n k=\operatorname{Im}(\epsilon). \tag{2b}
$$

Using the values of $\omega_{p}$ and $\nu$ that are calculat- ed from the observed resistivity of 100 ohm-cm yields a decline in index that is only about one-quarter the observed decline. If the procedure is reversed, and values of $\omega_{p}$ and $\nu$ are calculated from the observed values of refractive index, there should be a rise in the absorption coefficient in going toward lower wavenumber rather than the observed drop.

We have therefore ascribed the observed index changes to surface damage to the crystal incurred in the optical polishing process. This can occur with- out the observation of a corresponding rise in ab- sorption coefficient (as required by the Kramers- Kronig relation), because our measurements of index are much more precise than the absorption measure- ments. A small rise in the absorption resulting from surface damage may be completely masked by the natural drop in going away from the lattice bands.
