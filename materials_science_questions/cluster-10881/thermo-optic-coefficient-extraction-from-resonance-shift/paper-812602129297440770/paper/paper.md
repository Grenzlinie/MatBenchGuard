# Facet temperature distribution in broad stripe high power laser diodes

Toshiro Hayakawa
Miyanodai Technology Development Center, Fuji Photo Film Co., Ltd., 798 Miyanodai, Kaisei-machi, Ashigarakami-gun, Kanagawa, Japan 258-8538

(Received 23 November 1998; accepted for publication 12 July 1999)

Facet temperature distribution has been measured for broad stripe high power lasers using reflectance modulation. Temporal evolution of the two-dimensional facet temperature distribution is directly measured. Measurements have been made for devices with various stripe widths. Devices with wider stripes show higher temperature and broader region of high temperature because the lateral heat dissipation becomes more ineffective. © 1999 American Institute of Physics.
[S0003-6951(99)02836-3]

High power capability of broad stripe laser diodes has been significantly improved for the past decade. Presently these lasers are widely used for applications such as solid state laser pumping, thermal printing, soldering, and material processing. In high power laser diodes, the mirror facet is the most crucial part, which limits the reliability under high power operation. In particular, the local temperature of the mirror facet is considered to influence the lifetime and the catastrophic optical mirror damage (COMD) in diode lasers. To date, a variety of methods have been used to measure the local facet temperature, such as micro-Raman spectroscopy, cathode luminescence, photoluminescence, and reflectance modulation. $^{1-12}$ Among these techniques, the reflectance modulation is better than other methods with respect to high speed and high sensitivity of measurement. $^{6-8}$ There are limited studies on the facet temperature of broad stripe lasers, $^{10-12}$ although a lot of studies have been done on narrow stripe single transverse-mode lasers. $^{1-9}$

In this letter, the temperature distribution at the mirror facet is measured for broad stripe lasers by using reflectance modulation. In particular, the time evolution of the facet temperature distribution is directly measured by using the advantage of high speed measurement. Measurements on the devices with different stripe width indicate that the facet temperature distribution near the center of the stripe is almost determined by the one-dimensional heat transfer along the direction perpendicular to the stripe in broad stripe lasers.

Separate-confinement heterostructure single quantum well (SQW) laser diodes with an InGaAsP SQW, InGaP waveguide layers, and $Al_{0.65}Ga_{0.35}$As clad layers were grown by metalorganic vapor phase epitaxy as reported previously. $^{13}$ Broad stripe lasers with the width of 50 and $200\ \mu$m were fabricated by $SiO_{2}$ isolation. The emission wavelength was 810 nm. Laser chips were mounted on heat sinks with junction-down configuration unless otherwise noted.

Reflectance modulation technique was first used for measuring the facet temperature of laser diodes by Epperlein et al. $^{6-8}$ In this method, the change of light reflection due to the temperature dependence of refractive index of semiconductor laser facet is measured by applying the pulsed current. The laser was operated by pulsed current with a low frequency of 20 Hz and a duty cycle of 50%. We confirmed that the lower the frequency of current, the higher is the temperature since the driving approaches to continuous wave (cw) operation. The reflected probe beam is modulated mainly by the temperature change $\Delta T$ and the modulation depth of the reflected beam $I_{0}\Delta R$ is phase-sensitively measured with a lock-in amplifier, which has the lowest frequency limit of 20 Hz. The time constant of a lock-in amplifier was set to be much longer than the pulse duration, and the measurement time for each point was sufficiently longer than the time constant. The laser diode emission with 810 nm was almost completely excluded by using specially designed filters. This is essential to avoid the saturation of the photodetector and the front amplifier of a lock-in amplifier, which leads to measurement error. As a result, no signal was detected without a probe beam even when more than 3 W of laser light was emitted from a diode laser. A linearly polarized cw He–Ne laser beam with a wavelength of 632.8 nm and a low power of less than 1 mW was used for probing. The probe beam is impinged with an angle of $45^{\circ}$ and is focused into an oval shape spot $(<5\ \mu$m$\times7\ \mu$m). The electric field of the probe beam is in the incident plane. The laser diode was temperature controlled and was placed on the automated $XYZ$ stage, which was moved and is computer controlled for the spatial distribution measurement. The direct current reflectance $I_{0}R$ was measured with a mechanically chopped probe beam. The temperature change $\Delta T$ is measured by the relation $\Delta T=C\times\Delta R/R$. By neglecting the extinction coefficient at 632.8 nm, $C$ is expressed as

$$
C=[R(dR/dn)^{-1}]\times(\delta n/\delta T)^{-1}
$$

with

$$
R=\frac{\left(\cos i\sqrt{n^{2}-\sin^{2}i}-\sin^{2}i\right)^{2}}{\left(\cos i\sqrt{n^{2}-\sin^{2}i}+\sin^{2}i\right)^{2}}\frac{\left(\cos i-\sqrt{n^{2}-\sin^{2}i}\right)^{2}}{\left(\cos i+\sqrt{n^{2}-\sin^{2}i}\right)^{2}},
$$

where $i$ is the incident angle and $n$ is the refractive index. $C$ is obtained to be $1.8\times10^{3}\ \text{K}$ for $Al_{0.65}Ga_{0.35}$As with $i=\pi/4.^{14}$ This is about a half of the value, $4\times10^{3}\ \text{K}$, in Ref. 7. This is due to the difference in the wavelength and the incident angle of a probe beam in this study as compared with those in Ref. $7.^{15}$

Figure 1 clearly indicates the two- dimensional heat transfer with time, which is determined by the thermal diffusion and conduction as well as the boundary condition of the laser chip. At $10\ \mu$s, the temperature distribution is determined by the heat generation, which is approximately proportional to the optical near field pattern. After $250\ \mu$s to 25 ms, the heat spreads out into a GaAs substrate, then laterally, and finally into the heat sink through SiO₂. The heat transfer from the regions adjacent to the active stripe might be effective although the heat transfer is mostly through the $p$ electrode since the thermal conductance of the SiO₂ insulator is much lower than the metal electrode. In Fig. 2, facet temperature profiles along the direction perpendicular to the junction plane at the center of active stripe are shown as a function of the width of driving current pulses. At $5\ \mu$s, the heat generation determines the profile, whose resolution is limited by the diameter of a probe beam. At $250\ \mu$s, the single exponential profile becomes dominant, which indicates that the thermal transfer is limited by the diffusion process. This gives the diffusion length of $39\ \mu$m, which corresponds to the thermal diffusion time constant of $60\ \mu$s with using the diffusivity of GaAs of $0.257\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}.^{16}$ The real diffusion length might be smaller than the measured value since the lock-in amplifier gives nearly the maximum value over a period of $500\ \mu$s. At 25 ms, the temperature of entire facet increases and the variation at the center of the stripe becomes moderate since the heat dissipation is limited except for the bottom side of a laser chip. This is not the case for the single transverse-mode narrow stripe lasers shown later and is specific for the broad stripe high power lasers with large heat generation.

Figure 3 shows the temperature distribution for a device mounted with junction-up configuration. Since the heat dissipation is done through a thick GaAs substrate ($\approx 100\ \mu$m) toward a heat sink, which is placed to the opposite side of the heat source, the heat transfer is more isotropic to both vertical and horizontal directions as compared with the case of junction-down configuration shown in Fig. 1.

In Fig. 4, the temperature distribution is shown for devices with various stripe widths of (a) $\approx 5$, (b) 50, and (c) $200\ \mu$m. A commercial single transverse mode device, SDL5401G1, was used for $\approx 5\ \mu$m stripe single transverse-mode laser for comparison. Although the current density is set to be higher for narrower stripe width, the peak temperature of the 200-$\mu$m-wide laser is higher than that of the 50-$\mu$m-wide laser. In addition, the high temperature region is wider in the direction perpendicular to the junction plane as the stripe width increases, particularly for a 200-$\mu$m-wide laser. The higher facet temperature in the devices with wider stripe width would lead to higher degradation rate when operated at the same optical power density. For a narrower stripe, the heat dissipation from the active region can effectively be done in the direction parallel to the junction plane. This is a well known effect in single transverse-mode lasers with a narrow stripe as shown in Fig. 4(a). In the case of 200 $\mu$m stripe width, the heat dissipation from the center part of the stripe region is mainly carried out directly to the heat sink under the active region since the center part is neighbored by the active region. Therefore, the center of stripe region for the width of $\approx 100\ \mu$m can be treated as a one- dimensional model perpendicular to the junction plane. By contrast, the lateral heat dissipation from both sides of the active region is still effective for $50\ \mu$m lasers. This finding is particularly important when one designs the bar-type device, where broad stripe lasers are placed in an array configuration with the heat dissipation space between active stripes. For example, 100-$\mu$m-wide stripes are usually placed with the center-to-center spacing of $250\ \mu$m for effective heat sinking. Figure 4 clearly shows that $200\ \mu$m stripe width is too large to realize effective heat dissipation from the active region and $50\ \mu$m stripe width is more effective when the passively cooled heat sink is employed. The active cooling scheme using the microchannel heat sink$^{17}$ enables one to use wider active stripe and denser packing of the active region since it can absorb most of heat directly under the active region.

In summary, the facet temperature distribution has been measured for broad stripe high-power lasers using reflectance modulation. Temporal evolution of the two- dimensional facet temperature distribution is directly measured. Measurements have been made for devices with various stripe widths. These results indicate that device with wider stripe shows higher temperature and broader region of high temperature because the lateral heat dissipation becomes more inefficient. This is very important for designing high power laser diode arrays and bars by appropriately placing the heat dissipation spacing between active stripes.
