# Applicability of moiré deflection tomography for diagnosing arc plasmas

Chen Yun-yun, Song Yang, He An-zhi, and Li Zhen-hua*

Department of Information Physics and Engineering, Nanjing University of Science and Technology,
Nanjing 210094, China

*Corresponding author: zhenhuali@mail.edu.cn

Received 18 August 2008; revised 29 October 2008; accepted 22 November 2008;
posted 2 December 2008 (Doc. ID 100285); published 13 January 2009

The argon arc plasma whose central temperature, $1.90 \times 10^4$ K, is used as a practical example for an experiment to research the applicability of moiré deflection tomography in arc plasma flow-field diagnosis. The experimental result indicates that moiré deflection of the measured argon arc plasma is very small, even smaller than that of a common flame with the maximal temperature of nearly $1.80 \times 10^3$ K. The refractive-index gradient in moiré deflection tomography mainly contributes to the temperature gradient in essence when the probe wavelength and pressure are certain in plasma diagnosis. The applicable temperature ranges of moiré deflection tomography in the argon arc plasma diagnosis are given with the probe wavelength 532 nm at 1 atm in certain measuring error requirements. In a word, the applicable temperature range of moiré deflection tomography for arc plasma diagnosis is intimately related to the probe wavelength and the practical measuring requirements. © 2009 Optical Society of America

## 1. Introduction

Optical computerized tomography (OCT) has become the first chosen method for displaying and measuring the transient states of phase flow fields due to real-time, noncontact, and stable characters. Interferometry, holographic interferometry, and moiré deflection tomography have been widely used in various low-temperature flow-field diagnoses, including flame, wind tunnel, supersonic jet, and compressible flows [1–6]. Interferometry and holographic interferometry also have been used to diagnose some high-temperature flow fields, such as plasmas [7–12]. However, Radley, Jr. pointed out that the disadvantage of interferometry in plasma diagnosis is that slight relative changes between two interferograms with large overall fringe shift lead to considerable error in calculating electron number density [10]. Holographic interferometry also has defects, including complicated experimental structure and data processing and multiple sequences that cannot be easily obtained at a time.

Faris and Byer proposed beam-deflection optical tomography [13], which can be used to diagnose various flow fields of flame [14], supersonic jet [15], laser-produced [16], and DC arc-jet plasmas [17]. But its devices are complicated and have accuracy requirements especially for the detectors. In particular, because the transform lens also acts as a collection lens, care must be taken to avoid stray light, or it would cause noise at the detector [13].

Moiré deflectometry was first proposed by Kafri who showed that it could be used to measure the refractive index or refractive-index gradient for phase objects [18]. However, up to now, moiré deflection tomography has seldom been used to diagnose high-temperature flow fields, especially laser-produced and DC arc plasmas, even if it is famous for simple devices, big dynamic range, and low requirement for mechanical stability compared with interferometry and holographic interferometry. Therefore, the question whether such a superior optical method can be used to diagnose plasmas attracts many people, which is important to not only plasma's optical

## 2. Experiment and Results

### A. Experimental Setup and Materials
The schematic of moiré deflection tomography is depicted in Fig. 1. A laser with wavelength 532 nm is used as the probe light source, $B_1$ is an extender lens, and $L_1$ and $L_2$ are the collimating and focusing lenses, respectively. The measured phase object is the argon arc plasma, which is sprayed by a plasma-cutting machine in air as shown in Fig. 2. In order to protect the optical elements from being disturbed and destructed by the measured flow fields, the argon arc plasma is put between two observing windows, $W_1$ and $W_2$. Two Ronchi gratings, $G_1$ and $G_2$, with grating constant $p$ at 0.05 mm, are regarded as the main components of the whole testing system. In order to maximize the contrast of moiré fringes, they are tilted with an angle $+\theta/2$ and $-\theta/2$ relative to the $z$ axis and placed in parallel, and the distance between them must be integral times the Talbot distance $(d = p^2/\lambda)$. For the sake of obtaining improved signal-to-noise ratio moiré fringes, a pinhole, $f_1$, placed at the focus of $L_2$ serves as a filter that can only let the +1 frequency spectrum pass through and image. The moiré fringes formed by the measured flow field are detected and picked up by CCD on receiving screen $P_1$.

### B. Experimental Results
It has been reported that the argon arc plasma is an axisymmetric flow field [19], so the reconstruction of the refractive index can be realized by use of only one direction's data information for a given cross section. The deflected moiré fringes generated by the measured argon arc plasma are shown in Fig. 3(a), while 3(b) is the paralleled reference fringes. In contrast, Fig. 4(a) shows the deflected moiré fringes of a common flame combusted by propane in air with the maximal temperature of nearly $1.80 \times 10^3 \, \text{K}$ [20], while 4(b) is the measured flame. It is obvious that the moiré deflection of the argon arc plasma is much smaller than that of the measured flame.

## 3. Basic Theories
The deflected angle of moiré fringe can be expressed as [21]

$$
\phi_{x}(x, y)=\frac{1}{n_{0}} \int_{0}^{L} \frac{\partial n(x, y, z)}{\partial x} \mathrm{~d} z, \tag{1}
$$

where $\phi_x$ is the deflected angle in the $x$ direction at point $(x,y)$, $n_0$ is the environment's refractive index, and 0 and $L$ represent the boundaries of the measured flow fields. According to Eq. (1), it is obvious that the moiré deflection has an intimate relation with the refractive-index gradient $(\partial n(x,y,z))/\partial x$ of the measured phase objects.

The refractive index of gases is determined by temperature, pressure, components, and probe wavelength [22]. If the pressure and probe wavelength are certain, it only needs focusing on the components and temperature, which could affect the refractive index of the measured object. Fortunately, for plasmas, the components are related to temperature, so the variation of the refractive index can be mainly contributed to temperature. Therefore the refractive-index gradient in moiré deflection tomography should lie on the gradient of temperature when

The refractive index of atoms is [23]

$$
(n-1)_{a}=\frac{1}{L}\left(A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}\right) N_{a}, \quad(2)
$$

where $L$ is the Loschmidt number $(2.687 \times$
$10^{19} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ ), $A$ and $B$ are constants relevant to atoms
[24], $\lambda$ is the probe wavelength, and $N_{a}$ is the number
density of atoms.

The refractive index of electrons can be written
as [16]

$$
\begin{aligned}
(n-1)_{e} & \approx-\frac{1}{2} \frac{N_{e} e^{2} \lambda^{2}}{2 \pi m_{e} c^{2}} \\
& =-4.46 \times 10^{-14} \lambda^{2} N_{e}\left(\lambda: \mathrm{cm}, N_{e}: \mathrm{cm}^{-3}\right), \quad(3)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $N_{e}$ is the electron number density, $e$ is the
charge constant, $m_{e}$ is the electron mass, and $c$ is
the speed of light in vacuum. Equation (3) describes
that the electrons have a negative effect on the re-
fractive index of the measured whole flow field and
strongly depends on the probe wavelength.

Argon is monatomic, and the contribution of an ion
is $67 \%$ of an atom [25], so the whole refractive index
of the argon arc plasma can be obtained:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n-1= & (n-1)_{a}+(n-1)_{i}+(n-1)_{e} \\
= & \frac{1}{L}\left(A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}\right) N_{a}+0.67 \frac{1}{L}\left(A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}\right) N_{i}-4.46 \\
& \times 10^{-14} \lambda^{2} N_{e} \\
= & \frac{1}{L}\left(A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\left(N_{a}+0.67 N_{i}\right)-4.46 \times 10^{-14} \lambda^{2} N_{e}, \\
& (4)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $N_{i}$ is the ion number density of plasma.

By differentiating Eq. (4) with respect to tempera-
ture $T$, we can have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} T}= & \frac{1}{L} \cdot\left(A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}\right) \cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{a}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+0.67 \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} T}\right)-4.46 \\
& \times 10^{-14} \lambda^{2} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{e}}{\mathrm{~d} T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Equation (5) illuminates that $\mathrm{d} n / \mathrm{d} T$ is a nonlinear
function of the probe wavelength, and it is also in-
volved with the gradients of atom, ion, and electron
number density to temperature $(\mathrm{d} N_{a} / \mathrm{d} T, \mathrm{~d} N_{i} / \mathrm{d} T$,
and $\mathrm{d} N_{e} / \mathrm{d} T$ ). Substituting the constants $A$ and $B$
and the probe wavelength $5.32 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~cm}$, Eqs. (4)
and (5) can be reduced to

$$
\begin{aligned}
n-1= & 1.05959 \times 10^{-23} \cdot\left(N_{a}+0.67 N_{i}\right)-1.2623 \\
& \times 10^{-22} \cdot N_{e}, \quad(6)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} T}= & 1.05959 \times 10^{-23} \cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{a}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+0.67 \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} T}\right) \\
& -1.2623 \times 10^{-22} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{e}}{\mathrm{~d} T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

According to Eqs. (6) and (7), the contribution of an
electron to not only $n$ but also to $\mathrm{d} n / \mathrm{d} T$ is all nearly
10 times that of an atom or ion at $5.32 \times 10^{-5} \mathrm{~cm}$. It
implies that the electrons in the measured plasma
have an important contribution to $n$ and $\mathrm{d} n / \mathrm{d} T$.

In principle, all current OCT works out the distribution of key parameters density or temperature for various flow fields founded on the reconstruction of the refractive index from the experimental fringe information. Hence, in the vicinity of the temperature $T$, to be detected, the little temperature difference $\Delta T$, which is derived from the measurement of the refractive index $\Delta n$, can be expressed as

$$
\Delta T=\frac{\Delta n}{\left.\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} T}\right|_{T}}. \tag{8}
$$

Consequently, the measuring error at temperature $T$ can be written as

$$
e=\frac{\Delta T}{T}=\frac{\Delta n /\left.\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} T}\right|_{T}}{T}=\frac{\Delta n}{\left.\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} T}\right|_{T} \cdot T}. \tag{9}
$$

In order to obtain $n$ and $\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T$, $N_e$, $N_i$, $N_a$, $\mathrm{d}N_e/\mathrm{d}T$, $\mathrm{d}N_i/\mathrm{d}T$, and $\mathrm{d}N_a/\mathrm{d}T$ must be determined first. As a matter of fact, all these quantities can be determined by numerical calculation, which can be seen in Appendix A in detail. In short, $n$, $\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T$, and $e$ are all functions of temperature and probe wavelength at certain pressure.

### 4. Analysis and Discussion
Figure 5 is the relation between the refractive index and the temperature with the probe wavelength 532 nm at 1 atm. As expected, $n-1$ equals zero at approximately $1.20\times10^4$ K (Point A), which means the effects of electrons is almost equal to the whole effects of atoms and ions. Particularly, from this temperature on, the refractive index of argon arc plasma becomes less than one, with the probe wavelength 532 nm, that is to say, phase velocity in it will exceed the speed of light in vacuum. The dependence of the electron number density on temperature at 1 atm is displayed in Fig. 6, with the temperature ranges from $0.50\times10^4$ to $2.00\times10^4$ K. The maximal electron number density appears at $1.70\times10^4$ K (Point C), which matches very well with the previous results [26]. Because the electrons have negative effects on the refractive index, the bigger the electron number density means the smaller the refractive index; this can be proved by Points B and C, appearing at the same temperature.

![](./images/811876628907950080_6.jpg)

Fig. 5. Dependence of the refractive index on temperature with the probe wavelength 532 nm at 1 atm.

The electron number density and refractive-index gradients to temperature are presented in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively; they are all nonlinear functions of the temperature. According to Eq. (5), the bigger $\mathrm{d}N_e/\mathrm{d}T$ implies the smaller $\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T$, which can be proven by Points D and E, appearing at the same temperature, $1.30\times10^4$ K.

Combining Figs. 5 and 8, the minimal refractive index appears at Point B, while $\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T$ equals zero at Point F. $T_B=T_F$ implies the theoretical results are dependable. Figure 8 also indicates that $\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T$ is at the order of magnitude of $10^{-9}$, with the probe wavelength 532 nm at 1 atm in the given temperature range, which means the refractive index of the argon arc plasma is insensitive to the variation of temperature.

It is well known that the resolution of moiré deflection tomography in diagnosing the refractive index is at the order of magnitude of $1\times10^{-5}$ [27], based on which the applicable temperature ranges of moiré deflection tomography for diagnosing the argon arc plasma in certain measuring errors are given in Fig. 9, with the probe wavelength 532 nm at 1 atm. In order to clearly see the applicable temperature ranges, the curves in Area A' in Fig. 9(a) are shown in Fig. 9(b). The minimal measuring error of moiré deflection tomography in diagnosing the argon arc plasma appears at the temperature of $1.30\times10^4$ K, where $\mathrm{d}N_e/\mathrm{d}T$ and $|\mathrm{d}n/\mathrm{d}T|$ own their maximums. ## 5. Conclusion

The applicability of moiré deflection tomography for diagnosing arc plasmas is studied in this paper, which proposes a basis for determining whether moiré deflection tomography can be expanded from low-temperature to high-temperature flow-field diagnosis. It can be concluded that

![](./images/811876628907950080_11.jpg)

1. The refractive index of arc plasmas has an intimate relation to the electron number density, while the refractive index of common gases is only dominated by the molecules or atoms.

2. If moiré deflection tomography is used to diagnose plasmas, the refractive-index gradient mainly derives from the gradient of temperature in physical essence when the probe wavelength and pressure are certain.

3. It is obvious that the order of magnitude of $dn/dT$ is mainly at $10^{-9}$ when the temperature ranges from $0.50 \times 10^{4}$ to $2.00 \times 10^{4}$ K, with the probe wavelength 532 nm at 1 atm, which means the refractive index of the argon arc plasma is insensitive to the variation of temperature.

4. Moiré deflection tomography can be used to diagnose high-temperature flow fields in certain temperature ranges that are related to the probe wavelength and the permitted errors in practical measuring requirements at certain pressure.

In a word, moiré deflection tomography can be used to diagnose arc plasmas in certain temperature and probe wavelength ranges. It proves that moiré deflection tomography can be used to diagnose high-temperature flow fields rather than just for some common gas flow fields. However, the moiré deflection of the argon arc plasma is very small, which implies that new methods must be proposed to extract the fringe information for further diagnosis and calculation. This seems become one of the most important factors limiting the application of moiré deflection tomography in diagnosing arc plasma flow fields as well as the sensitivity and accuracy.

### Appendix A
Because the central maximal temperature of the measured argon arc plasma as high as $1.90 \times 10^{4}$ K, the first and second ionizations must both be considered [29], and ionization can be expressed by Sa–Ha equation

$$
\frac{N_{1} N_{e}}{N_{a}}=2 \frac{Z_{1}}{Z_{0}}\left(\frac{2 \pi \kappa T m_{e}}{h^{2}}\right)^{3 / 2} \exp \left(-\frac{E_{1}}{\kappa T}\right), \quad \text { (A1) }
$$

$$
\frac{N_{2} N_{e}}{N_{1}}=2 \frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\left(\frac{2 \pi \kappa T m_{e}}{h^{2}}\right)^{3 / 2} \exp \left(-\frac{E_{2}}{\kappa T}\right), \quad \text { (A2) }
$$

where $h$ and $\kappa$ are the Plank and Boltzmann constants, respectively, $T$ is the temperature of plasma, $N_{1}$ and $N_{2}$ denote the first and second ionized ion number densities, and $E_{1}$ and $E_{2}$ are the first and second ionization energies of an argon atom and equal 15.759 and 27.629 eV [24]. Meanwhile, $Z_{0}, Z_{1}$, and $Z_{2}$ correspond to the partition functions of atoms and first and second ionized ions, respectively, which can be calculated by means of the presented method (e.g., see [29]). Supposing

$$
\begin{aligned}
& 2 \frac{Z_{1}}{Z_{0}}\left(\frac{2 \pi \kappa T m_{e}}{h^{2}}\right)^{3 / 2} \exp \left(-\frac{E_{1}}{\kappa T}\right) \\
& \quad=C \frac{Z_{1}}{Z_{0}} T^{3 / 2} \exp \left(-\frac{E_{1}}{\kappa T}\right)=K_{1}, \quad \text { (A3) }
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
& 2 \frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\left(\frac{2 \pi \kappa T m_{e}}{h^{2}}\right)^{3 / 2} \exp \left(-\frac{E_{2}}{\kappa T}\right)=C \frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}} T^{3 / 2} \\
& \quad \times \exp \left(-\frac{E_{2}}{\kappa T}\right)=K_{2}, \quad \text { (A4) }
\end{aligned}
$$

where $C=2(2 \pi \kappa m_{e} / h^{2})^{3 / 2}$ is a constant. According to the quasi-neutrality and pressure equation,

$$
N_{1}+2 N_{2}=N_{e}, \quad \text { (A5) }
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P & =N_{t} \kappa T=\left(N_{a}+N_{i}+N_{e}\right) \kappa T \\
& =\left(N_{a}+N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{e}\right) \kappa T. \quad \text { (A6) }
\end{aligned}
$$

Combining Eqs. (A1)–(A6), we can obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
N_{e}^{3}+2 K_{1} N_{e}^{2}+\left(3 K_{1} K_{2}-K_{1} N_{t}\right) N_{e}-2 K_{1} K_{2} N_{t} & =0 . \\
(\mathrm{A} 7) &
\end{aligned}
$$

According to Eqs. (A3)–(A7), once the temperature is given, $N_{e}$ can be obtained at 1 atm, then $N_{1}, N_{2}$, and $N_{a}$ can also be gotten:

$$
N_{1}=\frac{N_{e}^{2}}{N_{e}+2 K_{2}}, \quad \text { (A8) }
$$

$$
N_{2}=\frac{K_{2} N_{1}}{N_{e}}, \quad \text { (A9) }
$$

$$
N_{a}=\frac{N_{1} N_{e}}{K_{1}}. \qquad \text{(A10)}
$$

According to the numerical calculation, it can be known that once the temperature is given at a certain probe wavelength and pressure, the refractive index (or $n-1$) can be obtained.

In order to obtain $\mathrm{d}N_{e}/\mathrm{d}T$, Eq. (A7) must be differentiated, then we can have

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{e}}{\mathrm{~d} T}=\frac{\left(2 K_{1} K_{2}+K_{1} N_{e}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{t}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+\left(2 K_{2} N_{t}-3 K_{2} N_{e}+N_{t} N_{e}-2 N_{e}^{2}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} K_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+\left(2 K_{1} N_{t}-3 K_{1} N_{e}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} K_{2}}{\mathrm{~d} T}}{3 N_{e}^{2}+4 K_{1} N_{e}+3 K_{1} K_{2}-K_{1} N_{t}}. \qquad \text{(A11)}
$$

Further,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{a}}{\mathrm{~d} T}=\frac{N_{1} K_{1} \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{t}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+K_{1} N_{e} \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}-N_{1} N_{e} \frac{\mathrm{d} K_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}}{K_{1}^{2}}, \qquad \text{(A12)}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{i}}{\mathrm{~d} T}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\left(N_{1}+N_{2}\right)}{\mathrm{d} T}=\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{2}}{\mathrm{~d} T}. \qquad \text{(A13)}
$$

It is easily known that $\mathrm{d}K_{1}/\mathrm{d}T$, $\mathrm{d}K_{2}/\mathrm{d}T$, $\mathrm{d}N_{t}/\mathrm{d}T$, $\mathrm{d}N_{1}/\mathrm{d}T$, and $\mathrm{d}N_{2}/\mathrm{d}T$ can be obtained from Eqs. (A3), (A4), (A6), (A8), and (A9), respectively.

Finally we can have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} T}= & \frac{1}{L} \cdot\left(A+\frac{B}{\lambda^{2}}\right) \cdot\left(\frac{N_{1} K_{1} \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{t}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+K_{1} N_{e} \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}-N_{1} N_{e} \frac{\mathrm{d} K_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}}{K_{1}^{2}}+0.67 \times\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+\frac{\mathrm{d} N_{2}}{\mathrm{~d} T}\right)\right)-4.46 \times 10^{-14} \lambda^{2} \\
& \cdot \frac{\left(2 K_{1} K_{2}+K_{1} N_{e}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} N_{t}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+\left(2 K_{2} N_{t}-3 K_{2} N_{e}+N_{t} N_{e}-2 N_{e}^{2}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} K_{1}}{\mathrm{~d} T}+\left(2 K_{1} N_{t}-3 K_{1} N_{e}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} K_{2}}{\mathrm{~d} T}}{3 N_{e}^{2}+4 K_{1} N_{e}+3 K_{1} K_{2}-K_{1} N_{t}}.
\end{aligned} \qquad \text{(A14)}
$$
