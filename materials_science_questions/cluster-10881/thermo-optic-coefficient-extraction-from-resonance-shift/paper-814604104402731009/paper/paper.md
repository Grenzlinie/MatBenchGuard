## Abstract
Using correct expressions for piezo-optic and elasto-optic effects, the analytical relations between thermal coefficients of the refractive index at zero stress and zero strain have been established for YAG-type cubic crystals. Using the plane strain and plane stress approximations, analytical expressions for thermal radial and tangential changes of the refractive index have been found. The relations between different expressions for photoelastic coefficients are established. Examples of the inconsistent usage of generalised thermo-optic coefficients are discussed. It is shown that their values are often significantly overestimated when the results of the plane stress approximation are applied to a laser rod with a length larger than its diameter.

Keywords: solid-state laser, thermo-optic coefficient, photoelastic coefficient, plane strain, plane stress

## 1. Introduction
Laser diode (LD) pumped high average power solid-state lasers (SSL) have made great progress recently [1, 2]. However, non-uniform temperature distribution in the active element (AE) together with thermally induced stresses and deformations causes change in the refractive index and bulging of the end-faces of the AE [3–5]. The thermal lensing problem for SSL is commonly solved in two steps. During the first step, the distribution of temperature in the AE is found. Afterwards, the thermal stresses and deformations are determined. The second step is quite complicated. Therefore, the plane strain and plane stress approximations [6, 7] are widely used for the evaluation of induced stresses and deformations.

For the uniform thermal loading of a cylindrical AE and taking into account only a radial heat flow, the temperature distribution with quadratic dependence on the radius is realised for the temperature independent thermal parameters of the AE [3–5]. In the plane strain or plane stress approximations the radial dependences of stresses and deformations are also square-law for this case. Based on these observations for uniform heat loading in the AE with temperature independent thermo-optical parameters, a generalised conclusion was made [8, 9] that the $r$-dependent thermally induced optical path difference for radial and tangential polarisations is simply proportional to the appropriate transverse temperature shift averaged along the AE axis for different temperature distributions. The proportionality coefficients were also called thermo-optic coefficients $\chi_{r,\theta}$ [4, 9] as previously the coefficient $\text{d}n/\text{d}T$ had been called [10]. To distinguish these coefficients, the term 'generalised thermo-optic coefficient' will be used for this coefficient $\chi_{r,\theta}$, which consists of three parts. The first one is due to the temperature-dependence of the refractive index, the second part is due to the photoelastic effect in the AE, and the last one is due to the AE end-face bulging.

For the photoelastic part of the thermally induced refractive index change, photoelastic coefficients $C_{r,\theta}$ in front of radially parabolic temperature terms were introduced [11]. In the recent review paper [4], it was properly indicated that these so-called 'photoelastic constants' are different when using plane strain or plane stress approximations. As will be shown below, different expressions for photoelastic coefficients $C_{r,\theta}$

using the same plane strain approximation have been obtained due to the use of different thermo-optic coefficients $dn/dT$ and different descriptions of the photoelastic effect in [3, 11] and in [4]. Therefore, the statement [4] that Koechner published incorrect values of these coefficients in his reference book [3] is improper.

In this paper, the correct use of the photoelastic effect is discussed and the analytical relations between thermo-optic coefficients at zero stresses and zero strains are found for YAG-type cubic crystals. Then, the expressions of plane strain and plane stress approximations are presented in a symmetric and more convenient form for finding the analytical expressions of thermal radial and tangential changes of the refractive index. Using these expressions, the relations between different expressions for photoelastic coefficients $C_{r,\theta}$ are established. The examples of inconsistent usage of the generalised thermo-optic coefficients $\chi_{r,\theta}$ are discussed. It is shown that the direct use of these coefficients significantly overestimates the values of optical path differences when the plane stress approximation is applied to a laser rod with a length larger than its diameter.

## 2. Correct description of the photoelastic effect and relationship between different expressions of thermo-optic coefficients
It is well known that the refractive index of optical materials is not a constant parameter within the temperature range used in optical systems [10]. The derivative of the refractive index with respect to temperature at a constant pressure $(\partial n/\partial T)_{\sigma}$ is usually called a thermo-optic coefficient and denoted as $dn/dT$ [10, 12]. Sometimes the longer term—temperature (or thermal) coefficient of the refractive index is used for this coefficient [13].

Different methods are used to determine the $dn/dT$ of laser crystals [10, 12, 13]. In all of them, $dn/dT$ is measured in some combination with the thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha_{T}$. It should be noted that in practically all these methods the material samples are heated uniformly or subjected to a linear temperature gradient. In general, when $\Delta T$ is a linear function of rectangular coordinates, the strain corresponding to the free thermal expansion of each element satisfies the conditions of compatibility and thermal stresses are absent [6]. Thus, in practically all cases the measured $dn/dT$ corresponds to $(\partial n/\partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ and should be not confused with the $(\partial n/\partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ term, which represents the partial derivative of the refractive index at zero strain.

There are several ways of phenomenological development of the optical effects in crystals [14, 15]. The application of external fields such as changes in temperature and stresses gives rise to thermo-optical and piezo-optical effects, respectively. For the description of these effects, the relative dielectric impermeability tensor, defined as $B_{ij}=\varepsilon_{o}(\partial E_{i}/\partial D_{j})$, may be expanded in a power series as a function of temperature change, stress etc. Here $E_{i}$ is the electric field, $D_{j}$ is the electric displacement and $\varepsilon_{0}$ is the vacuum permittivity. The refractive indices of a crystal are generally specified by an index ellipsoid called the optical indicatrix $B_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=1$, where the Einstein summation convention is used [14, 15].

Abridged to the linear terms only, the Taylor's series about the state of a reference temperature $T_{r}$ and zero stress (and zero all other fields) yields [15]

$$
B_{ij}(T,\sigma_{kl})=B_{ij}(T_{r},0)+(\partial B_{ij}/\partial T)_{T_{r},\sigma=0}\Delta T+(\partial B_{ij}/\partial \sigma_{kl})_{T_{r},\sigma=0}\sigma_{kl}+...,
\tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta T=T-T_{r}\equiv \tilde{T}$ is the local temperature change and $\sigma_{kl}$ are the components of a stress tensor. Thus, the impermeability tensor changes due to linear thermo-optic $\Delta B_{ij}^{(\sigma=0)}=(\partial B_{ij}/\partial T)_{T_{r},\sigma=0}\Delta T$ and linear piezo-optic $\Delta B_{ij}^{(\sigma)}=\pi_{ijkl}\sigma_{kl}$ effects, where $\pi_{ijkl}=(\partial B_{ij}/\partial \sigma_{kl})_{T_{r},\sigma=0}$ are the components of the piezo-optic tensor.

It is well known that for the initially optically isotropic crystal $B_{ij}(T_{r},0)=\delta_{ij}/n_{0}^{2}$. Thus, for the initially optically isotropic crystal equation (1) may be rewritten in the form

$$
B_{ij}(T,\sigma_{kl})=[n_{0}+(\partial n/\partial T)_{\sigma=0}\Delta T]^{-2}\delta_{ij}+\pi_{ijkl}\sigma_{kl}. \quad (2a)
$$

Using the generalised Hooke's law $\sigma_{ij}=c_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}^{\sigma}$ [5, 6] and the relation $p_{ijrs}=\pi_{ijkl}c_{klrs}$ between the elasto-optical $p_{ijrs}$ and piezo-optical $\pi_{ijkl}$ coefficients [14], equation (2a) becomes

$$
B_{ij}(T,\varepsilon_{rs}^{\sigma})=[n_{0}+(\partial n/\partial T)_{\sigma=0}\Delta T]^{-2}\delta_{ij}+p_{ijrs}\varepsilon_{rs}^{\sigma}, \quad (2b)
$$

where $\varepsilon_{ij}^{\sigma}=s_{ijkl}\sigma_{kl}$ is the stress-induced part (or elastic strain [11]) of the total strain $\varepsilon_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}^{\sigma}+\varepsilon_{ij}^{T}$, $\varepsilon_{ij}^{T}=\alpha_{ij}^{T}\Delta T$ is a part of the strain caused by the free thermal expansion, $c_{ijkl}$ are the components of the 4th order elasticity or the stiffness tensor, $s_{ijkl}$ are the isothermal components of the 4th order compliance tensor, and $\alpha_{ij}^{T}$ are the components of the thermal expansion tensor.

If one wants to use the full strain tensor $\varepsilon_{rs}$ [4] (not only elastic part $\varepsilon_{rs}^{\sigma}$), then another form of the change of the impermeability tensor should be used

$$
B_{ij}(T,\varepsilon_{kl})=[n_{0}+(\partial n/\partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}\Delta T]^{-2}\delta_{ij}+p_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}. \quad (2c)
$$

For the latter form (2c) the term elasto-optic effect is commonly used [15]. All forms (2) used for the description of the change of the impermeability tensor are united by the term photoelastic effect [14, 15].

Comparing expressions (2a)-(2c) for the photoelastic effect, the relation between $(\partial n/\partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ and $(\partial n/\partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ may be found for mechanically isotropic cubic crystals of the YAG type. The Voigt's suffix reduction rule gives the following matrix form of the photoelastic effect [14]

$$
\Delta B_{m}^{(\sigma)}(T)=\pi_{m n}\sigma_{n}(T)\ (m,n=1,2,...,6), \quad (3a)
$$

where due to the symmetry the coefficients $B_{ij}$ are written with only one suffix. The $\pi_{mn}$ coefficients are related to the $\pi_{ijkl}$ by the rules [14]:

$$
\pi_{mn}=\pi_{ijkl},\text{ when }n=1,2,\text{ or }3; \quad (4a)
$$

$$
\pi_{mn}=2\pi_{ijkl},\text{ when }n=4,5\text{ or }6. \quad (4b)
$$

The factors of 2 in (4b) appear due to the pairing of the shear stress terms in (3a) [14]. If we prefer to work in terms

of strains instead of stresses, then instead of (3a) we should use the equation

$$
\Delta B_{m}^{(e)}(T)=p_{m n} \varepsilon_{n}(T) \quad(m, n=1,2,..., 6), \quad(3 b)
$$

where the $p_{m n}$ coefficients are related to the $p_{i j r s}$ by $p_{m n}=p_{i j r s}$ for all $m$ and $n$ with no factor of 2 or 1/2 [14].

It should be noted that this matrix approach is strictly valid only for isotropic and cubic materials because, in general, elasto-optical coefficients are not symmetrical on the last two indices (see [16] and the references therein).

For crystals of cubic class $m 3 m$ the suffix reduction rule gives the following relations between the matrix elements of the elasto-optical matrix $\{p_{m n}\}$, the piezo-optical matrix $\{\pi_{m n}\}$, the stiffness matrix $\{c_{m n}\}$ and the compliance matrix $\{s_{m n}\}$ (designated for generality by the matrix $\{\Pi_{m n}\}$):

$$
\begin{gathered}
\Pi_{m n}, \quad m, n=1,2,...6, \quad \Pi_{11}=\Pi_{22}=\Pi_{33}, \quad \Pi_{44}=\Pi_{55}=\Pi_{66}, \\
\Pi_{m n}=\Pi_{12} \text { if } m \neq n, m, n \leqslant 3 ; \quad \Pi_{m n}=0 \text { if } m \neq n, m, n \geqslant 4.
\end{gathered}
$$

During the free expansion of a cubic crystal $\varepsilon_{k l}=\varepsilon_{k l}^{T}=\alpha_{T} \delta_{k l} \Delta T$, it retains a cubic symmetry [14]. A change in the isotropic refractive index due to the photoelastic effect is related to the change of principal values of impermeability tensor $\Delta B_{11}^{(e)}=\Delta B_{22}^{(e)}=\Delta B_{33}^{(e)}$ by relation

$$
\Delta n_{1}^{(e)}=-\frac{n_{0}^{3}}{2} \Delta B_{11}^{(e)}=-\frac{n_{0}^{3}}{2} p_{1 j} \varepsilon_{j}=-\frac{n_{0}^{3}}{2}\left(p_{11}+2 p_{12}\right) \alpha_{T} \Delta T.
$$

Thus, the total change in the refractive index due to free thermal expansion may be represented by

$$
\Delta n_{\mathrm{T}}^{(\sigma=0)}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0} \Delta T=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0} \Delta T+\Delta n^{(e)}. \quad(7)
$$

Now, using equations (6) and (7), the required relation between $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ and $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ is easily found

$$
\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0}-\frac{\alpha_{T} n_{0}^{3}}{2}\left(p_{11}+2 p_{12}\right). \quad(8 a)
$$

The relation between derivatives $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ and $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ can also be found in another way. It is well known [6,7] that the thermal expansion of a sample due to the homogeneous change in its temperature can be compensated by applying the corresponding hydrostatic pressure. It is interesting to note that all crystals except those belonging to the monoclinic and triclinic systems do not experience rotation of the principal axes on the applied hydrostatic pressure. Cubic crystals remain cubic under hydrostatic pressure. Zero deformations $\varepsilon_{k l}=0$ can be realised for cubic crystals in this case. Then, as follows from Hook's law [16, 17], the principal stresses will only occur:

$$
\sigma_{i j}=-c_{i j k l} \alpha_{k l}^{T} \Delta T=\beta_{\mathrm{T}} \delta_{i j} \Delta T,
$$

where $\beta_{\mathrm{T}}$ is the thermal compliance coefficient. For isotropic materials the stiffness tensor has a form [17]

$$
c_{i j k l}=\lambda \delta_{i j} \delta_{k l}+\mu\left(\delta_{i k} \delta_{j l}+\delta_{i l} \delta_{j k}\right). \quad(10 a)
$$

Lamé constants $\lambda$ and $\mu$ can be expressed through the more widely used Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$ [17]:

$$
\lambda=\frac{\nu E}{(1+\nu)(1-2 \nu)}, \mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}. \quad(10 b)
$$

Using equations (9) and (10), it is easy to obtain $\beta_{\mathrm{T}}=-\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E /(1-2 \nu)$.

The isotropic change in the refractive index due to the temperature change at $\varepsilon_{i j}=0$ is represented by

$$
\Delta n^{(\varepsilon=0)}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0} \Delta T=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0} \Delta T-\frac{n_{0}^{3}}{2} \pi_{1 k l} \beta_{\mathrm{T}} \delta_{k l} \Delta T.
$$

Thus, another relation between $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ and $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ is easily found

$$
\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0}+\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} n_{0}^{3} E}{2(1-2 \nu)}\left(\pi_{11}+2 \pi_{12}\right). \quad(8 b)
$$

As was mentioned previously, in experiments, the thermal refractive index coefficient $\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0}$ is usually measured directly, and the coefficient $\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0}$ can be calculated using relation (8a). It is clear from the derivation of formula (8b) that $\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0}$ can also be directly measured if a body is heated and prevented from expanding in any direction by applying the corresponding hydrostatic pressure.

In order to show an equivalence of relation (8b) with the (8a), we can find $\pi_{m n}$ components through the $p_{m k}$ components using formula $\pi_{m n}=p_{m k} s_{k l}$ [14], where the compliance components $s_{k l}$ for mechanically isotropic YAG crystal are expressed through Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$ [17]

$$
s_{11}=1 / E, \quad s_{12}=-\nu / E, \quad s_{44}=2(1+\nu) / E. \quad(12)
$$

Taking into account the symmetry of piezo-optical and elasto-optical components [14], the following relations are easily found

$$
\begin{gathered}
\pi_{11}=\left(p_{11}-2 \nu p_{12}\right) / E, \quad(13 a) \\
\pi_{12}=\left[-\nu p_{11}+(1-\nu) p_{12}\right] / E, \quad(13 b) \\
\pi_{44}=2(1+\nu) p_{44} / E. \quad(13 c)
\end{gathered}
$$

Inserting (13) into formula (8b), we get formula (8a), that is, relations (8a) and (8b) are equivalent. Contrary to the statement of [4], we think that the partial derivative at zero strain $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ has a real physical meaning, as explained above, and can be measured directly or can be easily calculated from $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ using well-known elasto-optic coefficients [3,4]. Thus, a microscopic approach [4] to the definition of $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ is not necessary because it is very easy to calculate the difference between $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ and $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ using formula (8a) and the well-known parameters of YAG [3,4,12, 13]. Thus, this difference is two orders of magnitude lower and has an opposite sign when compared with the values obtained in [4].

### 3. Thermal refractive index change

For calculations of thermal refractive index changes in the AE of SSL the plane strain and plane stress approximations are widely used [3, 4]. The corresponding expressions for strain and stress tensors are well known [6, 7]. They can be presented in a symmetric form, which is more convenient for calculations. The strain components for so-called generalised plane strain (GPSn) approximation are:

$$
\varepsilon_{r r}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} \frac{1+\nu}{2(1-\nu)}\left[\frac{1-3 \nu}{1+\nu} T^{(R)}+\widetilde{T}-\bar{T}\right], \tag{14a}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta \theta}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} \frac{1+\nu}{2(1-\nu)}\left[\frac{1-3 \nu}{1+\nu} T^{(R)}+\widetilde{T}+\bar{T}\right], \tag{14b}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{z z}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} T^{(R)}. \tag{14c}
$$

In the case of simple plane stress (SPSs) approximation the strains are:

$$
\varepsilon_{r r}^{(2)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\left[(1-\nu) T^{(R)}+(1+\nu) \widetilde{T}-(1+\nu) \bar{T}\right] / 2, \tag{15a}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta \theta}^{(2)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\left[(1-\nu) T^{(R)}+(1+\nu) \widetilde{T}+(1+\nu) \bar{T}\right] / 2, \tag{15b}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{z z}^{(2)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\left[(1+\nu) \widetilde{T}-\nu T^{(R)}\right]. \tag{15c}
$$

It is seen that in the polar coordinate system the strain and stress tensors have only the diagonal components and are expressed through the local temperature $\widetilde{T}(r)$ and average temperatures $T^{(r)}$ changes in the circles with radii $r$ and $R$:

$$
T^{(r)}=\frac{2}{r^{2}} \int_{0}^{r} \widetilde{T}(r) r d r, \bar{T}(r)=T^{(r)}-\widetilde{T}(r)=-\frac{1}{r^{2}} \int_{0}^{r} \frac{\mathrm{d} \widetilde{T}(r)}{\mathrm{d} r} r^{2} \mathrm{~d} r. \tag{16}
$$

In particular, simple expressions are found for stresses in approximations of the plane strain

$$
\sigma_{r r}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E_{\nu}(\bar{T}-\widetilde{T}) / 2, \tag{17a}
$$

$$
\sigma_{\theta \theta}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E_{\nu}(\bar{T}+\widetilde{T}) / 2, \tag{17b}
$$

$$
\sigma_{z z}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E_{\nu} \bar{T}, \tag{17c}
$$

and the plane stress

$$
\sigma_{r r}^{(2)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E(\bar{T}-\widetilde{T}) / 2, \tag{18a}
$$

$$
\sigma_{\theta \theta}^{(2)}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E(\bar{T}+\widetilde{T}) / 2, \tag{18b}
$$

$$
\sigma_{z z}^{(2)}=0. \tag{18c}
$$

Here the additional notations $E_{\nu}=E /(1-\nu)$ and $\bar{T}(r)=T^{(R)}-\widetilde{T}(r)$ are introduced.

It is apparent that formulae (17) and (18) are very suitable for calculations of thermal refractive index changes. However, in the literature the usage of a strain tensor is more often for this purpose. This is possibly caused by the fact that elastooptical coefficients $p_{i j}$ for the YAG crystal have been known for a long time and their values do not give rise to doubt [3, 4]. At the same time, the calculations of piezo-optical coefficients were often erroneous as noted in [18]. Using relations (13), there is no problem calculating the piezo-optical coefficients $\pi_{i j}$ in the crystallographic coordinate system.

It should be pointed out once again that equations

$$
B_{i j}=B_{0, i j}+\pi_{i j k l} \sigma_{k l}, B_{i j}=B_{0, i j}+p_{i j k l} \varepsilon_{k l}, \tag{19a}
$$

often used for the description of the photoelastic effect are not equivalent, as is commonly thought, because it is implicitly assumed that initial

$$
B_{0, i j}=\delta_{i j}\left\{n_{0}+(\mathrm{d} n / \mathrm{d} T)\left[T(x, y, z)-T_{0}\right]\right\}^{-2} \tag{19b}
$$

is the same in both equations. As was shown previously, different partial derivatives of the refractive index should be used in formula (19).

For simplicity of comparison with well-known results, we consider here the case of a Nd:YAG rod in which the $z$-axis is in the [1 1 1] direction. In the plane strain and plane stress approximations, the strain and stress tensors have principal components only. Therefore, the principal axes of the optical indicatrix are directed along the radial and tangential directions for this case. The changes in refractive indices in these directions are described by the formulae

$$
\Delta n_{r, \theta}^{(T)}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0} \Delta T-\frac{n_{0}^{3}}{2} \Delta B_{r r, \theta \theta}^{(e)}(T), \tag{20a}
$$

$$
\Delta n_{r, \theta}^{(T)}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0} \Delta T-\frac{n_{0}^{3}}{2} \Delta B_{r r, \theta \theta}^{(\sigma)}(T). \tag{20b}
$$

Using the last expression the stress induced changes of the impermeability tensor components are described by [18]

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta B_{r r}^{(\sigma)} & =\frac{1}{2}\left(\pi_{11}+\pi_{12}+\pi_{44}\right) \sigma_{r r}+\frac{1}{6}\left(\pi_{11}+5 \pi_{12}-\pi_{44}\right) \sigma_{\theta \theta} \\
& +\frac{1}{3}\left(\pi_{11}+2 \pi_{12}-\pi_{44}\right) \sigma_{z z},
\end{aligned} \tag{21a}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta B_{\theta \theta}^{(\sigma)} & =\frac{1}{6}\left(\pi_{11}+5 \pi_{12}-\pi_{44}\right) \sigma_{r r}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\pi_{11}+\pi_{12}+\pi_{44}\right) \sigma_{\theta \theta} \\
& +\frac{1}{3}\left(\pi_{11}+2 \pi_{12}-\pi_{44}\right) \sigma_{z z}.
\end{aligned} \tag{21b}
$$

Inserting equations (17) or (18) into equations (21) and (20b), we get the simple expressions for local refractive index changes in the plane strain $(j=1)$ or plane stress $(j=2)$ approximations:

$$
\Delta n_{r, \theta}^{(j)}=-A_{1}^{(\sigma j)} T^{(R)}+\left[\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0}+A_{1}^{(\sigma j)}\right] \widetilde{T} \pm A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} \bar{T},(22 a)
$$

where

$$
A_{1}^{(\sigma 1)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E_{\nu}}{12}\left(4 \pi_{11}+8 \pi_{12}-\pi_{44}\right), \tag{23a}
$$

$$
A_{2}^{(\sigma 1)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E_{\nu}}{12}\left(\pi_{11}-\pi_{12}+2 \pi_{44}\right), \tag{23b}
$$

$$
A_{1}^{(\sigma 2)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E}{12}\left(2 \pi_{11}+4 \pi_{12}+\pi_{44}\right), \quad(23 c)
$$

$$
A_{2}^{(\sigma 2)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E}{12}\left(\pi_{11}-\pi_{12}+2 \pi_{44}\right) \quad(23 d)
$$

and the upper (+) and lower (-) signs describe the radial and tangential components, respectively.

Using relation (13), it is easy to recalculate constants (23)
and (24) in terms of the elasto-optical coefficients $p_{i j}$:

$$
A_{1}^{(\sigma 1)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}}{6(1-\nu)}\left[2(1-2 \nu)\left(p_{11}+2 p_{12}\right)-(1+\nu) p_{44}\right], \quad(24 a)
$$

$$
A_{2}^{(\sigma 1)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}}{12(1-\nu)}(1+\nu)\left(p_{11}-p_{12}+4 p_{44}\right), \quad(24 b)
$$

$$
A_{1}^{(\sigma 2)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}}{6}\left[(1-2 \nu)\left(p_{11}+2 p_{12}\right)+(1+\nu) p_{44}\right], \quad(24 c)
$$

$$
A_{2}^{(\sigma 2)}=\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} E}{12}(1+\nu)\left(p_{11}-p_{12}+4 p_{44}\right). \quad(24 d)
$$

Obviously, the full change in the refractive index should not depend on the way of calculation if exact relations are used. Therefore, using the relation (12c) and the equations for impermeability tensor components changes [19]

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta B_{r r}= & \frac{1}{6}\left[\left(3 p_{11}+3 p_{12}+6 p_{44}\right) \varepsilon_{r r}+\left(p_{11}+5 p_{12}-2 p_{44}\right) \varepsilon_{\theta \theta}\right. \\
& \left.+\left(2 p_{11}+4 p_{12}-4 p_{44}\right) \varepsilon_{z z}\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta B_{\theta \theta}= & \frac{1}{6}\left[\left(p_{11}+5 p_{12}-2 p_{44}\right) \varepsilon_{r r}+\left(3 p_{11}+3 p_{12}+6 p_{44}\right) \varepsilon_{\theta \theta}\right. \\
& \left.+\left(2 p_{11}+4 p_{12}-4 p_{44}\right) \varepsilon_{z z}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

we should obtain an expression of the same functional form

$$
\Delta n_{r, \theta}^{(j)}=-A_{1}^{(\sigma j)} T^{(R)}+\left[\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0}+A_{1}^{(\varepsilon j)}\right] \tilde{T} \pm A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} T(22 b)
$$

as previously in (22a). Direct calculations confirm that expectation and the following expressions are obtained for

$$
A_{1}^{(\varepsilon 1)}=-\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}(1+\nu)}{6(1-\nu)}\left(p_{11}+2 p_{12}+p_{44}\right), \quad(26 a)
$$

$$
A_{1}^{(\varepsilon 2)}=-\frac{n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}}{6}(1+\nu)\left[2 p_{11}+4 p_{12}-p_{44}\right], \quad(26 b)
$$

Now, using formula (26) and relation

$$
\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\varepsilon=0}+A_{1}^{(\varepsilon j)}=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma=0}+A_{1}^{(\sigma j)}, \quad(27)
$$

following from (22), the relation (8a) between the partial temperature derivatives of the refractive index is again obtained.

## 4. Different expressions for photoelastic and generalised thermo-optic coefficients

The expression (2b) for the piezo-optic effect with elastic strains $\varepsilon_{i j}^{\sigma}$ was used in [11] and in [20, 21]. Knowing that just the authors of [11] measured the $\mathrm{d} n / \mathrm{d} T$ previously and that the authors of [20] referenced that paper, we can undoubtedly believe that in $[11,20,21]$ the required derivative $\beta_{\sigma}=(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ was used. Photoelastic coefficients $C_{r, \theta}$ were introduced in [11], not in [22], as it is claimed in [4]. These coefficients were accepted in the first edition of monograph [3], and widely used (often inconsistently) until now. The expressions for $C_{r, \theta}$ found in [11] are true because they should be used together with the thermo-optic coefficient $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$. Therefore, the statements in [4] that the term accounting for the thermal dilatation in the generalised Hooke's law was omitted in [22] and that the error made by Koechner in the numerical value of the photoelastic coefficients $C_{r, \theta}$ is about $20 \%$ (see [4], p 146) are incorrect. It should also be noted that the derivation of photoelastic coefficients was not published for the first time in [22] as it is claimed in [4]. The photoelastic effect was not even mentioned in [22].

A confusion with the photoelastic coefficients $C_{r, \theta}$ will be discussed in more detail for the temperature change [23, 24]

$$
\tilde{T}(r) \approx T_{0}+T_{2}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{2}+T_{4}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{4}, \quad(28)
$$

where $T_{0,2,4}$ are the coefficients depending (in principle) on the fitting radius $R_{\mathrm{f}} \leqslant R$ and the transversal pump beam distribution. A particularly simple solution is found for the case of the homogeneous heat loading $Q_{0}$ of a conventional cylindrical $\mathrm{AE}$ with temperature-independent thermal conductivity coefficient $k_{0}$ if only the radial heat flow is considered:

$$
T_{0}=Q_{0} / 2 h R+Q_{0} R^{2} / 4 k_{0}, T_{2}=-Q_{0} R^{2} / 4 k_{0}, T_{4}=0,(29)
$$

where $R$ is the radius of the rod, $h$ is a convective heat transfer coefficient, $R_{\mathrm{f}}=R[3,4]$. Unfortunately, this simplified version of the parabolic temperature distribution was mostly used in the literature. The parabolic pump or temperature dependence of thermal conductivity for homogeneous pumping leads to temperature distributions with $T_{4} \neq 0[23,24]$. The integrals (16) can be easily calculated

$$
T^{(r)}=T_{0}+\left(T_{2} / 2\right)\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{2}+\left(T_{4} / 3\right)\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{4}, \quad(30 a)
$$

$$
\tilde{T}=-\left(T_{2} / 2\right)\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{2}-\left(2 T_{4} / 3\right)\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{4}. \quad(30 b)
$$

The insertion of equation (30) into equation (22a) results in

$$
\Delta n_{r, \theta}^{(j)}(r)=n^{(0)} T_{0}+n_{r, \theta}^{(2)} T_{2}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{2}+n_{r, \theta}^{(4)} T_{4}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{4}, \quad(31 a)
$$

$$
n^{(0)}=\beta_{\sigma}+A_{1}^{(\sigma j)}\left(1-T^{(R)} / T_{0}\right), \quad(31 b)
$$

$$
n_{r, \theta}^{(2)}=\beta_{\sigma}+A_{1}^{(\sigma j)} \pm A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} / 2, \quad(31 c)
$$

$$
n_{r, \theta}^{(4)}=\beta_{\sigma}+A_{1}^{(\sigma j)} \pm 2 A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} / 3. \quad(31 d)
$$

Now, taking into account the definition of the photoelastic coefficients [3,4]

$$
n_{r, \theta}^{(2)}=\beta_{\sigma}+2 n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} C_{r, \theta}^{(\sigma j)}, \quad(32 a)
$$
the different expressions for these coefficients
$$
C_{r, \theta}^{(\sigma j)}=\left[A_{1}^{(\sigma j)} \pm A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} / 2\right] /\left(2 n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\right) \quad(32 b)
$$
may be found by the use of different presentations (23)-(24) for $A_{1}^{(\sigma j)}$ and $A_{2}^{(\sigma j)}$. It is now clear that the expressions for coefficients $C_{r, \theta}$ should depend on the plane strain or plane stress approximation and on particular thermo-optic coefficients, which are used in expressions (22) for the thermal refractive index change. Obtaining widely known coefficients $C_{r, \theta}^{(\sigma 1)}$ $[3,4,11]$, the plane strain approximation and the coefficient $\beta_{\sigma}$ should be used. Using the plane stress approximation, the new coefficients $C_{r, \theta}^{(\sigma 2)}$ may be found.

In the newly defined $C_{r, \theta}^{(\varepsilon j)}$-coefficients [4], the coefficient $\beta_{\varepsilon}=(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon}$ was used. Therefore, the term (31c) should be expressed as $n_{r, \theta}^{(2)}=\beta_{\varepsilon}+A_{1}^{(\varepsilon j)} \pm A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} / 2$. Thus, using relations (8) and formula (26), the following expressions are obtained
$$
C_{r, \theta}^{\varepsilon 1,2}=\left[A_{1}^{(\varepsilon j)} \pm A_{2}^{(\sigma j)} / 2\right] /\left(2 n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}\right)=C_{r, \theta}^{\sigma 1,2}-\frac{p_{11}+2 p_{12}}{4}. \quad(33 a)
$$

These relations appear as
$$
C_{r, \theta}^{\varepsilon 1,2}=C_{r, \theta}^{\sigma 1,2}-\frac{E}{4(1-2 \nu)}\left(\pi_{11}+2 \pi_{2}\right), \quad(33 b)
$$
if piezo-optical coefficients are used.

Hence, eight different expressions for coefficients $C_{r, \theta}$ may be used. Afterwards, the fourth order photoelastic coefficients $C_{r, \theta A b}^{(\sigma 1)}$ were calculated [23]. The set of new eight photoelastic coefficients may be found. However, we think that knowledge of all these coefficients is not as useful as was previously thought. The main reason for our opinion is the notion that they are applicable only to the special temperature distribution. Secondly, their expressions are not simpler than the expressions for coefficients $A_{1,2}^{(\sigma j)}$ or $A_{1,2}^{(\varepsilon j)}$, which can be used for different temperature distributions. For example, it is easy to see from (22) that the induced birefringence is proportional to $2 \bar{T}(r)=-T_{2}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{2}-\left(4 T_{4} / 3\right)\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{4}$ for the temperature distribution (30). In standard usage [4], the birefringence is proportional to the temperature difference $\delta T(r)=T(0)-T(r)=-T_{2}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{2}-T_{4}\left(r / R_{\mathrm{f}}\right)^{4}$. It is seen that the latter statement is formally true for parabolic temperature distribution only $\left(T_{4}=0\right)$ because the fourth order term $\left(T_{4} \neq 0\right)$ contributes differently to $2 \bar{T}(r)$ and $\delta T(r)$ as compared with the parabolic term.

The thermally induced refractive index change (22) is responsible for the main part of $\mathrm{OPD}_{r, \theta}(r)$ for the signal beam propagating through the rod-shaped AE because the bulging contribution is low in this case [5]. For the numerical evaluation of different terms in (22) the values of the mechanical and thermo-optical parameters of the YAG crystal should be known. A cubic crystal behaves mechanically as an isotropic medium if conditions $c_{44}=\left(c_{11}-c_{12}\right) / 2$ or $s_{44}=2\left(s_{11}-s_{12}\right)$ are fulfilled [14]. These conditions are best fitted by the following values $c_{11}=3.49 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{~m}^{-2}, \quad c_{12}=1.21 \times 10^{11}$ $\mathrm{N} \mathrm{m}^{-2}, c_{44}=1.14 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{~m}^{-2}$ [18]. Using these values and formulae from [14], the following values of compliance components are obtained: $s_{11}=3.57 \times 10^{-12} \mathrm{~m}^{2} \mathrm{~N}^{-1}$, $s_{12}=-9.19 \times 10^{-13} \mathrm{~m}^{2} \mathrm{~N}^{-1}, s_{44}=8.77 \times 10^{-12} \mathrm{~m}^{2} \mathrm{~N}^{-1}$. The values for $s_{11}$ and $s_{12}$ differ slightly from those obtained in [18]. Now, using relation (12), the Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio can be easily calculated: $E=1 / s_{11}=280$ GPa and $\nu=c_{12} /\left(c_{11}+c_{12}\right)=-s_{12} / s_{11} \approx 0.26$. The refractive index and elasto-optical coefficients $p_{i j}$ of the YAG crystal are well known $[3,4]: n_{0}=1.82, p_{11}=-0.029, p_{12}=0.0091$, $p_{44}=-0.0615$.

Using these values and the recently measured $\beta_{\sigma}=8.48 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~K}^{-1}$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{T}}=6.39 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~K}^{-1}$ [12], it is easy to evaluate the contribution of different terms in (22) for the YAG crystal at reference temperature $T_{\mathrm{r}}=300 \mathrm{~K}$.

Thus,theratio $\left(\beta_{\varepsilon}-\beta_{\sigma}\right) / \beta_{\sigma}=\alpha_{\mathrm{T}} n_{0}^{3}\left(p_{11}+2 p_{12}\right) / 2 \beta_{\sigma}=0.0245$ is less than $2.5 \%$ for the YAG, but for other crystals it may be higher. Perhaps this explains why the difference between $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\sigma=0}$ and $(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon=0}$ was not recognised for such a long time.

The ratio $A_{1}^{\sigma 1} / \beta_{\sigma}$ is less than $7 \%$ and $\left(-A_{2}^{\sigma 1}\right) / \beta_{\sigma}$ is slightly over $18 \%$. Thus, the birefringence term adds (or subtracts) less than $9 \%$ to the polarisation averaged refractive index change.

Let us continue this discussion by analysing the expression for the optical path difference [4]:
$$
\delta \Lambda_{r, \theta}(r)=\Lambda_{r, \theta}(0)-\Lambda_{r, \theta}(r)=\chi_{r, \theta}\langle\delta T(r, z)\rangle, \quad(34)
$$
where the generalised thermo-optic coefficient
$$
\chi_{r, \theta}^{(2)}=(\partial n / \partial T)_{\varepsilon}+2 n_{0}^{3} \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} C_{r, \theta}^{(\varepsilon 2)}+\left(n_{0}-1\right)(1+\nu) \alpha_{\mathrm{T}} \quad(35 a)
$$
is given in the plane stress approximation and the integrated temperature shift is given by
$$
\langle\delta T(r, z)\rangle=\langle T(0, z)-T(r, z)\rangle=\int_{0}^{L}(T(0, z)-T(r, z)) \mathrm{d} z.
$$

The choice of the first term was examined in detail above. The subscript in the first one and the superscript in the second one must be changed simultaneously. The choice of the second term depends on the choice of the first one and on the use of plane strain or plane stress approximations. The expression of the third term depends on the used approximation. Now we see that in equation (35a) the first two terms correspond to the choice of the elasto-optic effect (2c) and plane stress approximation for the description of the refractive index change. Then the latter term describes the total bulging of both faces of a thin disk in the plane stress approximation [4,5]. Thus, such a choice of terms is formally consistent for a disk-shaped AE. As was discussed previously, the use of photoelastic coefficients $C_{r, \theta}$ is valid for the parabolic lateral temperature distribution only.

The end-pumping is commonly understood as a requirement to use the plane stress approximation. As follows from the previous discussion, this is not a correct opinion. But if this so, the smaller coefficients $C_{r, \theta}^{(j 2)}=(1-\nu) C_{r, \theta}^{(j 1)}(j=\sigma, \varepsilon)$ should be used in (35a), not the coefficients $C_{r, \theta}^{(j 1)}$ as is often done, if the expression $\chi_{b g}^{(2)}=\left(n_{0}-1\right)(1+\nu) \alpha_{\mathrm{T}}$ is used for the

last term in (35a). It should also be noted that the coefficient 2 is sometimes absent in the photoelastic term.

Using the parameters of the YAG [12], it is easy to calculate the value of the bulging term $\chi_{dg}^{(2)} = 6.60 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{K}^{-1}$, which is comparable with the value of $\text{d}n/\text{d}T = 8.48 \times 10^{-6} \, \text{K}^{-1}$. Therefore, the contribution of a bulging term to the OPD is significantly overestimated if the expression (35a) is directly used for rather long rods. Actually, numerical 3D simulations [5] in the case of the homogeneous heating of the conventional YAG rod with radius $R = 1.5$ mm and length $L = 5.00$ mm showed that the directly calculated bulging was nearly three times smaller than the expected bulging in the inconsistently used PSs approximation. Besides, the end bulging is not proportional to the parabolic transverse temperature distribution and is rather strongly spherically aberrated. Numerical calculations show also that the PSs approximation describes the bulging effect in a conventional AE well enough when $L < R$. In composite laser rods, the face bulging of the undoped part is very small and the optical path difference cannot be described by the standard formula (35a). Thus, the contributions of face bulging to the total OPD are significantly overestimated when formula (35a) is used for the Nd:YAG rod $\emptyset 3 \times 8 \, \text{mm}^2$ in size [25]. Obviously, the use of free expansion for the long rod $(\emptyset 5 \times 126 \, \text{mm}^2)$ with unpumped ends also strongly overestimates the contribution of the face bulging [26].

## 5. Conclusions

The consistent application of the photoelastic effect and the plane strain or plane stress approximation for the description of a thermal change of the refractive index in the case of axisymmetric heat loading is analysed in detail. The analytical relations between the thermal coefficients of the refractive index at zero stresses and zero strains are found for YAG-type cubic crystals. The analytical expressions for thermal radial and tangential changes in the refractive index in [1 1 1] cut YAG crystal are found. Misunderstandings related to the so-called photoelastic coefficients are eliminated and different expressions for these coefficients are established. Examples of inconsistent usage of the generalised thermo-optic coefficients are discussed. It is shown that the direct use of these coefficients significantly overestimates the values of optical path differences when the plane stress approximation is applied to laser rods with lengths larger than their diameters.
