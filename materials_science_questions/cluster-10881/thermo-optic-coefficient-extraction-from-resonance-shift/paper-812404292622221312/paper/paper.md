# Studies of Time Resolved Spectra from Pulsed GaAs-Lasers
O. Steinvall
Department of Physics, Research Institute of National Defence, Stockholm, Sweden

## Abstract
Studies of time resolved spectra from pulsed GaAs-lasers. O. Steinvall (Department of Physics, Research Institute of National Defence, S-104 50, Stockholm 80, Sweden).
Physica Scripta (Sweden) 10, 186-190, 1974.

This paper reports measurements of time resolved spectra from pulsed GaAs injection lasers of single heterojunction type. A detailed study of the wavelength shift of the broad emission spectrum as well as individual axial modes is given. The transient temperature rise of the junction during the current pulse is calculated and the wavelength shift caused by this rise is compared with the observed shifts.

## 1. Introduction
The laser line of a pulsed diode laser shows a transient shift to an amount depending on the pulselength and current.

The energy separation between the initial and terminal laser levels decreases with temperature for GaAs semiconductor lasers. A heating of the junction will thus cause the laser wavelength to increase. Several sources can be responsible for this heating [1]: (1) joule power dissipation; (2) reabsorption of the spontaneous and stimulated radiation; and (3) nonradiative recombination in the junction region. Several authors have measured these transient wavelength shifts [1-4]. In [1, 2] it is shown that joule heating is the dominant mechanism causing the shift for pulse durations of the order of several microseconds while other measurements [3] do not find this. In [4] it is assumed that joule heating also dominates for shorter pulses (200 ns FWHM) with peak currents of 30 A. This paper reports measurements of time-resolved spectra from single heterojunction diodes with an emitting area of $2 \times 230 \ \mu m^2$ and a cavity length of $300 \ \mu m$.
The diodes were pulsed with 40-180 ns pulses (FWHM) of 10-40 A peak current which corresponds to current densities between $14\,000$-$56\,000 \ A\cdot cm^{-2}$. The results show that joule heating does not explain the observed line shifts. Numerical values of the observed shifts both for the individual modes and for the broad emission are given. As pointed out by Hebner et al. [4] it is important to distinguish between the shift of individual modes and the shift of the whole spectrum.

Detailed knowledge about the temporal behaviour of the pulsed laser diode spectra is necessary when using temperature tuning of a laser as a tool for spectroscopic absorption measurements of interferometry in plasma diagnostics [5]. It is also of importance when trying to improve the coherence properties both in longitudinal multimode and single mode operation [6-9].

## 2. Experimental technique
Three laser diodes of the single hetero-junction type (SH) were investigated. These SH-diodes, here labeled I, II and III were fabricated by RCA (model TA 7610) and specified for 13 W output at a maximum peak current of 40 A. Comparative measurements were also made with an earlier type of homojunction laser (model TAX 2711 from HAFO, The Institute of Semiconductor Research Inc., Sweden). All diodes were driven by a pulse generator which delivered bellshaped pulses with peak values up to 100 A and halfwidths (FWHM) of 40-180 ns. The repetition rate was 100 Hz. The diode could be cooled to 77 K, by mounting the diode on the coldfinger of a cryostat.

The diode spectra were examined with a 2 m Jarrel-Ash grating spectrograph with a dispersion of $8 \ \mathring{A}/mm$ in the first order. The stimulated emission from the laser was collected by a lens of 2.5 cm focal length and 3 cm diameter. The spontaneous emission was too weak to influence on the recorded spectra.
The diodes were mounted with the junction parallel to the spectrograph slit. After passing the spectrograph the laser pulse was detected with a silicon pin photodiode (EG & G type SGD 040). The detector signal was amplified by a low noise video-amplifier and fed into a Tektronix 564 oscilloscope equipped with a 3S2 sampling unit. The oscilloscope was manually set to sample at a particular time $t$ during the laser pulse and $t=0$ corresponds to the start of lasing. By scanning the spectrograph setting, a plot of the spectrum at the selected time was obtained on a $xy$-recorder connected to the oscilloscope output. By repeating this procedure, a whole set of spectra from different parts of the pulse made it possible to reconstruct the time history of the individual modes or the broad emission. The spectrograph resolution during these recordings were $0.8 \ \mathring{A}$ and $5 \ \mathring{A}$ respectively. The measurements were reproducible.

## 3. Results
### 3.1. Average spectrum
The shape of the non-resolved laser radiation and current pulse are shown in Fig. 1. The delay between the current and laser pulses varied between 20-50 ns depending on current with smaller delays at higher current values.

A recording of the laser spectrum at different times from the onset of lasing is shown in Fig. 2. The peak current in this case was 25 A and the threshold current 12 A. The longitudinal modes are clearly resolved and separated by $\Delta\lambda \simeq 3 \ \mathring{A}$ corresponding to a cavity length of $300 \ \mu m$ which also is the value given by the data sheet. A clear longitudinal mode structure was seen for a drive current up to 40 A, the maximum value permitted by the manufacturer. At the end of the pulse in Fig. 2, the oscillation of an adjacent group of lines (mode family, cf. sect. 3.2, 4.2) appears. The center wavelength shift mentioned in the rest of the paper follows the same and dominating mode family throughout the pulse.

By decreasing the resolution of the spectrograph to $5 \ \mathring{A}$ when the longitudinal modes were hardly resolvable, we could measure

### 3.2. Modal behaviour
The number of longitudinal modes that were excited increased with the current, with 10-20 modes simultaneously excited at the maximum output.

The wavelength $\lambda$ of each longitudinal mode is determined by the condition $q \cdot \lambda=2 n l$, where $q$ is an integer, $l$ the cavity length, and $n$ the refractive index of the cavity medium. Due to the dispersion and the temperature dependence of the refractive index and the linear expansion, each individual mode will shift its wavelength during the pulse as the temperature increases. This shift occurs towards longer wavelengths also during that part when the center of the broad emission is shifted towards shorter wavelengths.

A spectrograph resolution of $0.2 \AA$, permitted a temporal study of the wavelength shift of a selected longitudinal mode. Fig. 6 gives an example of the shift of a strong mode during a pulse with a peak current of 25 A. The strong modes in the center of the spectrum gave reproducible recordings, but not the weak modes. According to the theory of resonant modes given in [10] the satellite lines in Fig. 6 can be interpreted as resonances of the transverse field parallel to the junction and thus belonging to different mode number $n$. The separation between adjacent satellite lines was equal or less than $0.3$ Å.

The longitudinal modes were most often shifted towards longer wavelengths during the laser pulse and the rate of this shift corresponded very well with the rate of temperature increase estimated in section 4.1 (cf. Fig. 6). An average temperature coefficient was calculated for the mode shift. For the homojunction lasers it varied between $0.4 - 0.60$ Å/K and for the SH-lasers $0.15 - 0.30$ Å/K. The first coefficient is in good agreement with the estimation based on available data of the refractive index [12] and on linear expansion [13], while the second is lower by a factor 2.

Exceptions from the behaviour of the mode shift as given in Fig. 6 could be seen for the SH-lasers in that sudden mode shifts (mode jumps), larger than $1$ Å, could occur often in the beginning or at the end of the pulse within a time period of 10-20 ns (cf. the spectra at 10 and 30 ns and at 70 and 90 ns in Fig. 2).

According to the temperature shift of the center wavelength and the transient temperature rise estimated in section 4.1, these sudden shifts are too large to correspond to heating. They could also occur towards shorter wavelengths, opposite to the heating effect.

### 4. Discussion
#### 4.1. Average spectrum
The shift of the center of the spectrum towards longer wavelengths which took place during the whole laser pulse for the homojunction diodes and during the last part of the pulse for the SH-diodes, can accurately be described as a heating effect.

Assume that the diode crystal is a semi-infinite solid $0 < x < \infty$ with $x = 0$ corresponding to the heat sink remaining at constant temperature and $x = \infty$ to the thick wire contact. This gives us the solution $\Delta T_{c}(t)$ [3] for the temperature increase at the center of the junction when the current pulse has a rectangular form. Allowing the current and thereby the power dissipation $P$ to have an arbitrary pulse shape we get the following expression for the temperature rise at the center of the junction after a time $t$ [see e.g. 14]

\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\frac{1}{\varrho c V} \int_{0}^{t} P(\tau) \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} \tau} \Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}(t-\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau
\tag{1}
$$

where $\varrho$ is the density, $c$ the specific heat and, $V$ the volume of the junction. $P(\tau)$ is the power dissipated in the junction and is estimated as $P(\tau)=1.4\ I(\tau)$ where $I$ is the current in amps and 1.4 V correspond to a voltage drop across the junction corresponding to the photon energy. The joule heating is ignored in the calculations since the temperature increase was found to be linear against current. The following numerical values were inserted in (1): $I_{\text{max}}=40$ A, $\varrho = 5\ 370\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$, $c=320\ \text{J}\cdot\text{kg}^{-1}\ \text{deg}^{-1}$, $V=14\cdot 10^{-14}\ \text{m}^3$ and the current pulse was assumed to have the exponential form $I(t)=I_{\text{max}}\ (t/t_m)^2 \exp\left(-(2(t/t_m)) +2\right)$ with $I(t_m)$ $=I_{\text{max}}$. Fig. 9 shows the solution of (1) for different values of $t_m$, and a plot where the temperature rise has been calculated assuming adiabatic heating by a rectangular current pulse with a length equal to the FWHM-value of the pulse with $t_m=90$ ns.

The shift towards shorter wavelengths observed for the SH-lasers, was found to take place during the increasing part of the current. Several explanations have been given in the literature for the shift towards shorter wavelengths. In [16] the same effect has been observed for the same type of laser (close confinement type [17]) and there it is assumed that the shift to shorter wavelengths with increasing current is associated with the impurity profile of the GaAs-material resulting in a current dependence of the hole Fermi level.

Popov et al. [18] has shown that the influence of spatial inhomogeneity of excitation, due to electron diffusion, will cause successive oscillation of longitudinal modes of shorter wavelength when the current increases. This will lead to a shift of the whole spectrum towards shorter wavelengths unless the shift in opposite direction caused by heating is stronger.

### 4.2. Modal behaviour
Fig. 8 shows how the mode shift follows the temperature rise as calculated in the previous section. Exceptions from this behaviour were seen by those large and sudden shifts mentioned in section 3.2 which do not seem to follow the calculated temperature rise. Several effects can explain this. One is simply that the longitudinal modes recorded at a certain sampling time, later on will be mixed or replaced by adjacent modes belonging to another transverse mode family (with different $m$). Following the earlier notations, the mode $(m, n, q)$ disappears during the pulse and a new adjacent mode $(m', n, q')$ starts to oscillate, wrongly identified as $(m, n, q)$. Consequently the wavelength difference between $(m, n, q)$ and $(m', n, q')$ does not correspond to any real mode shift.

Lateral dispersion (parallel to the junction plane) can also

explain some of these mode jumps, which did not correspond to the temperature rise. Limiting the beam in the junction plane by an aperture in front of the diode permitted a study of different lateral portions of the beam. It was found that large mode jumps occurring in the beginning of the laser pulse were sensitive to the lateral angle (the angle, measured in the junction plane, between the observation line and the normal of the emitting surface).

Referring to the investigation of lateral dispersion of longitudinal modes by Sommers [19], a change of the dominant lateral angle of emission could cause the observed large mode shifts.

The measured wavelength spacing between three consecutive mode families (14 and 41 Å according to Fig. 7), rules out the presence of pure Hermite-Gaussian modes as that would imply equal spacings between consecutive mode groups. Instead, the use of the dispersion relation given by Sommers [19] for rectangular cavity modes leads to the following separation:

$$
(\Delta \lambda)_{m}=\lambda_{m+1, n, q}-\lambda_{m, n, q}=\frac{(2 m+1) \lambda_{0}^{3}}{2 \gamma(2 n d)^{2}} \tag{2}
$$

where $n$ is the refractive index of the cavity, $\lambda_{0}$ the free space wavelength with indices $(0,1,1)$ near the center of the spectrum, $\gamma$ a dispersion factor and $d$ the thickness of the junction. Assigning the three observed transverse modes with the mode numbers $m=0,1,2$ and setting $n=3.6$, $\gamma=1.3$ [19] and $d=2.0\ \mu\text{m}$, we get $(\Delta \lambda)_{0}=14.3$ and $(\Delta \lambda)_{1}=43.0$ Å which is in good agreement with the measured values.

Care has been taken when evaluating the temperature shift of the average spectrum described in section 3.1 to follow the time history of the same mode family as the switching between these does not correspond to the temperature increase.
