## ABSTRACT

Self phase modulation is a nonlinear effect that is observed when a laser beam is focused on to a high-absorbing thermal medium. A regular tea sample in a plastic cuvette is used as the nonlinear absorbing sample. The change in the refractive index of the medium occurs due to the heat generated by the focused pump beam, which in turn changes the refractive index. In this paper, self phase modulation is investigated in different ways. An Ar-Ion laser of 514 nm is used as the pump beam and a 632 nm He-Ne laser is used as the probe beam. The probe beam is introduced from the opposite side of the pump beam. Ring patterns are observed from the each side of the sample. Regular far field ring patterns are observed from the pump beam, and two sets of rings are observed with the probe beam. The behaviors of these inner and outer rings are monitored for different pump powers. The steady state heat equation is solved to obtain an exact solution for the radial heat distribution and far field ring patterns are simulated using the Fresnel–Kirchhoff diffraction integral. Ring patterns are theoretically explained using simulations results, and compared with experimental observations. Finally, an interferometric setup using the low power He-Ne laser is also used to determine the induced change in refractive index. Results are compared with those obtained directly from self-phase modulation and from the probe beam method.

**Keywords:** nonlinear refractive index, thermal medium, self phase modulation, interferometry

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The refractive index (RI) of a nonlinear material can be changed by focusing a high power laser beam on to the sample. When the sample is heated by a focused laser beam, the temperature change inside the material can be modeled using the heat equation [1]. This changes the density and hence the RI of the material, and ring patterns can be observed in the far field due to self phase modulation (SPM) of the incident pump beam. This is also called the thermal lensing effect. By investigating these ring patterns the change of the phase can be calculated and hence the change of the refractive index due to the pump beam can be calculated [2, 3]. SPM has been studied using a pump-probe interaction in a high absorbing thermal medium [4, 5]. It has been shown that a solution of ethanol and chlorophyll with an absorption coefficient of roughly 70/m with a power of 100mW pump beam can generate large deflection angles (~0.001 rad) of a probe beam [4]. The behavior of internally focused Gaussian beam inside a cubically nonlinear material has been studied to calculate the magnitude and the sign of the nonlinear part of the refractive index [6]. Also a simple and fast technique similar to Z-scan has been developed to calculate the sign and the value of the nonlinear refractive index [7]. SPM can also be used to determine the thermo-optic parameters of nonlinear materials. For instance, the temperature coefficient ($dn/dT$) and the nonradioactive quantum efficiency have been calculated for CdSe/ZnS core shell nanoparticles and ink solutions [8]. Liquid crystals also can show the effect of SPM with a focused low power CO₂ laser beam in the infrared region and the nonlinear refractive index coefficient ($n_2$) can be determined by investigating the far field ring patterns [2,9]. SPM diffraction patterns due to thermal lensing can have the effect of convection. The asymmetry (oval shape) of these rings is due to the convection; this has been studied experimentally and theoretically modeled by using Fresnel-Kirchhoff diffraction theory [10].

In this paper we investigate the induced change of RI due to thermal lensing in three different ways. The first entails analysis of the ring patterns in the far-field due to SPM of the 532 nm Ar-ion pump beam. The second method probes the induced RI due to thermal lensing using a low power 633nm HeNe laser. The third method is a Mach-Zehnder based interferometric technique using the 633 nm HeNe laser in which the probe beam passing through the induced refractive index interferes with a reference beam from the same 633 nm HeNe

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

The experimental setup used for the study is shown in Figure 1. For the first part of the experiment a simple setup is created using two lasers and a regular tea sample as the non-linear sample. As can be seen in the inset, a sample of tea solution, which acts as the nonlinear sample due to thermal lensing is placed inside a plastic cuvette. A high powered Ar-ion laser with a wavelength of 514nm is used to heat the nonlinear sample and a HeNe laser at 633nm is used as the probe beam. The pump beam is focused on to the sample using a 300mm plano-convex lens to a focal spot size of radius approximately 32 microns, and a 633 nm unfocused probe beam of radius 1 mm is incident contrapropagating to the focused 514 nm pump. Care is taken to ensure that the probe beam passes through the pump beam, and hence, the induced refractive index profile. Furthermore, the nonlinear sample is placed in a translation stage so that it can be adjusted easily. CCDs 1 and 2 are placed in the far field about 30 cm away from the sample on both sides, and ring patterns at 514 nm and at 633 nm are captured. Filters for green are used when capturing the probe beam pattern on CCD 2, and neutral density filters are also used to avoid damaging the CCDs. For the interferometric part of the experiment, a part of the 633 nm HeNe beam traveling outside of the sample (which is called the reference) is recombined with the probe which has the information of the induced RI profile in a Mach-Zehnder setup, and the resulting interference pattern is recorded using CCD 2. Interference patterns are recorded on CCD 2 before and after heating the sample.

## 3. THEORETICAL MODEL OF INDUCED REFRACTIVE INDEX PROFILE

In this Section, a mathematical model based on the steady state heat equation is used to derive the induced RI profile due to thermal lensing. When a high power laser beam focused on to an absorbing material, the temperature change can be modeled by the heat equation which can be written as [1]

$$
\nabla^{2} \Delta T-\frac{1}{\kappa} \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=-\frac{Q(r, t)}{k},
\tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta T$ is the temperature change from ambient conditions, $\kappa$ is the diffusivity, $k$ is the thermal conductivity, $Q$ is the heat deposited per unit volume, $t$ denotes time, and $\nabla^{2}$ is the (transverse) Laplacian. In steady state, Equation (1) becomes

$$
\nabla^{2} \Delta T=-\frac{Q(r)}{k}.
\tag{2}
$$

Now, the heat deposited by per unit volume, $Q$, can be expressed as

$$
Q(r)=I(r) \alpha=\frac{2 \alpha P}{\pi w^{2}} exp\left(-\frac{2 r^{2}}{w^{2}}\right),
\tag{3}
$$

where $r$ denotes a radial coordinate, $\alpha$ is the absorption coefficient of the sample, $I$=$I(r)$ represents the intensity profile, $P$, is the beam power, and $w$ is the $1/e^{2}$ (waist) radius of the (Gaussian) pump beam. Upon substituting Equation (3) in Equation (2), the analytical solution of Equation (2) is

$$
\Delta T(r)=\frac{\alpha P}{4 \pi k}\left[E i\left(-\frac{2 r^{2}}{w^{2}}\right)-E i\left(-\frac{2 a^{2}}{w^{2}}\right)-2 ln \frac{r}{a}\right].
\tag{4}
$$

In Equation (4), $a$ denotes the half width of the sample (plastic cuvette) and $Ei$ is the exponential integral.

The RI change can be related to the temperature change through [1]

$$
\Delta n(r)=n(r)-n_{0}=\frac{d n}{d T} \Delta T(r),
\tag{5}
$$

where $dn/dT$ is the temperature coefficient of RI change, and $n_{0}$ is the linear part of the RI.

Assuming a thin sample (i.e., Rayleigh range of pump greater than the thickness of the sample), the far-field self-diffraction pattern of the focused Gaussian pump $G_{pump}(r)$ due to SPM can be determined by taking the (scaled) 2D spatial Fourier transform of the pump beam multiplied by a complex exponential $exp - j\Delta\varphi_{pump}(r)$ representing the phase shift $\Delta\varphi_{pump}(r)=k_{pump}\Delta n(r)L$ due to SPM in the nonlinear material of thickness $L$. The far-field pattern of the Gaussian probe beam $G_{probe}(r)$ passing through the same induced RI profile can be found in a similar fashion by taking the (scaled) 2D spatial Fourier transform of the probe beam multiplied by the complex exponential $exp - j\Delta\varphi_{probe}(r)$ where $\Delta\varphi_{probe}(r)=k_{probe}\Delta n(r)L$ denotes the phase change of the probe beam through the RI profile.

## 4. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND COMPARSION WITH THEORY

Figures 2(a)-(c) show typical experimental results for the far-field patterns for the pump beam for incident powers of 100mW, 200mW, and 300mW, respectively. Note that the number of rings increase with increasing power, as expected, due to stronger thermal lensing and hence larger phase shifts. The peak RI change can be approximately evaluated using the relation

$$
\Delta \varphi_{peak}=N(2 \pi)=k_{pump} \Delta n_{peak} L.
\tag{6}
$$

For example, the peak change of the refractive index at $300mW$ is $3.598 \times 10^{-4}$.

Numerical simulations were performed to determine self-diffraction patterns for the pump beam using the procedure outlined in Section 3. Figures 3(a) and (b) shows the temperature change profile and the refractive index change profile inside the sample for the pump beam waist $w$=$32\mu m$, effective absorption coefficient $\alpha$=$130m^{-1}$, pump power $P$=$300mW$, thermal conductivity $k$=$0.58Wm^{-1}K^{-1}$, and $a$=0.5cm. Note that at $r = 0$, Equation (4) becomes

$$
\Delta T(r=0)=\frac{\alpha P}{4 \pi k}\left[\gamma+ln\left(\frac{2 a^{2}}{w^{2}}\right)\right],
\tag{7}
$$

where $\gamma = 0.5772$ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant [12]. With the experimental values inserted, $\Delta T(r = 0) = 22.7584\ K$, which is in close agreement with the simulation value of $22.7541K$ (from Figure 3(a)). Also, the

computed value for the RI change from Figure 3 is $2.2754 \times 10^{-4}$, which is of the same order as the approximate estimation based on the number of rings, as discussed above.

In a similar fashion, the observed far-field diffraction patterns for the probe passing through the RI profiles created by the pump with incident powers of $100mW$, $200mW$, and $300mW$ are shown in Figures 5(a)-(c). Note that these figures are similar to those for the self-diffracted pumps shown in Figures 2(a)-(c), except that there are additional rings near the center. We believe that these additional rings arise due to the fact that the probe

beam of dimension 1mm traverses a larger area of the RI profile induced by the pump (see Figure 3(b)). For comparison, the numerical simulations for the far-field pattern for the probe for a pump power of 200 mW is shown in Figure 6, showing, once again, additional rings nearer to the center. This is possibly due to the nonparabolic nature of the induced RI profile seen by the wider probe beam, as clearly evident from Figure 3(b); this is currently under further investigation.

Finally, the 633 nm probe beam passing through the RI profile created by the pump is interfered with a reference beam at the same wavelength in a Mach-Zehnder interferometric setup as explained in Section 2. Figures 7(a),(b) show the interferograms before and after the 300 mW 532 nm pump beam is applied. Although the effect of the phase change due to the RI profile created by the pump is not clearly evident from visual inspection of the figures, a simple analysis reveals the information, as outlined below.

The optical fields of the 633 nm reference and the probe which are incident on CCD 2 before (b) and after (a) heating the nonlinear sample with the 532 nm pump can be written as

$$
E_{b, a}=E_{r} e^{-j k_{p r o b e} L_{r}}\left[1+\alpha_{0} e^{j k_{p r o b e} L_{r}} e^{-j \varphi_{b, a}}\right], \tag{8}
$$

where $L_r$ is the length of the reference arm of the interferometer, and the length of the probe arm has been assumed to be the same as the reference arm, for simplicity, $\alpha_0$ is the amplitude ratio of the probe to the reference, and $\varphi_{b,a}$ denote the phase shifts incurred by the probe through the sample before and after heating. Using (8), the respective intensities $I_{b,a}$ , such as shown in Figures 7(a,b) can be calculated. If one performs the difference of these intensities numerically, the result is expected to represent

$$
I_{a}-I_{b}=\Delta I \propto \sin \left(\frac{\varphi_{a}+\varphi_{b}-2 k_{p r o b e} L}{2}\right) \sin \left(\frac{\varphi_{a}-\varphi_{b}}{2}\right)=\sin \left(\frac{\varphi_{a}+\varphi_{b}-2 k_{p r o b e} L}{2}\right) \sin \left(\frac{\Delta \varphi_{p r o b e}}{2}\right). \tag{9}
$$

Equation (9) shows that the relevant phase profile created by the heating is embedded in the last term on the right hand side. In fact, numerical subtraction and some post-processing yields Figure 7(c), which clearly show the effect of the induced phase due to the pump beam. Upon counting the number of bright rings and using Equation (6), the peak phase difference and the change of RI can be calculated. The computed value for the RI change is $1.266 \times 10^{-4}$. This is because the tea sample used in the interferometric experiment had an absorption coefficient which is approximately 1/3 of that of the samples used in the pump and probe experiments.

## 5. CONCLUSION

We have investigated the effect of the self-phase modulation for a regular tea sample which can be considered as an absorbing material where the refractive index changes due to thermal lensing when a high power focused pump beam is incident on the material. We have observed the far-field diffraction patterns for different pump powers to demonstrate the effect of SPM due to thermal lensing, and compared the results with numerical simulations of the temperature and RI profiles and propagation of the pump through the self-induced RI profile. We have also monitored propagation of a probe beam through the thermal lens and compared our experimental results with numerical simulations. The presence of additional rings near the center of the far-field pattern arises possibly due the fact that the unfocused probe encounters a larger area of the induced RI profile. Finally, we have reported on interferometric measurements of the induced RI profile using a Mach-Zehnder type setup. Upon processing the interference patterns before and after the pump beam is applied, the induced RI can once again be deduced, and is in agreement with the pump SPM and probe beam measurement results.
