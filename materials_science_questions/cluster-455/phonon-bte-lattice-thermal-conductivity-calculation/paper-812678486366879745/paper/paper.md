# Elastic inhomogeneity and anomalous thermal transport in ultrafine Si phononic crystals

Kouhei Takahashi$^{1,*}$, Masaki Fujikane$^{1}$, Yuxuan Liao$^{2}$, Makoto Kashiwagi$^{2}$, Takashi Kawasaki$^{1}$, Naoki Tambo$^{1}$, Shenghong Ju$^{2}$, Yasuyuki Naito$^{1}$, and Junichiro Shiomi$^{2,\#}$

$^{1}$Technology Innovation Division, Panasonic Corporation, Seika-cho, Kyoto 619-0237, Japan

e-mail: *takahashi.kohei@jp.pansonic.com, $^{\#}$shiomi@photon.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp

### Abstract

Controlling nanoscale thermal transport via phonon engineering is a promising path for novel thermal management in electronic devices and high performance thermoelectrics. Here we report that ultrafine nanofabrication allows us to change the lattice vibrational properties such that reduces the material thermal conductivity in an unusual manner. This is demonstrated in ultrafine silicon (Si) phononic crystals with two-dimensional arrays of through-holes. We reveal that thermal conductivity of Si can be reduced far below the theoretical limit predicted from a phonon particle model when the through-holes are arranged at sub-100 nm periods. Significant elastic softening is identified in these fine phononic crystals, which indicates change in the lattice vibrational properties. Interestingly, we find that the change in elasticity occurs locally and non-uniformly, suggesting that additional phonon scattering mechanism or a change in sound velocity needs to be considered in phononic crystals. The present result provides new thinking for understanding thermal transport of solids and opens a new avenue to tailor phonon transport in thermal-energy-related materials and devices.

Keywords: phononic crystal, thermal conductivity, elastic modulus, softening, thermoelectrics

### 1. Introduction

Materials with nanometer feature sizes show unique thermal transport properties compared to the original bulk material. This has been demonstrated both experimentally and theoretically in various nanomaterials such as superlattices [1,2], nanowires [3-8], and also in a new class of materials called phononic crystals (PnCs), which contain periodic arrangement of nanopores inside their structure [9-18]. The role of nanostructures is to change the behavior of phonons which governs the thermal property of solids. Based on the kinetic theory of gases, thermal conductivity $\kappa$ can be described as,

$$
\kappa=\frac{1}{3} \int c(\omega) v_{g}(\omega) \Lambda(\omega) d \omega, \tag{1}
$$

where $c$ is the specific heat including phonon density of states, $v_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the phonon group velocity, and $\Lambda$ is the phonon mean free path (MFP). The integration is taken over the entire frequency regime. In nanomaterials, modification in $\kappa$ can be expected by either or both of the following two mechanisms; (i) enhanced boundary scattering of phonon particles, which affects $\Lambda$ and (ii) change dispersion relation of phonon waves, which affects $v_{\mathrm{g}}$. The former concept is quite intuitive and traditional where the newly added interface boundary disturbs the motion of particle-like phonons that exhibit MFP greater than the distance between two arbitrary interfaces. On the other hand, the latter concept is rather complicated and novel where the newly added interface provides a platform for the wave-like phonons to create new vibrational modes.

Among various nanomaterials, PnCs have attracted much attention due to their unusual thermal transport properties [9-18]. Previous studies have revealed that PnCs exhibit much lower $\kappa$ than that of nanowires with similar feature sizes even though the nanowires exhibit much higher surface-to-volume (S/V) ratio, which should be structurally advantageous for phonon boundary scattering. Some studies claim that the unusually low $\kappa$ in PnCs is related to coherent interference of phonon waves reflected at periodic interfaces, which reduces $v_{\text{g}}$ by band folding [10,13,14], whereas the others claim that it is within the scope of boundary scattering of phonon particles provided that additional backscattering takes place in PnCs [11,15-17], and recent theoretical studies have reported the difficulties in achieving coherent interference of thermal phonons at room temperature [19,20]. In other words, despite that much progress has been made in terms of both the wave and particle nature of phonons, the mechanism for the unusually low $\kappa$ in PnCs is still under debate and remains to be an open question, and thus, a deeper understanding of the unusual thermal transport properties in PnCs is still needed to clarify the underlying mechanism, especially when their feature size falls below 100 nm.

Here, we have investigated the room temperature thermal transport properties of Si PnC films with two-dimensional arrays of through-holes designed with various pitch $p$ sizes ranging widely from 34 nm to 2000 nm. We show that in ultrafine PnCs with sub-100 nm feature sizes, the magnitude of $\kappa$ falls much below the theoretical limit calculated for phonons

that experience fully diffuse boundary scattering, which is known as the Casimir limit. We discuss that this anomalous reduction in $\kappa$ is neither related to enhanced surface scattering of phonon particles nor to the coherent interference of phonon waves. Instead we demonstrate that it is associated with local elastic softening of Si, which essentially reduces the sound velocity as well as promote internal phonon scattering due to elastic non-uniformity. The present result suggest that phonon transport is highly inhomogeneous in PnCs and provides a new thinking for understanding nanoscale thermal transport in solids.

## 2. Methods

Si PnCs examined here were fabricated on single crystalline SOI wafers by a semiconductor wafer process technology. The electrical resistivity of Si(100) device layer was 14-22 $\Omega$cm. The thickness of the Si device layer was 150 nm for the 100--2000 nm-pitch samples, whereas that of the 34 nm-pitch samples was 50 nm. Figure 1(a)-1(c) show typical scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the PnCs. The PnCs were fabricated in a suspended bridge structure with a total length and width of 110 $\mu$m and 10 $\mu$m, respectively. A square-shaped Al pad with a dimension of 6 $\mu$m $\times$ 6 $\mu$m $\times$ 100 nm was fabricated on the bridge center by sputtering. Electron beam lithography was used to fabricate through-hole patterns for the 100-2000 nm-pitch PnCs. Block copolymer lithography was used to pattern through-holes for the 34 nm-pitch PnCs. The diameter of the through-holes of the 100-2000

nm-pitch PnCs were designed such that the porosity would be fixed at 50% for each $p$. For instance, the diameter of the 2000 nm-pitch sample was 1600 nm, while that of the 100 nm-pitch sample was 80 nm. Due to imperfection of the block copolymer self-assembly pattern, the hole diameter of the 34 nm-pitch PnCs varied typically around 25±3 nm. The porosity of the 34 nm-pitch PnCs was carefully determined by a histogram analysis of SEM images taken at different spots, which were found to be 48±4%. An average hole diameter of 25 nm was used for analyzing the experimental results for the 34 nm-pitch PnCs. Deep reactive ion etching was used to etch through the Si device layer. Vapor HF process was performed to finally release the Si bridges from the substrate. Suspended bridge structures without through-holes were also fabricated to investigate the thermal transport properties of bare Si films.

Figure 1(d) shows schematic of the time-domain thermal reflectance method which was used to measure the thermal transport properties of Si PnCs. A typical two-color pump-probe setup was used for the thermal reflectance measurement. Two types of diode laser with a wavelength $\lambda$ of 852 nm and 785 nm were used for the pump beam and the probe beam, respectively. The pump diode laser, which was used to heat the Al pad, was electrically modulated to create a quasi-pulsed beam with a duration of 3 $\mu$s. A continuous wave beam delivered from the probe diode laser was used to monitor the reflectance change of the Al pad. The power of the probe beam and the pump beam was carefully adjusted in order to keep the

temperature rise of the Al pad less than 10 K. The temporal change of the probe beam power was measured by a balance photo-detector coupled with a digital oscilloscope. The transient thermal response of the samples was carefully analyzed by a FEM simulation using ANSYS software, which finally provides us both the material-$\kappa$ and the effective-$\kappa$ of the Si PnCs (see Supplementary note 1 for details). Note that conversions from the material-$\kappa$ to the effective-$\kappa$ were also done by FEM analysis. All measurements were carried out at room temperature under vacuum.

## 3. Results and Discussion

Fig. 2(a) and 2(b) describes the transient thermal response of the PnCs upon pulsed laser heating. We see that the thermal relaxation time increases by more than an order of magnitude for the 34 nm-pitch PnC compared to those of PnCs with $p > 200$ nm. This demonstrates the high impact of sub-100 nm scale ultrafine nanostructuring on the thermal transport properties.

Figure 2(c) shows the $p$ dependence of $\kappa$ of the Si PnCs. Both the material-$\kappa$ as well as the effective-$\kappa$, which includes the classical geometric effect of porous media, are depicted in the figure. We initially measured $\kappa$ of bare Si films with a thickness of 150 nm and 50 nm, which were found to show good agreement with the previous report [21]. The material-$\kappa$ of the 2000 nm-pitch Si PnCs were measured to be $65\ \mathrm{Wm^{-1}K^{-1}}$, which was slightly smaller than that of the bare film with the same thickness $(75\ \mathrm{Wm^{-1}K^{-1}})$. Phonon propagation is thus disturbed

even in PnCs with $p$ as large as 2000 nm. Further suppression in $\kappa$ is observed with decreasing the size of $p$, where we see its steep reduction when $p$ falls below 500 nm. The material-$\kappa$ reaches a significantly low value of $3.2\ \text{Wm}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ for the 34 nm-pitch PnC. The effective-$\kappa$, which is often more important for practical application, was found to be as low as $1.1\ \text{Wm}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ for this finest PnC, realizing an equal or even higher thermal insulation than the amorphous continuum [22].

Reductions in $\kappa$ with respect to the thin film value have been reported in PnCs with other designs and feature sizes [11-18]. The reduced $\kappa$ in PnCs were mostly attributed to the increased boundary scattering of phonon particles at the side walls of the newly added through-holes, which reduces the effective-MFP. In order to identify the mechanism of the reduced $\kappa$ in the current PnCs, we have carried out a thermal transport simulation based on a Monte Carlo ray tracing (MCRT) transmission model, which quantitatively describes the transport properties of phonon particles (Supplementary note 2) [23]. Note that the MCRT method has been verified against Monte Carlo method solving the phonon distribution function [24-27]. Since we consider that phonons scatter fully diffusively when they hit surface boundaries, this simulation provides us the low $\kappa$ limit for phonon particles that experience fully diffuse boundary scattering, i.e., the Casimir limit. The MCRT simulation incorporates the effect of backscattering and its validity has been confirmed by studies on various other Si nanostructures [16,17,28]. Note that the MCRT simulation was carried out in

3D space.

In Fig. 3(a), we compare the measured thermal conductivity $\kappa_{\text{ex}}$ with those simulated by the MCRT method $\kappa_{\text{sim}}$. The two quantities show good agreement with one another for PnCs with $p \geq 500$ nm. This indicates that thermal transport properties of PnCs with relatively large feature sizes can be explained within the scope of boundary scattering mechanism of phonon particles. However, $\kappa_{\text{ex}}$ and $\kappa_{\text{sim}}$ start to show different values when $p$ is reduced below 500 nm. Namely, $\kappa_{\text{ex}}$ starts to fall below the Casimir limit at this size scale. The percentage difference $\Delta_{\text{tc}}$ between $\kappa_{\text{ex}}$ and $\kappa_{\text{sim}}$ is plotted as a function of $p$ in Fig. 3(b). We see that $\Delta_{\text{tc}}$ increases with decreasing $p$ below 500 nm, exceeding 100% in PnCs with $p \leq 100$ nm. Note that the pitch size and array of through-holes are irregular in Figure. 1(c) because the 34 nm-pitch samples were fabricated by block-copolymer lithography whereas the other ones were fabricated by electron-beam lithography. The defects of self-assembly in block copolymers lead to a less organized feature. However, the irregularity should not affect the thermal conductivity, as clarified in Supplementary note 3.

Anomalous reduction in $\kappa$ beyond the Casimir limit has long been a subject of great debate also in Si nanowires [4,6-8]. Many of the theoretical studies claim that these low values are within the scope of boundary scattering mechanism if surface disorder is taken into account [29-31]. However, these studies either consider excessive disorder or use first order perturbation theory that highly overestimates the effect of surface scattering. In fact, Carrete

et al. have carried out a Green's function calculation of the frequency-dependent surface scattering rate of phonons in Si nanowires with different degrees of surface roughness and found that $\kappa$ would be very close to the Casimir limit if the roughness-depth is less than ~20% of the nanowire diameter [32]. We have examined the side-wall roughness of our PnC films by a critical dimension SEM and found that the root-mean square (rms) roughness was only 2.1 nm. This corresponds to about 10% and 23% of the size of the narrowest region of the 100 nm-pitch PnC and the 34 nm-pitch PnC, respectively. These features indicate that additional scattering due to surface disorder should not be the dominant mechanism that reduces $\kappa$ below the Casimir limit in the current PnCs.

Indeed, Ravichandran et al. have previously reported that unusually low-$\kappa$ found in ultrafine nanomeshes can be explained by considering a surface disorder layer [10,19]. They have conducted a theoretical calculation by solving the Boltzmann transport equation assuming that presence of a surface disorder layer effectively increases the pore size of the nanomeshes. To further claim that excess scattering due to surface disorder is not the main cause of the unusually low-$\kappa$ in the current PnCs, we have carried out additional MCRT simulation under presence of a surface oxide layer assuming that it effectively increases the pore size as examined in Ref. 19. In fact, we found that addition of oxide layers does reduce $\kappa$ beyond the Casimir limit. However, we also found that we needed to assume unnaturally thick oxide layers, varying from 5-15 nm depending on the pitch size, to reproduce the current

experimental results. Detail result of the additional MCRT simulation is described in supplementary note 4. Using x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, we confirmed that the native oxide layer thickness of our PnCs was less than 1.8 nm, which was much smaller than the size expected from the MCRT simulation. This further supports that surface disorder is not the main mechanism of the low-$\kappa$ beyond the Casimir limit in our PnCs.

The well-established theories that treat phonons as particles thus fail to explain the thermal transport properties of the fine PnCs. We therefore consider discussing the mechanism of the low-$\kappa$ based on the wave nature of phonons. One of the highlighted features predicted in PnCs is the flattened phonon dispersion curve due to band folding effect which originates from the coherent interference of phonon waves diffracted at the side-walls of the periodically arranged holes [9]. The flattened phonon dispersion gives rise to significant reduction in $v_{\mathrm{g}}$, which can thus reduce $\kappa$ below the Casimir limit.

We have calculated the phonon dispersion of the PnCs by solving the elastic wave equation using the acoustic module in COMSOL finite element method (FEM) software (Supplementary note 5) [20]. An additional MCRT simulation was then carried out by partially substituting the $v_{\mathrm{g}}$ obtained from the dispersion of PnCs for the $v_{\mathrm{g}}$ of bulk Si. By partially, we mean that we define a switching frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{s}}$ where the phonons at frequencies below $\omega_{\mathrm{s}}$ interfere coherently, while phonons at frequencies above $\omega_{\mathrm{s}}$ interfere incoherently. As we applied this model to the 100 nm-pitch PnC, we found that $\omega_{\mathrm{s}} / 2 \pi$ as high as 5.5 THz is

necessary to reproduce the measured $\kappa$. This means that phonons with a wavelength as small as ~1.8 nm need to travel freely for several 100 nm without losing their phase information as they cross multiple periodic interfaces. Furthermore, in order for these phonons to interfere coherently with the secondary waves reflected at periodic interfaces, the side-walls of the through-holes need to be extremely smooth with sub-nm roughness so that the phonons can scatter specularly upon collision at the side-walls. However, since the rms roughness of the side-walls of our PnCs is ~2 nm, many of the phonons with wavelengths at this size scale are expected to scatter diffusely. This implies that coherent interference has little impact on reducing $\kappa$ below the Casimir limit in our PnCs.

To consider an alternative mechanism of the unexpected reduction in $\kappa$, we have focused on the general correlation between the thermal and the elastic properties of solids. Wingert *et. al.* have previously reported an extremely low-$\kappa$ in Si nanotubes with sub-10 nm thickness, which was accompanied by a marked reduction in the Young's modulus $E$ [33]. The correlation between $E$ and $\kappa$ that they found is reasonable given that phonons are quantization of lattice vibrations. The speed of sound $v_s$ in solids is defined as, $v_s = (E/\rho)^{1/2}$ where $\rho$ is the mass density. Since $v_s$ is equivalent to the low-frequency limit of $v_g$, change in $E$ can indeed give rise to change in $v_g$, and thus, modify $\kappa$ as well.

Figure 4(a)-4(c) summarize the elastic properties of our PnCs measured by nanoindentation using a probe with a nominal tip radius of 40 nm. Note that only $E$ of PnCs

with $p \geq 200$ nm are shown here to assure accuracy of the data. This is because the distance between the holes at opposing corners of the 100 nm-pitch PnCs becomes comparable to the diameter of the hemispherical plastic deformation zone generated by the nanoindentation measurement. It is known that spatial homogeneity of the material should expand over the plastic deformation zone to assure validity of the measurement [34]. Here, we confirmed that $E$ measured at the bare film region was ~150 GPa, which was consistent with the $E$ measured for a plain Si(100) wafer. This value was obtained with good accuracy at a probe penetration depth of less than 10 nm. Since the plastic zone radius is known to be ~5 times the nanoindentation penetration depth [35], we assume that the plastic deformation zone created in the current measurement develops in a hemisphere with a radius of ~50 nm centered around the probe tip. Detailed experimental conditions are described in supplementary note 6.

In contrast to the bare film region, which exhibited similar elastic property as bulk Si, we identified a clear signature of elastic softening in the holey PnC region. Figure 4(a) shows the $p$ dependence of $E$ measured around the PnC unit cell center. We see that $E$ starts to decrease steeply for PnCs with $p$ smaller than 500 nm. This is consistent with the feature in Fig. 3(b) where $\kappa$ starts to fall below the Casimir limit when $p$ is reduced below 500 nm.

The result supports that elastic softening plays a crucial role in reducing $\kappa$ beyond the Casimir limit in the PnCs. We further show in Fig. 4(b) the $p$ dependence of $\kappa_{\mathrm{ex}}/\kappa_{\mathrm{sim}}$ as well as $(E_{\mathrm{PnC}}/E_{\mathrm{film}})^{1/2}$, where $E_{\mathrm{PnC}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{film}}$ are $E$ measured around the PnC unit cell center and that at

the bare film area, respectively. We see that the two quantities show a similar dependence on $p$.

Since $(E_{\text{PnC}}/E_{\text{film}})^{1/2}$ is equivalent to the reduction ratio of $v_{\text{s}}$ with respect to the bare film, this implies a reduction of $v_{\text{g}}$, and hence, change of phonon dispersion in the fine PnCs with respect to bulk Si.

Another interesting feature we found by nanoindentation is that the magnitude of $E$ differs between different local positions inside the PnC region. As we show in Fig. 4(c), we identified that greater elastic softening occurs near the narrow area between the adjacent holes compared to the area around the PnC unit cell center (also see supplementary note 7). Elastic softening thus occurs locally and non-uniformly inside the PnC. This provides an interesting insight that $E$, $v_{\text{g}}$, and hence, phonon transport is highly inhomogeneous in PnCs. Thus, a model based on single dispersion relation or a single $v_{\text{g}}$ may not be sufficient to describe the phonon transport properties of ultrafine PnCs. Furthermore, such non-uniformity in $E$ can possibly increase the phonon scattering rate with respect to bulk Si. Recent studies have shown that local elastic fluctuations in amorphous materials promote phonon scattering which is responsible for their low-$\kappa$ [36]. The feature shown in Fig. 4(c) indicates that similar internal phonon scattering mechanism may also take place in our single crystalline PnCs, which can alternatively be the cause of low-$\kappa$ below the Casimir limit.

Elastic softening has been identified in other nanomaterials including nanoparticles and nanowires. This is often understood by surface reconstruction accompanied with saturation,

contraction, elongation, and loss of atomic bonds near the surface, which induces strain in the bulk [37,38]. The influence of surface states on bulk elasticity becomes significant in nanomaterials because of their large S/V ratio. This agrees with the fact that greater elastic softening is observed in PnCs with smaller $p$. In addition, from a microscopic perspective, the local S/V ratio varies depending on the local target area inside the PnC structure. The nonuniform $E$ described in Fig. 4(c) presumably reflects such variation of local S/V ratio in PnCs. However, it is also noteworthy that greater elastic softening was observed in the PnCs compared to Si nanowires and nanoparticles with similar feature sizes, but with higher S/V ratios [38,39]. This indicates that S/V ratio is an important but not the only parameter that dominates the change in elasticity. We assume that different degree of elastic softening between PnCs and nanowires/nanoparticles is related to difference in surface tension, where tensile strain is expected for PnCs with concave surfaces, while compressive strain is expected for nanowires/nanoparticles with convex surfaces [37,40,41].

Lastly, in order to qualitatively explain the deviation of experimental data and the Casimir limit via elastic modulus softening, we combined our MCRT with first-principles, as follows:

Firstly, we assume that the softening of elastic modulus is due to elastic strain in the materials, which can be effectively captured by expanding the lattice constant. Then, the strain rate ($s$)-dependent elastic modulus and thermal conductivity at 300 K are obtained by

first-principles-based lattice dynamics (Table 1). The results show that elastic modulus and thermal conductivity significantly decreases when $s$ is increased from 0% to 10%. Then, the next step is to include the results from the first-principles calculations into MCRT. We consider a set of PnCs with elastic modulus listed in Table 1. The relation between their strain rate $s$ and the normalized elastic modulus using the data in Table 1 is shown in Fig. S10 (a) in Supplementary note 8. With the measured normalized elastic modulus as a function of the pitch shown in Fig. S10(b) plotted by using the data in Figure 4(a), we can identify the pitch of such a set of PnCs by a linear interpretation (blue dots in Fig. S10(b)). Then we can calculate the thermal conductivity of these PnCs with strains from 0% to 10% ($\kappa_{\text{strain}}$) as a function of periodicity (the blue dots in Fig. S11). The normalized value $\kappa_{\text{strain}}/\kappa_{\text{sim}}$ is plotted by the green dots in Figure. 4(b). The results show that the thermal conductivity reduction of the PnCs can be well explained when the strain effect is included. Although we can not measure the modulus of PnC with periodicity smaller than 200 nm, the results shown by the blue dots are able to clearly capture the trend of the measured thermal conductivity. Therefore, we finally conclude that the discrepancy between the experiment and diffusive-limit calculation results from the elastic softening of Si. The current results raising a new possibility of manipulating thermal transport via nanostructures, which is of crucial importance in the field of phonon engineering.

### 4. Conclusion

We have investigated the thermal transport properties of Si PnCs with various $p$-sizes ranging widely from 34-2000 nm. We revealed that PnCs with sub-100 nm feature sizes exhibit unusually low-$\kappa$, which cannot be explained within the scope of diffuse boundary scattering mechanism of phonon particles. We also rule out the possibility of coherent wave interference effect which is generally highlighted in PnCs. We identified that the anomalous thermal transport properties of the ultrafine PnCs is associated with local elastic softening of Si, which potentially reduces $v_g$ of phonon waves as well as induce additional internal scattering of phonons. The present result demonstrates that ultrafine nanostructuring with controlled geometry is highly useful in modifying material elasticity, and thus, modify phonon transport with respect to the bulk. Cutting-edge wafer process technology shall enable us to control phonon transport locally at intended microscopic regions in semiconductor wafers.

Our findings not only provide new insights into understanding the nanoscale thermal transport in solids but may also offer a promising technique to precisely manipulate heat flow in microelectronic and thermoelectric devices.

**Highlights:**

-   Silicon phononic crystals with periodicity smaller than 100 nm exhibit unusually low thermal conductivity, significantly below the diffusive limit.
-   Significant local modulus softening in ultrafine phononic structures is experimentally observed.
-   The measured unusually low thermal conductivity is explained by the local modulus softening.
-   The results demonstrate that the thermal conductivity of phononic materials can be tuned by modifying material elastic modulus with ultrafinely-controlled nanostructures.
