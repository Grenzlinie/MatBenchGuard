# Pressure dependence of the phase behaviour of polystyrene in methylcyclohexane

S. Enders, Th.W. de Loos *

Laboratory of Applied Thermodynamics and Phase Equilibria, Delft University of Technology, Julianalaan 136,
2628 BL Delft, Netherlands

Received 20 March 1997; accepted 10 May 1997

## Abstract

The influence of pressure on the upper critical solution temperature (UCST) as well as on the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behaviour of the system methylcyclohexane (MCH)/polystyrene PS 405 was studied. Cloud-point pressures up to 120 bars were measured with a Cailletet apparatus. Cloud-point and spinodal pressures were obtained with a pressure pulse-induced critical scattering apparatus (PPICS) up to 800 bars. Also, some three-phase equilibria liquid-liquid-vapour were determined. The lower demixing temperature is found to decrease with increasing pressure. The upper demixing temperature increases with increasing pressure. The experimental data were described qualitatively with a simple $G^{\text{E}}$-model. This $G^{\text{E}}$-model is able to describe the dependence of the demixing temperature on the pressure. Also, the model is able to predict the merging of the regions of immiscibility that can occur in systems of methylcyclohexane/polystyrene. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Model; Data; Critical state; Liquid-liquid equilibria; Activity coefficient; Vapour pressure; Mixture

## 1. Introduction

Phase diagrams of polymer solutions are classified into two types by the shape of the cloud-point curve in the temperature-concentration plot. One is characterised by the existence of a maximum. This behaviour is called UST (upper solution temperature) behaviour, the liquid-liquid critical point is an upper critical solution temperature (UCST). The other type of cloud-point curve, which lies at temperatures in the vicinity of the gas-liquid critical temperature of the solvent, has a concave shape with a minimum. This curve is named lower solution temperature (LST) curve. In this case, the

critical point is a lower critical solution temperature (LCST). Immiscibility occurs if the temperature of the polymer solution is raised above the LCST or lowered below the UCST at constant pressure. The two regions of immiscibility can merge in systems of a polymer and a poor solvent, which results in an hour glass-shaped temperature-composition diagram. The main goal of this study was to investigate the influence of the polydispersity of the polymer on the UCST and LCST region of the system methylcyclohexane/polystyrene.

Saeki et al. [1] were the first who found both curves for the system methylcyclohexane/poly- styrene (MCH/PS). These authors studied the influence of the molecular weight of the polymer on the UST and LST curve in sealed glass tubes. The cloud-point curve was measured by aid of the pattern of a He-Ne laser beam passing through the solution while the temperature was slowly changed. At the demixing temperature, the intensity of the transmitted laser beam decreased dramatically. These authors used polymers with molecular weights ranging from 37 to 2700 kg/mol. The polymer samples were nearly monodisperse. They found that the UCST shifts towards higher temperature and lower concentration with increasing molecular weight of the polymer. With increas- ing molecular weight of the polymer, the LCST moves to lower temperatures and lower concentra- tions. A merging of the two types of demixing was not found.

Vanhee et al. [2] measured cloud-point and spinodal pressures for the same system using the pressure pulse-induced critical scattering technique based on the theory of Debye [3] and Debye et al. [4,5]. The light scattering experiments were carried out isothermally in a large concentration range for a polymer sample with weight average molar mass of 17.5 kg/mol and a uniformity of 0.06. The light scattering experiments lead to isopleths which allow the construction of the phase diagram displaying cloud-point and spinodal curves. These authors [2] were the first who found hour-glass shaped cloud-point curves for the system MCH/PS in pressure-composition diagrams. However, this behaviour is not connected to the merging of the UCST and LCST regions of demixing, but is caused by the fact that the critical curve of the UCST region shows a temperature minimum in a $P-T$ plot. These findings are supported by the work of Wells et al. [6] who carried out similar PPICS experiments for the system MCH/PS ($M_{\rm w}=22$ kg/mol). In this work, it was found that at pressures less than 400 bars, increasing pressure decreases both the spinodal and binodal temperatures. However, at pressures greater than 500 bars, the opposite trend was observed.

From cloud-point measurements in the pressure range up to 90 MPa and theoretical calculations, Hosokawa et al. [7] found that for monodisperse polymer solutions, the merging process in the pressure-concentration diagram takes place over a double critical point.

Up to now, the dependence of the LCST and UCST curves on the pressure was measured only for monodisperse polymer samples. It is known both theoretically and experimentally that the average molecular weight and the molecular weight distribution can have a significant effect on the location and shape of phase boundaries. In this paper, experimental and theoretical results of the phase behaviour of a polydisperse polystyrene sample are discussed.

## 2. Experiments

The system investigated was the system MCH/PS. The polystyrene was produced by BASF and has a mass-average molecular weight of 405 kg/mol. The uniformity, defined as the ratio of the mass average molecular weight and the number average molecular weight minus 1, is 1.832.

Cloud-point pressure measurements were carried out in a Cailletet apparatus [8]. This apparatus allows the visual observation of phase transitions of a mixture at temperatures up to 500 K and pressures up to 120 bar. The cloud-point pressures were determined for mixtures of constant composition as function of temperature by decreasing the pressure at constant temperature until the solution became turbid. At high temperature (LCST-behaviour), bubbles occur when the pressure is further decreased. This pressure was taken as the bubble-point pressure of the solution. Ten mixtures of MCH and PS were investigated. For every polymer concentration, three different curves were determined. The first one is the UST curve, the second one is the LST curve and the last one is the bubble-point curve. To avoid the thermal degradation of the polystyrene a negligible amount of 2,6 di-tert. p-cresol (Fluka, Switzerland), an antioxidant, was added. A possible thermal degradation of polystyrene was checked by repeating the determination of some UST cloud points after carrying out the experiments at high temperature. The measured points on the UST curve were reproducible within the experimental error. This means that the use of this antioxidant prevents thermal degradation. The experimental error in the cloud-point pressure is approximately 0.5 bar.

The PPICS-equipment is described in detail elsewhere [6]. The polymer solution with a given polymer concentration is situated in a high pressure optical cell rated at pressure of 1000 bar, which is placed in a thermostated water bath. Four optical fibres are fitted into the cell behind sapphire windows. One carries light from a 10 mW He-Ne laser source to the solution. A second fibre is located opposite the beam inlet to collect the light transmitted directly through the solution. Two further fibres are situated at $38^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$ to the incident beam to collect light scattered from the solution. The intensity of the transmitted and the scattered light is recorded and saved in a computer file.

The temperature of the cell can be controlled to within $\pm 0.02$ K and is determined by a Pt100 resistance thermometer embedded in the wall of the cell. The pressure in the unit is generated by piston pump driven by a stepping motor. The system pressure is monitored by a Trandico 2090P pressure transducer/indicator and a separate transducer located above the scattering volume. The stepping motor is controlled by a computer and allows pressure jumps at constant temperature. During this pressure jump the temperature, the pressure, the transmitted and the scattered light intensity at two angles were recorded and saved by the computer. From the breakdown of the transmitted light intensity, the cloud-point pressure was determined. The spinodal pressure is found from the maximum of the light scattering intensity. This maximum of the light scattering intensity occurred at practically the same pressure at the angles of $38^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$. The cloud-point and spinodal pressures at different temperatures were measured for six polymer solutions, differing in polymer concentration. With the PPICS-apparatus, only the UCST behaviour was studied.

## 3. Theory

The phase diagram calculations were carried out using continuous thermodynamics [9,10]. Continu- ous thermodynamics permits the thermodynamic treatment of systems containing a polydisperse polymer by direct use of a continuous molecular weight distribution function such as obtained experimentally by GPC. All molecules of the system are imagined to be divided into segments of equal size. This leads to segment numbers which are proportional to the degree of polymerisation and the molar mass.

Considering a polymer described by the distribution function $W(r)$, where $r$ is the segment number, then the distribution function is defined in such a way that $W(r)\mathrm{d}r$ gives the segment fraction of all polymer species with segment numbers between $r$ and $r+\mathrm{d}r$. For treating a solution in solvent A, the segment molar Gibbs energy of mixing is given by [9,10]:

$$
G=\frac{(1-X)}{r_{\mathrm{A}}} \ln (1-X)+\int_{r} \frac{X W(r)}{r} \ln X W(r) \mathrm{d} r+\frac{G^{\mathrm{E}}}{R T}
\tag{1}
$$

where $r_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $r$ are the segment numbers of the solvent and of the different polymer species, while $X$ is the segment mole fraction of the total polymer ensemble. The first two terms represent the Flory-Huggins [11] entropy of mixing and the excess term $G^{\mathrm{E}}$ describes the deviations with respect to this expression. In the case of a polymer solution, it can assumed that $G^{\mathrm{E}}$ is independent of the distribution function $W(r)$.

In the framework of continuous thermodynamics, the spinodal condition reads:

$$
\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{A}}(1-X)}+\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{W}} X}+\frac{\partial^{2}\left(G^{\mathrm{E}} / R T\right)}{\partial X^{2}}=0
\tag{2}
$$

where $r_{\mathrm{W}}$ is the mass-average segment number of the polymer. The critical condition reads:

$$
\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{A}}(1-X)^{2}}-\frac{r_{z}}{r_{\mathrm{W}}^{2} X^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{3}\left(G^{\mathrm{E}} / R T\right)}{\partial X^{3}}=0
\tag{3}
$$

where $r_{z}$ is the $z$-average segment number of the polymer. The critical point also has to fulfill Eq. (2).

The phase equilibrium conditions are given by the equality of the chemical potentials in the different phases. In framework of continuous thermodynamics, the condition for the polymer holds for all continuous polymer species within the total segment number range of the system.

The chemical potential of the solvent is given by:

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{A}}=\mu_{\mathrm{A}}^{*}+R T\left[\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{A}}} \ln (1-x)+\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{A}}}-\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{L}}}\right]+R T \ln f_{\mathrm{A}}
\tag{4}
$$

It is the sum of the chemical reference potential, the athermic contribution and an activity-coefficient term. $r_{\mathrm{L}}$ is the number average segment number of the considered phase. The quantity $f$, called segment-molar activity coefficient, can be obtained from the quantity $G^{\mathrm{E}}$. The equation of the chemical potential of the polymer ensemble contains the distribution function $W$ and reads:

$$
\mu(r)=\mu^{*}(r)+R T\left[\frac{1}{r} \ln X W(r)+\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{L}}}\right]+R T \ln f
\tag{5}
$$

For a given distribution function $W(r)$ and a given $G^{\mathrm{E}}$-model, the phase diagram (cloud-point curve, shadow curve, spinodal and critical point) can be calculated.

Polystyrene samples can be characterised by the Schulz-Flory distribution function:

$$
W(r)=\frac{k^{k+1}}{r_{\mathrm{N}} \Gamma(k+1)}\left(\frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{N}}}\right)^{k} \exp \left(-k \frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{N}}}\right)
\tag{6}
$$

where $r_{\mathrm{N}}$ is the number-average segment number of the polymer. The symbol $\Gamma$ represents the gamma function. The parameter $k$ is related to mass and the number average of $r$ by:

$$
k=\frac{1}{\frac{r_{\mathrm{w}}}{r_{\mathrm{N}}}-1} \tag{7}
$$

Choosing the solvent molecule as the standard molecule $(r_{\mathrm{A}}=1)$, then we find for the polystyrene investigated: $r_{\mathrm{N}}=1456$, $r_{\mathrm{w}}=4124$ and $k=0.5457$.

For the calculations, a relation for $G^{\mathrm{E}}$, similar to relation used by Qian et al. [12], was chosen:

$$
G^{\mathrm{E}} / R T=X(1-X)(1-d X) \chi(T, P)
$$

$$
\text{with } \chi(T, P)=a_{1}+a_{2} / T+a_{3} \ln (T /[K]) \tag{8}
$$

$$
\text{with } a_{i}=a_{i, 1}+a_{i, 2} P+a_{i, 3} P^{2}
$$

The concentration dependence of the interaction parameter is described by the linear term $(1-d X)$. To represent both UCST and LCST behaviours, a temperature dependence of $\chi$ with at least three terms, like Eq. (8), is needed. The parameter $d$ does not depend on pressure. This implies that the critical concentration also does not depend on pressure.

Using the Schulz-Flory Distribution function Eq. (6) and a $G^{\mathrm{E}}$-Model, which does not depends on the distribution function, all occurring integrals may be solved analytically. After rearrangement of the equations, as suggested by Šolc [13], a simple cloud-point equation is found. The cloud points can be now calculated by searching for the root of one equation.

Altogether it can be concluded that the model is able to describe the phase behaviour of the system MCH/PS. With the model, the UCST and LCST behaviours can be modelled simultaneously. The theoretical dependence of the phase diagram on the pressure can be calculated qualitatively correct. To improve the model, it will be desirable to measure at more polymer concentrations.

Vanhee et al. [2] found that the LST cloud-point curve in systems of MCH and PS can show a low pressure part and a high pressure part which merge into an hour glass-shaped pressure concentration diagram at temperatures below a temperature minimum in the UCST curve. They found that this temperature minimum shifts to higher pressure with increasing molecular weight of the polystyrene. In our experiments, which were performed with a polystyrene of much higher molecular weight than used by Vanhee et al. [2], we only found the low pressure part. Nevertheless, the model calculations using parameter set 2 predicts this merging process connected with the hour glass structure, although the temperature minimum of the critical curve is predicted at much too low pressure.

The model, using parameter set 2, predicts three critical lines in the pressure-temperature diagram (Fig. 12). The critical concentration is at all temperatures and pressures found at a polymer weight fraction of 0.078. The reason for this lies in the separated concentration and pressure dependence of the $G^{\rm E}$-model. The first critical line is situated at higher pressures and lower temperatures and shows a temperature maximum. This temperature maximum is connected to the disappearance of the hour glass-shaped spinodals at temperatures below 325.745 K. There is no experimental evidence that this critical curve can be found in reality or is a mathematical artefact of the chosen model. The second critical line is located at low pressures and higher temperatures. This critical curve is connected to the experimentally determined UST behaviour. It shows a temperature minimum at $T=328.62$ K and $P=104.85$ bars. The data obtained in the PPICS equipment (see Fig. 4) suggest that this calculated temperature minimum should be located at a pressure of 800 bars or higher. The third critical curve is found at high temperature and correspond to the LCST behaviour which was found experimentally. This calculated critical curve is in very good agreement with the experimental data.
