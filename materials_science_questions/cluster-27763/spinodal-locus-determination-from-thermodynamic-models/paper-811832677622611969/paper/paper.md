# Particle Size Effects on the Coherent Phase Equilibria of Binary Nanoparticles

J. Y. Huh $^{1, *}$ , H. Lee, $^{1}$ and William C. Johnson $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Korea University, 1 Anam-dong 5-ga, Seongbuk-gu, Seoul 137-731, Korea
$^{2}$ Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22903-2442, USA

The phase stability of isolated, radially symmetric nanoparticles of a binary system that exhibits a miscibility gap was analyzed by constructing coherent phase diagrams which account for both the surface stress $(\hat{T}_{s})$ and the second-order compositional dependence of the lattice parameter $(\eta_{cc})$ . Although the elastic stress field in a two-phase coherent particle with a concentric core-shell structure is heterogeneous and nonhydrostatic at equilibrium, the appropriate free energy extremized for equilibrium could be expressed as a function solely of the temperature $(\theta)$ , composition (c), and effective pressure (P), which are homogeneous in each phase at equilibrium. The construction of coherent phase diagrams in the three-dimensional $\theta-c-P$ space showed that the miscibility gap can be either extended or reduced by decreasing the particle radius, depending on the sign of $\hat{T}_{s} \eta_{cc}$ , and that the tie-lines lie in the c-P plane.

Keywords: nanoparticle, coherent phase diagram, miscibility gap, surface stress, compositional strain

## 1. INTRODUCTION

For decades, it has been known that the increased contribution of an excess energy associated with a surface can alter the phase stability of an isolated small system when its size decreases to the nanometer scale. For example, the melting points of elemental nanoparticles can be lowered by as much as several hundred degrees Kelvin compared to those of the bulk elements [1,2], and the solubility and vapor pressure of an alloying element can also be significantly changed by decreasing the particle size [3,4]. With the increasing technological importance of nano-scaled materials in industry, interest is growing in the size effect on the thermodynamic stability of such materials [5-7].

Solid surfaces are associated with two different excess energy quantities: the surface free energy and surface stress [3]. The surface free energy is the reversible work involved in creating new surface without changing the surface structure or properties per unit area, while the surface stress is the reversible work per unit area required to elastically stretch a surface without changing the number of surface atoms. Stability requires that the surface free energy be positive, while the surface stress can have either sign. The surface free energy can affect the thermodynamic stability of a small system through its dependence on the composition or surface structure [8]. The surface stress engenders a long-range stress field in the bulk material beneath the surface and, thus, interacts with the compositional or misfit strains in the bulk, thereby resulting in a change in the thermodynamic behavior of a small crystalline system.

Recently, Johnson [9] examined the effect of the long-range interaction between the surface stress and compositional strain on the spinodal decomposition in a radially symmetric sphere. He showed that the surface stress influences the phase stability only through its interaction with the second compositional derivative of the lattice parameter $(\eta_{cc})$ and that, depending on the sign of $\eta_{cc}$ , the stability of a homogeneous alloy against phase decomposition can be either enhanced or diminished by decreasing the particle radius. However, owing to the diffuse nature of the interface in these simulations, each of the coexisting phases in the particle was inhomogeneous in terms of its composition and elastic fields and, thus, phase diagrams could not be constructed for such particle systems.

The purpose of the present work is to construct a coherent phase diagram for the radially symmetric particles of a binary alloy system, assuming a sharp, coherent interface between the two coexisting phases. By defining the effective pressure as minus one third of the trace of the stress tensor, it is first shown that a two-phase coherent particle with a con-

## 2. THERMODYNAMIC FORMULATIONS

### 2.1. System model
We consider coherent phase equilibria in the isolated, spherical particles of a substitutional $A$-$B$ binary alloy, which exhibits a miscibility gap in the absence of stress. The two isostructural phases, $\alpha$ and $\beta$, differ in composition $c$, as measured in mole fractions of component $B$, and are assumed to be isotropic in all material aspects. The elastic constants of the alloy are independent of its composition, but the lattice parameter is a nonlinear function of the composi- tion. As depicted in Fig. 1, the two coexisting phases in a spherical particle of radius $R_{o}$ are assumed to be configured as a concentric core-shell structure with a sharp, coherent interface between them, so that the particle exhibits radial symmetry. The core with an outer radius, $R_{o}^{\beta}$, is designated as the $\beta$ phase and the shell as the $\alpha$ phase. Any excess ther modynamic quantities associated with the coherent $\alpha$-$\beta$ interface are neglected, but the particle surface possesses an isotropic surface stress, $\hat{T}_{ij}=\hat{T}_{s}\delta_{ij}$, ($i,j$ in the plane of the sur- face) that is independent of the composition and deforma- tion. The particle is also subjected to an external traction, $t^{o}$, acting normal to the surface.

![](./images/811832677622611969_1.jpg)

Fig. 1. Schematic diagram depicting the concentric core-shell struc- ture of a spherical, two-phase particle. The core is the $\beta$ phase embed- ded coherently in the $\alpha$ phase of the shell. There is an external traction, $t^{o}$, acting normal to the particle surface that is also related to the isotropic surface stress, $\hat{T}_{s}$.

In the following treatment, a small-strain linear elasticity is applied and the stress-free state of component $A$ is taken as the reference state for the measurements of the strain and displacements. Owing to its radial symmetry, the overall free energy of the spherical particle to be minimized for equilib- rium at a given set of temperature ($\theta$), alloy composition ($c_{o}$), external traction ($t^{o}$), and particle radius ($R_{o}$) is expressed in functional form [9]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F= & 4 \pi \int_{0}^{R_{o}^{\beta}} f_{v}^{\beta}\left(\theta, c, E_{i j}\right) r^{2} d r+4 \pi \int_{R_{o}^{\beta}}^{R_{o}} f_{v}^{\alpha}\left(\theta, c, E_{i j}\right) r^{2} d r \\
& +4 \pi R_{o}^{2}\left\{f_{s}^{o}+\left(\frac{2 \hat{T}_{s}}{R_{o}}-t^{o}\right) u\left(R_{o}\right)\right\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

where $f_{v}$ is the Helmholtz free energy density of the phase designated by the superscript and is a function of the local composition ($c$) and the linearized strain tensor ($E_{ij}$), $f_{s}^{o}$ is the surface free energy density in the strain-free state that will be assumed to be a constant, $u\left(R_{o}\right)$ is the radial component of the displacement vector at the particle surface, and $r$ is the radial position coordinate.

### 2.2. Elasticity
Mechanical equilibrium requires that the divergence of the Cauchy stress tensor, $T_{ij}$, vanish in each phase:

$$
T_{ij,j}=0.
\tag{2}
$$

The stress tensor ($T_{ij}$) and the strain tensor ($E_{ij}$) are related by

$$
T_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\left[E_{kl}-\varepsilon(c)\delta_{kl}\right],
\tag{3}
$$

where $C_{ijkl}$ is the elastic stiffness tensor, which is isotropic and composition-independent. $\varepsilon(c)$ is the compositional strain engendered by the composition-dependent lattice parameter and is assumed to be a quadratic function of the composition:

$$
\varepsilon(c)=\eta_{c} c+\frac{1}{2} \eta_{c c} c^{2},
\tag{4}
$$

where $\eta_{c}$ and $\eta_{cc}$ are constants.

The coherency constraint in the particle requires the conti- nuity of displacement and traction at the $\alpha$-$\beta$ interface, $r=R_{o}^{\beta}$. Because of the assumed symmetry, the displacement vector has only the radial component, $u\left(r\right)$, and only the diagonal components of the strain and strain tensors are non- zero. Thus, the continuity conditions are expressed by

$$
u^{\beta}\left(R_{o}^{\beta}\right)=u^{\alpha}\left(R_{o}^{\beta}\right) \text{ and } T_{rr}^{\beta}\left(R_{o}^{\beta}\right)=T_{rr}^{\alpha}\left(R_{o}^{\beta}\right).
\tag{5}
$$

The mechanical boundary condition at the external surface,

$r=R_{o}$, is assured by
$$
T_{r r}^{\alpha}\left(R_{o}\right)=t^{o}-\frac{2 \hat{T}_{s}}{R_{o}}\left(\equiv-P_{o}\right),
\tag{6}
$$
where $P_{o}$ can be considered as an effective external pressure exerted on the particle. It is noted that the effective external pressure $(P_{o})$ is inversely proportional to the particle radius $(R_{o})$ at a given surface stress $(\hat{T}_{s})$ and external traction $(t^{o})$.

When each of the coexisting phases in the core-shell structured particle is assumed to be uniform in composition, the displacement fields in the core $(\beta)$ and shell $(\alpha)$ phases, satisfying the mechanical equilibrium condition, Eq. 2, are, respectively,
$$
u^{\beta}(r)=A^{\beta} r \text { and } u^{\alpha}(r)=A^{\alpha} r+\frac{B^{\alpha}}{r^{2}},
\tag{7}
$$
where $A^{\beta}, A^{\alpha}$, and $B^{\alpha}$ are constants to be determined from the three conditions in Eqs. 5 and 6. Substituting the nonzero strain components of $E_{\theta \theta}=E_{\phi \phi}=u / r$ and $E_{r r}=d u / d r$ into Eq. 3 shows that the trace of the stress tensor, $T_{i i}$, is uniform in each phase as
$$
T_{i i}^{\beta}=9 K\left(A^{\beta}-\varepsilon^{\beta}\right) \text { and } T_{i i}^{\alpha}=9 K\left(A^{\alpha}-\varepsilon^{\alpha}\right),
\tag{8}
$$
where $K$ is the bulk modulus. Chemical equilibrium requires that the diffusion potential [10] be uniform throughout the system. The diffusion potential in an isotropic system depends on the elastic state only through the interaction of the compositional strain with the trace of the stress tensor. Therefore, the uniformity of the trace of the stress tensor in each phase guarantees that chemical equilibrium, as well as mechanical equilibrium, can be obtained for the particle with a homogeneous composition field in each of the coexisting phases.

When the effective pressure, $P$, in each phase is defined as $\mathrm{P}=-T_{i i} / 3$, the coherency constraint, Eq. 5, ensures that
$$
\frac{\Gamma}{3 K} P^{\beta}-\varepsilon^{\beta}=\frac{\Gamma}{3 K} P^{\alpha}-\varepsilon^{\alpha}(\equiv e)
\tag{9}
$$
where $\Gamma=(3 K+4 \mu) / 4 \mu$, with $\mu$ being the shear modulus. Then, the mechanical boundary condition at $r=R_{o}$, Eq. 6, can be rewritten as
$$
P_{o}=z P^{\beta}+(1-z) P^{\alpha}
\tag{10}
$$
where $z=\left(R_{o}^{\beta} / R_{o}\right)^{3}$ is the phase fraction of the $\beta$ phase in the particle. Eq. 9 indicates that an effective strain defined as $e=(\Gamma / 3 K) P-\varepsilon$ must be uniform everywhere in the two-phase coherent particle at equilibrium, whereas Eq. 10 implies that the effective pressure, $P$, can be classified as a thermodynamic density variable [11] similarly to the composition, $c$.

### 2.3. Governing equations
In the following treatment, the particle is assumed to satisfy both the mechanical and chemical equilibrium conditions, while each of the coexisting phases is uniform in composition. The system free energy, $F$ given by Eq. 1, can be recast into the average free energy density of the system, $\overline{F}_{v}$, that must be minimized for equilibrium at a given set of temperature $(\theta)$, alloy composition $(c_{o})$, and effective external pressure $(P_{o})$:
$$
\overline{F}_{v}\left(\theta, c_{o}, P_{o}\right)=z \overline{f}_{v}^{\beta}\left(\theta, c^{\beta}, P^{\beta}\right)+(1-z) \overline{f}_{v}^{\alpha}\left(\theta, c^{\alpha}, P^{\alpha}\right) \quad(11)
$$
where $\overline{f}_{v}$ is the average free energy density of the phase designated by the superscript and is a function solely of the temperature, composition, and effective pressure:
$$
\overline{f}_{v}(\theta, c, P)=f_{v}^{o}(\theta, c)-\frac{\Gamma}{2 K} P^{2}+3 \varepsilon P.
\tag{12}
$$

In Eq. 12, $f_{v}^{o}$ is the Helmholtz free energy density in the stress-free state and, for the purpose of the this study, will be assumed to obey regular solution thermodynamics. It is noted that, even if the shell phase, $\alpha$, is nonhydrostatically stressed with inhomogeneous elastic fields, the average free energy density of the phase, $\overline{f}_{v}^{\alpha}$, is expressed as a function of the thermodynamic variables $(\theta, c^{\alpha}, P^{\alpha})$ that are homogeneous at equilibrium, in the same form as that of the core phase, $\beta$. In addition, Eq. 11 implies that the equilibrium compositions and effective pressures of the two coexisting phases are independent of the spatial distribution of the phases in the core-shell structure of the particle.

For a prescribed temperature $(\theta)$, alloy composition $(c_{o})$, and effective external pressure $(P_{o})$, the equilibrium state of the system can be described by the phase densities $(c^{\beta}, c^{\alpha}, P^{\beta}$ and $P^{\alpha})$ and the phase fraction of the $\beta$ phase $(z)$. These five unknowns can be found from the common-tangent construction when the free energy density, $\overline{f}_{v}$ in Eq. 12, is plotted as a function of the density variables, $c$ and $P$, at the prescribed temperature. The tie-line connecting the equilibrium phase densities of the two phases lies in $c-P$ space. Equivalently, the five unknowns can be found by simultaneously solving the five equations consisting of Eq. 10 and the following four equations:
$$
c_{o}=z c^{\beta}+(1-z) c^{\alpha},
\tag{13}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial \overline{f}_{v}^{\beta}}{\partial c}\right)_{\theta, c=c^{\beta}, P=P^{\beta}}=\left(\frac{\partial \overline{f}_{v}^{\alpha}}{\partial c}\right)_{\theta, c=c^{\alpha}, P=P^{\alpha}}\left(\equiv \rho_{o} M_{B A}\right), \quad(14)
$$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial \overline{f}_{v}^{\beta}}{\partial P}\right)_{\theta, c=c^{\beta}, P=P^{\beta}}=\left(\frac{\partial \overline{f}_{v}^{\alpha}}{\partial P}\right)_{\theta, c=c^{\alpha}, P=P^{\alpha}}(\equiv-3 e),
\tag{15}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \overline{f}_{v}^{\beta}\left(\theta, c^{\beta}, P^{\beta}\right)-\rho_{o} M_{B A} c^{\beta}+3 e P^{\beta} \\
& \quad=\overline{f}_{v} \theta, c^{\alpha}, P^{\alpha}-\rho_{o} M_{B A} c^{\alpha}+3 e P^{\alpha}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
$$

Eqs. 10 and 13 represent the conservation of the density variables, $P$ and $c$, respectively. Eqs. 14 and 15 ensure that the thermodynamic conjugates to the density variables, $M_{BA}$ and $e$, must assume identical values in the coexisting phases. $M_{B A}$ is the diffusion potential [10] and $e$ is the effective strain defined in Eq. 9. $\rho_{o}$ is the density of lattice sites per unit reference volume. Eq. 16 corresponds to the interfacial equilibrium condition [13,14] that guarantees equilibrium with respect to a change in volume fraction. The phase boundaries delineating the miscibility gap from the homogenous single-phase region are given by the phase fraction lines of $z=0$ and $z=1$ that can be found by solving Eqs. 10 and 13-16 after setting $z=0$ and $z=1$, respectively.

The intrinsic stability is the stability of a homogeneous system against infinitesimal fluctuations in its density variables. For the coherent system considered here, the only permissible fluctuations are those that are subjected to the coherency constraint, as well as to the radial symmetry of the system. The criterion for the intrinsic stability against such coherent fluctuations [12] is expressed by

$$
\rho_{o}\left(\frac{\partial M_{B A}}{\partial c}\right)_{\theta, e}=\frac{\partial^{2} \bar{f}_{v}}{\partial c^{2}}-\frac{\left(\partial^{2} \bar{f}_{v} / \partial c \partial P\right)^{2}}{\partial^{2} \bar{f}_{v} / \partial P^{2}}>0.
$$

The limit of the intrinsic stability that corresponds to the coherent spinodal occurs when the left-hand side of Eq. 17 becomes zero. Thus, substituting Eq. 4 into Eq. 12 and using Eq. 17 result in the expression for the coherent spinodal:

$$
\frac{\partial^{2} f_{v}^{o}}{\partial c^{2}}+\frac{9 K}{\Gamma}\left(\eta_{c}+\eta_{c c} c\right)^{2}+3 \eta_{c c} P=0.
$$

The second term on the left-hand side of Eq. 18 reflects the elastic energy contribution due to the coherency constraint, which is positive and thus always enhances the intrinsic stability. It is also independent of the particle size. The third term represents the contribution of the work done by the effective external pressure, $P=2 \hat{T}_{s} / R_{o}-t^{o}$, that arises from the surface stress and the external traction. It can either enhance or diminish the intrinsic stability depending on the sign of $\eta_{c c} P$, and the surface stress contribution is inversely proportional to the particle radius, as shown previously [9]. The surface stress and external traction affect the intrinsic stability only by interacting with the second order dependence of the compositional strain, $\eta_{c c}$. This is because, when the lattice parameter depends linearly on the composition such that $\eta_{c c}=0$, the overall volume of the particle remains unchanged during the compositional fluctuations.

The point at which the coherent spinodal coincides with the phase boundary of the miscibility gap is called a consolute critical point. Thus, the consolute critical point for the system considered here occurs under the condition [12]:

$$
\rho_{o}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} M_{B A}}{\partial c^{2}}\right)_{\theta, e}=\frac{\partial^{3} f_{v}^{o}}{\partial c^{3}}+\frac{27 K}{\Gamma} \eta_{c c}\left(\eta_{c}+\eta_{c c} c\right)=0,
$$

in addition to the condition of Eq. 18.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Before conducting the numerical analyses for the construction of coherent phase diagrams, the governing equations are recast into dimensionless form. Using regular solution thermodynamics for $f_{v}^{o}$, the free energy density $\bar{f}_{v}$ in Eq. 12 can be nondimensionalized as a dimensionless free energy, $\phi$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(t, c, \Pi)= & t[c \ln c+(1-c) \ln (1-c)]+2 c(1-c) \\
& -\frac{9}{2} \Pi^{2}+3 \sqrt{\Lambda} \varepsilon \Pi
\end{aligned}
$$

where the scaled thermodynamic variables

$$
t=\frac{2 k_{B} \theta}{\Omega} \text { and } \Pi=\frac{P}{\sqrt{9 K \rho_{o} \Omega /(2 \Gamma)}}
$$

have been substituted for the temperature $(\theta)$ and effective pressure $(P)$, respectively. $k_{B}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $\Omega$ is the regular solution constant which is assumed to be positive here. In addition, $\Lambda=18 K / \Gamma \rho_{0} \Omega$ is a (positive) dimensionless parameter representing the relative strength of the elastic and chemical energies of the alloy system. Then, the conservation equation, Eq. 10, and the equilibrium conditions, Eqs. 14-16, can be recast into the corresponding dimensionless forms, so that the equilibrium phase densities $\left(c^{\beta}, c^{\alpha}\right.$, $\Pi^{\beta}$ and $\Pi^{\alpha}$ ) and phase faction $(z)$ can be determined for a given set of the scaled temperature $(t)$, alloy composition $\left(c_{o}\right)$, and dimensionless effective external pressure $\left(\Pi_{o}\right)$.

Using Eqs. 4 and 20, Eq. 19 for the coherent spinodal can be rewritten as

$$
t=c(1-c)\left[4-\Lambda\left(\eta_{c}+\eta_{c c} c\right)^{2}-3 \eta_{c c} \sqrt{\Lambda} \Pi\right]
$$

and the additional condition for the consolute critical point, Eq. 19, as

$$
t=\frac{[c(1-c)]^{2}}{1-2 c} 3 \Lambda \eta_{c c}\left(\eta_{c}+\eta_{c c} c\right) .
$$

Eq. 23 indicates that the critical composition can be either greater or less than 0.5 , depending on the sign of $\eta_{c c}\left(\eta_{c}+\eta_{c c} c\right)$. If $\eta_{c c}=0$, the consolute critical point occurs at $c=0.5$ and $t=1-\Lambda \eta_{c}^{2} / 4$, regardless of the effective external pressure (or particle radius).

In order to estimate the dimensionless parameters, using $\rho_{0} \Omega=2 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{ergs} / \mathrm{cm}^{3}, K=10^{12} \mathrm{ergs} / \mathrm{cm}^{3}$, and $\Gamma=3$, as representative properties of the bulk FCC metals, yields $\Lambda=300$.

The surface stress, $\hat{T}_s$, is known to be positive for most metals, and of the order of several thousands dynes per centimeter [15]. Noting that $P_o=-t^o+2\hat{T}_s/R_o$ and assuming the values, $\hat{T}_s=4\times10^3$ dynes/cm and $t^o=0$, a dimensionless effective external pressure of $\Pi_o=0.1$ corresponds to a particle radius of $R_o\approx4.6$ nm. It is noted that, for a given set of material parameters and external traction, an increase of the dimensionless effective external pressure corresponds to a decrease in the particle size, since the effective external pressure is inversely proportional to the particle radius.

Fig. 2 shows three coherent phase diagrams when $\Lambda=350$, $\eta_c=-0.05$, and $\eta_{cc}=0.04$. The complete phase diagram drawn in the $t-c-\Pi$ space spanned by the three externally controlled thermodynamic variables is shown in Fig. 2(a). The phase boundaries are depicted by the thick-solid lines for several, dimensionless temperatures and effective pressures. As the (dimensionless) effective external pressure increases, or in other words, as the particle radius decreases, the miscibility gap shrinks. This is because, when $\eta_{cc}>0$, the particle volume is increased by the phase separation and, thus, a homogeneous single-phase state is increasingly stabilized by a larger value of the effective external pressure.

The two-dimensional phase diagrams sectioned from Fig. 2(a) at $t=0.8$ and $\Pi=0.1$ are shown in Figs. 2(b) and (c), respectively, where the coherent spinodals are also depicted by dashed-lines. Since the tie-lines lie in the $c-\Pi$ plane, they can be drawn such that they end on the phase boundary in the $c-\Pi$ phase diagram at a constant $t$, as depicted by the thin-solid lines in Fig. 2(b). However, they cannot be drawn in the $t-c$ phase diagram at a constant $\Pi$ as shown in Fig. 2(c) and, thus, the phase boundary in Fig. 2(c) does not give the equilibrium phase densities of the coexisting phases. Since the phase with a greater composition has a larger lattice parameter for the assumed values of $\eta_c=-0.05$ and $\eta_{cc}=0.04$, the $\beta$ phase of the two existing phases always possesses a lower effective pressure than the $\alpha$ phase, as indicated by the tie-lines in Fig. 2(b). Furthermore, in accordance with the previous prediction [9], the composition of the $\alpha$ phase increases, while the composition of the $\beta$ phase decreases, as the alloy composition $(c_o)$ increases within the miscibility gap at a given temperature $(t)$ and effective external pressure $(\Pi_o)$.

It is also noted in Fig. 2(c) that, in contrast to an incoherent binary system, the critical temperature (point C) does not coincide with the maximum temperature of the miscibility gap (point M). Since $\eta_{cc}(\eta_c+\eta_{cc}c)<0$, both points C and M occur at compositions greater than $c=0.5$ and the critical composition (point C) is greater than the composition corresponding to point M. The loci of points C and M in the $t-c-\Pi$ space are shown by the dotted line connecting the filled circles and by the thick-solid line connecting the filled squares, respectively, in Fig. 2(a).

Fig. 3 shows the $t-c-\Pi$ phase diagram when the value of $\Lambda$ of the system in Fig. 2 is reduced to $\Lambda=100$. A lower value of $\Lambda$ implies that the alloy system has a lower bulk modulus $(K)$ or a larger enthalpy of mixing $(\Omega)$. The shrinkage of the miscibility gap with increasing (dimensionless) effective external pressure is less significant as compared to that depicted in Fig. 2(a). Even when considering that the scale of the dimensionless effective pressure also depends on the values of $\Lambda$ and $\Omega$, this indicates that the effect of the particle size on the phase stability is lower when the particle alloy is elastically softer or has a larger enthalpy of mixing. Since the effect of nonzero $\eta_{c c}$ on the phase stability decreases as $\Lambda$ decreases, the difference between the loci of the consolute critical points (dotted line connecting filled circles) and the maximum temperatures of the miscibility gap (thick-solid line connecting filled squares) is also lower, as compared to that in Fig. 2(a).

Figs. 4(a) and (b) are the $t-c-\Pi$ phase diagrams corresponding to the situation where the coherent, spherical system with $\Lambda=100$ in Fig. 3 has different, compositional dependences of the lattice parameter; $\eta_{c}=-0.03$ and $\eta_{c c}=0$ for Fig. 4(a), and $\eta_{c}=-0.01$ and $\eta_{c c}=-0.04$ for Fig. 4(b). All of the combinations of $\eta_{c}$ and $\eta_{c c}$ employed for Figs. 3 and 4 result in a monotonic decrease in the compositional strain to $\varepsilon=-0.03$ as the composition increases to $c=1$. When Vegard's law holds; i.e., $\eta_{c c}=0$, the lattice parameter depends linearly on the composition and phase decomposition does not result in a change in the particle volume. Then, as shown in Fig. 4(a), the miscibility gap is not affected by the effective external pressure (or the particle radius). The consolute critical point occurs at $c=0.5$ and $t=1-\Lambda \eta_{c}^{2} / 4$, regardless of the effective external pressure, and the critical point coincides with the maximum temperature of the miscibility gap. The miscibility gap at a given value of $\Pi$ is symmetric with respect to the composition. Furthermore, since the phase compositions are independent of the effective external pressure, as shown by the tie-lines on the isothermal plane of $t=0.8$ in Fig. 4(a), the phase boundaries in the $t-c$ phase diagram at a constant $\Pi$ give the equilibrium compositions of the coexisting phases. These characteristics of the coherent phase diagram at $\eta_{c c}=0$ are the consequence of the fact that the effective external pressure affects the intrinsic stability of a homogeneous phase only by interacting with a nonlinear component of the compositional strain.

system considered in Fig. 4(b), the locus of consolute critical points (dotted line) is located at lower compositions than the locus of the maximum temperatures of the miscibility gap (solid line connecting filled squares). The tie-lines drawn at $t=0.8$ in Fig. 4(b) indicate that, as the alloy composition $(c_{o})$ increases at a given external pressure (or particle radius), the composition of the $\alpha$ phase decreases, whereas that of the $\beta$ phase increases.

It is noted that the coherent phase diagrams constructed in the present study are independent of which phase is located at the center of the sphere. This is primarily due to the assumption that no excess thermodynamic quantities are associated with the coherent $\alpha-\beta$ interface. If this assumption is relaxed so that the $\alpha-\beta$ interface can have a finite energy, the phase diagram and thus the phase stability will depend on the spatial distribution of the coexisting phases in the core-shell structure, as shown previously [9]. This is because the radius of the core phase for a given two-phase state varies depending on the spatial distribution and, thus, the contribution of the interfacial energy to the overall system free energy depends on the spatial distribution. It is also noted that the dependence of the phase stability on the spatial distribution can occur if the assumption of the surface free energy density being independent of composition is relaxed.

Finally, we note that the coherent phase diagrams for core-shell structured particles were able to be constructed, because the free energy densities of the individual phases can be expressed as a function of only those thermodynamic variables ($\theta$, $c$ and $P$) that are uniform in each phase at equilibrium, as described in Eq. 12. Since the stress and strain fields in the shell phase are inhomogeneous even at equilibrium, the spatial distribution of the phases in the core-shell structure changes the detailed elastic states of the coexisting phases at equilibrium, although this affects neither the phase stability nor the phase diagram.

## 4. SUMMARY

The influence of the surface stress, compositional strain, and particle radius on the phase stability of isolated, isotropic particles of a binary system that exhibits a miscibility gap was examined by constructing the coherent phase diagrams. The two phases coexisting in the particles are considered to be configured in the form of a concentric core-shell structure with a sharp, coherent interface that is not related to any excess thermodynamic quantities. The effective pressure ($P$) defined from the trace of the stress tensor $(T_{ii})$ as $P=-T_{ii}/3$ was identified as a thermodynamic density variable owing to the coherency constraint. This identification allowed the free energy extremized at equilibrium to be expressed as a function of only those thermodynamic variables ($\theta$, $c$ and $P$) that are uniform in each phase at equilibrium and, thus, coherent phase diagrams to be constructed in the three-dimensional $\theta$-$c$-$P$ space.

In agreement with previous work assuming a diffuse interface between two coexisting phases, it was shown that the surface stress $(\hat{T}_{s})$ and particle radius $(R_{o})$ affect the phase stability of the particles only by interacting with the second-order compositional dependence of the lattice parameter $(\eta_{cc})$. The construction of coherent phase diagrams showed that the miscibility gap can be either extended or shrunk by decreasing the particle radius, depending on the sign of $\hat{T}_{s}\eta_{cc}$, and that the effect of the particle size on the phase stability for a nonzero value of $\hat{T}_{s}\eta_{cc}$ becomes more significant when the particle alloy is elastically harder and has a lower enthalpy of mixing. Since the tie-lines lie in the $c$-$P$ plane, the phase boundaries in a $\theta$-$c$ phase diagram at constant $P$ do not give the equilibrium compositions of the coexisting phases, and the consolute critical point does not coincide with the maximum temperature of the miscibility gap, unless $\eta_{cc}=0$.
