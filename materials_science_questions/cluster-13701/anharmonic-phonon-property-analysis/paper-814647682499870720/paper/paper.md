CrSiTe₃ has attracted recent interest as a candidate single-layer ferromagnetic semi-
conductor, but relatively little is known about the bulk properties of this mate-
rial. Here, we report single-crystal X-ray diffraction, magnetic properties, thermal
conductivity, vibrational, and optical spectroscopies and compare our findings with
complementary electronic structure and lattice dynamics principles calculations. The
high temperature paramagnetic phase is characterized by strong spin-lattice interac-
tions that give rise to glassy behavior, negative thermal expansion, and an optical
response that reveals that CrSiTe₃ is an indirect gap semiconductor with indirect and
direct band gaps at 0.4 and 1.2 eV, respectively. Measurements of the phonons across
the 33 K ferromagnetic transition provide additional evidence for strong coupling
between the magnetic and lattice degrees of freedom. The Si-Te stretching and Te
displacement modes are sensitive to the magnetic ordering transition, a finding that
we discuss in terms of the superexchange mechanism. Spin-lattice coupling constants
are also extracted. © 2015 Author(s). All article content, except where otherwise
noted, is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Unported License.

With little fanfare, chalcogenides have emerged as electronic materials par excellence. For
example, most of the best thermoelectrics such as $Bi_2Te_3$ and PbTe are chalcogenides, as are second
generation (thin film) solar cells which are primarily made from direct band gap absorbers such
as CdTe, $CuIn_xGa_{1-x}Se_2$ (CIGS), or $Cu_2ZnSnS_4$ (CZTS). $^{1,2}$ Many of the best 3D topological insu-
lators such as $Bi_2Se_3$, $Bi_2Te_3$, and $Sb_2Te_3$ are chalcogenides. $^{3}$ Phase change memory is primarily
made from a chalcogenide alloy of $GeTe-Sb_2Te_3$ called GST. Even the highest $T_C$ “pnictide” super-
conductor is in fact a chalcogenide: single layers of FeSe have been found to superconduct with
transition temperatures greater than $100$ K. $^{4}$

Very recently, in the area of 2D semiconductors, attention has shifted from graphene (which
has no bandgap) to single layers of $MoS_2$ and other dichalcogenides that are true semiconductors.
High performance field-effect transistors and phototransistors have been reported in single-layer
devices. $^{5-9}$ With a band gap between 1 and 2 eV and large spin-orbit coupling in the single-layer,
transition metal dichalcogenides also make interesting candidates for spintronic applications. $^{10}$ In

As many chalcogenides form layered structures and can be exfoliated, the attractiveness of combining the complexity of bulk materials with the tunability of single- and few-layer materials has become apparent. One of the most interesting possibilities involves the creation of robust, single-layer magnetic semiconductors. In this context, the ferromagnetism of $CrSiTe_3$ has been predicted by first principles calculations to survive in single layers. $^{11,12}$ Intriguingly and counterintuitively, the Curie temperature is predicted to increase in single layers compared to the bulk, and to increase even further when $CrSiTe_3$ layers are strained. $^{13}$ One of the motivations of the present paper is to gain a better understanding of the bulk properties of $CrSiTe_3$ so that future studies on single-layer $CrSiTe_3$ will have a firmer foundation.

The synthesis of $CrSiTe_3$ was initially reported by Ouvrard, *et al*. $^{14}$ It was found to be a layered semiconductor with clear structural similarities to the heavily studied $MPX_3$ (M = V, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Zn, Cd, or Hg; X = S or Se) materials. $^{15}$ Magnetically, $CrSiTe_3$ was found to be a quasi-2D Ising ferromagnet with $T_C = 33$ K. $^{16}$ The spins were found to point along the $c$-axis. The germanium analog, $CrGeTe_3$, has also been reported with $T_C = 61$ K. $^{17,18}$

$CrSiTe_3$ single crystals were grown using a self-flux technique. Starting materials Cr (99.999% Alfa Aesar), Si (99.999%, Alfa Aesar), and Te (99.9999%, Alfa Aesar) were placed in a 5 ml alumina crucible with a molar ratio of 1:2:6. A catch crucible containing quartz wool was mounted on top of the growth crucible and both were sealed in a quartz tube under approximately 1/3 atmosphere of high-purity argon gas. The sealed ampoule was heated to $1150\ ^\circ$C over 8 h, held at $1150\ ^\circ$C for 16 h, and then cooled to $700\ ^\circ$C at a rate of $3\ ^\circ$C/h. Once the furnace reached $700\ ^\circ$C, the excess flux was decanted from the crystals. The as-grown crystals are plate-like with the in-plane dimension limited by the growth crucible. The plates can be as thick as 5 mm. The crystals are soft and can be easily exfoliated.

Elemental analysis of the crystals was performed on cleaved surfaces using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. A Hitachi-TM3000 microscope equipped with a Bruker Quantax 70 EDS system was used for the measurements. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction patterns were collected on a Rigaku CrystalClear-SM Expert single crystal diffractometer using Mo-$K_\alpha$ radiation. Magnetic properties were measured with a Quantum Design (QD) Magnetic Property Measurement System in the temperature interval $1.8\ \text{K} \leq \text{T} \leq 300$ K. Temperature-dependent specific heat data were collected using a 14 T QD Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS) in the temperature range from 1.9 to 200 K. Thermal conductivity was measured from 1.9 to 320 K using the Thermal Transport Option (TTO) from QD and a 14 Tesla PPMS. A rectangular bar with the dimension of $0.6\ \text{mm} \times 1.0\ \text{mm} \times 8.0\ \text{mm}$ was cut from a large crystal and used for the TTO measurements. Silver epoxy (Epo-Tek H20E) was used to provide mechanical and thermal contacts for the thermal transport measurements.

The optical properties of $CrSiTe_3$ were investigated with a series of spectrometers and an open-flow cryostat covering a wide frequency and temperature range ($30$-$52{,}600\ \text{cm}^{-1}$; $3.7\ \text{meV}$-$6.5\ \text{eV}$; 10–300 K). Data were taken sweeping temperature both up and down to check for hysterisis effects. A Kramers-Kronig analysis was used to relate the measured reflectance to the optical constants. $^{19}$ Here, we focus mainly on the absorption coefficient, $\alpha$. Raman spectra of bulk $CrSiTe_3$ were collected using a Renishaw Raman spectrometer using 532 nm laser wavelength excitation and a $50\times$ optical microscope objective. The exposure time was set at 60 seconds to ensure high resolution spectra. Asymmetry analysis combined with lattice dynamics calculations were used to assign the infrared- and Raman-active vibrational modes and understand their displacement patterns. The optical response of $CrSiTe_3$ was compared with the results of first-principles electronic structure calculations. A traditional bandgap analysis was used to reveal the indirect and direct gaps.

Density-functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the projector augmented wave method and local-density approximation as implemented in the plane-wave code VASP. $^{20-22}$ For all calculations, a cut-off energy of 400 eV for the plane wave basis set was used. The $k$-point sampling used the Monkhorst-Pack$^{23}$ scheme and employed a $\Gamma$-point centered $9 \times 9 \times 1$ mesh for the unit cell of bulk $CrSiTe_3$. The atomic positions were optimized until the interatomic forces were

CrSiTe₃ is a rare example of a layered chalcogenide with silicon pairs. These Si pairs form $(Si_2Te_6)^{6-}$ ethane-like groups.¹⁴ Since the Cr ions have an oxidation state of 3+, CrSiTe₃ has a nominal charge balance of $Cr_2^{3+}Si_2^{3+}Te_6^{2-}$. Each unit cell comprises three CrSiTe₃ layers stacked in an ABC sequence. In this structure, the $Cr^{3+}$ ions are located at the center of a slightly distorted octahedron of Te atoms. The Cr ions and Si pairs, in a 2:1 ratio, form the octahedral site plane and are sandwiched between Te planes. There is nothing between the next layer of Te planes, leading to a van der Waals gap of about 3.3 Å. The Cr ions form a honeycomb sublattice with the Si pairs at the center of the hexagons. The crystal structure of CrSiTe₃ is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Single crystal X-ray diffraction measurements have been carried out on CrSiTe₃ in order to characterize the structure. Measurements were made at 293, 105, and 28 K, and the space group refined from the indexed peaks was found to be $R\overline{3}$ (Space Group No. 148), matching previous reports.¹⁴,²⁵,²⁶ The lattice constants and Wyckoff positions of the atoms at each temperature are given in Table I.

The structures were refined using SHELX from 3183, 3621, and 7774 diffraction peaks, respectively, over a $\theta$-range from 2.9 to $33.8^\circ$. The $R(\sigma)$ values of all refinements were less than 0.15. The lowest residuals were measured at 105 K, so these data were used to determine the errors in the atomic positions, given in the table. It was noted during the refinement that the crystals were twinned along the c-axis, introducing what appeared to be violations of disallowed peaks. It was determined to be a twinning feature since there was no space-group symmetry that matched the systematic absences observed, possibly a result of stacking faults. Additionally, when this was incorporated into the refinement, the residuals decreased dramatically, and all peaks were able to be indexed according to the given structure.

We observe that the lattice parameters have increased at 28 K, relative to the data at 105 K, with $\Delta a/a = 1.32\%$ and $\Delta c/c = 0.62\%$. This corresponds to an increase in the cell volume of 3.3%, indicating that this material has a negative thermal expansion below 105 K. There is little change in the relative atomic positions, and there is no change in the Te octahedra: at all temperatures measured, there is a small anisotropy in the Te octahedra along the $c$-axis. This leads to an easy axis for the magnetic ordering that occurs below 33 K, but there is no change in the crystal symmetry or coordination as seen by the X-ray measurements below the magnetic ordering temperature.

Figure 2(a) shows the temperature dependence of the magnetization divided by the applied field, $M/H$, measured with a magnetic field of $H = 1$ kOe applied along the $ab$-plane and $c$-axis, respectively. The evolution of $M/H$ with temperature indicates that a ferromagnetic transition takes place at $T_C \approx 33$ K, and the anisotropy suggests that the crystallographic $c$-axis is the easy axis. The temperature dependence of $H/M$ is also plotted in Fig. 2(a). Below 150 K, the $H/M(T)$ curves deviate from the linear temperature dependence that is observed at higher temperatures from 150 to 300 K. This suggests that short range magnetic correlations persist to temperatures on the order of 150 K. The coexistence of this deviation and the negative thermal expansion observed by X-ray diffraction indicate a strong spin-lattice coupling in CrSiTe₃. A linear fit of the $H/M$ data in the temperature range of 150 to 300 K gives a Weiss constant of 60(2) K, which is about twice the value of $T_C$. The effective moment $\mu_{\text{eff}} = 3.75(2)\ \mu_{\text{B}}$ obtained from $H \perp c$ data is slightly smaller than $\mu_{\text{eff}} = 3.88(2)\ \mu_{\text{B}}$ from $H \parallel c$ data. The latter is in good agreement with the theoretical value expected for $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ of $3.87\ \mu_{\text{B}}$.

Figure 2(b) shows the field dependence of magnetization measured at 2 K, which also suggests that the easy axis is along the c-axis. The saturation moment at 2 K is 3.08 $\mu_\text{B}$, which agrees with the expected value of 3 $\mu_\text{B}$ for Cr$^{3+}$ with three unpaired spins. Our crystals show little hysteresis. The remanent magnetization is 0.007 $\mu_\text{B}$ for $H \perp c$ and 0.001 $\mu_\text{B}$ for $H \parallel c$, respectively. The coercive field is 20 Oe for $H \perp c$ and 1 Oe for $H \parallel c$.

Figure 3 shows the temperature dependence of specific heat measured from 1.90 to 200 K. A lambda-type anomaly at 33 K further confirms the long range magnetic order. In order to calculate the entropy release across the magnetic transition, we estimated the lattice specific heat with the Thirring model. $^{27}$ The calculated entropy change is shown in the inset of Fig. 3. The entropy change of 1.53 J/mol K across the transition in the temperature range 25 K $< T <$ 50 K is only a small portion of the expected total magnetic entropy $R \ln(2S+1) = 11.53$ J/molK. It is also notable that about 40% of the calculated entropy release takes place above $T_C$ as shown in the inset to Fig. 3. Together with the deviation of the $H/M(T)$ curves from the high-temperature linear relation, this suggests that short-ranged, two-dimensional magnetic correlations persist to very high temperatures, and that much of the missing entropy is released gradually as short-ranged order develops. Strong spin fluctuations below $T_C$ may also contribute to the missing entropy.

The thermal conductivity of CrSiTe$_3$ crystals was measured in the temperature range of 1.90 to 300 K with heat flow within the $ab$-plane only. The crystals can be easily exfoliated which makes the measurement with heat flow along the $c$-axis rather difficult. Figure 4 shows the temperature dependence of thermal conductivity within the $ab$-plane, $\kappa_{ab}$. Three features are noteworthy: (1) $\kappa_{ab}$ shows a glassy behavior in the paramagnetic state and decreases from a room temperature value of $\approx$3 W m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$ when cooling, (2) when cooling across $T_C$, $\kappa_{ab}$ shows a rapid increase, and (3) in addition to the peak at $\approx$8 K, a shoulder at about 24 K was also observed and is highlighted in the difference $\Delta\kappa = \kappa_{ab} - \kappa_L$ in the inset to Fig. 4.

In general, crystalline solids are good heat conductors and lattice thermal conductivity peaks below 100 K and then decreases with increasing temperature following a $1/T$ dependence. However, a phonon glass behavior was observed in the paramagnetic state of CrSiTe$_3$. The rapid increase of $\kappa_{ab}$ cooling across $T_C$ implies that the phonon glass behavior in the paramagnetic state results from phonon scattering by magnetic fluctuations. Such kind of phonon glass behavior has been observed in various systems with strong spin-orbital-lattice coupling or rattling motion of loosely bound filling atoms.$^{28–30}$ The low thermal conductivity of CrSiTe$_3$ at room temperature, which is comparable to that of filled skutterudite antimonides,$^{30}$ and the phonon glass behavior above $T_C$ implies strong spin-lattice coupling in CrSiTe$_3$.

In the long-range magnetically ordered state below $T_{C}$, a regular lattice thermal conductivity is expected. However, the highest $\kappa_{a b}$ of $CrSiTe_{3}$ is about $5.5 ~W ~m^{-1} ~K^{-1}$ at $8 ~K$, which is still low for a crystalline solid. In addition, the fit of the low temperature thermal conductivity using the Debye model gives a rather low thermal conductivity in the paramagnetic state. $^{31}$ These sug gest that the phonon thermal conductivity is not fully recovered below $T_{C}$ . It is unlikely that the shoulder below $T_{C}$ is due to the heat transport by magnetic excitations. Instead, it suggests that the regular phonon thermal conductivity is not fully recovered below $T_{C}$ due to temperature dependent magnetic fluctuations. This, in turn, indicates a strong spin-lattice coupling in $CrSiTe_{3}$ .

As expected, phonons are the most important heat carriers in semiconducting $CrSiTe_{3}$ . There fore, we proceed to characterize the phonon behavior of $CrSiTe_{3}$ using a combination of theoretical and experimental tools. The calculated phonon dispersion of bulk $CrSiTe_{3}$ is shown in Fig. 5(a). As can be seen, a remarkable feature of the phonon spectrum is the abundance of flat bands. This leads to the majority of sharp peaks in the phonon density of states (PDOS) depicted in Fig. 5(b). Similarto the $MPX_{3}$ layered compounds, $^{33}$ e.g., $FePS_{3}$ , the flatness of the phonon branches in $CrSiTe_{3}$  originates from the comparatively large mass of the $Cr$ and $Te$ atoms that reduces the coupling between the vibrations of the Si dimers.

A symmetry analysis is an important complement to the aforementioned lattice dynamics calculations. $CrSiTe_{3}$ exhibits space group $R \overline{3}$ (No. 148) with a $C_{3 i}$ point group. Spin-phonon coupling constants can be calculated from frequency shift data using the well-
known expression³⁶,⁴⁰

$$
\omega^{2}=\omega_{0}^{2}+\lambda\left\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\right\rangle, \tag{2}
$$

where $\omega$ is the frequency of the phonon mode, $\omega_{0}$ is the limiting low temperature frequency of the unperturbed mode, $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle$ is the nearest-neighbor spin-spin correlation function, and $\lambda$ is the spin-phonon coupling constant which is related to the magnetic exchange. There are several ways to obtain $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle.^{36,41}$ Here, we estimate it from an analysis of the spin configuration and compare with that extracted from inelastic neutron scattering measurements. Assuming Cr is the only magnetic ion, $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle$ will go as $S^{2}$ at 0 K. For $\mathrm{Cr}^{3+}, S=3 / 2$, so $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle=9 / 4$. This approximate value is reasonably close to the $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle=2.8733$ extracted from low temperature neutron scattering.⁴² Once $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle$ is determined, the $\lambda$'s can be calculated.

In describing the spin-phonon coupling, it is typical for spectroscopists to utilize a shorthand version³⁶,⁴³,⁴⁴ of Eq. (2),

$$
\omega \approx \omega_{0}+\lambda^{\prime}\left\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\right\rangle. \tag{3}
$$

Using this adaptation and $\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle=2.8733$, $\lambda^{\prime}=\Delta \omega /\langle\mathbf{S}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{S}_{j}\rangle$. The sign of the frequency shift (softening or hardening) is dropped in this analysis. Therefore, $\lambda^{\prime} \approx 0.1$ and 0.2, for the 90.5 and $369.3 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ modes, respectively. The coupling constants are summarized in Table II. The $369.3 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ Si-Te stretch shows the strongest spin-lattice coupling. The Te-Cr-Te bending at $219 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ is, by contrast, not sensitive to the transition.

The ferromagnetic origin of magnetic transition is due to the $90^{\circ}$ Cr-Te-Cr super-exchange interactions. The $\mathrm{Cr}^{3+}$ ion direct exchange is antiferromagnetic, though the large inter-Cr distance of $3.987 \AA$ should make the direct exchange weak.⁴⁵,⁴⁶ The $\mathrm{Cr}^{3+}$ ions are also in a nearly perfect octahedral environment, which precludes spin-orbit effects, and the midpoint of the Cr-Cr distance is a point of local centrosymmetry, ruling out Dyaloshinskii-Moriya interactions. Finally, the Cr octahedra are edge-sharing and the Cr-Te-Cr angle is $88.8^{\circ}$, very close to perfectly orthogonal. This arrangement of the ions would suggest that super-exchange of the type described by the Goodenough-Kanamori rules⁴⁷⁻⁴⁹ would be ferromagnetic, consistent with the observations of ferromagnetism in other layered $\mathrm{Cr}^{3+}$ compounds.⁴⁶ Strikingly, the infrared-active vibrational modes with the largest spin-lattice coupling constants modulate the Si-Te bond and move the Te centers with respect to the $\mathrm{Cr} \cdots \mathrm{Te} \cdots \mathrm{Cr}$ super-exchange pathway. Figure 8(a) displays the optical absorption spectrum of bulk $\mathrm{CrSiTe}_{3}$. The shape is characteristic of a magnetic semiconductor and is similar to other chalcogenides. We carried out a traditional band gap analysis by plotting $(\alpha \cdot E)^{2}$ and $(\alpha \cdot E)^{0.5}$ against energy¹⁹ to expose the direct and indirect band gaps. This is expressed as

$$
\alpha(E)=\frac{A}{E}\left(E-E_{g, d i r}\right)^{0.5}+\frac{B}{E}\left(E-E_{g, i n d} \mp E_{p h}\right)^{2}, \tag{4}
$$

where $\alpha(E)$ is the absorption coefficient, $E_{g, d i r}$ is the direct gap energy, $E_{g, i n d i r}$ is the indirect gap energy, $E_{p h}$ is the phonon energy mediating any indirect gap component, $E$ is the photon energy, and $A$ and $B$ are coefficients. This traditional band gap analysis is, of course, oversimplified in a number of ways. Even so, it is able to reveal ball-park values for these important quantities.⁵¹ In the case of bulk $\mathrm{CrSiTe}_{3}$, our analysis reveals that $E_{g, \text { indir }}=0.4 \mathrm{eV}$ and $E_{g, \text { dir }}=1.2 \mathrm{eV}$ at $300 \mathrm{~K}$ (Figs. 8(b) and 8(c)). $\mathrm{CrSiTe}_{3}$ is therefore an indirect gap material like silicon. It should be mentioned that the coupling phonon energy that is revealed in the indirect gap analysis of Fig. 8(c) is on the order of $100 \mathrm{meV}$. This energy scale is much larger than that of the fundamental phonon modes suggesting that overtone and/or combination modes play a role. According to the spin-polarized bands and projected densities of states (Figs. 8(d) and 8(e)), these features emanate from excitations in the majority channel, with the top of the valence band at $\Gamma$ and the bottom of the conduction band at a minimum between $\Gamma$ and $K$. The density of states around the Fermi level mainly consists of $T e-5 p$ and $C r-4 d$ orbitals, which illustrates that the superexchange interaction between $\mathrm{Cr}^{3+}$ ions is mediated by $T e-5 p$ orbitals. In conclusion, single crystals of CrSiTe₃ were grown from a flux, and the crystals were char- acterized using a variety of techniques. X-ray diffraction revealed that the crystals are twinned along the $c$-axis, possibly a result of stacking faults. The thermal expansion is negative at low temperatures, most likely due to exchange striction effects, but no further bulk structural distortion or magnetostriction appears below the magnetic transition at $T_C = 33$ K. Most importantly, the Te octahedra are not disturbed by the magnetic ordering. Magnetization measurements revealed that local moments are formed consistent with $Cr^{3+}$ ions. These moments order along the $c$-axis and show easy axis anisotropy. Thermal conductivity measurements show very strong magnon-phonon scattering effects. Above $T_C$, the in-plane thermal conductivity is small and rises gradually with temperature, resembling a glass. As CrSiTe₃ is cooled below $T_C$, the thermal conductivity rises dramatically. It is likely that the phonon scattering length is inversely related to the magnetic correlation length, but this conjecture needs further study to be made quantitative. To uncover the microscopic aspects of spin-lattice coupling, we combined variable temperature infrared spectros- copy with complementary lattice dynamics calculations. The frequency vs. temperature trends for the three infrared-active phonons are best fit with a Boltzmann sigmoidal model, a signature of both glassy behavior and strong spin-lattice interactions. An analysis of the low temperature deviations to this model and the displacement patterns reveals that the $393.3$ cm⁻¹ Si-Te stretching mode is most strongly coupled to the 33 K magnetic ordering transition by moving the Te centers in the $90^\circ$ Cr$\cdots$Te$\cdots$Cr superexchange pathway. By contrast, the more straightforward Te-Cr-Te bend- ing mode is not involved in the coupling mechanism. Finally, combined optical property measure- ments and first principles electronic structure calculations reveal that CrSiTe₃ is an indirect gap magnetic semiconductor. The 0.4 eV indirect gap is in the majority channel and goes from $\Gamma$ to a minimum between $\Gamma$ and $K$. A higher energy direct gap is observed at 1.2 eV.
