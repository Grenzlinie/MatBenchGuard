Akitaka Michigoe$^{1,*}$, Takumi Hasegawa$^{1}$, Norio Ogita$^{1}$, Takayuki Ichikawa$^{2}$, Yoshitsugu Kojima$^{2}$, Shigehito Isobe$^{3}$, and Masayuki Udagawa$^{1}$

(Received January 16, 2012; accepted July 10, 2012; published online August 21, 2012)

Raman scattering spectra of $\text{LiNH}_2$ have been measured from 3.4 to 673 K. Precise polarization dependence of the single crystalline $\text{LiNH}_2$ and first principles calculation have successfully assigned all observed peaks. Li vibration with the lowest energy at $133\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ shows the anomaly, where its energy decreases with decreasing temperature. This anomaly shows that the Li vibration is highly anharmonic with large amplitude. In addition, the energy of $133\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ gives the very small force constant of $0.05\ \text{m dyn}/\text{\AA}$ between Li and $\text{NH}_2$ in the diatomic model. This weak interaction suggests that $\text{LiNH}_2$ easily decomposes to Li and $\text{NH}_2$. Below 100 K, we have found new peaks in the energy range from 100 to $700\ \text{cm}^{-1}$. No additional degrees of freedom for vibrations conclude that rotational motion of $\text{NH}_2$ molecule freezes below 100 K. With increasing temperature, the bond angle of $\text{H-N-H}$ and bond length $\text{N-H}$ in $\text{NH}_2$ become narrow and long, respectively. At the reaction temperature region, the correlation between the integrated intensity of $\text{N-H}$ vibration in $\text{Li}_2\text{NH}$ and back ground slope has been observed and the background change is originated from the desorption of $\text{NH}_3$ molecules. Final materials due to chemical reactions are different between single crystal and powder samples. In the single crystal, the spectrum similar with that at room temperature appears at 673 K and this suggests that the reaction finishes only at the thin surface layer, not entire volume. However, the powder sample of $\text{LiNH}_2$ decomposes to $\text{Li}_2\text{NH}$ and finally change to the $\text{Li}_3\text{N}$ for the sake of $\text{NH}_3$ desorption.

KEYWORDS: $\text{LiNH}_2$, light element hydrogen storage material, Raman scattering, mode assignment, anharmonic vibration, $\text{NH}_2$ freezing at low temperature, $\text{NH}_3$ desorption

## 1. Introduction

The hydrogen energy has attracted attention as a clean energy. Especially for the application to motor vehicles, the light-weight storage materials are suitable. Chen *et al.* pointed out the possibility of the absorption and desorption of hydrogen due to the chemical reaction in Li compounds.$^{1)}$ This triggered the studies of the following hydrogen storage materials composed with light-weight elements; $\text{LiNH}_2$, $\text{LiAlH}_4,^{2)}$ $\text{LiBH}_4,^{3-5)}$ $\text{NaAlH}_4,^{6-8)}$ $\text{NaBH}_4,^{9)}$ $\text{Mg(NH}_2\text{)}_2,^{10-12)}$ and $\text{Ca(NH}_2\text{)}_2.^{13,14)}$

$\text{LiNH}_2$ is recognized as one of the typical materials for the hydrogen storage, but it needs the high reaction temperature above around 473 K at this stage. Thus, the lower reaction temperature and rapid reaction rate are necessary for the real application of $\text{LiNH}_2$. To obtain the good performance, the following different trials have been proceeded: Introduction of catalyst,$^{15)}$ Nano particle method by ball milling,$^{15)}$ Cation substitution$^{16,17)}$ and Film.$^{18)}$ Although these trials have been done, still the better performance of the $\text{LiNH}_2$ system has been required. For more progress with the performance, it becomes very important to understand the precise characteristics of the material. In addition, since $\text{LiNH}_2$ emits $\text{NH}_3$ by chemical reaction as described later, the relationship between the chemical reaction and atomic dynamical properties is also important even for the fundamental material science. The lattice dynamical research in the chemical reaction is important not only for the hydrogen storage but also the material science. Our aim of this study is to clarify the microscopic properties of $\text{LiNH}_2$.

Mixture of $\text{LiNH}_2$ and LiH release hydrogen at around 473–673 K in the following successive reactions for the sake of $\text{NH}_3$:

$$2\text{LiNH}_2 \rightarrow \text{Li}_2\text{NH} + \text{NH}_3 \tag{1}$$

and

$$\text{LiH} + \text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{LiNH}_2 + \text{H}_2. \tag{2}$$

Each reaction is usually irreversible.$^{15,19)}$ However, the successive reaction becomes reversible and the entire reaction is written as

$$\text{LiNH}_2 + \text{LiH} \Leftrightarrow \text{Li}_2\text{NH} + \text{H}_2. \tag{3}$$

We note the importance of $\text{NH}_3$ desorption from the $\text{LiNH}_2$ at the first step for this entire reaction as shown in eqs. (1) and (2).

Ichikawa *et al.* reported the temperature dependence of the thermal desorption of ammonia from powder $\text{LiNH}_2$ above room temperature.$^{15)}$ It shows that very small amounts of $\text{NH}_3$ are emitted from around 323 K, small amounts from around 473 K and very large amounts above around 573 K. This report is closely related with the present study.

We can point out the following Raman scattering reports that relate to the atomic vibrations and the mode assignments in $\text{LiNH}_2$. Bohger *et al.* measured the IR absorption and Raman scattering spectra of polycrystalline $\text{LiNH}_2$ at 298 and 110 K.$^{20)}$ They assigned the stretching and bending modes of $\text{NH}_2$ by the deuterations. Chellappa *et al.* measured the pressure dependence of Raman scattering spectra of powder $\text{LiNH}_2$ in a diamond anvil cell.$^{21)}$ They found the first order structural phase transition at $\sim 10\ \text{GPa}$, judging from the change of Raman scattering spectra.

Not only the macroscopic characteristics of $\text{LiNH}_2$ but also the microscopic mechanism of the reactions at atomic level is not well understood. For example, dynamical properties of Li and $\text{NH}_2$ are not understood during the reactions, where $\text{LiNH}_2$ changes to $\text{Li}_2\text{NH}$ by the emission of $\text{NH}_3$ as shown in eq. ## 2. Experimental
### 2.1 Sample and Raman scattering
Powder LiNH₂ sample with purity 95% was purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich. The employed single crystalline LiNH₂
was grown by the Tamman method from melting LiNH₂.²²)

In this study, we made three different measurements. The
first one was for the mode assignment, where we employed
the as-grown single crystalline LiNH₂. Polarization de-
pendence was measured at the room temperature by the
microscope systems with the ×10 objective lens. This is so
called micro Raman scattering. This sample was kept in
argon atmosphere to avoid crystal damage due to oxidation
and water adsorption. The direction of crystal axis was
determined by the precise angle dependence of the Raman
scattering spectra as described later (§3.2.1). The 2nd
measurement was the temperature dependence below room
temperature. We measured the Raman spectra from 3.4
to 300 K with 50 K interval. Since the single crystalline
specimen was placed in the cryostat, macro Raman
scattering method was used. The last measurement was
the temperature dependence from room temperature up to
673 K, where sample was placed in the heating cell, and
the micro Raman scattering was adopted. For the single
crystalline LiNH₂, we measured the spectra under the
constant heating 5 K/min with low pressure of ~10 Pa. For
the powder LiNH₂, since it needed the long time measure-
ment to obtain the analyzable spectra, each temperature was
kept during the measurement and was heated by 50 K step up
to 673 K in an Ar flow rate of 20 cm³/min.

The incident beam direction was normal to the sample
surface, and the backscattered light was detected. The
polarization geometry is given by a symbol of (I S), where I
and S denote the polarization directions of the incident and
scattered light, respectively. We employed two polarization
geometries denoted by (HH) and (HV). H means the
polarization direction of the incident beam, and V is the
direction of perpendicular to H at the sample surface.

The Raman scattering spectra were measured by a triple-
monochromator (JASCO NR-1800) with a CCD detector
(Princeton Instruments LN/CCD-1100-PB) cooled by liquid
N₂. An Ar⁺ laser with the wavelength 514.5 nm was used as
the excitation beam and was linearly polarized. Power of the
laser beam was 2.5 mW at the specimen in the microscope
system and 15 mW for the macro measurement.

### 2.2 Raman active phonons in LiNH₂ and their polarization selection rule
The crystal structure of LiNH₂ at room temperature is
tetragonal with space group of $\mathrm{I}\overline{4}$.²³⁻²⁴) Its unit cell is shown
in Fig. 1. Atomic positions in Cartesian coordinates are
listed below;²⁴)
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Li}_{1}(2\mathrm{a}):&(0,0,0)\\
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(2\mathrm{c}):&(0,0.5,0.25)\\
\mathrm{Li}_{3}(4\mathrm{f}):&(0,0.5,0.001)\\
\mathrm{N}(8\mathrm{g}):&(0.2298,0.247,0.1147)\\
\mathrm{H}_{1}(8\mathrm{g}):&(0.226,0.1328,0.1905)\\
\mathrm{H}_{2}(8\mathrm{g}):&(0.394,0.349,0.1249).
\end{aligned}
$$

In this symmetry and structure, the group theory gives us 35
modes at Brillouin zone center (wave vector $\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{0}$). They
are classified into $10\mathrm{A}+12\mathrm{B}+13\mathrm{E}$, where acoustic modes
belong to $\mathrm{B}+\mathrm{E}$. Here, $\mathrm{A}$, $\mathrm{B}$, and $\mathrm{E}$ are irreducible
representations and $\mathrm{E}$ modes are doubly degenerates. They
are all Raman active and also infrared active because of the
lack of inversion symmetry. Among them, $7\mathrm{A}+8\mathrm{B}+9\mathrm{E}$
are the phonon modes, which appear in the low energy
region, and $3\mathrm{A}+3\mathrm{B}+3\mathrm{E}$ are molecular vibrations of
NH₂.

The phonon modes with each irreducible representation
appear in the following polarization geometries, where $a$, $b$,
and $c$ are the principle-axis directions of the unit cell:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(a\,a),(b\,b),(c\,c)&\to A,\\
(a\,b),(a+b\,a-b)&\to B,\\
(a\,c),(b\,c)&\to E.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, if the normal direction of the crystal surface is parallel
to one principle axis, the assignment of the observed peak
can be easily made by one spectrum. However, if the
direction of crystal axes of the employed single crystal was
not understood at the initial stage, the additional experi-
ments, such as the detailed angle dependence, should be
necessary. To determine both the symmetry of modes and
$c$-axis direction, we have estimated the angle dependent
intensity for each $\mathrm{A}$, $\mathrm{B}$, or $\mathrm{E}$ mode in the polarization
geometries (HH) or (HV) as described in the next §3.2. Then,
we have determined both the symmetry of modes and the
inclined angle of the $c$-axis from the normal direction of the
sample surface, referring to the detailed angle dependence.

### 2.3 First principles calculation specifications
We also employed the first principles calculation for the assignment, vibrational energy and its atomic displacements.
The calculation was performed by ABINIT package. $^{25,26)}$
This calculates electronic structure of materials within density-functional theory using pseudopotentials and plane- wave basis. In the present calculation, we applied local density approximation for exchange-correlation energy parameterized by Perdew and Wang, $^{27)}$ and used Troullier Martins type $^{28)}$ pseudopotentials produced by fhi98PP. $^{29)}$
Phonon energy and atomic displacement were obtained by density-functional perturbation theory, $^{30)}$ through perturba tion of the atomic displacement with the wavevector $k=0$ .
The structural parameters were taken from the experimental values obtained by Sørby et al. $^{24)}$ Then atomic positions were optimized to minimize the total energy with the fixed lattice constants. The obtained atomic positions are $(0,0.5$ , $0.0076)$ for $Li 3,(0.2256,0.2479,0.1141)$ for $N,(0.2250$ , $0.1167,0.1909)$ for $H 1$ , and $(0.4078,0.3379,0.1221)$ for $H 2$ .
The calculation parameters are as follows. The cut-off energy of plane-wave basis was $68 Ry(1 Ry=13.6 eV)$ andsampling mesh in the reciprocal space was $4 \times 4 \times 4$  Monkhost-Pack grid. $^{31)}$ From the comparison with more accurate calculations, we have estimated the accuracy of the calculated phonon energy as $3 ~cm^{-1}$ .

## 3. Results and Discussion
### 3.1 Overview of Raman spectra of single crystal at room temperature
Peaks appear at three different energy regions as shown in Fig. 2. The first region is called as phonon region below $700 ~cm^{-1}$ , where the lattice vibrations due to $Li$ and $NH_{2}$ appear. The second and third ones are the regions of $NH_{2}$  molecular vibrations. The bending modes with the weakest intensity appear around $1500 ~cm^{-1}$ and the stretching modes with the strongest intensity around 3200 and $3300 ~cm^{-1}$ .

A\left(\begin{array}{ccc}

As shown in Fig. 3, the $c$ -axis of the sample inclines from the normal axis of the sample surface with the angle of $\gamma$ . The incident laser light direction is normal to the sample surface and the backscattered light is detected. The polarization direction of the incident and scattered light is on the horizontal plane. We employed two polarization geometries denoted by $(HH)$ and $(HV)$ as described in the previous section. $\theta$ is the angle between $x$ -axis and the polarization direction of the incident beam (H-polarization). $\phi$ is the angle between $x$ -axis and the crystal $a$ -axis.
Raman scattering intensity of eq. (4) depends on the sample rotation angle $\theta$ . The $\theta$ -dependence of the scattered intensity for the A, B, and E symmetries is a function of Raman tensor components, $\gamma$ and $\phi.^{32)}$ The scattered intensity expressions at the polarization $(HH)$ and $(HV)$ for the A, B, and E symmetries are given as follows.
For the A symmetry,
$$I_{\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{HH}}=\left(A 1+c_{\mathrm{A}} \sin ^{2} \theta\right)^{2}\qquad(6)$$
and
$$I_{\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{HV}}=r \frac{c_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}{4} \sin ^{2} 2 \theta,\qquad(7)$$

where
$$c_{\mathrm{A}}=(A 2-A 1) \sin ^{2} \gamma.\tag{8}$$

For the B symmetry,
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{HH}}= & c_{\mathrm{B}}^{2}\left\{\cos 2 D^{\prime}\left(\cos ^{2} \theta-\cos ^{2} \gamma \sin ^{2} \theta\right)\right. \\
& \left.+\cos \gamma \sin 2 D^{\prime} \sin 2 \theta\right\}^{2}
\tag{9}
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{HV}}= & r \frac{c_{\mathrm{B}}^{2}}{4}\left\{-\left(1+\cos ^{2} \gamma\right) \cos 2 D^{\prime} \sin 2 \theta\right. \\
& \left.+2 \cos \gamma \sin 2 D^{\prime} \cos 2 \theta\right\}^{2},
\tag{10}
\end{aligned}
$$
where
$$
\begin{gathered}
c_{\mathrm{B}}=\sqrt{B 1^{2}+B 2^{2}} \\
\tan 2 D=\frac{B 2}{B 1} \\
D^{\prime}=D-\phi.
\end{gathered}
\tag{11}
$$

For the E symmetry,
$$I_{\mathrm{E}, \mathrm{HH}}=c_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}\left(\sin ^{2} \gamma \sin ^{2} 2 \theta+\sin ^{2} 2 \gamma \sin ^{4} \theta\right)\tag{12}$$
and
$$I_{\mathrm{E}, \mathrm{HV}}=r c_{\mathrm{E}}^{2} \sin ^{2} \gamma\left(\cos ^{2} 2 \theta+\cos ^{2} \gamma \sin ^{2} 2 \theta\right),\tag{13}$$
where
$$c_{\mathrm{E}}=\sqrt{E 1^{2}+E 2^{2}}.\tag{14}$$

The variables $A1$, $A2$, $B1$, $B2$, $E1$, and $E2$ are the Raman tensor components in eq. (5). The coefficient $r$ in the HV intensity expressions is the intensity ratio $I(\mathrm{HV})/I(\mathrm{HH})$ in our experimental conditions.

As the representative assignment procedure, we present the case of the $133\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ peak, which is marked by arrow in Fig. 2. Figures 4(a) and 4(b) show the calculated intensity for the E mode estimated by the eqs. (12) and (13). As shown in eqs. (12) and (13) for the condition of $c_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}=E1^{2}+E2^{2} \geq 0$, $\theta$-dependence of the intensity depends only on the angle $\gamma$, which is the inclined angle of the $c$-axis from normal direction of the sample surface. Thus, in the E symmetry we can experimentally determine the angle $\gamma$. Figures 4(c) and 4(d) show the observed $\theta$-dependence in the $15^{\circ}$ step at (HH) and (HV). The representative spectra of the $\theta$-dependence in (HH) and (HV) are shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), respectively. Intensities of the $133\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ in the Figs. 5(a) and 5(b) correspond to the values in the Figs. 4(c) and 4(d). The obtained $\theta$-dependence shows that the symmetry of the $133\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ mode is E. By the best fitting, the inclined angle is determined as $63.7^{\circ}$.

The same method has been also applied for the A and B symmetries, and the observed peaks have been successfully assigned, except for the peaks at 343 and $322\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. The experimental intensities of $343\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ can be fitted by both expressions of A and B. This peak have been assigned as A mode, since there is only A mode at this energy region according to the results of the first principles calculation. For the latter at $322\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, the intensity change is peculiar, but it has been assigned by the combination of B and E. In addition, the peak around $238\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ survives at $0^{\circ}$ in HH as shown in Fig. 5(a). This suggests that this peak belongs not only to E mode but also to A or B mode. The assigned results are also depicted in Fig. 5.

### 3.2.2 Molecular vibrations of NH₂
Figure 7 shows the representative θ-dependence of the bending and stretching vibrations of NH₂ molecules in (HH) and (HV).

We have observed two bending vibrations as shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b), in spite of the estimated three modes (A + B + E) by group theory. For the lower energy peak at 1536 cm⁻¹, its intensity is zero at θ = 0° in (HH), and is strong at 0° in (HV). This dependence clearly shows that this vibration is E mode. On the other hand, the higher energy peak at 1560 cm⁻¹ cannot be explained by A or B. Thus, this vibration mode at 1536 cm⁻¹ is concluded as the overlap mode with A and B.

![](./images/813287622448250880_7.jpg)

Fig. 7. θ-dependence of bending and stretching modes in NH₂. (a) and (b) are bending modes in (HH) and (HV), respectively. (c) and (d) are stretching modes in (HH) and (HV), respectively.

In the spectra of NH₂ stretching mode, we have observed 3 peaks as shown in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d). Group theory predicts A, B, and E symmetry for both symmetric and anti-symmetric modes (total 2A + 2B + 2E). The lower energy peak at 3260 cm⁻¹ was already assigned as the symmetric stretching modes.²⁰ In the present case, the 3260 cm⁻¹ peak was also assigned as symmetric stretching modes, since the scattered intensity of symmetric vibration is stronger than that of the anti-symmetric one, and the first principles calculation gives us the same results.

The symmetric stretching vibrations looks like one peak, but we observe the slight energy difference in the θ-dependence at (HV). Therefore this peak is constructed at least with 2 peaks. The first principles calculation gives almost the same energy for A, B, and E modes, and we have not observed any peaks around this energy. Then we conclude that this peak is the overlap peak with A, B, and E.

Among the anti-symmetric stretching mode, the lower energy mode at 3310 cm⁻¹ shows the θ-dependence for E symmetry. The peak at 3321 cm⁻¹ shows the similar θ-dependence with the overlapped mode with A and B in the bending mode. Thus, we conclude this peak is the overlap of A and B modes.

### 3.3.2 Low temperature phase

As shown in Fig. 8(a), the following notable spectral changes have been observed below room temperature. With decreasing temperature all peaks become sharp and new peaks appear below 100 K at the phonon region as depicted by open triangles. These new peaks suggest the structural phase transition at low temperature. However this change is different from the ordinary structural transition accompany- ing with symmetry break down. Since the structure is too simple, there are no extra degrees of freedom for the atomic vibration even at room temperature. However, as shown in Table I, we have not observed all predicted modes at room temperature. Here, we compared the observed energy with the calculated energies. The agreement is good and the new peaks are un-assigned modes at room temperature. In addition, the new peaks exist in the energy region of translational and rotational vibrations of $\text{NH}_2$ molecules. Thus, these new peaks appear by the dynamical change of $\text{NH}_2$ molecules, that is, the rotational movement of $\text{NH}_2$ freezes below 100 K. For the new peaks at $445\text{cm}^{-1}$, the first principles calculation has predicted the possibility of B and E. Tentatively we assign as the B mode by the energy judgment.

### 3.3.3 Li vibration at $133\text{cm}^{-1}$

The summarized temperature dependence of the phonon energies is shown in Fig. 9, where not only the low temperature data but also higher temperature one than room temperature are plotted. With decreasing temperature from room temperature, the energy of the most peaks increases, except for the lowest energy mode at $133\text{cm}^{-1}$ marked by star in Fig. 9. The former temperature dependence is an ordinary case, since the energy increase is understood by the increase of force constants due to thermal shrinkage of volume. However, as depicted by the star in Fig. 9, the vibration energy of the Li mode shows the different temperature dependence: its energy decreases with decreasing temperature. This phenomenon has been reported in the many caged compounds (type-I clathrate, $^{33-35)}$ $\text{KO}_2\text{O}_6$, $^{35,36)}$ filled skutterudites, $^{37,38)}$ and $\text{La}_3\text{Pd}_{20}\text{Ge}_6$ $^{35,39)}$) and layered superconductor $\text{CaAlSi}.^{40)}$ The temperature dependence has been already understood by the fourth order anharmonic effect. $^{33)}$ Thus, the Li atom at $133\text{cm}^{-1}$ vibrates with large amplitude in the highly anharmonic potential. The detailed temperature dependence of this mode is shown in Fig. 10. The anharmonic character continues until 473 K. According to the report by Ichikawa et al.,$^{15)}$ the arrow at 323 K is the starting temperature of very small amount of $\text{NH}_3$ desorption, and the closed triangle at 473 K is the small amount desorption. Since the energy due to anharmonicity linearly changes at high temperature, the saturation tendency above 300 K suggests the different state.

In order to estimate the force constant $(k)$ between Li and NH₂ diatomic model is employed, and $k$ is given by
$$
k=(2 \pi c v)^{2} \mu, \quad(18)
$$
where $c$ is speed of light, $v$ is the vibrational frequency in $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ unit, and $\mu$ is the reduced mass $[\mu=m_{\mathrm{Li}} m_{\mathrm{NH}_{2}}/(m_{\mathrm{Li}}+m_{\mathrm{NH}_{2}})$, here $m_{\mathrm{Li}}$ is the mass of Li atom and $m_{\mathrm{NH}_{2}}$ mass of NH₂ molecule]. The obtained force constant is $0.05 \mathrm{mdyn}/\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$ for the $133 \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ mode. For comparison, we also calculated the force constant $(k')$ due to coulomb interaction between 2 charged particles $(q_{1},-q_{2})$ separated by distance $r$. The force constant is given by the derivative of force with displacement $\Delta r$ as
$$
F=-\frac{q_{1} q_{2}}{4 \pi \epsilon_{0} r^{2}} \to k'=\frac{q_{1} q_{2}}{2 \pi \epsilon_{0} r^{3}}. \quad(19)
$$

For the case of $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$and $\mathrm{NH}_{2}{ }^{-}$with the bond length of $2.078 \mathring{\mathrm{A}},{ }^{24)}$ the force constant is $0.5 \mathrm{mdyn}/\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$. The very week obtained force constant $0.05 \mathrm{mdyn}/\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$ from diatomic model suggests that $\mathrm{LiNH}_{2}$ easily decompose to $\mathrm{Li}$ and $\mathrm{NH}_{2}$.

### 3.3.4 NH₂ molecular vibration
Temperature dependence of the energy of the NH₂ molecular vibrations from 0 to 673 K is shown in Fig. 12, where (a) and (b) are the results of bending and stretching modes, respectively. The energies of the NH₂ stretching vibration increases with decreasing temperature, while those of the bending $(1536 \mathrm{cm}^{-1})$ decrease. Among three stretching modes, the symmetric stretching vibration at $3260 \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ is the overlapped mode of A, B, and E. However, in the antisymmetric modes are different between the energies of E-symmetry at $3310 \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and the overlap A + B at 3321 $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. This difference is very important to clarify the microscopic property of NH₂ molecule by the *simple model* based on $\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ molecule.

In $\mathrm{LiNH}_{2}$, two $\mathrm{H}$ atoms of the $\mathrm{NH}_{2}$ molecule are inequivalent sites, but the difference of the bond length of $\mathrm{N-H}$ is $1\%$, according to the report of crystal structure. $^{24)}$ Thus, we can employ the *simple model* with the same distance. By the model, the vibration energies of the bending and stretching modes are expressed as the function of longitudinal force constant $(k_{1})$ of the $\mathrm{N-H}$ bond, bending force constant $(k_{2})$ of bond angle $(2 \alpha)$ of $\mathrm{H-N-H}.^{43)}$ The frequency of anti-symmetric stretching vibration is given by
$$
\omega_{a}^{2}=\frac{k_{1}}{m_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1+\frac{2 m_{\mathrm{H}}}{m_{\mathrm{N}}} \sin ^{2} \alpha\right), \quad(20)
$$
and frequencies of symmetric stretching and bending modes are given by the roots of the following equation,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega^{4} &-\omega^{2}\left\{\frac{k_{1}}{m_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1+\frac{2 m_{\mathrm{H}}}{m_{\mathrm{N}}} \cos ^{2} \alpha\right)+\frac{2 k_{2}}{m_{\mathrm{H}}}\left(1+\frac{2 m_{\mathrm{H}}}{m_{\mathrm{N}}} \sin ^{2} \alpha\right)\right\} \\
&+\frac{2 \mu k_{1} k_{2}}{m_{\mathrm{N}} m_{\mathrm{H}}^{2}}=0. \quad(21)
\end{aligned}
$$

Three parameters $(k_{1},k_{2},\alpha)$ were obtained by the fitting against the observed energy. The experimental energies of bending, symmetric stretching and anti-symmetric stretching are fitted by these expressions. We employed two cases for the fitting, where E mode or A + B overlapping mode was employed as the anti-symmetric mode. The obtained fitting parameters of $k_{1},k_{2}$, and $2 \alpha$ is shown in the Fig. 13, where the results for the E mode are shown by open circles and solid line and those for the A + B mode shown by open triangles and broken line, respectively.

### 3.3.5 Temperature dependence at $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ desorption region
As shown in Fig. 8, broad back ground appear above room temperature. Its center energy is almost $1500 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and it exists up to $3300 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ as shown Fig. 8(f). With the increase of temperature, it once becomes small at 423 K, and increase again from 483 to 573 K. Above 603 K, the similar spectra to the room-temperature one have been observed. Above room temperature, the backgrounds are different in three temperature ranges; room temperature to around 473 K, 473-573 K, and 573-673 K. These changes correspond to the $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ desorption process in the report by Ichikawa et al. $^{15)}$

As shown in Fig. 8(f), a weak peak around $3180 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ becomes clear above 368 K and exists even at 673 K. Since this peak is the NH stretching vibration in $\mathrm{Li}_{2} \mathrm{NH}^{44)}$ its observation clearly shows that the chemical reaction of eq. (1) occurs above 423 K. Temperature dependence of integrated intensity of the N-H stretching mode is shown in Fig. 14, where open circles denote its intensity and closed circles show the slope of background. The background slope is estimated by the difference between the intensity at $3100 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and that at $3500 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. Since the integrated intensity of the N-H mode corresponds to the desorption reaction of $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$, the good correlation between the integrated intensity and background slope suggests that the background is generated by the chemical reaction process. In the single crystal, the Raman spectrum at 673 K is similar with that at room temperature. This implies that the reaction is finished at 673 K and the reaction occurs only at the surface of the crystals, not the entire volume. This is quite different from the powder case as described in the next §3.4.

These large backgrounds in powder spectra are similar to them at $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ desorption reaction in the case of crystal, and predict that slow and small amount of $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ desorption reaction starts even at room temperature. The large backgrounds are probably caused by the fraction of surface at the powder, and indicate the fraction of the surface area is important for the reaction. ### 3.5 Other difference between single crystal and powder sample
Temperature dependence of vibration energies between single crystal and powder LiNH₂ is shown in Fig. 19, where (a) is the NH₂ stretching vibrations and (b) is Li vibration at $133\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ above room temperature. Black squares are the single crystal and open circles the powder. The energies at room temperature are close between them, but energy of powder is always smaller than that of single crystal at higher temperature. This indicates that the lattice constant of powder is larger than that of single crystal at the same temperature due to the increase of the surface fraction.

### 4. Summary
We have studied the phonon properties of LiNH₂ by Raman scattering in the temperature region between 3.4 and 673 K. Precise polarization dependence of the single crystalline LiNH₂ and first principles calculation have successfully assigned all observed peaks.
