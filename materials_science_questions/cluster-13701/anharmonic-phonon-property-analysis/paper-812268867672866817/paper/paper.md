# Temperature Dependence of the Raman Spectrum in Anatase TiO₂

Toshiaki OHSAKA

National Institute for Researches in Inorganic Materials,
Sakura-mura, Niihari-gun, Ibaraki 305

(Received December 12, 1979)

The frequency and linewidth of the four Raman active modes in anatase have been investigated over the temperature range 20 to 1100 K. The temperature ($T$) dependence of the frequency of the $639\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ mode showed typical behavior in normal ionic crystals, whereas the other modes at 399, 197 and $144\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ did anomalous behavior. These $T$ dependent results were combined with pressure dependent results, thermal expansion and compressibility data to evaluate the pure-volume and pure-temperature contributions to the $T$ dependence of each frequency. The results indicate that the anomalous $T$ dependence of the $399\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ mode reflects the near cancellation of the cubic and quartic contributions to the frequency shift, and that of the modes at 197 and $144\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ arises from strong quartic contributions. The $T$ dependence of the linewidth is well explained qualitatively in terms of simple anharmonic effects involving three phonon processes.

## §1. Introduction

The purpose of this note is to report the results of a study of effects of temperature on the Raman spectra of the anatase phase of TiO₂. Anatase is one of three naturally occurring TiO₂ polymorphs. It has a tetragonal structure (space group $D_{4h}^{19}$) containing two formula units per primitive unit cell. Each Ti atom is approximately octahedrally coordinated by oxygens. According to the factor group analysis anatase has six Raman active modes ($A_{1\mathrm{g}}+2B_{1\mathrm{g}}+3E_{\mathrm{g}}$) and three infrared active modes ($A_{2\mathrm{u}}+2E_{\mathrm{u}}$). The Raman spectrum at room temperature was investigated by Beattie and Gilson¹⁾ and Ohsaka *et al.*²⁾ The six allowed bands in the first-order Raman spectrum were identified at $639\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ($E_{\mathrm{g}}, v_{1}$), $519\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ($B_{1\mathrm{g}}, v_{2}$), $513\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ($A_{1\mathrm{g}}, v_{3}$), $399\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ($B_{1\mathrm{g}}, v_{4}$), $197\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ($E_{\mathrm{g}}, v_{5}$), and $144\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ($E_{\mathrm{g}}, v_{6}$). Here, $v$'s are the same notations as used in ref. 2. Ohsaka *et al.*³⁾ furthermore, investigated the high pressure Raman spectrum of anatase up to 60 kbar and observed a pressure-induced phase transition and the anomalous pressure ($P$) dependence in the $v_{5}$ mode.

In the present work we have now measured the $T$ dependence of the frequency and linewidth of the Raman active modes in anatase in the temperature range 20–1100 K and the results are discussed in the light of anharmonic crystal theories. Anharmonicities in a lattice potential for a crystal are known to be important in the lattice dynamics because many of the physical properties of solids are determined by the lattice vibrations and a detailed description of these properties demands an understanding of the anharmonicities.

The anharmonic terms in the lattice potential give rise to two main effects. First, the phenomenon of thermal expansion is now allowed. Second, the anharmonicities allow interactions between the normal modes. Anharmonic interactions among the normal modes result in $T$ dependent frequency and linewidth for the normal modes. The thermal expansion also causes a $T$ dependent shift of each phonon frequency from the harmonic normal mode value. One of the ways to understand these processes is to examine the variation of the frequency and linewidth of the normal modes with temperature. When temperature dependent data are combined with pressure dependent data, we can distinguish the pure-volume contribution due to thermal expansion and the pure-temperature contribution due to the higher-order anharmonicities to the isobalic $T$ dependence of each frequency.

## §2. Experimental and Results

Samples of single crystals of anatase and a spectrometer used for the Raman measurements

have been previously described. $^{2)}$ Raman spectra were taken using 4880 and $5145 \AA$ lines from an argon-ion laser at a power of 15~100 mW in a right-angle scattering geometry. The low temperature Raman measurements were made using a CF-100 cryostat (Oxford Instrument Company) with an alloy temperature sensor, a copper/constantan thermocouple and a copper/gold-2.1 at. $\%$ cobalt thermocouple, which were calibrated at three points 4.2, 77, and 273 K. For temperatures above 290 K, the sample was mounted in a small electricfurnace which was monitored with a chromel/ alumel thermocouple.

The $T$ dependence of the frequencies of the $v_{1}, v_{4}, v_{5}$ , and $v_{6}$ modes in anatase is shown in Fig. 1, where each point represents the average of at least three different measurements. The frequency shift measurement is believed to be accurate within \pm 0.2 to $\pm 1.5 ~cm^{-1}$ depending on temperature. As seen in Fig. 1, the frequency shifts of the observed modes reveal interesting variations with temperature. The frequency of the $v_{1}$ mode is constant up to $\sim 100 ~K$ and then shifts approximately linearly toward lower frequencies with increasing temperature. This behavior is typical of normal ionic crystals. The peak frequency of an unresolved doublet of the $v_{2}$ and $v_{3}$ modes near $516 ~cm^{-1}$ shifted obviously to lower frequencies with tempera- ture, though it was not given in Fig. 1.

In contrast to the usual behavior of these modes, the other modes with lower frequencies exhibited the anomalous $T$ dependence. The frequency of the $v_{4}$ mode increases with tem perature and attains a maximum near 250 K and then decreases steadily. On the other hand, the frequencies of the $v_{5}$ and $v_{6}$ modes increase with temperature. Unlike all of the other Raman active modes, the $v_{5}$ mode is anomalous also in the $P$ dependence $^{3)}$ in which the frequency decreases with increasing pressure. However, in all cases the frequency variation with tempera- ture is approximately linear for temperatures above 400 K.

### §3. Discussion

The variations of the frequencies of the normal modes with temperature at constant pressure arise from two contributions: (i) a pure-volume contribution which results from thermal expansion and (ii) a pure-temperature contribution which results from cubic and quartic anharmonicities. The latter is known as the self-energy shift. Treating the frequency $\omega$ as a function of volume $V$ and temperature $T$ yields$^{4)}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{\partial \ln \omega}{\partial T}\right)_{P} & =\left(\frac{\partial \ln V}{\partial T}\right)_{P}\left(\frac{\partial \ln \omega}{\partial \ln V}\right)_{T}+\left(\frac{\partial \ln \omega}{\partial T}\right)_{V} \\
& =-\frac{\beta}{\kappa}\left(\frac{\partial \ln \omega}{\partial P}\right)_{T}+\left(\frac{\partial \ln \omega}{\partial T}\right)_{V}, \quad(3.1)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\beta \equiv(\partial \ln V / \partial T)_{P}$ is the volume thermal expansion coefficient and $\kappa \equiv-(\partial \ln V / \partial P)_{T}$ is the isothermal volume compressibility. The term on the left-hand side of eq. (3.1) is the frequency change as a function of temperature at constant pressure. The first term on the right-hand side is the frequency change as a function of volume at constant temperature, i.e., the pure-volume contribution, and the second term on the right is the frequency change as function of temperature at constant volume, i.e., the pure-temperature contribution. Thus the pure-temperature contribution can be evaluated from the measurable isobaric temperature and isothermal pressure derivatives of a frequency and a knowledge of $\beta$ and $\kappa$.

Anharmonicities in the lattice potential cause a frequency shift and also a finite linewidth (inverse lifetime) corresponding to the damping of the lattice vibration. General expressions for the frequency shift and lifetime of the phonon have been derived by several authors using the Green's-function techniques of many-body theory.$^{5-7)}$

Following Cowley, $^{7)}$ the quasinormal frequency $\omega(q j)$ of a mode of wave vector $q$ and branch $j$ is given as

$$
\omega(q j)^{2}=\omega_{0}(q j)^{2}+2 \omega_{0}(q j) \cdot D\left(q j j^{\prime}, \Omega\right), \quad(3.2)
$$

where $\omega_{0}$ is the harmonic frequency of the normal mode and $D(q j j', \Omega)$ is the anharmonic contribution to the self-energy of the mode. The frequency $\Omega$ depends on the measurement technique. The self-energy $D$ (all subscripts are dropped for simplicity) is a complex quantity which can be written

$$
D=\Delta-i \Gamma, \quad(3.3)
$$

where the real part $\Delta$ measures the anharmonic frequency shift and the imaginary part $\Gamma$ is the reciprocal of the phonon lifetime.

The real part of $D$ is given$^{4)}$ to second order in the cubic term and to first order in the quartic term in the perturbation theory by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta(q j, \Omega) & =\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}(q j)+\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}(q j, \Omega) \\
& \equiv \Delta^{\mathrm{E}}+\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{4}, \quad(3.4)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ is a frequency-independent term arising from the thermal expansion, $\Delta_{3}$ the frequency shift due to cubic anharmonicities and $\Delta_{4}$ the frequency shift due to quartic anharmonicities. The combined contribution $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}\left(=\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{4}\right)$ is the pure-temperature contribution to the isobalic $T$ dependence of the frequencies. We are therefore able to relate the first term on the right-hand side of eq. (3.1) with $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ and the second term with $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$. In this lowest approximation, $\Delta_{3}$ is negative in the frequency region of the phonon considered here, while $\Delta_{4}$ may be either positive or negative, which, in some case, allows us to distinguish the origin of the anharmonicities.

As indicated above, $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ and $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ can be separately determined for each measured mode from the $P$ and $T$ dependences of the mode frequencies. Since $\omega=\omega(V, T)$, we can rewrite eq. (3.1) as follows.$^{4)}$

$$
\left(\Delta \omega_{T}\right)_{P}=-\left(\Delta \omega_{P}\right)_{T}+\left(\Delta \omega_{T}\right)_{V}, \quad(3.5)
$$

where $\left(\Delta \omega_{T}\right)_{P}$ is the measured change in frequency on raising temperature from 0 to $T \mathrm{~K}$ at constant pressure (1 bar), $\left(\Delta \omega_{T}\right)_{V}$ is the change in frequency on raising temperature from 0 to $T \mathrm{~K}$ at constant volume (the volume at $T \mathrm{~K}$ and 1 bar), and $-\left(\Delta \omega_{P}\right)_{T}$ is the change in frequency on raising pressure at a constant temperature $T$ from 1 bar to a value of pressure $P$ sufficient to decrease volume of the crystal from it's value at $T \mathrm{~K}$ and 1 bar to it's value at $0 \mathrm{~K}$ and 1 bar.

The quantity $\left(\Delta \omega_{P}\right)_{T}$, i.e., the contribution to the frequency shift due to thermal expansion, can be evaluated from the following equation

$$
\left(\Delta \omega_{P}\right)_{T}=\omega \int_{0}^{T} \gamma(T) \beta(T) \mathrm{d} T, \quad(3.6)
$$

where $\gamma(T)$ is the mode Grüneisen parameter

and $\beta(T)$ the volume thermal expansion coefficient. Consequently, the quantity $(\Delta \omega_{T})_{V}$ can be determined from eq. (3.5).

According to Lowndes, $^{8)}$ furthermore, the quantities $(\Delta \omega_{P})_{T}$ and $(\Delta \omega_{T})_{V}$ are related to $\Delta^{E}$ and $\Delta^{A}$ , respectively, in the following manner. $\Delta^{E}$ is simply given by
$$\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}=-\left(\Delta \omega_{P}\right)_{T}.\qquad(3.7)$$

If $\Delta^{A}$ is not too large compared to $\omega(T)$ for a given mode, we can write
$$\left(\Delta \omega_{T}\right)_{V}=\Delta^{\mathrm{A}} \simeq \Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{A}}-\Delta_{0}^{\mathrm{A}},\qquad(3.8)$$
where $\Delta_{0}^{A}$ is the anharmonic contribution to the phonon energy associated with zero-point motion, and $\Delta_{T}^{A}$ is the T dependent contribution of the higher-order anharmonicities $(\Delta_{3}+\Delta_{4})$ . Since in this lowest approximation both the cubic and quartic contributions vary linearly with temperature at sufficiently high temperatures, an estimate of $-\Delta_{0}^{A}$ can be obtained from an extrapolation of the linear region of $\Delta^{A}$ back to 0 K.

We turn now to a discussion of the linewidth of the phonon. The imaginary part of D may bewritten to lowest order in the simple form $^{5,9)}$  as
$$\begin{aligned}
\Gamma(\omega)=\sum_{q_{1} j_{1} j_{2}} & \left\{A\left(q_{1} j_{1} ;-q_{1} j_{2}\right)\left(n_{1}+n_{2}+1\right) \delta\left(\omega-\omega\left(q_{1} j_{1}\right)-\omega\left(-q_{1} j_{2}\right)\right)\right. \\
& \left.+B\left(q_{1} j_{1} ;-q_{1} j_{2}\right)\left(n_{1}-n_{2}\right) \delta\left(\omega-\omega\left(q_{1} j_{1}\right)+\omega\left(-q_{1} j_{2}\right)\right)\right\},
\end{aligned}\qquad(3.9)$$
 where A and B are essentially the Fourier transforms of the anharmonic terms in the lattice potential and $n_{t}=[\exp (\hbar \omega_{t} / k T)-1]^{-1}$ is the phonon occupation number. The first term on the right-hand side represents the decay of the observed phonon into two lower energy phonons and the second term the scattering of a thermal phonon by the observed phonon. The decay and scattering processes must both satisfy conservation of energy and momentum.

The constant 1 inside the round brackets of the first term is there as a result of the fact that phonons are believed to have zero-point energy in their ground state. The parameter A cor- responds to the linewidth of the phonon at T=0 K. In the high temperature limit, the occupation number $n_{t}$ is proportional to tem perature so that in this lowest approximation eq. (3.9) becomes proportional to the absolute temperature.

We now discuss some conclusions from theresults in Figs. 3 and 4. Considering first the $v_{1}$  mode, we note that both the thermal expansion and anharmonicities cause the negative fre- quency shifts $\Delta^{E}$ and $\Delta^{A}$ at all temperatures. The negative frequency shift of $\Delta^{A}$ leaves the origin of the anharmonicities ambiguous, because as described earlier, cubic anharmonicities lead to a negative $\Delta^{A}$ and on the other hand quartic anharmonicities to a negative or positive $\Delta^{A}$ . The $v_{1}$ modes exhibits behavior typical of

normal ionic crystals in which the isobalic $T$ dependence of the frequency is dominated by the thermal expansion term.

A peculiar feature of the results is that the pure-temperature contribution $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ is positive for the $v_{4}, v_{5}$ and $v_{6}$ modes through the entire temperature range investigated. For $v_{4}$ mode, the somewhat weak $T$ dependence of the $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ at temperatures above 300 K, presumably reflects the near cancellation of the cubic and quartic contributions to the frequency shift, while the $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ is negative and increases with temperature. As a result the frequency maximum occurs near 250 K in the $T$ dependence of the frequency in this mode, as shown in Fig. 1.

For the $v_{5}$ mode, which exhibits the anomalous $P$ dependence as described above, the frequency shift due to thermal expansion, $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ is small and positive. The positive $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ reflects that the Grüneisen parameter $\gamma$ is negative for this mode. The frequency shift due to the anharmonicities $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ is positive and large. This fact suggests that the $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ may be dominated by the quartic anharmonicities. On the other hand, in the $v_{6}$ mode the $\Delta^{\mathrm{E}}$ is negative, but the $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ is positive and very large, amounting to $19 \%$ of the phonon energy at 1000 K. Again, the fact that $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ is positive suggests that $\Delta^{\mathrm{A}}$ may be dominated by the quartic anharmonicities in this case. Thus, the anomalous $T$ dependence of the frequency of the $v_{5}$ and $v_{6}$ modes can be understood by these dominant quartic anharmonicities.

### 3.2 Linewidth
We turn now to a discussion of the linewidth. The fact that as seen in Fig. 2, the linewidth of the observed modes varies linearly with temperature at high temperatures suggests that eq. (3.9) is applicable to an explanation of the $T$ dependence of the linewidth. As described earlier, however, a knowledge of the phonon dispersion curves and many-body theoretical calculations are required to gain an under-

standing of the behavior of the linewidth of the phonon. $^{9)}$ No dispersion curves of anatase have been known and the numerical calculations by the many-body theory for crystals having several phonon branches such as anatase are very complex. Such calculations have been somewhat limited and confined to a few simple compounds. $^{7,12)}$ We shall, thus, give a qualita tive explanation based on the following two assumptions.

First, the observed phonon decays into two phonons of equal energy and opposite wave vector. Second, the factors $A$ and $B$ in eq. (3.9) are used as adjustable parameters $^{9)}$ and the energy of the thermal phonon scattered by the observed phonon is determined so as to satisfy conservation of energy and to give a good fit to the observed linewidth. The first assumption is frequently used $^{13,14)}$ when no dispersion curves are known. The second seems also not to be unreasonable because there are many phonon dispersion curves, where energies of thermal phonons would distribute nearly continuously. This assumption should not lead to large errors in the discussion on the linewidth.

The results calculated in the above manner are represented for each of the $v_{1}, v_{4}, v_{5}$ and $\dot{v}_{6}$ modes as the following equations and shown in Figs. 2 and 6.

$$
\Gamma_{1}=6.6\left[2 n\left(\frac{1}{2} \omega_{1}\right)+1\right]+4.3\left[n\left(\frac{1}{8} \omega_{1}\right)-n\left(\frac{9}{8} \omega_{1}\right)\right] \mathrm{cm}^{-1}, \quad(3.10)
$$

$$
\Gamma_{4}=5.0\left[2 n\left(\frac{1}{2} \omega_{4}\right)+1\right]+3.8\left[n\left(\frac{1}{5} \omega_{4}\right)-n\left(\frac{6}{5} \omega_{4}\right)\right] \mathrm{cm}^{-1}, \quad(3.11)
$$

$$
\Gamma_{5}=0.5\left[2 n\left(\frac{1}{2} \omega_{5}\right)+1\right]+3.0\left[n\left(\omega_{5}\right)-n\left(2 \omega_{5}\right)\right] \mathrm{cm}^{-1}, \quad(3.12)
$$

$$
\Gamma_{6}=0.44\left[2 n\left(\frac{1}{2} \omega_{6}\right)+1\right]+4.0\left[n\left(\omega_{6}\right)-n\left(2 \omega_{6}\right)\right]-10\left[n\left(2 \omega_{6}\right)-n\left(3 \omega_{6}\right)\right] \mathrm{cm}^{-1}. \quad(3.13)
$$

Fig. 6. Plot of the linewidth of the $v_{6}$ mode in anatase as a function of temperature. The full circles represent experimental data. The solid line is the curve calculated from eq. (3.13). The lines $D, S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ are, respectively, the calculated curves of the first, second, and third terms on the right-hand side of eq. (3.13).

The calculated curves of the linewidth vs $T$ for the four modes are seen to be in a good agreement with the plots of the observed values. For the three modes $v_{1}, v_{4}$ and $v_{5}$, the variations of the observed linewidth as a function of temperature were explained by considering one decay process and one scattering process, while for the $v_{6}$ mode by considering one decay and two scattering processes. Thus, the $T$ variations of the linewidth for the observed modes were well interpreted qualitatively in terms of simple anharmonic effects involving only three-phonon decay and scattering processes.
