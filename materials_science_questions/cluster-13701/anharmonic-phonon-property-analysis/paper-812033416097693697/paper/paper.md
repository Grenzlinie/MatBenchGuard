JOURNAL OF MAGNETIC RESONANCE 36, 1-6 (1979)

# Determination of the Effect of Lattice Expansion and Vibration on the Spin Hamiltonian Parameters of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ in MgO

A. MANOOGIAN

Department of Physics, University of Ottawa, Ottawa K1N 9B4, Canada

Received November 14, 1978; revised January 18, 1979

An empirical method is described for separating the temperature dependence of the spin-Hamiltonian parameters of paramagnetic ions in solids into their constituent parts due to the effect of lattice expansion and crystal vibration. The analysis is done for the hyperfine parameter $A$ of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ in cubic sites of MgO, and for the crystal field splitting parameter $D$ of $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ in [100]-type axial sites of MgO. An individual local mode frequency was found to produce the best fit of the theory with the experimental results for both ions. The values obtained are 420 and 810 K for $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cr}^{3+}$, respectively. The effect of lattice expansion was found to produce a negligible contribution to the temperature dependence of both $A$ and $D$. The static value of $A$ at 0 K for $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ was found to be in agreement with that reported in the literature.

## INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this paper is to describe an empirical method for separating the temperature dependence of the spin-Hamiltonian parameters of impurity ions in solids into their constituent static (i.e., lattice expansion) and vibrational parts. The analysis is done for the $A$ vs $T$ curve of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ impurities in cubic sites of MgO (1), where $A$ is the hyperfine interaction parameter, and for the $D$ vs $T$ curve of $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ in [100]-type axial sites of MgO $(1,2)$, where $D$ is the axial crystal field splitting parameter. Attempts in this direction have been made previously by others using different methods or points of view, but in those cases only a part of the contribution to the parameters was calculated (either the static or vibrational part), with the remaining part being deduced from experiment or not considered at all. The model presented in the present work allows both contributions to be obtained simul- taneously, with results that are found to be in agreement with available experimental results or theoretical calculations.

Walsh et al. (1) performed pressure studies on the hyperfine parameter $A$ of $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ in MgO and found that the effect of lattice expansion did not contribute to the temperature dependence of $A$ within the range of 0 to 400 K. With the aid of this result Simanek and Orbach (3) demonstrated that dynamic phonon effects were responsible for the observed temperature dependence. This was done by fitting the $A$ vs $T$ curve using the Debye approximation for lattice vibrations. Orbach and Simanek (4) then calculated the static value of $A$ at 0 K using a simple model and estimated its value to be $-82.3\ (10^{-4}\text{cm}^{-1})$.

Wertz and Auzins (5) studied the electron spin resonance of $Cr^{3+}$ ions in [100]-type axial sites in MgO at room temperature. They suggested that the axial symmetry might be due to $Mg^{2+}$ ion vacancies or unipositive ions such as $Li^{+}$ in such vacancies, located in next-nearest-neighbor positions to the $Cr^{3+}$ ions and acting as charge compensators. No pressure studies have been done on the $Cr^{3+} D$ parameter in MgO and so it is not known experimentally whether or not the temperature dependence of $D$ is affected by lattice expansion. Calculations have nevertheless been done in an attempt to account for the temperature dependence. In the work of Walsh et al. (1) nearly the entire effect was attributed to thermal expansion alone, while in the work of others (2, 6) it was attributed entirely to lattice vibrations.

Hartman et al. (7) studied the temperature dependence of the spin relaxation of $Cr^{3+}$ impurities in cubic sites of MgO, and they found it necessary to consider the effects of localized modes in order to explain their experimental results. They concluded that the relaxation was governed by a narrow frequency band of oscil- lations localized around each $Cr^{3+}$ ion, and calculated the average frequency of the band to be $\sim 355 ~cm^{-1}$. Ham et al. (8) calculated the dynamic Jahn-Teller effect for $Fe^{2+}$ impurities in MgO. In their treatment of the problem they assumed that the principal coupling of the $Fe^{2+}$ ion was with the six oxygens which are nearestneighbors to it, and that only two discrete modes of vibration of the $Fe^{2+} \cdot 6 O^{2-}$  complex were important. In the calculation procedure they chose arbitrary pairs of frequencies in the interval 300 to $400 ~cm^{-1}$, which spans the major part of the frequency distribution for lattice phonons in MgO, and obtained good agreement with experiment. Moreover, they stated that the best agreement was obtained when the two vibrational frequencies were equal to each other and had a value of $\sim 350 ~cm^{-1}$.

## THEORY

The following empirical expression, which includes the effect of lattice expansion and vibration, is used in the present work to describe the temperature dependence of the spin-Hamiltonian parameters

$$
E=E_{s}^{0}\left(1+\alpha T^{x}\right)+\beta\left[\theta_{1} \operatorname{coth}\left(\theta_{1} / 2 T\right)+\theta_{2} \operatorname{coth}\left(\theta_{2} / 2 T\right)\right]. \tag{1}
$$

Here $E$ is the value of the spin-Hamiltonian parameter at a given temperature $T$, and $E_{s}^{0}, \alpha, x, \beta, \theta_{1}$, and $\theta_{2}$ are parameters which are determined by fitting Eq. [1] to a given $E$ vs $T$ curve. The first term in Eq. [1] represents the effect of lattice expansion. It is a function of $T$ raised to the power $x$, and is a simple monotonic curve which has the flexibility to account for any general static expansion expected for the crystal lattice. The two coth functions in the second term represent the contributions due to two vibration frequencies affecting a given $E$ vs $T$ curve, and $\theta=h \nu / k$ with $\nu$ the frequency of the exciting vibration. In practice, the inclusion of two frequencies is found to be sufficient to describe the experimental results. When only one frequency is required then Eq. [1] can be written as

$$
E=E_{s}^{0}\left(1+\alpha T^{x}\right)+E_{v}^{0} \operatorname{coth}(\theta / 2 T). \tag{2}
$$

In order to fit Eq. [1] to the $Mn^{2+}\ A$ vs $T$ curve (1) in the range of 0 to 400 K, values of $A$ were obtained from the best curve drawn through the experimental points in steps of $T=10$ K, with each value having an accuracy of ±0.003 G. In the case of the $Cr^{3+}\ D$ vs $T$ curve (1, 2), points were obtained in steps of $T=10$ K in the range 0 to 300 K and in steps of $T=50$ K in the range 300 to 900 K, with each value having an accuracy of ±0.03 G. Values at 0 K were not used in either case. The parameters of Eq. [1] were found using the determined $D$ and $A$ values separately in a least-squares-fit computer program as follows: For given values of $x$ and $\theta_{2}$, Eq. [1] was fitted with a list of $\theta_{1}$ values ranging from 10 to 1500 K in steps of 5 K. The parameters $E_{s}^{0}$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$ are obtained along with the least-squares deviation for each combination of $\theta_{2}$ and $\theta_{1}$. In a single computer run the procedure is repeated for 20 different values of $x$ and the same $\theta_{1}$'s and $\theta_{2}$. The lowest least-squares deviation value is marked off the output data for each of the $x$ values, and then the lowest of these 20 values is selected as the best-fit value for the given $\theta_{2}$. The above procedure is repeated for many other $\theta_{2}$ values taken in steps of 5 K.

It was found that the best-fit value of Eq. [1] for both the $A$ vs $T$ and $D$ vs $T$ curves occurs when $\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}$ and $x=0.5$, and hence only one coth term is required, as given in Eq. [2]. The values of the parameters of Eq. [2] obtained at best fit are listed in Table I. Using these parameters the terms $E_{\mathrm{s}}^{0}(1+\alpha T^{x})$ and $E_{v}^{0}\coth(\theta/2T)$ are plotted in Figs. 1 and 2 for $A$ and $D$, respectively. The starting $A$ vs $T$ and $D$ vs $T$ curves are also shown in the respective figures.

## DISCUSSION

The result of the analysis, as shown in Figs. 1 and 2, is to attribute essentially the entire temperature dependence of both the $A$ and $D$ spin-Hamiltonian parameters to the effect of lattice vibrations, with the contribution due to lattice expansion being negligible. For the case of the $A$ parameter this result is in accord with pressure studies (1), and for the $D$ parameter it is in accord with the assumptions of Marshall *et al.* (2) and Shrivastava (6). Moreover the static value of $A$ at 0 K which is found to be $A_{s}^{0}=-87.881$ G, or $-82.10\ (10^{-4}\mathrm{\ cm}^{-1})$, is nearly identical to the value of $-82.3\ (10^{-4}\mathrm{\ cm}^{-1})$ calculated by Orbach and Simanek (4) using a simple model. Evidence for the existence of localized mode frequencies of impurity ions in MgO was mentioned above for the case of $Fe^{2+}$ and of $Cr^{3+}$ in cubic sites. Van Vleck (9) has in fact previously pointed out that such a situation was likely to exist in his studies on hydrated crystals. He stated that the oscillations affecting the $Cr^{3+} \cdot 6H_{2}O$ magnetic complexes in alums may not be typical of the crystal as a whole but may have a different distribution law. However, the energy measurements might be difficult to make in practice because of the low intensities obtained from a small number of impurities present; and also because the effect would be swamped by the bulk lattice vibrations, which occur in the same frequency range. If the local mode frequencies form a fairly wide band then the $\theta$ values would represent averages over the band frequencies.

In conclusion it should be mentioned that the fitting of Eq. [1] to the temperature dependence of an energy parameter will not automatically produce a nearly temperature-independent static component nor a single vibration frequency. We have previously applied Eq. [1] to the energy band gap curves for indirect transitions in germanium and silicon semiconductors (10). It was found in those cases that two $\theta$

values were important and that the $\theta$ values corresponded closely to the average values of the optical and acoustic lattice vibrational frequencies in both Ge and Si. The static contribution for Ge at 300 K was found to be 11% of the total gap variation, and for Si it was found to be 12% at 400 K. These results are in agreement with what is generally expected for the semiconductors.
