# Study of the solubility process of impurities in $A^3B^5$ compounds

**Abstract.** Analysis of the process of dissolving impurities in $A^3B^5$ compounds has been carried out. A method has been suggested for determination of the heat of dissolution of impurities in $A^3B^5$ compounds on the basis of relationships describing the equilibrium of the liquid-phase doping reaction with experimental values used for the coefficients of distribution. The thermodynamic properties of solid solutions of tellurium and zinc in gallium phosphide have been determined: the change of enthalpy and entropy, in particular, in the process of formation of a substitutional solid solution, and the heat of dissolution of impurities.

## 1. Introduction

Many experimental papers have appeared concerning the growth of epitaxial layers of $A^3B^5$ compounds. In cases where obtaining pure layers or doping with a particular impurity is the purpose the choice of optimum conditions for carrying out the process requires information about the thermodynamic properties of solid solutions of impurities in $A^3B^5$ compounds. It is to be regretted that there is a lack of published papers devoted to studies from the thermodynamic point of view on the process of dissolving impurities in these compounds, which is so important for semiconductor electronics.

In this connection a thermodynamic analysis of the process of dissolving impurities in $A^3B^5$ compounds is carried out in the present work and the possibility of determining their equilibrium concentration in growing epitaxial layers of $A^3B^5$ from both gaseous and liquid phase is pointed out. Also, the thermodynamic properties of substitutional solid solutions of some impurities (tellurium, zinc) in gallium phosphide are determined.

## 2. Results and discussion

The reaction of solution of monoatomic vapours of the impurity I in the binary semiconductor $A^3B^5$ leading to the formation of substitutional solid solution $\text{I}_\text{B}(\text{A}^3\text{B}^5)$ (with the component B being substituted) may be written in the form [1]

$$
\text{I}_\text{(gas)} + \text{A}^3\text{B}^5_\text{(sol)} \Leftrightarrow \text{I}_\text{B}(\text{A}^3\text{B}^5) + \text{B}_\text{(gas)}. \tag{1}
$$

The equilibrium constant of this reaction is

$$
K_1 = X^0_{\text{I}_\text{B}} p_\text{B}/p_\text{I} \tag{2}
$$

where $X^0_{\text{I}_\text{B}}$ is the molar part of un-ionized impurity atoms on site B and $p_\text{B}$ and $p_\text{I}$ are the monoatomic vapour pressures of the components B and I respectively.

A similar expression may be written for an impurity substituting the component A.

The equilibrium constant of reaction (1) is related to the variation of the isobaric-isothermal potential, $\Delta G$, of the reaction

$$
-RT \ln K_1 = \Delta G_\text{T} = \Delta H_1 - T \Delta S_1 \tag{3}
$$

where the changes of enthalpy and entropy are denoted by $\Delta H_1$ and $\Delta S_1$ respectively.

In general, the dependence of $\Delta H_1$ and $\Delta S_1$ on temperature should be taken into consideration. This could be neglected when carrying out calculations over a small temperature range or when the variation in heat capacity is small.

The process of formation of a solid solution on reaction (1) may be schematically divided into two stages. First an atom of the component B is released from the crystal lattice of the $A^3B^5$ substance and next an atom of the impurity I is introduced from the gas phase into the vacant site. Then, the heat effect, $\Delta H_1$, of the reaction of impurity dissolution is determined from the energy, $W_{\text{A-B}}$, of breaking the four bonds in the host crystal and the heat of dissolution, $Q_1$, of the impurity according to the expression

$$
\Delta H_1 = 4W_{\text{A-B}} - Q_1. \tag{4}
$$

There are no available data on $\Delta S_1$ for the different impurities and their correct determination is hindered. The magnitude of the change of the vibrational entropy of the system at transition of the impurity from its own lattice at a melting temperature $T_\text{I}$ into the lattice of the host compound at a melting point $T_\text{H}$ may be estimated in

the following way. At temperatures largely exceeding the Debye temperature of the crystal the vibrational entropy, $S_{\text{vibr}}$, may be expressed by [2]
$$
S_{\text{vibr}}=4 k+3 k \ln \left(k T / h v^{m}\right) \tag{5}
$$
provided the entropy of the crystal is zero at absolute zero temperature. In equation (5) $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $h$ is Planck's constant and $h v^{m}$ is the frequency of the highest vibrational mode of the crystal.

If we accept the reasonable assumption that the vibrational spectrum of the host crystal is not substantially changed on introducing the impurity then the variation of the vibrational entropy accompanying the transition of the impurity atom is given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta S_{\text{vibr}} & =4 k+3 k \ln \left(k T_{\mathrm{H}} / h v_{\mathrm{H}}^{m}\right)-\left[4 k+3 k \ln \left(k T_{\mathrm{I}} / h v_{\mathrm{I}}^{m}\right)\right] \\
& =3 k \ln \frac{T_{\mathrm{H}} v_{\mathrm{I}}^{m}}{T_{\mathrm{I}} v_{\mathrm{H}}^{m}}
\end{aligned} \tag{6}
$$
the indices I and H referring to the crystals of the impurity and the host substance respectively. By substituting $h v$ with $k \theta$ [2], where $\theta$ denotes the Debye temperature, we get
$$
\Delta S_{\mathrm{vibr}}=3 k \ln \frac{T_{\mathrm{H}} \theta_{\mathrm{I}}}{T_{\mathrm{I}} \theta_{\mathrm{H}}}. \tag{7}
$$

Because of the fact that for gallium phosphide and the impurities under consideration here the ratio $T_{\mathrm{H}} \theta_{\mathrm{I}} / T_{\mathrm{I}} \theta_{\mathrm{H}}$ is of the order of unity [3], we shall neglect $\Delta S_{\text{vibr}}$ in the calculations. The situation with the other A³B⁵ compounds is similar. Thus, the assumption for invariability of the vibrational spectrum of the semiconductor crystal in dissolving the impurity corresponds to the presumption that the entropy of the impurity in the host crystal is equal to the entropy of the crystal itself. Therefore, the change of entropy in dissolving the impurity in the crystal on reaction (1), $\Delta S_{\mathrm{I}}$, can be determined as a difference between the entropy of the gaseous component B, $S_{\text{B(gas)}}$ and the entropy of the impurity I, $S_{\text{I(gas)}}$:
$$
\Delta S_{\mathrm{I}}=S_{\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{gas})}-S_{\mathrm{I}(\mathrm{gas})}. \tag{8}
$$

The entropies of monoatomic vapours of elements are to be taken from [4–6].

For A³B⁵ compounds the magnitudes of the bond energy, $W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{B}}$, are given in the literature [7] (for example for gallium phosphide it is $170815 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$). When such data are not available the magnitude of $W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{B}}$ can be determined on the basis of publications concerning the thermodynamic properties of A³B⁵ compounds, namely the change of enthalpy in the reaction
$$
\mathrm{A}_{\text{(solid)}}^{3} \mathrm{~B}^{5} \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{A}_{(\mathrm{gas})}+\mathrm{B}_{(\mathrm{gas})}\left(\Delta H=\Delta H_{(\mathrm{sub}) \mathrm{A}^{3} \mathrm{~B}^{5}}=4 W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{B}}\right).
$$

In the gas phase the impurity can be present in the form of polyatomic molecules of the sort $\mathrm{I}_{n}$, where $n$ is the number of impurity atoms in a molecule or compound with the transporting substance $\mathrm{I}_{n} \mathrm{X}_{m}$ for gas transport techniques. In these cases it is necessary to calculate the equilibrium of the gas phase. Calculations of this sort do not encounter serious difficulties.

Hence, the equilibrium of reaction (1) can be calculated if the amount of heat, $Q_{\mathrm{I}}$, is determined. This heat is released in the transition of monoatomic impurity vapour from the gas phase into the lattice sites of the A³B⁵ crystal vacated by the corresponding component. The heat of dissolution of the impurity is determined from the binding energy of an impurity atom with the neighbouring atoms of the host substance, $W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{I}}$, and the strain energy, $W^{\text{strain}}$, introduced by the impurity atom since the tetrahedral radius is generally different from the tetrahedral radius of the host substance:
$$
Q_{\mathrm{I}}=4 W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{I}}-W^{\text{strain}}. \tag{9}
$$

The quantity $W^{\text{strain}}$ is calculated along the following lines. When an atom of the host substance is substituted by an impurity atom whose normal tetrahedral radius [8] differs from that of the host substance by $\Delta r$, the four nearest-neighbouring atoms are displaced radially by $\Delta r_{1}$, which is less than $\Delta r$ due to shortening of the impurity atom–host atom bonds. In the discussion which follows it is assumed that the impurity atom is larger than the host atom. This is true for most impurities, but in particular for those discussed in this paper. The treatment in the case with smaller impurity atoms, however, does not differ substantially from that presented here. The displacement of the four nearest neighbours from the first shell will cause a displacement of the second shell atoms by $\Delta r_{2}$, which in turn will induce a displacement of the third shell atoms $\Delta r_{3}$ and so on. Due to counteraction of the atoms to any displacement from their equilibrium positions it must hold that $\Delta r_{1}>\Delta r_{2}>\Delta r_{3}$, etc. Therefore, the induced disturbance in the order will soon die down with distance. In order to simplify the calculations we shall ignore the atomic nature of the displacements outside the shell comprising the four nearest neighbours and we shall approximate the host substance outside by a continuous medium with macroscopic elastic properties (figure 1). On the basis of Hooke's law and the classical theory of elasticity the strain energy, $W^{\text{strain}}$, can be expressed as follows:
$$
W^{\text{strain}}=4\left[\frac{1}{2} g K_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\Delta r-\Delta r_{1}\right)^{2}\right]+8 \pi G r_{0}\left(\Delta r_{1}\right)^{2}. \quad(10)
$$

The first term describes the compression of the four impurity–host atom bonds and will be equal to zero if the bonds are incompressible, so that the four nearest neighbours would be displaced by $\Delta r$. The stiffness constant of these bonds is denoted here by $g K_{\mathrm{c}}$, where $K_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the force constant of two atoms from the host semiconductor substance and $g$ is a dimensionless parameter of the order of unity. The second term in (10) is the energy stored in the surrounding matrix due to expansion of the cavity with a radius $r_{0}$ (i.e. the interatomic distance of the host crystal) by $\Delta r_{1}$. Here $G$ is the shear modulus of elasticity of the host crystal.

We can eliminate $\Delta r_{1}$ from equation (10) by imposing the requirement
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} W^{\text{strain}}}{\mathrm{d}\left(\Delta r_{1}\right)}=0
$$
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} W^{\text{strain}}}{\mathrm{d}\left(\Delta r_{1}\right)}=4 g K_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\Delta r-\Delta r_{1}\right)+16 \pi G r_{0} \Delta r_{1}=0 \quad(11)
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta r_{1}}{\Delta r}=\frac{2 g K_{\mathrm{c}}}{2 g K_{\mathrm{c}}+8 \pi G r_{0}}. \tag{11a}
$$

In the absence of a generally agreed value for the stiffness constant $K_{\rm c}$ we shall proceed as follows. In the case of a cubic lattice with only nearest-neighbour forces of importance, it is shown in [16] that the stiffness constant of the bonds is equal to the lattice constant multiplied by one of the elastic stiffness constants of the lattice, $c_{11}$. For this particular case ($c_{12}=0$), $c_{11}$ equals the inverse of the linear compressibility, which for cubic crystals equals $c_{11}+2c_{12}$. We shall therefore assume that, in general for a cubic crystal, the stiffness constant is proportional to the inverse of the linear compressibility, the proportionality constant being approximately equal to the lattice constant

$$
K_{\rm c}=ma(c_{11}+2c_{12}) \tag{11b}
$$

where $a$ is the lattice parameter, $c_{11}$ and $c_{12}$ are the elastic stiffness constants and $m$ is a dimensionless proportionality factor of the order of unity.

Substituting equation (11b) into equation (11a) we thus obtain

$$
\frac{\Delta r_{1}}{\Delta r}=\frac{2gm(ac_{11}+2ac_{12})}{2gm(ac_{11}+2ac_{12})+8\pi Gr_{0}}. \tag{11c}
$$

Finally, inserting equation (11c) into equation (10) we can express the strain energy as a function of $\Delta r$:

$$
W^{\rm strain}=C(\Delta r)^{2} \quad (\rm J\ mol^{-1}) \tag{12}
$$

where $\Delta r$ is the difference between the value of tetrahedral radii of the impurity and the substituted host atom (in metres) and

$$
C=\frac{2gma(c_{11}+2c_{12})}{1+2gma(c_{11}+2c_{12})/8\pi Gr_{0}}.
$$

is a constant characteristic for the particular substance. Using the values of the corresponding quantities for the given compound we can obtain the value of this constant $C$. For gallium phosphide the numerical value of $C$ is equal to $8.235\times10^{25}$ (the values of the constant $C$ for InAs and GaAs are respectively equal to $5.672\times10^{25}$ and $7.199\times10^{25}$). The values of the tetrahedral radii $r$ are presented in [8].

The bond energy $W_{\rm A-I}$ is defined as a half of the sum of the bond energies of atoms in the crystalline substances I and A [9]:

$$
W_{\rm A-I}=-\frac{1}{2}(W_{\rm I-I}+W_{\rm A-A})=-\frac{1}{4}(\Delta H_{\rm subl.I}+\Delta H_{\rm subl.A}). \tag{13}
$$

In table 1 we list the values of $S_{\rm I.(gas)}$, $\Delta H_{\rm subl.I}$, $r$, and the computed values of the strain energy $W^{\rm strain}$, the bond energy $W_{\rm A(B)-I}$, the heat of dissolution $Q_{\rm I}$, the heat effect $\Delta H_{\rm I}$, the entropy change $\Delta S_{\rm I}$, and the equilibrium constant $K_{\rm I}$ of reaction (1), for the impurities of tellurium and zinc substituting for phosphorus and gallium, respectively, in gallium phosphide. Similar data can also be presented for other impurities in gallium phosphide, replacing gallium and phosphorus, respectively, as well as for impurities in other $\rm A^{3}B^{5}$ compounds.

Doping from the liquid phase can also be reduced to reaction (1). It is sufficient to take into account that the equilibrium of a mixture of the liquid and the gaseous phase is described by the reaction

$$
\rm I_{liq}\leftrightarrow I_{(gas)}. \tag{14}
$$

The equilibrium constant of this reaction is

$$
K_{14}=p_{\rm I}/\gamma_{\rm I}X_{\rm I}. \tag{15}
$$

$K_{14}$ is related to the variation of the isobaric-isothermal potential of this reaction

$$
-RT\ln K_{14}=\Delta G_{\rm T}=\Delta H_{\rm evp.I}-T\Delta S_{\rm evp.I}.
$$

The heat of evaporation and the entropy of evaporation are denoted by $\Delta H_{\rm evp.I}$ and $\Delta S_{\rm evp.I}$ respectively, and for most elements these can be determined [4-6]. The activity coefficient of impurity in the liquid phase, $\gamma_{\rm I}$, can be obtained from the liquidus curve of the system under examination.

The above-described method for determination of the heat of dissolution of impurities and afterwards the change in enthalpies at the formation of substitutional solid solutions makes use of available thermodynamic data.

It would be interesting to compare the values of $Q_{\rm I}$, $\Delta H_{\rm I}$ and $K_{14}$ derived here with estimates obtained in another way. The heat of dissolution of the impurity in the semiconductor lattice can be calculated if experimental

data are available regarding the equilibrium of the impurity with the host semiconductor even under certain particular experimental conditions. The coefficients of impurity distribution in the particular A³B⁵ substance are convenient for use as starting data.

By adding (1), (14) and the reaction
$$\mathrm{B}_{(\text{gas})} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{B}_{(\text{liq})}$$
we obtain the reaction of dissolution of impurity at doping from the liquid phase
$$\mathrm{I}_{(\text{liq})}+\mathrm{A}_{(\mathrm{sol})}^{3} \mathrm{B}_{(\mathrm{sol})}^{5} \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{B}}\left(\mathrm{A}^{3} \mathrm{B}^{5}\right)+\mathrm{B}_{(\text{liq})}. \tag{16}$$

The equilibrium constant of this reaction is
$$K_{16}=X_{\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{B}}}^{0} a_{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{liq}} / a_{\mathrm{I}}^{\text{liq}} \tag{17}$$
where $a_{i}^{\text{liq}}$ is the activity of the corresponding components in the liquid phase.

As far as the activity is equal to the product of the molar part of the component $X_{i}^{\text{liq}}$ and its activity coefficient $\gamma_{i}^{\text{liq}}$, so
$$K_{16}=X_{\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{B}}}^{0} X_{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{liq}} \gamma_{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{liq}} / X_{\mathrm{I}}^{\text{liq}} \gamma_{\mathrm{I}}^{\text{liq}} \tag{18}$$
where $\gamma_{i}^{\text{liq}}$ are activity coefficients of the corresponding components in the liquid phase.

$K_{16}$ can be presented through the change of the isobaric-isothermal potential of reaction (16), $\Delta G_{16}$, in the following way:
$$-R T \ln K_{16}=\Delta G_{16}=\Delta H_{16}-T \Delta S_{16}$$

For comparing $K_{16}$ it is necessary for $\Delta H_{16}$ and $\Delta S_{16}$ to be known. Assuming as before that the vibrational spectrum of the crystal remains unchanged upon dissolving impurities the change in entropy $\Delta S_{16}$ can be found from
$$\Delta S_{16}=S_{\mathrm{B}(\text{liq})}-S_{\mathrm{I}(\text{liq})}. \tag{19}$$

The thermal effect of reaction (16) is
$$\Delta H_{16}=4 W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{B}}-Q_{\mathrm{I}}+\Delta H_{\mathrm{evp.I}}-\Delta H_{\mathrm{evp.B}}. \tag{20}$$

The distribution coefficient $k$ in molar parts is defined for each component as
$$k=X_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{sol}} / X_{\mathrm{I}}^{\text{liq}}. \tag{21}$$

In the expression (18) one finds the molar part of the neutral atoms of impurity $X_{\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{B}}}^{0}$ on site B. In order to convert to the overall concentration let us denote the fraction of un-ionized impurity atoms by $F_{\mathrm{n}}$, $F_{\mathrm{n}}=X_{\mathrm{I}}^{0} / X_{\mathrm{I}}$. Then the constant $K_{16}$ can be rewritten as
$$K_{16}=k F_{\mathrm{n}} X_{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{liq}} \gamma_{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{liq}} / \gamma_{\mathrm{I}}^{\text{liq}} \tag{22}$$
and
$$\begin{gathered}
-R T \ln K_{16}=\left(4 W_{\mathrm{A}-\mathrm{B}}-Q_{\mathrm{I}}+\Delta H_{\mathrm{evp.I}}-\Delta H_{\mathrm{B}}\right) \\
-T\left(S_{\mathrm{B}(\text{liq})}-S_{\mathrm{I}(\text{liq})}\right).
\end{gathered} \tag{23}$$

The fraction of neutral impurity atoms can be determined using the Fermi-Dirac distribution [10]. To find $F_{\mathrm{n}}$ it is necessary to calculate the Fermi level by solving numerically the electrical neutrality equation taking into account the particular parameters of the zone structure of the A³B⁵ compound doped with the corresponding impurity. In the present study we used the procedure described in [17,18] for determination of the Fermi level and $F_{\mathrm{n}}$. The impurity distribution coefficients used in (22) are measured in doping A³B⁵ compounds from melts near to stoichiometry [11] at conditions of sufficiently high impurity concentration in the solid phase. In such a case, when calculating the Fermi level, the possibility of degeneration of electrons or p-carriers in the semiconductor is to be taken into account.

The heat of dissolution of impurities, $Q_{\mathrm{I}}$, and hence the change of enthalpy at the formation of substitutional solid solutions, $\Delta H_{1}$, can be determined from (22) and (23) by assigning values to the corresponding parameters.

In table 2 we present the necessary values for calculation of $Q_{\mathrm{Te}}$ and $Q_{\mathrm{Zn}}$ in gallium phosphide from (22) and (23) as well as the results obtained for $Q_{\mathrm{Te}}$, $Q_{\mathrm{Zn}}$ and $\Delta H_{1}$.

The values of the activity of phosphorus and gallium at the melting temperature of gallium phosphide were taken from [12] and are equal to 0.5 and 0.338, respectively. The values of $\gamma_{\mathrm{Te}}^{\text{liq}}$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{Zn}}^{\text{liq}}$ were estimated from data in [13], where dependences of the activity coefficients of tellurium and zinc on the composition of the liquid phase are presented. The distribution coefficients of tellurium and zinc were taken from [14].

Comparing the values of the heat of dissolution $Q_{\mathrm{I}}$ of tellurium and zinc in gallium phosphide determined from the bond energy (see table 1) with the estimates based on the distribution coefficient (see table 2) it is clear that they are very close to each other (the difference being no more than 3%). For zinc in gallium arsenide these values are even closer (the difference being less than 0.5%).

The heat of dissolution of zinc in GaAs has also been evaluated from the solidus curve [15]. The obtained experimental value $Q_{\mathrm{Zn}}=432.496 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ is very near to the calculated estimates based on the distribution coefficient $Q_{\mathrm{Zn}}=427.977 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ and the bond energy $Q_{\mathrm{Zn}}=429.147 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$. This shows that both methods described above for determination of the heat of dissolution of impurities in A³B⁵ compounds yield good results and can be useful in cases when experimental data regarding the solubility of impurities are not available.

The comparison made above for the $Q_{\mathrm{l}}$ values determined from both the bond energy and the distribution coefficient of tellurium and zinc in gallium phosphide gives grounds to evaluate $\gamma^{\text{liq}}$ in the way that follows. In (22) and (23) the assigned values of the corresponding quantities are inserted (in our case $Q_{\mathrm{l}}$ is substituted with the value based on the bond energy, the distribution coefficients for most of the impurities being known). The activity coefficient of the impurity comes out from equations (22) and (23) solved simultaneously. ### 3. Conclusions

Studies of the processes of doping epitaxial layers of $\mathrm{A^{3}B^{5}}$ compounds prove to be necessary for controlled growth of layers with prescribed properties and for clarification of the nature and sources of uncontrolled impurities, in particular. In this connection, a thermodynamic analysis has been carried out in the present work regarding the process of dissolving impurities in $\mathrm{A^{3}B^{5}}$ compounds. On the basis of the analysis, the thermodynamic properties of solid solutions of tellurium and zinc in gallium phosphide were determined. From the relationships describing the reaction equilibrium in liquid phase doping and with experimental values of distribution coefficients being used we suggested a method for determination of the heat of dissolution of impurities in $\mathrm{A^{3}B^{5}}$. The heats of dissolution of tellurium and zinc in gallium phosphide were determined in this way.

Comparison of the heat of dissolution calculated from the bond energy of the impurity and the host semiconductor with the estimate based on the distribution coefficient has shown that the method of evaluating the heat of dissolution from the bond energy yields good results and can be useful in cases where data for solubilities of impurities in the liquid phase are not available. On this basis a method for determination of activity coefficients in melts of $\mathrm{A^{3}B^{5}}$ compounds was developed. The values of the heat of dissolution and the activity coefficient in the liquid phase for some impurities (e.g. zinc in GaAs) calculated along the lines of the suggested method are in good agreement with the corresponding experimental data.
