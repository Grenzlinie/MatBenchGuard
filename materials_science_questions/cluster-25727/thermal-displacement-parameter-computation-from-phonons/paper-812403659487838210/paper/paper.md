# Quantum thermal contraction of Au nanoparticles

W.-H. Li,* S. Y. Wu, C. C. Yang, F. C. Tsao, S. K. Lai, and K . C. Lee

Department of Physics, National Central University, Chung-Li, Taiwan 32054

Received 2 July 2002; accepted 20 October 2002

## Abstract

We report on the successful fabrication of a fine Au powder with a mean particle size of 4 nm. A crossover from a positive thermal expansion at low temperatures to a negative thermal expansion at high temperatures was observed in the fcc cell parameter at around 125 K. Using a discrete electronic energy level scheme for nanoparticles, we introduce a weakly temperature dependent level spacing $\Delta(T)$ to show how the potential energy is lowered by lattice expansion at low temperatures and by contraction at high temperatures. Anomalies were also observed in the AC magnetic susceptibility and the heat capacity measurements, which can be interpreted by the same model.

**Keywords:** Electron-solid interaction, equilibrium thermodynamics, x-ray diffraction, chemical vapor deposition

The anharmonic lattice potential is known to give rise to a positive thermal expansion, characterized by the Gruneisen parameter [1] for systems where no structural changes have occurred. Here, we consider the effects of the valence electron potential on the equilibrium lattice separation in systems (specifically for nanoparticles) having discrete electronic energy levels. It is known that the allowed energies for valence electrons in nanoparticles can be taken as being inversely proportional to the cube of the particle diameter, which may vary with temperature and may be taken to be $\Delta(T) \propto 1 / R^{3}$, where $R$ is the unit cell parameter. The attractive binding energy of thermally excited valence electrons, at a finite temperature, is taken to be approximately $U_{v}=-N_{e} \sum_{n} \Delta_{n}(R) f(\Delta_{n})$, where $N_{e}$ is the number of valence electrons and $f(\Delta_{n})$ is the Fermi-Dirac population function of the $\mathrm{n}^{\text {th }}$ level. The requirement of an energy minimum for equilibrium atomic positions reads $\left[d\left(U_{0}+U_{v}\right) / d R\right]_{R=a}=0$ , where $(d U_{0} / d R)_{R=a}=0$ describes the equilibrium lattice constant $a$ at T=0. Figure 1 shows the variation of $d U_{v} / d R$ with temperature, for an equal spacing $(\Delta_{n}=n \Delta)$ ten-level system. A minimum occurs at $\mathrm{T}_{\text {min }} \approx 2.19 \Delta / k_{B}$, indicating that the effects on the lattice parameters are different for temperature below and above $\mathrm{T}_{\text {min }}$. A crossover from a positive thermal expansion to a negative thermal expansion can then be anticipated at $\mathrm{T}=2.19 \Delta / k_{B}$. Another characteristic temperature of interest is $\mathrm{T}_{0} \approx 4.05 \Delta / k_{B}$, at which $d U_{v} / d R=0$. This is the temperature at which $U_{v}$ reaches its maximum, but no correction to the equilibrium lattice positions from the excited valence electrons is made.

We use Au nanoparticles to examine this quantum contraction effect, by measuring the variation of cell parameters with temperature. Au nanoparticle powder was fabricated by employing the gas condensation method [2]. The sample is in a powder form, is assembles of individual nanoparticles. There is no substrate. X-ray diffraction and atomic force microscope (AFM) were used to characterize the powder. As expected, the diffraction peaks of the powder are much broader than those of the ingot, reflecting the finite-size effect. A quite symmetrical size distribution was obtained from the AFM images, which may be described using a Gaussian function, with its center at 4.0 nm and a relatively narrow half-width of 1.2 nm. Using the peak profiles of the ingot as the instrumental resolution and assuming a Gaussian size distribution of half-width 1.2 nm, we obtained a mean particle diameter of 4.0(4) nm for the powder, by fitting the nanoparticle pattern to the diffraction profiles of finite sized particles. It appears that the average diameter obtained from the x-ray pattern and from the AFM images agrees well. Although it is commonly expected that the structure of finite-size particles may differ from those of the bulk [3], the crystal structure of the 4 nm Au nanoparticles remains in an fcc symmetry, as that of Au ingot. The room temperature cell parameter of the nanoparticle ($a$=4.0682 Å) is ~0.2% smaller than that of the ingot ($a$=4.0761 Å). This behavior has been attributed [4] to an increase in the surface stress in nanoparticles, due to their high surface to volume ratio.

Figure 2 shows the temperature dependence of the nanoparticle cell parameter for $T \leqq 390$ K. The open and filled symbols indicate the data from two separate experiments. Below 100 K, a positive thermal expansion is seen, with a linear expansion coefficient of $\alpha = +3.2 \times 10^{-5}$ K⁻¹, which is a factor of 2.3 times larger than that of the ingot [5] ($\alpha = +1.4 \times 10^{-5}$ K⁻¹), signifying the existence of effects due to excitations other than lattice effects. A downturn in the temperature dependence of the cell parameters occurs at around 125 K. Above 150 K, the linear expansion coefficient becomes negative ( $\alpha = -2.5 \times 10^{-5}$ K⁻¹), showing a lattice contraction, rather than expansion, when the temperature is increased. These behaviors are consistent with those discussed above. We believe, however, that both the lattice and valence electrons will contribute to the thermal behavior of the cell parameters. Unfortunately, no data on the lattice expansion coefficient of Au nanoparticles is available. As a reasonable estimate, we adopt the expansion coefficient of the ingot for that of the nanoparticle. The inset to Fig. 2 shows the temperature dependence of $1/a^{*}$, where $a^{*}$ is the cell parameter corrected for the lattice contribution, by using a linear expansion coefficient of $+1.4 \times 10^{-5}$ K⁻¹. The solid line indicates the theoretical curve for $\Delta$=3.5 meV,. This value of $\Delta$=3.5 meV obtained for 4 nm Au particles agrees well with the 3.24 meV predicted by using Kubo theory [6,7].

Both AC magnetic susceptibility and heat capacity measurements were also performed. Paramagnetic responses were observed at all temperatures, with a clear peak at ~160 K. Relative heat capacity of the nanoparticles, measured by employing an AC calorimeter, reveals an anomaly at 125 K. Recalling that the Pauli paramagnetic susceptibility is proportional to the difference between the spin up and spin down populations [1], it is reasonable to expect that the maximum paramagnetic response will appear at the temperature at which the thermally excited population reaches its maximum. This temperature can be taken to be at $T_{0}$=4.05$\Delta/k_{B}$. Using the same $\Delta$=3.5 meV$\approx$40.6 K, as that obtained from the cell parameter data, the maximum paramagnetic response will then be expected to be at 40.6×4.05=164 K, which agrees very well with the data obtained (not shown). We finally remark that lattice shrinking results in an increase in the level separation when the temperature is increased, which on the one hand reduces the number of electrons occupying the excited states as dictated by the Fermi-Dirac factor, and on the other hand, raises the thermal energy of individual electron in the excited states. These two factors compete delicately to achieve a lower electronic potential energy. According to the analysis, the crossover from lattice expansion to lattice shrinking should occur above room temperatures for systems having a level spacing greater than 12 meV.
