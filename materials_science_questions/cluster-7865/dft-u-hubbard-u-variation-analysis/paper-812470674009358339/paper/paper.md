nitrogen oxides and cyanate salts, both of which are harmful to humans and aquatic ecosystems (Krausfeldt et al., 2019; Shaw and Bordeaux, 1955). Traditional wastewater treatment methods to remove urea such as high pressure-temperature hydrolysis, bio- logical decomposition, and decomposition by strong oxidants, provide sustainable solutions for closing nitrogen cycle, but fail to extract value from this energy-rich compound (Urbańczyk et al., 2016). Electrochemical oxidation of urea is an attractive candidate to replace current urea-removal methods as it enables the capture of the chemical energy stored in the bonds of urea, and is facilitated by inexpensive catalyst materials (Zhu et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2020; Sayed et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2018). In this regard, UOR continues to attract considerable attention as an alternative to direct water oxidation as an anodic reaction in the electrochemical generation of hydrogen and $CO_2$ electrolysis to value-added products because of the lower voltage required to drive the reaction as well as the aforementioned abundance of environment-polluting urea avail- able in wastewater streams (Chen et al., 2021; Medvedeva et al., 2020; Zhang, 2019). Thus far, Ni(II-IV)-based materials such as ox- ides, hydroxides, sulfides, and others have shown the best catalytic performance towards UOR compared to other catalysts due to the in situ generation of reactive nickel sites that act as oxidants towards urea (Zhang et al., 2019; Vedharathinam and Botte, 2014). However, the activity of these materials is known to become sluggish over time due to the generation of carbonate at the surface over the course of a reaction (Forslund et al., 2016; Wang and Botte, 2014; Lu and Botte, 2017). To reduce changes in the surface and further improve the intrinsic activity of the catalyst towards UOR, re- searchers have begun to investigate the performance of binary and ternary multimetal electrocatalysts (Zhu et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2020; Sayed et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2018).

Among the binary transition metal catalysts studied for UOR, mixtures of Ni and Co with non-zero valences have attracted attention due to their increased current densities, reduced onset potentials, and prolonged stability relative to other Ni(II-IV)-based systems mixed with a secondary metal (Zhu et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2018; Khalafallah et al., 2020; Tesfaye et al., 2019). While the optimal composition in $Ni_{1-x}Co_x(OH)_2$ catalysts is reported as $Ni_{90}Co_{10}(OH)_2$ or $Ni_{80}Co_{20}(OH)_2$ depending on compositions stud- ied, the nature of the enhancement and the cause for its maximi- zation between 10 and 20% Co incorporation still remains unclear (see Table S2 in SI for a literature summary of $Ni_{1-x}Co_x(OH)_2$ based catalysts for UOR). Several studies have concluded that changes in electrical conductivity due to Co incorporation or increases in the electrochemically active surface area are not the origin of enhanced UOR (Xu et al., 2014; Ramesh and Vishnu Kamath, 2009; Sun et al., 2019). In addition, it is known that adding Co can increase the number of Ni(IV) surface sites (postulated to be the most active toward UOR) (Zhang et al., 2019; Armstrong et al., 1988; Armstrong and Charles, 1989). The number of Ni(IV) sites is expected to be maximized with a Co incorporation for either $Ni_{60}Co_{40}(OH)_2$ or $Ni_{50}Co_{50}(OH)_2$, which conflicts with the trend discussed above for the best performing $Ni_{1-x}Co_x(OH)_2$ UOR catalyst (Tesfaye et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2014; Armstrong et al., 1988; Armstrong and Charles, 1989). Thus, the origin of UOR enhancement is thought to be caused by Co-induced changes to the electronic structure of Ni however, more in-depth studies are needed to verify this (Hao, 2020; Sha, 2019; Yan, 2020).

Herein we explore the effect of Co incorporation on Ni UOR activity and investigate Co-induced changes to Ni electronic structure by combining electrochemical analysis and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The prepared catalysts were characterized by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), and UV-Vis spectroscopy.

Through electrochemical screening of each composition by cyclic voltammetry (CV) we observed that the number of available surface sites and their intrinsic activity can be tuned by altering the Co fraction in $Ni_{1-x}Co_x(OH)_2$. The influence of electronic structure of neighbouring Co atoms on the electronic structure of Ni in $Ni(OH)_2$ surface doped with Co was elucidated by DFT, allowing us to draw conclusions about enhanced UOR activity.

## 2. Experimental methods

### 2.1. Synthesis of materials

**Synthesis of $Ni_{1-x}Co_x(OH)_{2-y}Cl_y$.** The synthesis of $Ni_{1- x}Co_x(OH)_{2-y}Cl_y$ was adapted from elsewhere with modifications to improve reaction mixing and temperature control (Zhang et al., 2019; He et al., 2018). To begin, a total of 0.9 mmol of Ni(II) $Cl_2·6H_2O$ (Sigma; 99.9% trace metals basis) and Co(II)$Cl_2·6H_2O$ (Sigma; 98% ACS Grade) was added to centrifuge tubes and dis- solved in 4.0 mL absolute ethanol (>99.9% ACS grade). The tubes were then sonicated for <10 s to ensure a homogenous distribution of metal ions was achieved followed by the fast addition of 1.4 mL of propylene oxide (Sigma; ≥99.5% GC basis) (PO). Immediately after PO addition, the tubes were capped and sealed with parafilm, then shaken using an Eppendorf Thermomixer C at $30\ ^{\circ}C$ at 450 RPM for 24 h. Afterwards, each tube was filled with ~11 mL anhydrous acetone (Sigma; ≥99.9 HPLC Grade), then decanted to remove the supernatant which contained the majority of unreacted precursors. The tubes were then filled with anhydrous acetone and allowed to sit for 24 h to undergo solvent exchange from the pores of the gel. After 24 h, the supernatant was decanted and replaced; this process was repeated three more times for a total of five solvent exchange cycles. After the final solvent exchange, the tubes were decanted and dried using a rotary evaporator at $30\ ^{\circ}C$ under a vacuum of ~730 mm Hg for 8 h to form a xerogel. The xerogels were then ground into a powder and placed under vacuum at $25\ ^{\circ}C$ for 60 h to remove residual solvent.

### 2.2. Physical characterization

**ICP-MS measurements.** Powdered catalysts (29-30 mg) were digested in 20 mL 2% $HNO_3$ for 72 h in glass scintillation vials. Two successive 10x dilutions were then performed to get a final total Co + Ni concentration of approximately 100 ppm. Samples were submitted to the Metal Isotope Geochemistry Laboratory in the Earth Science department at the University of Waterloo. Class A volumetric glassware was used for preparing solutions used in this analysis. A $200\ \mu L$ autopipette was calibrated and used for diluting the digestate. All glassware and pipette tips were soaked in 2% $HNO_3$ prior to rinsing with MilliQ water before use.

**SEM-EDX sample measurements.** Catalyst inks were made by mixing 2 mg of catalyst material with 1 mg Vulcan Carbon Black (FuelCellStore), $400\ \mu L$ absolute ethanol (>99.9% ACS grade) and $100\ \mu L$ MilliQ water $(18.2\ \Omega)$. Ink mixtures were sonicated for 1 h after which the catalyst ink was drop cast onto Toray 60 Carbon paper (FuelCellStore); the volume of ink deposited was adjusted to get $0.226\ \text{mg}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ on each sample. EDX analysis including elemental and phase mapping was performed on an FEI Quanta FEG 250 environmental scanning electron microscope.

**SEM and TEM imaging.** For high magnification imaging, catalyst inks were made by mixing catalysts with MilliQ water $(18.2\ \Omega)$ in a 4:3 mg: mL ratio and sonicated for 10 min. Ink droplets were dropcast onto Cu/C TEM grids and imaged on a Hitachi HF3300 microscope. Low magnification imaging was performed using an FEI Quanta FEG 250 environmental scanning electron microscope.

**PXRD sample preparation.** PXRD measurements were

conducted at room temperature on a PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer with Cu-K$\alpha$ radiation equipped with a PIXcel bidimensional detector. Powdered samples were measured on zero-background substrate using a Bragg-Brentano geometry. Phase pattern simulations were done by modifying the $c$ axis of CIF files ($\beta$-Ni(OH)$_2$: ICSD-161894, $\beta$-Co(OH)$_2$: ICSD-26763) to match the experimental interlamellar spacing. The modified CIF files used in the complete integration method in real space (CIREALS) method described elsewhere reproduced the experimental peak positions and relative order of intensity for both pure Ni and Co phases (Jiang et al., 2018).

UV-Vis measurements. The absorbance spectra of powdered catalyst samples were measured by reflectance UV-Vis using a R200-Angle Ocean Optics Reflection probe at a distance of ~3 cm with a deuterium-halogen light source (DH-2000-BAL) and Ocean Optics Flame Miniature Spectrometer. The background reflectance spectrum for all measurements was taken from a polished slab of PTFE.

Raman Spectroscopy measurements. Raman spectroscopy was conducted with a Renishaw inVia Raman microscope. All spectra were acquired using a 532 nm laser filtered to 10% laser power with neutral density filters. Each spectrum had an acquisition time of 300 s, except 100% Co which required 150 s due to laser damage. Spectra were analyzed in Renishaw WiRE V5.3 software to perform polynomial baseline subtractions and curve fitting.

X-ray photoelectron measurements. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was performed using the ThermoFisher Scientific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer. Samples were prepared by activating anodes as described below, followed by immediately rinsing with MilliQ water and drying under a stream of Ar. The as-prepared samples were stored under Ar prior to the analysis.

### 2.3. Electrochemical assessment
Anode Preparation. Catalyst inks were made by mixing 2 mg of catalyst material with 1 mg Vulcan Carbon Black (FuelCellStore) then adding 390 $\mu$L absolute ethanol (>99.9% ACS grade) and 100 $\mu$L MilliQ water (18.2 $\Omega$). The mixture was sonicated briefly (<3 s) to disperse the powder in the solvent before the addition of 10 $\mu$L of Nafion D-520 dispersion (Alfa Aesar; 42118). Ink mixtures were sonicated for 1 h, after which five layers of catalyst ink were dropcast onto a $0.58 \pm 0.04$ cm$^2$ piece of Toray 60 Carbon paper (FuelCellStore) such that the total volume of ink deposited was adjusted to get 0.226 mg cm$^{-2}$ on each anode.

Evaluation of UOR activity. Evaluation of UOR activity was performed in 1 M aqueous KOH (Sigma; $\geq$85% ACS Grade) electrolyte containing 0.33 M urea (Sigma; $\geq$99.5% ReagentPlus) in deionized water (18.2 $\Omega$). The electrolyte was purged with Ar prior to use. All electrochemical measurements took place in an undivided three-electrode cell with a double junction Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl) reference electrode and Pt mesh counter electrode. Catalysts were first activated in 50 mL 1 M KOH by CV cycles scanning from $-0.4$ to 0.8 V vs Ag/AgCl at 100 mV/s for 50 scans using a potentiostat (Biologic SP-300). This was followed by CV cycles within the same range at 20 mV/s for 20 scans to achieve a steady state. CV was used to assess the activity differences between activated catalysts with different Ni:Co ratios in 50 mL 1 M KOH and 50 mL 1 M KOH +0.33 M urea by scanning from $-0.4$ to 0.8 V vs Ag/AgCl at 20 mV/s for 10 scans. Potentials were converted to V vs RHE using equation (1) below and assuming that the bulk pH was the same as the local pH at the electrode.

$$
E_{RHE}=E_{Ag/AgCl,3.5M\ KCl} + 0.059\ pH + E^{^\circ}_{Ag/AgCl,3.5M\ KCl} \tag{1}
$$

Current densities were normalized by both the geometric surface area of the catalyst film and the number of redox active surface sites (RASS). The RASS was determined by performing a linear integration of a baseline subtracted current in the redox active region. The Butler-Volmer equation was first used to fit the raw current data between 1.23 and 1.64 V vs RHE to subtract the theoretical OER current from the redox-active region (Gu et al., 2020). The capacitance current (from 0.8 to 1.23 V vs RHE) and remaining OER current (1.64 to 1.831 V vs RHE) were then fit to polynomials to construct a baseline. Each baseline was then subtracted from the raw current in the corresponding potential region. All baselines for each composition with subtracted current data are shown in the SI. To convert the charge from the integrated redox region into the number of RASS, we assumed that the entire region was associated with a 1e$^-$transfer process, as reported recently in work by Anantharaj et al. (Anantharaj and Kundu, 2019).

### 2.4. Theoretical methods
PDOS and Charge Density Difference Calculations. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) within the Python-based Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) (Larsen et al., 2017; Kresse and Furthmüller, 1996a, 1996b; Kresse and Hafner, 1993, 1994). To account for the self-repulsion of elections in the d-orbitals that has been previously reported to result in spurious occupation and bandgap prediction, the generalized gradient approximation with Hubbard correction potential (GGA + U) method was used with the BEEF-vdW exchange-correlation functional (Majewski and Vogl, 1992; Wellendorff et al., 2012; Szotek and Temmerman, 1993). Spin-polarization calculations were carried out in the antiferromagnetic configuration with initialized magnetic moments of 1.6 $\mu$B and 2.2 $\mu$B for Ni and Co atoms, respectively, which allowed us to get final magnetic moments similar to experimental values (See Figs. S29–33). The $\beta$-Ni(OH)$_2$ bulk unit cell (mp-27912 primitive cell structure) was relaxed until the atomic forces were minimized to be less than 0.05 eV/Å using an energy cut-off of 800 eV and sampled with a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of ($16 \times 16 \times 8$); this cell relaxation was carried out twice consecutively to remove Pulay stress from the final optimized structure. The optimized bulk cell was then propagated into a ($2 \times 2 \times 4$) supercell with a vacuum length of 15 Å between repeating layers in the $z$ direction. Interactions between periodic images were decoupled in the $z$ direction with a dipole correction (Yu et al., 2008). For geometry optimizations, the bottom two layers of the supercell had their positions constrained and the rest of the cell was relaxed with an energy cut-off of 500 eV and sampled with a with a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of ($4 \times 4 \times 1$). To calculate the projected density of states and the charge density difference, the optimized ($2 \times 2 \times 4$) supercells first underwent a self-consistent calculation with an energy cut-off of 500 eV and sampled with a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of ($4 \times 4 \times 1$), to get an approximation of the starting wavefunctions and charge density for use in subsequent calculations. Following this, a non-self-consistent calculation starting from the previous wavefunctions and charge densities sampled with a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of ($9 \times 9 \times 1$) was conducted. To obtain the projected density of states, VASPKIT was used to parse the calculated data (Wang et al., 2020). The charge density differences of Co doped surfaces were generated in VESTA based on equation (2) below (Momma and Izumi, 2011).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\rho_{doped\ surface} &= \rho_{doped\ supercell} - \rho_{isolated\ dopant\ atom(s)} \\
&+ \rho_{isolated\ Ni\ atom(s)\ in\ dopant\ site(s)} - \rho_{undoped\ supercell} \tag{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Determination of Hubbard Potentials. Due to the large variation of U potentials for Ni and Co oxides and oxyhydroxides, along with the lack of reported U potentials for Ni and Co in the β-M(OH)₂ lattice, we systematically determined the correct potentials by evaluating two of the most common methods: 1) linear response theory and 2) optimizing with different values of U and comparing results to known experimental parameters. When evaluating the results of linear response theory based on the script posted in the VASP manual, U = 4.64 eV for Ni in β-Ni(OH)₂ (Cococcioni and De Gironcoli, 2005; Calculate, 2019). We found that when used in self-consistent calculations, this value of U resulted in unrealistic bandgap and local Ni magnetic moments compared to the experimental literature (E₉ = ~3.0–3.5 eV; 2.0 ± 0.2 μB) (Szytula et al., 1971; Hermet et al., 2011), indicating that the electronic states have not been fully corrected. In contrast, fitting to experimental parameters provided reasonable results at the cost of an increased number of computations. From the TDOS of a (2 × 2 × 2) slab and fitting to experimental bandgaps, we found that the optimal U parameters for Ni and Co were 5.5 and 3.72 eV, respectively.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Catalyst characterization

Inspired by recent reports of high UOR activity catalysts and the known ability to achieve homogenous doping, we optimized the epoxide sol-gel synthesis to controllably produce Ni₁₋ₓCoₓ(OH)₂ catalysts with specific Ni:Co ratios (Zhang et al., 2016, 2019; He et al., 2018). In order to verify that the element distribution is uniform, select Ni:Co sol-gel catalysts were imaged by SEM with spatial EDX mapping. As is evident from Fig. 1a and Figs. S1–S5, the mixing of metal cations across the surface was homogeneous. In addition, Fig. 1b shows that the experimentally determined atomic ratio of Ni:Co by both EDX and ICP-MS was in good agreement with the ratio of metal precursors used in synthesis (see Figs. S1–S6 for additional EDX data). Selected area EDX spectra show that the Cl content rises with increasing Co incorporation, suggesting different stochiometric ratios between the pure Ni and Co phases. To better understand the atomic structure of the Ni and Co phases and their evolution with changing Ni:Co ratio, we used a combination of powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), UV–Vis Spectroscopy, Raman Spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to extract structural and electronic changes.

The PXRD patterns in Fig. 1c show a gradual transition between the pure Ni phase (x = 0) and pure Co phase (x = 1), both of which appear to be the α-polymorphs of Ni(OH)₂ and Co(OH)₂ and match the results obtained from other Ni or Co epoxide sol-gel protocols, calculated PXRD patterns (see Fig. S7 in the SI), and XRD patterns reported in the literature (Cui et al., 2009; Bette et al., 2015; Hall et al., 2015). Qualitatively, the peak widths became narrower with increasing Co incorporation. This trend can be attributed to increased crystallinity of Ni₁₋ₓCoₓ(OH)₂ with increasing Co content, which was confirmed by high magnification transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. S9). While all studied compositions showed uniform surface coverage based on conventional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis (Fig. S10), high magnification TEM and SEM analysis revealed an increase in the number and size of observable crystallite domains and an associated change in nanoscale surface roughness. This trend in the morphology is in agreement with the commonly observed increase in the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) and single crystal size in Ni₁₋ₓCoₓ(OH)₂ with increasing Co content (Ramesh and Vishnu Kamath, 2009; Sun et al., 2019).

From the UV–Vis absorption spectra in Fig. 1d, a gradual transition between the pure Ni and Co phases can be observed. In particular, the pure Ni phase in this work (x = 0) has transitions assigned to Ni²⁺ in an octahedral environment (393 nm ³T₁g(P)←³A₂g(F); 678 nm ¹E₉(G)←³A₂g(F) and a shoulder between 719 and 760 nm ¹E₉(D)←³A₂g(F)) which have been previously reported for Ni(OH)₂ phases (Poul et al., 2000; Davidson et al., 1996). In addition to this, the transition at 310 nm suggests the presence of Ni³⁺ based on reports of a Ni(III) transition in Ni₂O₃ and Ni(III) complexes being in a similar range (Davidson et al., 1996; Kimura et al., 1982). The Co(OH)₂ phase shows the presence of Co²⁺ transitions in octahedral (462 nm ⁴T₁g(P)←⁴T₁g(F); 510 nm ⁴A₂g(F)←⁴T₁g(F)) and tetrahedral (546 nm ⁴T₁(P)←⁴A₂(F); 641 nm ⁴T₂(F)←⁴A₂(F)) environments (Poul et al., 2000; Jayashree and Kamath, 1999; Khassin et al., 2002; Ma et al., 2006; Frolova et al., 2004). The presence of the tetrahedral Co environment in a layered hydroxide structure was previously demonstrated to occur in the presence of hydroxide substitution with chloride using both PXRD Rietveld refinements and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (Ma et al., 2006). Based on these results, we hypothesize that the origin of the linear correlation between Co and Cl concentrations observed in the elemental analysis is caused by the formation of tetrahedral Co sites in the lattice.

The calculated binding energies showed that the Ni 2p and Co 2p peaks are reduced with increasing fraction of Co incorporated in the catalyst (Fig. S14). The change of binding energies for the Ni 2p₁/₂ and 2p₃/₂ states typically observed in XPS for Ni oxides and hydroxides was previously attributed to the nature of the ligand coordinated to Ni (Nesbitt et al., 2000). The reduction in binding energy is believed to originate from an increase of kinetic energy of the outgoing photoelectrons caused by the localization of unoccupied orbitals right above the Fermi level in a potential well (Nesbitt et al., 2000). In other words, the more unoccupied states become localized in the potential well, the larger the reduction in binding energy of the Ni 2p peaks we would expect to see. It is worth noting that the Co 2p peaks are moving towards the pure Co phase sample positions, while the Ni 2p peaks are moving below the pure Ni phase (Fig. S14). This trend suggests that increasing the Co concentration increases the number of electrons that are localized at the Ni centers in these catalysts. During electrolysis, the valence states of catalysts of this type tend to increase, based on the reported in situ studies (Gu et al., 2020; Ismail and Badawy, 2000; Zheng et al., 2018).

Raman spectroscopy measurements (Fig. 1e) show that the pure nickel hydroxide phase has a peak at 455 cm⁻¹ wavenumbers which was assigned to the Ni–OH lattice vibration (ν(M-OH) in Fig. 1e). Since this peak is in the range of reported experimental peak assignments for α- and β-Ni(OH)₂ vibrational modes (α-peaks 451-464 cm⁻¹; β-peaks 445-453 cm⁻¹), we cannot assign this peak to the Ni–OH lattice vibration from either of these two polymorphs (Hall et al., 2015; Klaus et al., 2015). The introduction of 10% Co led to the emergence of a new peak at 517 cm⁻¹ (ν(M-O) in Fig. 1e), which could originate from proton vacancies analogous to NiOOH regions within the bulk of the crystal lattice, as was previously reported for Ni₁₋ₓCoₓ(OH)₂ with Co concentrations as low as 1% (Bernard et al., 1996; Audemer et al., 1997). The analysis of the ratio between the peak areas for ν(M-O) and ν(M-OH) modes (Fig. 1f) revealed three regions across the composition range in these materials: 1) M-OOH rich region (blue line), 2) transition region (red line), and 3) M-OOH deficient region (green line). The transition region correlates with the key changes in the PXRD patterns and UV–Vis spectra correlates and confirms the change in the number of M-OOH sites in the structure of Ni₁₋ₓCoₓ(OH)₂₋yClᵧ.

### 3.3. DFT calculations

In general, numerous reports indicate that changing the elec- tronic structure of Ni is the most promising strategy for maximizing the intrinsic activity of UOR (Sayed et al., 2019). To investigate how Co will change the electronic states in the $Ni_{1-x}Co_x(OH)_2$ catalysts

and subsequently deduce how this may influence intrinsic activity towards UOR, we performed DFT calculations for structures with increasing Co doping. It has been established that the degree of modification of the electronic structure depends on the way the dopant is incorporated into the catalyst and the concentration of the dopant (Zhang et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2019). While cation substitution can induce several forms of structural disorder in $\alpha$- or $\beta$-polymorphs of $\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ (point defects, stacking faults, intercala- tion of foreign ions between layers, and different degrees of hy- dration, and $\alpha / \beta$-interstratification to name a few), it is not necessary to model them to observe the effects Co atoms have on the electronic states in $\mathrm{Ni}_{1-x} \mathrm{Co}_{x}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ catalysts (Hall et al., 2015). We chose to use $\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ as the base material in our model due to observations in this work and the literature that Co sites have very little activity towards UOR (Zhang et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2020). For both $\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ and $\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$, it is known that through both electro- chemical activation and ageing in KOH electrolyte that intercalated ions (e.g. $\mathrm{Cl}^{-}$in this work) and water are removed from between the layers, resulting in the material's transformation into the $\beta$ - phase which is the most thermodynamically stable phase for both $\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ and $\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ (Zhang et al., 2019; Hall et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2005). Even though other different phases and materials such as $\alpha$ $\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ and $\gamma$-NiOOH are known to form in situ, $\beta-\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ is likely the most dominant phase in our experiments due to chemical ageing prior to electrochemical testing. Thus, we modelled our system by studying the change in the electronic structure of $\beta$ $\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ with increasing concentration of Co in the top layer.

The initial magnetic moments in our models were set to be antiferromagnetic between neighbouring layers (along the $c$ axis) based on neutron diffraction and magnetometer experiments re- ported in the literature (Szytula et al., 1971; Hermet et al., 2011). To account for the self-repulsion of electrons in the d-orbitals that have been previously reported to result in inaccurate occupancies and bandgaps, the generalized gradient approximation with a Hubbard correction potential (GGA + U) method was used with the BEEF-vdW exchange-correlation functional (Majewski and Vogl, 1992; Wellendorff et al., 2012; Szotek and Temmerman, 1993). From an examination of the Hubbard potentials determined in the literature, we noticed two key points. First, reports that determined U for $\mathrm{Ni}(II)$, did so for NiO or NiOOH using the Perdew-Burke- Ernzerhof (PBE) functional (See Table S4 in the SI). (Perdew et al., 1996) In addition, these reported values vary between 4 and 7 eV depending on the method used (Alidoust et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2006; Li and Selloni, 2014; Doyle et al., 2017). Since it is not clear which value should be used, we decided to first compare the two most commonly used methods to determine the Hubbard potential in semiconductor materials to ensure that our calculation of the electronic state was accurate.

The first method is linear response theory, which is based on determining how the number of electrons at the atom of interest changes between self-consistent and non-self-consistent calcula- tions over a range of Hubbard potentials (Cococcioni and De Gironcoli, 2005). A series of calculations were carried out over a range of U values (-0.20 to +0.20 V) for the bulk $\beta-\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ unit cell, resulting in a predicted Hubbard potential of 4.64 eV (see Fig. S21 in the SI for calculation details). (Calculate, 2019) Then, we conducted a series of calculations that consist of two-unit cell re- laxations followed by one geometric relaxation (for calculation details, see SI). From these results, Hubbard potentials of 5.5 eV and 3.72 eV were used for $\mathrm{Ni}(II)$ and $\mathrm{Co}(II)$ for all remaining calculations in this work.

To understand how increasing the Co fraction in $\mathrm{Ni}_{1-x} \mathrm{Co}_{x}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ catalysts would alter the electronic structure, we first calculated the change in electron charge density from the pristine $\beta-\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ surface by doping the top monolayer in $25 \%$ increments, as shown in Fig. 3a. Here, we can see that the charge density difference (CDD) surrounding the hydroxide moieties changes by decreasing the density along with the $\mathrm{Co}-\mathrm{OH}$ bond. The excess charge density is then distributed between the neighbouring Ni atoms bound to the perturbed hydroxide. This observation coincides with the experi- mental result that shows the redox potential continuously shifting towards more negative values based on the inductive effect described above. Furthermore, as the concentration of Co in the monolayer increases, we see that the charge accumulation becomes more aligned with the $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{OH}$ bond and that the charge density distribution surrounding the Ni atoms is perturbed.

While the observations in the CDD maps confirm the inductive effect occurs, we still lack an understanding of which electronic states are responsible for the observed changes. To address this, we performed projected density of states (PDOS) calculations using the same structural models used in the CDD calculations, as seen in Fig. 3c. It appears that the addition of Co to the surface layer alters the Ni 3d states in several different ways. First, the anti-bonding states are shifted down monotonically as Co doping increased, which would alter the adsorption energy of different reaction in- termediates in addition to oxidizing Ni. Hence, these results explain the experimental trends observed for the change in valence states for the Ni 2p and Co 2p measurements in Fig. S14. Furthermore, we observed large changes in the Ni 3d density distribution with in- creases in Co concentration through an orbital decomposition of the projected density of states (see Figs. S23-S27). Each decom- posed projected state density will continuously shift downwards with increasing Co content of the surface layer, with the largest shift in energy occurring at the transition from 0 to $25 \%$ Co. The maximum population of each state was found to change when transitioning from 25 to $75 \%$ Co. S24 and 25). The $3 \mathrm{~d}_{z}^{2}$ state has a similar decrease in population for the majority spin at $-1.80 \mathrm{eV}$, but a larger increase in the majority spin between -5 and -6 eV. Additionally, as the Co content in the surface layer in- creases there is an increase in the population of the minority spin at around $-1.80 \mathrm{eV}$. The $3 \mathrm{~d}_{\mathrm{xz}}$ and $3 \mathrm{~d}_{\mathrm{yz}}$ orbital states, in contrast to the previously mentioned Ni 3d-states have the smallest overall change in population density when increasing the amount of Co in the surface layer, which is likely caused by the low occupation of these states in pure $\beta-\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$.
