## ABSTRACT

Spin-polarised periodic density functional simulations were used to study the stability of oxygen on $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ slab models with (100) and (110) terminations in order to understand better the oxidative properties of $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$. We observed that the PBE + U method gives binding energies and geometrical parameters similar to those obtained for atomic oxygen adsorption at low coverage using the revised version of screened hybrid exchange functional HSE06. Atomic oxygen is most stably bound on a $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}(110)$-terminated surface where the oxygen adatom binds above the bridge sites of cobalt atoms with a binding energy of $401 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$. On-surface $\mathrm{O}$ recombination to $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ leaving a bare slab from the $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}(110)$ terminations is $116-123 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ more activated than recombination of $\mathrm{O}$ on (100) surfaces. Removal of surface lattice oxygen by an on-surface oxygen producing $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ and leaving a slab with an $\mathrm{O}$ vacancy site is at least $65 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ more activated in the (110) surface than in the (100) surface.

## 1. Introduction

Spinel materials currently find a wide range of applications in areas such as super conductors, magnetic cores, ceramics and batteries. The use of spinel materials has significantly expanded since the mid-20th century, largely due to their oxidative properties [1-4]. The oxygen bond energy to a cation and the reactivity of surface oxygen species are key factors that determine the activity and selectivity of these catalysts [2,5].

$\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ is a very attractive oxidation catalyst because of its high activity in oxidation processes. $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ is an active catalyst for the oxidation of CO [6-9], ammonia [10,11] and hydrocarbons [12,13]. The high activity is ascribed to the formation of oxygen vacancies at the surface which can be generated through disruption of a weak lattice Co-O bond [14]. Vacancies can be generated by heat treatment during preparation of the catalyst [15] or during the catalytic oxidation reaction [16]. Vacancy sites can rapidly absorb molecular oxygen from the gas phase through a so-called Mars-van Krevelen redox cycle [16], regenerating the oxygen stoichiometry of the surface layer and leaving an adsorbed surface oxygen atom.

Reaction mechanisms for oxidation reactions on $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ have proposed different active oxygen species, namely highly reactive on-surface oxygen [16,17] and weak crystal field lattice oxygen [18,19]. The oxygen species in $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ have been studied using Temperature Program Desorption (TPD) experiments. During a typical $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ desorption process, $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ releases molecular oxygen with at least three distinct desorption temperatures, which are attributed to at least three types of oxygen species [16,20-22]. Takita et al. [20] studied the $\mathrm{O}_{2}$-TPD profiles of $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ samples pre-oxidised with $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ above $473 \mathrm{~K}$ and found a distinct high temperature desorption peak that starts at $600 \mathrm{~K}$ ( $\gamma$ peak), a middle temperature peak centred at $500 \mathrm{~K}$ ( $\beta$ peak) and a low temperature desorption peak between $363 \mathrm{~K}$ and $383 \mathrm{~K}$ ( $\alpha$ peak) that shifts by $20 \mathrm{~K}$ depending on the oxidation conditions. The $\alpha$ peak was assigned to desorption of an atomic oxygen species on the $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ surface with an activation energy of $66.9 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$. However, Asano et al. [21] assigned the $\alpha$ peak to desorption from molecular oxygen since it only appeared with $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ pre-oxidation and not with $\mathrm{N}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ pre-oxidation. Xue et al. [23] analysed samples of $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ pre-oxidised with $\mathrm{N}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ and ascribed the $\beta$ peak to desorption of surface oxygen species such as $\mathrm{O}_{2}^{-}$and $\mathrm{O}^{-}$and the $\gamma$ peak to desorption from lattice oxygen atoms. Wang et al. [8] were able to observe four different broad $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ desorption peaks with $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ samples calcined at $573 \mathrm{~K}$ and pre-oxidised with $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ at $473 \mathrm{~K}$. A desorption peak centred at $498 \mathrm{~K}$ was assigned to molecularly adsorbed $\mathrm{O}_{2}^{-}$species; two other desorption peaks centred at $712 \mathrm{~K}$ and $\sim 793 \mathrm{~K}$ were assigned to desorption of surface $\mathrm{O}^{-}$species and a high temperature desorption peak that starts at $970 \mathrm{~K}$ was ascribed to evolution of lattice oxygen. Studies of $\mathrm{O}_{2}$-TPD profiles of air pre-treated $\mathrm{Co}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ samples [18] showed four $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ desorption peaks centred at $263 \mathrm{~K}, 400 \mathrm{~K}$, $653 \mathrm{~K}$ and a high temperature desorption peak that starts above $670 \mathrm{~K}$. These $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ desorption peaks were assigned to desorption from physically adsorbed $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ on vacancy sites, weak crystal field lattice oxygen, strong crystal field lattice oxygen and bulk oxygen, respectively.

Molecular dynamics studies on the initial oxidation of Co(0001) surface at coverage below 0.5 ML showed a spontaneous dissociative adsorption of $O_2$ at 298 K with a final oxygen state characterised by O sitting on the hollow sites of the top most surface [24]. At 600 K, the diffusion of O to the sub-surface forming a $Co_3O_4$-like structure as the oxygen coverage increased to 1.67 ML was observed [24]. Molecular modelling studies on the stability of different oxygen species on the surface of $Co_3O_4$ are only starting to appear. These studies in general predict a reasonably stable oxygen species on the cobalt oxide. Studies of $N_2O$ decomposition on $Co_3O_4$(100) using embedded cluster DFT methods predict a high diffusivity of on-surface oxygen atoms with a relatively large (98.3 kJ mol⁻¹) energy barrier for O recombination above the surface [25], making these surface species rather stable thermally. In a separate study, periodic density functional theory using PBE functional predicts a binding energy for $O_2$ above the surface of $Co_3O_4$(110) of 180 kJ mol⁻¹, suggesting that $O_2$ is molecularly strongly bound to the surface. Detailed studies on the activity of different oxygen species using periodic density functional theory using PBE [26] and PBE + U [19] functionals have shown that weak crystal field lattice oxygen atoms (low coordinated lattice oxygen) dominate the surface catalytic capability of $Co_3O_4$(110) to oxidise CO. However, no molecular modelling reports are available for the $O_2$ recombination involving on-surface and lattice oxygen species.

The available studies on the stability of oxygen species on $Co_3O_4$ surfaces are insufficient to draw conclusions on the local site selectivity of adsorbed oxygen, stability of surfaces under different oxidation conditions and oxygen dissociation/association pathways. Here, we present the results of oxygen interactions with $Co_3O_4$ surfaces using density functional theory. We aim to address (i) local site selectivity for the adsorption of atomic oxygen, (ii) the effect of O coverage on oxygen adsorption, (iii) associative desorption of $O_2$, and (iv) formation of vacancy sites due to desorption of $O_2$ from the surface.

## 2. Method

### 2.1. Electronic calculations

Spin-polarised Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed with the Vienna simulation package VASP [27]. The Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) approach was applied to describe the electron-ion interaction [28]. The Kohn-Sham equations have been solved by means of the PBE gradient-corrected exchange-correlation functional [29]. In this study, the effect of 3d electron correlation was accounted for by considering on-site Coulomb (U) and exchange (J) interactions using the rotationally invariant formulation of the on-site model given by Dudarev et al. [30]. First principles linear response models predict values of $U = 4.4$ eV and $U = 6.7$ eV for the $Co^{2+}$ and $Co^{3+}$ that fits well the band gap of bulk $Co_3O_4$. However, smaller values such as $U_{eff}=(U-J)=3.3$ eV [31] and 3.52 [32] have been found to fit the experimental oxidation energies of CoO to $Co_3O_4$ with good predictions of the band gaps. Since we are interested in the thermodynamics of adsorption of systems with different Co to O ratios at the surface, we have used a $U_{eff}=3.3$ eV in our calculations. Differences in the binding energies of O above the $Co_3O_4$(100) surface when using $U_{eff}=3.3$ eV and $U_{eff}=3.52$ eV are at most 2 kJ mol⁻¹. Computations using the revised version of screened exchange functional of Heyd-Scuseria-Ernzerhof (HSE06) [33] with a screening length ($\mu$) of $0.2$ Å⁻¹, were used in order to evaluate the deviations of the PBE functional with $U_{eff}=0$ eV and $U_{eff}=3.3$ eV in predicting thermodynamics and atomic configurations of O on surfaces of $Co_3O_4$. The pre-converged wavefunction and the relaxed structure obtained from PBE + U relaxation were used as input for HSE06 calculations.

The Brillouin zone integrations were performed using a $4×4×1$ and $4×3×1$ Monkhorst-Pack scheme for (100) and (110) terminated surfaces. Test calculations on the binding energy of oxygen at 0.25 ML coverage in the (100) and (110) surfaces show that the predicted binding energy is converged within 1.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The convergence criteria for the electronic self-consistent iteration and the forces acting on unconstrained atoms were set to 0.0001 eV/atom and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively. The wave functions were truncated with an energy cut-off of 450 eV. Convergence tests showed that increasing the cut-off energy from 450 eV to 550 eV lowered the binding energy of O on the $Co_3O_4$(100) surface between 1 and 2 kJ mol⁻¹. The Methfessel-Paxton method [34] with a smearing of 0.1 eV is used in order to set the partial occupancies of each wave function.

The initial magnetic moments of adsorbates were chosen after testing many different magnetic moments by means of single point energy calculations. The set of magnetic moments resulting in the lowest energy were taken as initial magnetic moments for the surface relaxation.

The binding energy of atomic oxygen above the surface was calculated with respect to the energy of an oxygen atom using the following equation:

$$
E_{b}^{o}=\left[E_{slab}+n × E_{o}(g)-E_{slab-o}\right] / n \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{slab}$ is the energy of the slab, $E_{o}(g)$ is the electronic energy of O in the gas phase, $E_{slab-o}$ is the total energy of the slab with adsorbed O, and $n$ is the number of adsorbed oxygen atoms per unit cell. The oxygen coverage ($\theta_O$) has been defined as the ratio of the number of adsorbed oxygen atoms to the number of cobalt sites in the top-most layer. The formation energy of single vacancies $E_{vac}^{O_2}$ on the top-most layer of the slabs is obtained with respect to the energy of oxygen in the gas phase following the equation below:

$$
E_{vac}^{O_{2}}=E_{slab}^{v}+\frac{1}{2} E_{o_{2}}(g)-E_{slab} \tag{2}
$$

where $E_{slab}^v$ is the energy of the optimised slab with the oxygen vacancy, and $E_{o_2}(g)$ is the energy of oxygen molecule. The total energies of isolated gas phase species were calculated in a cubic cell of side length 15 Å with spin polarisation corrections.

To find reaction energy barriers and transition states, the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method was used [35]. The tolerance for electronic self-consistent iteration was set to 0.0001 eV/atom and each image was relaxed until the forces were less than 0.05 eV/Å. Local and saddle points along the reaction coordinated were checked by inspecting the vibrational frequencies. Vibrational frequency calculations were performed by diagonalization of the Hessian matrix which is constructed using a finite difference method. Each atom of adsorbate was displaced by 0.02 Å from its equilibrium position, while the surface atoms were fixed.

### 2.2. Surface models

We have studied surface terminations of $Co_3O_4$(100) and $Co_3O_4$(110). Our previous calculations showed that among the low-index surfaces of $Co_3O_4$, the (100) terminations are thermodynamically preferred at any given condition of temperature and oxygen partial pressure [36]. Although the $Co_3O_4$(110) surfaces are energetically less favoured than the (100) surfaces, they are observed under UHV conditions when films of $Co_3O_4$ are grown electrolytically from a molybdate flux [37,38] and on $MgAl_2O_4$(110) single crystals [39].

Two different terminations exist for $Co_3O_4$(100). One contains cobalt atoms only at the top layer in tetrahedral positions ($Co^t$). We will term this as the $Co_3O_4$(100)-A surface. The other surface

contains oxygen and cobalt atoms in octahedral positions ($\text{Co}^\text{o}$) at the top layer, $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)-\text{B}$. The (110) stacking of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4$ forms two distinct terminations: the top-most layer of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)-\text{A}$ contains $\text{O}$, $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ and $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ while that of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)-\text{B}$ contains $\text{O}$ and $\text{Co}^\text{o}$.

The surface slabs were created from bulk $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4$ with an optimised lattice constant of $8.15\ \text{Å}$. The (100) surface slabs contain a $(\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2})\text{R}45^\circ$ unit cell and the (110) slabs contain a $(1 \times 1)$ unit cell. All slabs are periodically repeated with a vacuum region of $15\ \text{Å}$ between the surfaces. The vacuum region was sufficient to obviate the need for dipole correction between slabs since we found that the total energy difference of the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)-\text{B}$ terminated surface with $\text{O}$ adsorbed above $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ site with and without dipole corrections is less than $0.01\ \text{eV}$. The $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)-\text{A}$ and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)-\text{B}$ slabs contain six and five atomic layers, respectively, while $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)-\text{A}$ and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)-\text{B}$ contain five layers each. The $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)-\text{A}$ slab is stoichiometric and non-symmetric along the surface normal plane, while the other three slabs are non-stoichiometric and symmetric. All atoms are relaxed except the bottom three layers to simulate the effect of bulk to the exposed layers.

Since the surface slabs contain multiple unique positions, and the number of layers of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)-\text{A}$ and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)-\text{A}$ slabs differ from our previous study [36], we describe next the most important features of the top layers of the bare slabs relaxed using the PBE + U method. ### 3.3. Results

#### 3.1. Local site selectivity of atomic oxygen on $Co_3O_4(100)$ and $Co_3O_4(110)$

Table 1 summarises the binding energies of O at low coverage on the different adsorption sites of the (100) and (110) surface terminations and Fig. 2 shows the molecular configurations together with some selected geometrical information after the oxygen-surface system is relaxed using the PBE + U method.

The local site selectivity of O adsorption was explored on $Co_3O_4(100)$-A at $\theta_O=0.16$ ML and on $Co_3O_4(100)$-B at $\theta_O=0.25$ ML. We found that the most stable sites for O adsorption on $Co_3O_4(100)$-A are top cobalt sites, with a thermodynamic preference towards adsorption on $Co^{\text{t}}$ sites. Atomic oxygen binds on $Co^{\text{t}}$ with $E_b^o=282$ kJ mol⁻¹, which is 50 kJ mol⁻¹ stronger than the adsorption on $Co^{\text{o}}$. Due to the increase in the $Co^{\text{t}}$ coordination number, the adsorption pulls the $Co^{\text{t}}$ atom away from the second layer, Fig. 2a, to a position closer to that observed in the DFT-bulk configuration. As a result, the $Co^{\text{t}}$ atom moves from the four-hollow site in the relaxed slab and sits just off the oxygen bridge site. On the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B surface, atomic oxygen thermodynamically prefers to bind on the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge site, as shown in Fig. 2e, with $E_b^o$ = 260 kJ mol$^{-1}$. The next energetically preferred site for O adsorption is above a $\text{Co}^\text{o}$, Fig. 2f ($E_b^o$ = 240 kJ mol$^{-1}$). Despite the relatively small difference between the binding energies of O adsorbed on $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge and on $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ sites ($\Delta E_b^o$ = 20 kJ mol$^{-1}$), the calculated energy barrier for the diffusion of adsorbed O from $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge to $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ site is 78 kJ mol$^{-1}$. Therefore, the potential energy surface for diffusion of O is not flat and the adsorbed O is localised on the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge. An important reason for this localisation can be discerned in the relaxed configurations of O adsorbed on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B, in that the adsorption on the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge site entails a strong interaction with the $\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ lattice site. In this configuration, the optimised $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-O and $\text{O}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bond lengths are 1.84 Å and 1.39 Å, respectively. These observations imply that a nascent $\text{O}_2$ molecule is formed with one O from the top adsorption and one O from a lattice site. The formation of a vacant lattice site due to desorption of $\text{O}_2$ to the gas phase from this oxygen system will be discussed in detail in Section E. The geometrical configuration of O above $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B is similar to that of O on $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-A and the binding energies in the two cases differ only slightly ($\Delta E_b^o$ = 8 kJ mol$^{-1}$). This suggests that the $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ site has little effect on adsorption of O on $\text{Co}^\text{o}$.

Other adsorption sites for O on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B are above the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{2\text{o-t}}$ bridge site, Fig. 2g, and above the $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ site which is exposed to the gas phase from the inner layers, Fig. 2h. The binding energies of O on the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{2\text{o-t}}$ bridge site and above $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ are 28 kJ mol$^{-1}$ and 53 kJ mol$^{-1}$, respectively, lower than that of O on the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge site.

In order to provide further insight into the bonding characteristics of O on cobalt sites, Fig. 3 shows the partial density of states (PDOS) of the on-surface O atoms and the cobalt adsorption sites on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-A and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B before and after O adsorption. The $\text{Co}^\text{t}$-3d band shifts towards the Fermi level in the O-adsorbed state. There is an overlap between $-$2.5 eV to 0.0 eV of the O 2p-band and the $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ 3d-band. The overlapping of the O 2p-band with the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ is more diffuse, ranging from $-$4.5 eV to 0.0 eV. Bader charge analysis revealed that the partial charges of the $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ and $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ atoms each increase by +0.08e due to the binding of O on these sites. The charge of O above the $\text{Co}^\text{t}$ on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-A is $-$0.69e and above the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$ on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B is $-$0.45e revealing a slight tendency for a better electron donation from the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-A surface to O.

We identified two transition states for the O diffusion process from the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ to the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$ site; one corresponding to the dissociation of the $\text{O}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bond before moving above the Co site (Fig. 2k-m) with an energy barrier of 92 kJ mol$^{-1}$ and a second transition state for the formation of the $\text{O}$-$\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$ bond from the Co site (Fig. 2m-l) with an energy barrier of 33 kJ mol$^{-1}$. The O diffusion process will compete with the formation of a vacant lattice site which accompanies desorption of $\text{O}_2$ to the gas phase from these oxygen states, as will be discussed in detail in Section E.

### 3.2. Accuracy of the data

DFT predictions using GGA methods of heats of formation and energy gaps for highly electron-correlated systems such as $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4$ are not in good agreement with experimental data [31]. Hybrid functionals such as HSE06 tend to give better agreement with experiments for calculated heats of formation of solids, and adsorption energies on surfaces compared with conventional GGA functionals [40-42]. However, the use of hybrid functionals requires enormous computational resources. The GGA + U method tends to improve the predictions of electronic properties compared to GGA methods for strongly correlated systems [43]. GGA + U is not a predicted method since the calculated energies and other properties depend on the $U_\text{eff}$ value [44,45].

We compared the predictions of $E_b^o$ on the most stable sites using GGA, GGA + U and hybrid functionals using the PBE, PBE + U and HSE06, respectively. Table 2 shows the $E_b^o$ values and Fig. 4 shows the atomic configuration of the oxygen states. The binding energy of O on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B is predicted within 18 kJ mol$^{-1}$ using the different DFT methods, and the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-O and $\text{O}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bond lengths are predicted within $\pm$0.02 Å. We observe larger discrepancies for O on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-A where PBE overpredicts $E_b^o$ by 57 kJ mol$^{-1}$ and underpredicts the $\text{Co}^\text{t}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o-t}}$ bond length by 0.11 Å as compared with the relaxed configuration using HSE06. ### 3.4. Vacancy structures and formation energies

We used the (100) and (110) terminations to investigate the energy required to remove a single lattice oxygen from the top-most layer among the oxygen atoms marked in Fig. 1. The slab with an oxygen vacancy was relaxed by keeping the bottom three layers fixed at the DFT bulk positions once the oxygen is removed. Table 3 presents the energy required for the vacancy formation, $E_{vac}^{O_{2}}$ (calculated using equation E2), as well as selected changes in the geometric parameters of the oxygen atom sharing the same surface cobalt atoms, referred to in Table 3 as the geminal oxygen, $O_{gem}$. The absolute value of $E_{vac}^{O_{2}}$ should be taken as a guide only, due to the well-known deficiencies of the PBE functional in treating the $O_{2}$ molecule in the gas phase. The relative stability of the surface lattice oxygen is not affected by the absolute energy of the $O_{2}$ molecule since $E_{vac}^{O_{2}}$ is a constant.

We compute $E_{vac}^{O_{2}}=94\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $198\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ due to formation of vacancies in positions $\text{O}^{3o}$ and $\text{O}^{2o-t}$ respectively at the top layer of $Co_{3}O_{4}(100)$-B slab. Vacancy formation at $\text{O}^{3o}$ is energetically less demanding than at $\text{O}^{2o-t}$ position even though the coordination number for each of these two lattice oxygen is three, two with surface cobalt atoms and one with sub-surface cobalt atoms. This is due to a weaker coordination of $\text{O}^{3o}$ with cobalt in the third layer with a bond distance of $1.97\ \text{Å}$ as opposed to the coordination of $\text{O}^{2o-t}$ with cobalt in the second layer with a bond distance of $1.93\ \text{Å}$. Removal of $\text{O}^{3o}$ and $\text{O}^{2ot}$ from $Co_{3}O_{4}(100)$-B generates a displacement of the geminal oxygen towards the Co-Co bridge position that reduces the $\text{Co}^{o}$-$O_{gem}$ bonds (Table 3) by $\sim 0.05\ \text{Å}$, giving stability to the surface slab. The geminal oxygen sits at $0.15\ \text{Å}$ above the Co-Co bridge when the vacancy is formed in the $\text{O}^{3o}$ position and $0.14\ \text{Å}$ below the Co-Co bridge when the vacancy is at $\text{O}^{2o-t}$, indicating a slight normal translation of the geminal oxygen during the vacancy formation process. We find that the slab with a vacancy at $\text{O}^{3o}$ is the most stable of all vacancy structures of the (100) termination since formation of vacancies at $\text{O}^{2o-t}$ and $\text{O}^{3o-t}$ positions in the $Co_{3}O_{4}(100)$-A surface produce unstable slabs with $E_{vac}^{O_{2}}=263$ and $291\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ respectively. Although $\text{O}^{2o-2t}$ and $\text{O}^{3o-t}$ are exposed surface sites, they belong to the second top-most layer and are coordinated to four cobalt atoms. The tendency for oxygen vacancy formation to occur preferentially in the $\text{O}^{3o}$ position has been found experimentally reported for the $\text{Fe}_{3}\text{O}_{4}(100)$ surface, where surface reconstructions similar to those observed for $Co_{3}O_{4}(100)$-B surface were identified [46].

Among the $Co_{3}O_{4}(110)$ terminations, we observed that formation of vacancies from the low coordinated oxygen atoms, in particular $\text{O}^{o-t}$ from the $Co_{3}O_{4}(110)$-B terminated surface is less energetically demanding, $E_{vac}^{O_{2}}=174\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. Formation of vacancies due to removal of $\text{O}^{3o}$ and $\text{O}^{2o-t}$ from the $Co_{3}O_{4}(110)$-B and $Co_{3}O_{4}(110)$-A surfaces are $64\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $113\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$, respectively, more energetically demanding than vacancy formation in the $\text{O}^{o-t}$ position. As can be observed in Table 3, removal of the lattice oxygen atoms generates a contraction of the Co-$O_{gem}$ bonds, due to the loss of coordination of the Co atoms after the vacancy is generated. As previously also observed by Xu et al. [26], we find that the most stable vacancy position in the $Co_{3}O_{4}(110)$-B termination is at position $\text{O}^{o-t}$. However, this is not the most stable vacancy position overall, since, as observed in this work, vacancy formation in the $\text{O}^{3o}$ position of the $Co_{3}O_{4}(100)$-B terminated surface is more energetically favourable.

### 3.5. Energetics of $O_2$ desorption from oxygen states

We have calculated a binding energy of O in the isolated $O_2$ molecule to be $291\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, while the experimental value is $247\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ [47]. The difference arises due to a well-known poor description of the oxygen dimer by plane-wave DFT using the PBE functional. However, we are mostly concerned here with relative energies, which are less affected than absolute values by errors incurred due to the prediction of the oxygen dimer energy using plane-wave GGA functionals.

The binding energy of atomic oxygen is higher than the DFT calculated value in the isolated $O_2$ for the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A surface and lower for the other surface terminations. This implies that oxygen thermodynamically prefers to stay bound only on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A. However, an energy barrier to associative $O_2$ desorption may exist and could keep the O atoms on the surface, even for surface oxygen states that are thermodynamically unstable. The on-surface O recombination to $O_2$ and formation of vacancies due to the interaction of on-surface oxygen with surface lattice oxygen will be analysed in this section.

This is due to the electron donation from the cobalt atoms to $\text{O}_2$, facilitated by the $\text{O}_2$ adsorption above the bridge Co-Co site in the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-A and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A surfaces.

It should be noted that, using the pure PBE functional, we predict an adsorption energy of $193$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ for molecular $\text{O}_2$ above the bridge of cobalt sites on the surface of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$, which compares well with a PBE value of $180$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ reported previously [26]. However, inclusion of electron correlation effects (PBE + U, with $U_\text{eff} = 3.3$ eV) leads to the prediction of a much weaker association ($53$ kJ mol$^{-1}$). Based on the evaluation above of the accuracy of the PBE + U, the lower value is likely to be more accurate.

This was studied at low $\theta_O$ by the direct interaction of one on-surface oxygen atom with a lattice oxygen located at the surface. We studied $\text{O}_2$ desorption from $\text{O-Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-B with $\theta_O = 0.50$ ML to produce a vacancy at either $\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ or at $\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$. On the other hand, while O adsorbed at the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$ bridge site is $15$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ less stable than at the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ bridge site, desorption of this oxygen as $\text{O}_2$, leaving a vacancy at $\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$, is just $165$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ endothermic, with no energy barrier above this endothermicity. Even though diffusion of O from above the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$ site to the $\text{Co}^\text{o}$-$\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$ bridge site was found to be $92$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ activated, it is apparent that vacancy formation at $\text{O}^{\text{o-t}}$ is overall much more likely than at $\text{O}^{3\text{o}}$.

This equilibrium state would not be stable and will either revert to the initial state with no energy barrier or, at increasing temperatures, desorb $\text{O}_2$ to the gas phase.

## 4. Conclusions

We studied the interaction of atomic and molecular oxygen with surfaces of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$ and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$ using periodic, spin polarised density functional methods. Addition of on-site coulomb and exchange interaction for Co atoms gives results for geometry and energetics similar to those obtained for atomic oxygen adsorption at low coverage using the revised version of screened hybrid exchange functional HSE06.

The most stable adsorption site of O on the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4$ surfaces is above the bridge sites of cobalt atoms and becomes less strongly bound when O sits just above a Co atom. Since the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A is the only surface for which bridge cobalt sites are available for adsorption, this surface is identified as the most reactive surface to uptake oxygen from the gas phase.

The binding of the atomic oxygen is $\sim 120$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ more exothermic on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A than on the (100) surfaces at low coverage due to the availability of cobalt bridge sites which are absent in the other (100) and (110) surface terminations. The instability of the oxygen adsorbed states for the (100) terminations is a result of the high stability of the bare (100) slabs. On this surface at low coverage, on-surface oxygen prefers to bind above cobalt sites and above the bridge of a lattice Co-O bond. For the former, a thermodynamic preference to bind on the cobalt atoms in tetrahedral sites $(\text{Co}^\text{t})$ is observed. For the latter, a strong oxygen interaction between on-surface and lattice oxygen is identified, giving rise to an oxygen adsorbate with a superoxide character.

On-surface oxygen is thermodynamically unstable with respect to oxygen in the gas phase on all surfaces of $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$ and $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(100)$-B terminations, and stable on the $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A surface. The energy barrier of atomic oxygen recombination to $\text{O}_2$ on $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4(110)$-A is $201$ kJ mol$^{-1}$, implying that the oxygen atoms are stable on this surface. However, we computed a maximum energy barrier of $85$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ for O recombination above the surface for the (100) terminations that keeps the O atoms bound to the surface at low temperature. The recombination of adsorbed O results in weak molecular adsorption of $\text{O}_2$.

Formation of vacancy sites can arise from the recombination of on-surface oxygen and lattice oxygen. We conclude that a surface oxygen atom in the (100)-B termination that sits above a cobalt site located in the third layer is thermodynamically most favoured for formation of a vacant site among all the (100) and (110) terminations. The difference in binding energy and O recombination energies and oxygen vacancy formation suggest that the (100) and (110) surfaces behave markedly differently in oxidative reactions over $\text{Co}_3\text{O}_4$.
