# A correlated insulator at the surface of the polar metal $\mathrm{Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$

We investigate the electronic structure at the surface of the correlated oxide $\mathrm{Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$, a low-symmetry ruthenate oxide which hosts an unconventional polar-metal phase. From a combination of angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy and scanning tunneling spectroscopy measurements, we demonstrate that the surface hosts an insulating phase, a distinct departure from metallicity within the bulk. Utilizing quantitative low-energy electron diffraction in conjunction with electronic structure calculations, we show how this results from a combined surface structure relaxation and the impact of marked electronic correlations in this system. Our findings highlight the proximity of $\mathrm{Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ to an insulating metallic state, and illustrate how subtle structural distortions can control its emergent electronic phases.

In materials where the electron-electron interactions are strong, numerous nearly degenerate ground states can arise from the complex interplay of various degrees of freedom, including lattice, orbital, charge, and spin [1]. In these so-called strongly correlated electron materials, a subtle perturbation to any of these can result in a significant change in the material's physical properties. Consequently, strongly correlated materials exhibit diverse phase diagrams that encompass exotic many-body quantum phases, including strange metallicity, unconventional superconductivity, and Mott insulators [2, 3]. The Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) ruthenates are an ideal materials class in which to explore this physics. They host a wide range of ground states which can be accessed via only rather minor modification of the crystal structure and composition, including unconventional superconductivity, magnetism and correlated insulating states [4–7].

In particular, $\mathrm{Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$, the bilayer member of the RP series of calcium ruthenates, displays competing electronic and magnetic orders, and appears to lie on the verge of multiple electronic and magnetic instabilities [8–10]. Upon cooling from a 'bad-metal'-like metallic state at room temperature, it undergoes a sequence of magnetic and structural phase transitions. A Néel ordering occurs to an anti-ferromagnetic (AFM) phase at $T_{\mathrm{N}} \approx 56$ K, where the spins orient along the crystallographic $a$-axis, aligning ferromagnetically within a bilayer but antiferromagnetically between neighboring bilayers ($\mathrm{AFM}_a$). At $T_{s} \approx 48$ K, the spins reorient to point along the $b$-axis ($\mathrm{AFM}_b$), coupled with an isostructural phase transition which leads to a large jump in the measured resistivity [11–14]. While there has been debate regarding the nature of the ground state, the observation of quantum oscillations unambiguously points to a low carrier-density semi-metallic state [15]. However, a marginal doping of the Ru sites with non-magnetic Ti is sufficient to drive a transition an insulating state at $\approx 4\%$ Ti doping [16, 17], as shown in figure $1$b.

This change in the electronic ground state of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ is accompanied by a change in the low-temperature magnetic structure. Increasing the Ti concentration, the material transitions from the ${\rm AFM}_b$ phase to an ${\rm AFM}_G$ phase, with $G$-type AFM order and spins canted along the $c$-axis [19]. Interestingly, the Ti-doping into the crystal lattice drives a change in the rotation and tilt of the ${\rm RuO_6}$ octahedra [18], highlighting the sensitivity of the physical properties of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ to subtle distortions of its constituent ${\rm RuO_6}$ octahedra.

This sensitivity offers rich opportunities to explore different correlated phases. Within prior studies, pressure and strain have been utilized to alter the structure of the ${\rm RuO_6}$ octahedra, and hence, the magnetic and electronic properties of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ [20, 21]. The local structure can also, however, be different at the surface of the crystal to the bulk [22, 23], providing an ultra-clean route to track the impact of subtle structural distortions on the electronic states and phases that form. In this letter we report on the impact of such surface structural distortions in ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$, demonstrating how they not only tune the electronic structure but, in fact, also allow for a distinct, correlated insulating ground state to be stabilized.

Single crystal samples of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ were grown by the floating zone method [24]. Both antiphase and polar domains are typically present in growths of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$. The spatial extent of antiphase domains is such that we could select mono-domain samples for angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) using polarized light microscopy [25] or measure diffracted intensity from a single antiphase domain in low energy electron diffraction (LEED) (see supplementary figure 1). In comparison, polar domains are significantly smaller [26]. Consequently, our LEED measurements average over an approximately equal population of polar domains (see supplementary figure 2).

All samples were cleaved *in situ* before the measurements. For ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$, the intra-bilayer coupling is much stronger than the inter-bilayer coupling [27]. This means the crystal cleaves between bilayers, exposing a ${\rm CaO_2}$ terminated surface [28]. ARPES measurements were performed on a freshly cleaved surface using the BLOCH beamline of the Max-IV synchrotron. The samples were measured at a temperature of $T\approx18$ K using 33 eV synchrotron light. Scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) measurements were performed using a home-built low temperature scanning tunneling microscope (STM) operating in a vector magnet [29]. The measurements were performed at 4.2 K unless stated otherwise. The bias voltage was applied to the sample with the tip on virtual ground. The differential conductance was recorded using a standard lock-in technique, adding an AC modulation to the sample bias. Samples for STM are cleaved using a cleaving stage mounted to the 20 K-plate of the insert. LEED measurements were performed using an OCI LEED BDL800IR optics mounted onto a ultra-high vacuum system. Measurements were performed at a temperature of $T\approx10$ K. Images of the LEED pattern were acquired using a CCD camera, and taken in steps of 1 eV between 150 and 500 eV. Experimental $I(V,{\bf Q})$ spectra were extracted from the LEED images using Python code before being analyzed using the CLEED package [30, 31]. To estimate the uncertainties associated with our LEED analysis, we measured and fit two different samples of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$. The displacements presented below are the mean best-fit displacement, and uncertainties are one standard deviation from the mean.

To model the surface electronic structure of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) code [33–35] and local density approximation exchange-correlation functional. Both spin-orbit coupling (SOC) and magnetic order along the $b$-axis were included within the calculations and correlations were included by adjusting the $U$ value of the Ru atoms within the Dudarev approximation [36]. To model the effect of structural distortions at the surface while maintaining acceptable computational cost, calculations were performed for a free-standing bilayer of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$, with a $15$ Å gap to the next bilayer. In figure 1c, we show our ARPES measurements over a broad energy range below the Fermi level. Consistent with prior studies, we observe a significant spectral weight in the deep-lying valence band, originating from Ru $t_{2g}$ states [25, 37, 38], but with broad linewidths that point to rather incoherent states. Close to the Fermi level, this spectral weight becomes strongly suppressed. Over a narrow energy range below the Fermi level, we observe a vanishing spectral weight. Nonetheless, with an enhanced contrast, we can observe sharp but extremely weak states close to and crossing the Fermi level (figure 1d), before their spectral intensity becomes dominated by the incoherent states beyond $\sim$ 100 meV. The finite albeit small DOS at the Fermi level, figure 1e, is consistent with the ground state of $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$ as being a low carrier-density metal, as known from bulk studies [15]. However, within this picture, the almost vanishingly-small coherent spectral weight at $E_{\text{F}}$ as compared to the incoherent weight at higher binding energies would suggest an extremely small quasiparticle residue.

To better understand the electronic structure, we have therefore performed STS measurements, displayed also in figure 1e. These data, taken on the same batch of crystals as the ARPES measurements, display a full gap of $\sim$ 150 meV asymmetrically distributed around the Fermi level. This indicates an insulating state, seemingly at odds with our photoemission measurements. We will show below that this is due to a distinct surface and bulk electronic structure, and different depths of the two techniques.

First, we consider the geometry of the $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$ (001) surface, as shown in figure 2a. In figure 2b, we present a topographic image of this CaO-terminated surface. Prominent within the topography are stripes. These stripes represent channels with a decreased (darker) or increased (lighter) density of oxygen atoms [39], resulting from the tilting of the $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra, as illustrated in figure 2a. As the tilting of the $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra is unidirectional in $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$, we observe stripes only along the [010] crystallographic direction, and thus within the Fourier transform of the topography (inset in figure 2b) the (1,0) and (-1,0) Bragg peaks are present whilst the (0,1) and (0,-1) Bragg peaks are extinct. The unidirectional tilt of the $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra is also observed in our LEED analysis of a pristine (001) surface of $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$, figure 2c. A tilting of the $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra in RP crystals destroys one glide line in the two-dimensional unit cell. If the octahedral tilt is along a single axis, only a single glide line symmetry is broken whilst the orthogonal glide line is preserved. This is illustrated by the dot-dashed lines in the top-down view of the two-dimensional unit cell in figure 2a. Therefore, in our LEED measurements, we observe spots along a single direction, the [100] direction, whilst those along the orthogonal [010] direction are extinct.

Together, these measurements suggest a surface symmetry consistent with the one expected from the bulk crystal structure. In fact, at all incident electron beam energies measured, we observe only LEED spots corresponding to the bulk-terminated unit cell, indicating that $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$ does not undergo a structural reconstruction at its surface. Surface relaxation can still occur, however, resulting in changes in $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedral rotation, tilt angle or vertical displacements of atoms from their bulk positions. Indeed, the symmetry-preserving structural phase transition in the bulk indicates a strong coupling of electronic, magnetic and intra-unit-cell structural degrees of freedom in this system, necessitating searching for structural changes beyond simple reconstruction effects. This motivates quantitative LEED measurements, recording and modeling the intensity of the LEED spots as a function of energy in order to extract quantitative information about atomic displacements of the surface atoms.

The results of this LEED $I(V, \mathbf{Q})$ analysis indicate a surface structure of $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$ which is markedly changed from the bulk-like structure, despite the lack of a symmetry- breaking surface reconstruction. Specifically, we find $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra at the surface which are slightly $(\approx 2^\circ)$ more ro tated than those within the bulk crystal $(\theta_{\text{bulk}} \sim 10.9^\circ)$ [32], while their tilt angle $(\phi)$ is essentially unaffected by the cre ation of a surface. Importantly, we find notable vertical dis- placement of surface atoms from their bulk positions. In par- ticular, a significant vertical displacement of both Ca atoms indicates an asymmetric uniaxial compression at the surface of $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$. Similarly, we find that both apical O atoms dis- place from their bulk positions towards the underlying bulk, leading to a rigid shift of the surface $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra away from the vacuum interface, while the Ru atoms undergo a vertical displacement which modifies the local symmetry of the surface octahedra, breaking the inversion symmetry of the surface bilayer. Due to destructive and constructive interfer- ence of the low-energy electron beam diffracted by the lattice, LEED $I(V, \mathbf{Q})$ is highly sensitive to such atomic displace ments, and we show in figure 3b that the experimental and fit- ted $I(V, \mathbf{Q})$ spectra for such a surface are markedly different from one with a bulk-like truncation. Including such structural relaxation leads to a marked reduction of Pendry's R-factor $(R_p)$ [40] for almost all of the extracted LEED spots (figure 3d). The global $R_p$ factor is reduced from the rather large value of $R_p^{\text{bulk}} \approx 0.44$ to a best-fit value of $R_p^{\text{surface}} \approx 0.20$.

To investigate the impact of these atomic displacements on the surface electronic structure, we have performed a series of DFT calculations. In figure 4a and b DFT band structure cal- culations are shown for a free-standing bilayer of $\text{Ca}_3\text{Ru}_2\text{O}_7$ within the low-temperature magnetic phase, $\text{AFM}_b$. The crys- tal structure of the bilayer was fixed to either the bulk structure (gray) or surface structure (purple) from our LEED $I(V, \mathbf{Q})$ analysis.

We find that the surface relaxation alone does not cause a significant change in the electronic structure within a normal DFT calculation. Despite an increased rotation angle of the surface $\text{RuO}_6$ octahedra, there is no substantial narrowing of the bandwidth. However, when including electronic correla- tions through a local Coulomb repulsion $U$, we find a marked difference for the electronic structures. As shown in figure 4c, a band gap opens at the Fermi level with increasing $U$, point- ing to a correlation-driven metal-to-insulator transition (MIT). While susceptibility to such a correlated-insulating state is not surprising in and of itself, intriguingly our calculations find that the surface relaxation causes this transition to occur at a different (slightly lower) $U$ value than in the bulk. For a nar-

row range of $U$ values ($\sim2.5$ to $2.75$ eV), we calculate that the bulk crystal is metallic, while for the surface structure we find a correlated insulator, as shown for $U = 2.625$ eV in figure 4d and e.

These DFT+$U$ calculations provide a natural explanation for the seemingly contradictory results introduced in figure 1d. While both ARPES and STS are surface-sensitive probes, they probe the electronic structure with different effective information depth. Within ARPES measurements, where the surface sensitivity is determined by the mean free path of the escaping photoelectrons, there is a finite contribution to the signal from the bulk-like layers immediately below the surface. In contrast, STS probes the tails of the wavefunctions of the surface electrons, and is thus practically only sensitive to the surface-most layer. Consequently, within our ARPES measurements in figure 1c, we observe both the surface and (with lower intensity) bulk-like electronic states. STM, however, probes only an insulating surface layer. We thus assign the metallic bands with weak spectral weight visible in ARPES to be those of the bulk crystal, consistent with prior transport studies which reveal metallicity in the bulk crystal [15, 27]. Contrastingly, we attribute the incoherent states located at energies $> 100$ meV away from the Fermi level, which carry the dominant spectral weight in ARPES and which will dominate the gap edges in STS, as the lower and upper Hubbard bands of a Mott-like correlated insulating phase. We thus conclude that, while the subtle structural distortions of the surface layer are insufficient to significantly modify the electronic structure on their own, they facilitate the formation of a correlation driven insulating state.

In summary, via a combined approach of precise surface structural and spectroscopic measurements, accompanied by electronic structure calculations, we have found that a structural relaxation at the surface of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ facilitates the formation of a correlation-driven insulating phase. This allows us to reconcile seemingly conflicting spectroscopic measurements, and reveals the striking role that surface relaxations can have, even when they do not lead to a symmetry-lowering reconstruction. Our calculations highlight that this insulating phase is rather fragile, being on the verge of metallicity. This raises an opportunity to drive a phase transition into a metallic state with moderate external stimuli, for example, via the application of strain or electric field. Furthermore, the subtle structural relaxations at the surface of ${\rm Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ and the narrow $U$ window within which we calculate the surface to be insulating while the bulk remains metallic indicate that the bulk material is also on the verge of a MIT, raising the possibility of driving the bulk through a phase transition.

## ANTIPHASE DOMAINS

![](./images/1243871637422473225_5.jpg)

Supplementary Fig. 1. (a) and (b) Top view of the $\mathrm{Ca_3Ru_2O_7}$ (001) surface and the two antiphase domains. Between antiphase domains the direction of the preserved glide line (dot-dashed line) is rotated by $90^\circ$. (c) and (d) Simulated LEED patterns at an incident electron beam energy, $E_\mathrm{beam}$, of 175 eV for the two antiphase domains. As the direction of the preserved glide line is rotated by $90^\circ$ between antiphase domains, the direction of the extinct LEED spots is also rotated by $90^\circ$ between antiphase domains. (e) $R_p$ factor as a function of antiphase domain occupancy within the calculated $I(V,\mathbf{Q})$ spectra. The $R_p$ value has a minimum close to 100% occupancy, suggesting only a single antiphase domain is present within the region of the probing LEED spot. This is consistent with the observation of only a single extinct glide line within our LEED measurements.
