## ABSTRACT

Using the first-principles method based on the density functional theory, we have studied magnetic and optical properties of V-doped GaN. For 12.5% V-doped GaN, total energy calculations show that the ferromagnetic state is 255 meV lower than the antiferromagnetic state and is thus predicted to be the ground state with a Curie temperature above room temperature. The magnetic moments are localized at the V atoms and ferromagnetic exchange interaction is short ranged. The analysis of optical properties shows that V-doped GaN is a promising dielectric material and has potential applications in optoelectronic devices.

## 1. Introduction

Since the discovery of carrier-induced ferromagnetism in InMnAs [1] and GaMnAs [2–4], III-V type dilute magnetic semiconductors (DMSs) have attracted intense attention as they offer the potential to integrate magnetism with well-established semiconductor based technologies, which could lead to a new generation of multifunctional spintronics devices [5,6]. According to Dietl et al.'s theory [4], based on the p-d exchange Zener model [7], wide band-gap semiconductors are more likely to have high Curie temperature ($T_C$). Thus, new researches are mainly focused on wide band-gap semiconductors. Now, GaN is a well-known wide band-gap (3.39 eV) semiconductor. The incorporation of transition metals (TMs) such as Mn [8–11], Cr [12], Ni [13] and Cu [14] and rare earth material Gd [15] in GaN exhibits room temperature (RT) ferromagnetism. As to V-doped GaN, Sato and Yoshida [16] studied the density of states (DOS) of disordered GaN with various TMs and found the ferromagnetism in V-doped GaN using coherent potential approximation with local density approximations, while Lee et al. [17] found that V ion-implanted GaN showed paramagnetic behavior. Gonzalez et al. [18] investigated the main structural characteristics of (Ga, V)N by means of The Full-Potential Linearized Augmented Plane Wave method. In conclusion, the research on magnetic properties of TM V-doped GaN is rare although there are reports about ferromagnetism in V-doped ZnO [19,20] and AlN [21].

Optical properties of V-doped GaN also deserve to enough study due to the discovery of near infrared luminescence in it [22,23]. Souissi et al. [24–26] have studied photoluminescence of V-doped GaN thin film grown by metal–organic vapor phase epitaxy. To our knowledge, there was not research on optical properties of V-doped GaN theoretically.

In this work, we calculate the magnetic and optical properties V-doped GaN systems using first-principles calculations. We find that V atoms induce ferromagnetism in GaN, and $T_C$ above RT can be achieved. We also find that V-doped GaN has different optical properties from pure GaN, indicating potential applications in optoelectronic devices.

## 2. Computational details

We have performed calculations using the density functional theory, as implemented in CASTEP code [27], with generalized gradient approximation of Perdew et al. [28] and ultrasoft pseudopotentials [29]. We also employ the GGA + $U$ method to explore the Coulomb correction $U$ on the electronic and optical properties. Ga-3d and V-3d are included in the valence. The cutoff energy for the plan-wave expansion is 520 eV. A 32 atom supercell, which consists of $2 \times 2 \times 2$ primitive wurtzite cells is used for calculations. We use Monkhorst-Pack $4 \times 4 \times 2$ k-point mesh for Brillouin zone integration [30]. During calculations, the atomic geometries are fully optimized by using Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS), which makes the tolerance for maximum energy change, maximum Hellmann–Feynman force, maximum stress and maximum displacement to be $1 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/atom, 0.03 eV/Å, 0.05 GPa and 0.001 Å, respectively.

The spin polarization scheme has been carried out to investigate 6.25% and 12.5% V-doped GaN, which are obtained by replacing one/two Ga atom/atoms with V atom/atoms from a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ supercell respectively. For 12.5% V-doped GaN, we explore five different spatial configurations to find out what is the most likely configuration and its magnetic state. In each case, we use the

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Electronic structure and magnetic properties

The structural optimization is first performed for pristine bulk GaN. The optimized lattice parameters are $a = 3.225\,\mathring{\text{A}}$ and $c = 5.254\,\mathring{\text{A}}$ with the internal parameter $u = 0.3767$, which agree with the experimental values of $a = 3.190\,\mathring{\text{A}}$, $c = 5.189\,\mathring{\text{A}}$ and $u = 0.377$ [31]. The electronic properties of the GaN were also calculated. In our theoretical calculation, the band-gap of GaN is 1.66 eV, which is in accordance to the previous theoretical prediction by Moses et al. [32] but smaller than the experimental band-gap value of $\sim$3.4 eV. This underestimation is due to the well-known limitation of DFT calculations [33]. The total and partial DOS are shown in Fig. 1. From this figure we could observe that the valence band maximum (VBM) is predominated by p states. The conduction band maximum (CBM) is almost equally predominated by s and p states. Between $-16$ and $-11$ eV d states appears as sharp peaks reflecting their localized nature. Our DOS correspond with the results of Persson et al. [34].

To study the magnetism of V-doped GaN, the lattice parameters are also optimized. When one V replaces one Ga in the $2 \times 2 \times 2$ supercell, The relaxed N–V bond length is $1.975\,\mathring{\text{A}}$ in the basel plane and $2.009\,\mathring{\text{A}}$ in the [0001] direction, while the corresponding values for N–Ga bond in pure GaN are 1.979 and $1.971\,\mathring{\text{A}}$. The obtained results are due to different atomic radius between V and Ga. When two V replace two Ga, the lattice parameters $a$ and $c$ change slightly. V is still sitting in the tetrahedral environment in the GaN lattice. This situation is similar to Cr-doped GaN [35].

In the following, two Ga atoms are replaced with two V atoms to study their magnetic coupling. The results for five different configurations are summarized in Table 1. In the first column, we specified the Ga atoms that are replaced by V (Fig. 2). We calculate total energy and magnetic moment for each configuration with and without spin polarization. The relative energies between nonmagnetic, FM and AFM states and magnetic moments are listed in Table 1. It is found that configuration I is the ground state and the FM state is 0.255 eV lower in energy than the AFM state. The estimation of $T_{\text{C}}$ is performed by using a mapping on the Heisenberg model in a mean field approximation: $K_{\text{B}}T = 2\Delta E/3c$, where $K_{\text{B}}$ is Boltzmann constant, $c$ the concentration of substitutional V atoms [36]. Meantime, according to Wu et al. [37]. The $\Delta E$ is larger than that of Cu-doped ZnO (42 meV) which is known to be observed a relatively high $T_{\text{C}}$ of 350 K experimentally. As a result, $T_{\text{c}}$ for V-doped GaN above RT can be expected. Configurations III, IV and V are energetically degenerate and their FM and AFM states are almost degenerate, displaying a weak magnetism. The higher energy values for Configuration III, IV and V imply clustering is favored by the magnetic ground state. Similar behavior is predicted for Mn-doped GaAs [38]. As $\Delta E$ decreases sharply with the distance of two V atoms increasing, and magnetic moments are concentrated on V atoms, showing that FM exchange interaction in V-doped GaN is short ranged, which is similar to Mn-doped GaN [39]. In addition, the magnetic moment per supercell keeps $4\,\mu_{\text{B}}$ for each configuration because V atom has a $3\text{d}^{2}$ electronic configuration.

The left channel of Fig. 3 represents the calculation with GGA, showing the total and partial DOS for FM 12.5% V-doped GaN. The tetrahedral crystal field of the surrounding N ligands splits the fivefold degenerate V-3d states into twofold generate low-lying e (d$_{z^2}$ and d$_{x^2-y^2}$) and threefold denenerate high-lying t$_2$ (d$_{xy}$, d$_{xz}$, and d$_{yz}$) states. Further splitting within e and t$_2$ states is due to the Jahn-Teller effect which does not exist in the V-doped GaN as indicated by Fig. 3b. The crystal field splitting energy (defined as the energy difference between the e and t$_2$ states) is smaller than the exchange splitting energy (defined as the energy required to pair up electrons in the same orbitals). This means that the two electrons in V-3d states are both spin up due to the $3\text{d}^{2}$ electronic configuration of V atom. The Fermi level ($E_{\text{F}}$) is localized at the bottom of the conduction band. The V dopants are found spin polarized. The V states are somewhat hybridized with the bottom of the conduction band, in contrast to Cu-doped GaN [37] where the Cu states are hybridized with the top of the valence band. As shown in partial DOS of V and N in Fig. 3, V introduces partially occupied bands, and V-3d level dominates the DOS near the $E_{\text{F}}$ with a small contribution due to N-2p level. Although in the majority spin channel at $E_{\text{F}}$ V-3d overlap with N-2p state, and in both spin channels V-3d and N-2p are responsible for producing unoccupied states. The hybridization between V and N is very weak. Then the FM state is favored by energy gain from d electrons hopping to d state of neighboring V. Hence the ferromagnetism in the V-doped GaN arises from Zener's double exchange mechanism [40]. The right channel of Fig. 3 represents the calculation with GGA + $U$ ($U = 4$ eV). The addition of the $U$ term, as expected, causes an increase splitting between the filled and empty orbitals in the V-3d manifold. The exchange splitting energy is now increased to $\sim$4 eV, and the crystal filed splitting energy also increases significantly. The e states, which are localized in the gap in the GGA calculation, now shift down in energy and hybridize strongly with the N-2p states. We find magnetic moment on the N atom between the two V atoms increases to $-0.20\,\mu_{\text{B}}$ from previous $-0.12\,\mu_{\text{B}}$. When we continue increase the $U$ value, a transition from half-metallic to insulating when going from the GGA to GGA + $U$ happens. This is similar to Cu-doped ZnO, where $U \geqslant 3$ eV makes the system transforms to an insulating ground state from previous half-metallic ground state [41].

The real part $\varepsilon_{1}(\omega)$ is derived from the imaginary part $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ by the Kramers-Kronig transformation. All other optical constants, such as the absorption coefficient $\alpha(\omega)$, reflectivity $R(\omega)$, and energy loss spectrum $L(\omega)$, are derived from $\varepsilon_{1}(\omega)$ and $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ [42].

To better understand the optical properties of the materials, we calculated the absorption coefficient, imaginary part of dielectric function, refractive index, reflectivity, optical conductivity and energy loss spectrum of GaN and doped GaN. The results are shown in Fig. 4. With regard to absorption coefficient, Fig. 4a shows three peaks at 7.8, 11.3 and 18.1 eV and one shoulder at around 2.8 eV for pure GaN. The absorption edge is about equal to the band-gap of 1.66 eV from our calculation. The peak at 7.8 eV mainly comes from the electron transition between the Ga-3d and N-2p states in the valence band. The transition between N-2p and Ga-3d states in the valence band also leads to the peak at 11.3 eV. The peak at 18.1 eV is derived from the transition between N-2p states in the conduction band and Ga-3d states in the valence band. And the shoulder at around 2.8 eV originates from the electronic transition between N-2p states in the upper valence band and Ga-4s states in the lowest conduction band. Above analysis is based on Fig. 1. After V doping, the main optical absorption peak at 7.8 eV shifts slightly to lower energy side and its intensity becomes weak. The peak at 11.3 eV is replaced by a shoulder. A new absorption peak appears around 39.5 eV which is due to the transition between V-3d impurity band (Fig. 3) and V-3p states (which is not plotted in DOS). But for the 12.5% V-doped GaN, another peak appears at around 1.36 eV which is similar to the situation in Cr-doped ZnO [43]. The peak appears at around 1.36 eV arises from electronic transition between V e states and Ga-4s states in the conduction band (Fig. 3). When GGA + U is employed, the peak at around 1.36 eV disappears, we also find the V e states is far away from the conduction band (Fig. 3). This is to say that the explanation about the peak at around 1.36 eV is reasonable. On the whole, Absorption coefficient of V-doped GaN is similar to that of V-doped AlN [21].

Fig. 4b represents imaginary part of dielectric function. The curve for pure GaN shows a sharp peak at 6.0 eV which agrees with the previous result [44] when taking band-gap correction into consideration. The curve has three peaks located at 2.8, 6.0 and 11.3 eV, which correspond to three intrinsic plasma frequencies. The peak at 2.8 eV should be caused by the transition between N-2p in the highest valence band and Ga-4s in the lowest conduction band (Fig. 1), which is close to the calculated band-gap (1.66 eV). The peak at 6.0 eV is due to the electron transition between the Ga-3d and N-2p states in the valence band. The transition between N-2p and Ga-3d states in the valence band also results in the peak at 11.3 eV. For doped GaN, a new peak is generated at 1.0 eV, The new peak comes from electronic transition between V e states and Ga-4s states in the conduction band. When GGA + U is employed, the peak at around 1.0 eV disappears. Besides, the line shape indicates that the difference is mainly in the low energy range.

The refractive index and reflectivity in Fig. 4c and d increase in the low energy range with doping concentration increasing. The curve of refractive index for the pure GaN is similar to the result of Kawashima et al. [45]. In addition, the refractive index increases in the energy range of 0–1.3, 2.6–5.0, 12–15 eV with doping concentration increasing. The reflectivity curve of pure GaN has two peaks and one valley at 9.5, 12.3 and 10.8 eV, respectively. The second peak becomes weak after doping. For the 12.5% V-doped GaN, the valley nearly disappears. At the energy range of 5.3–15 eV, the refractive index and reflectivity show an opposite trend. Fig. 4e presents optical conductivity. Since optical conductivity is proportional to imaginary part of dielectric function [46], the conductivity behavior appears to follow the pattern of imaginary part of dielectric function.

The energy loss spectrum is shown in Fig. 4f. The energy loss spectrum $L(\omega)$ is an important optical parameter describing the energy loss of a fast electron traversing in a certain material. The peaks in $L(\omega)$ spectra represent the characteristics associated with the plasma resonance [47]. In addition, The peaks of the $L(\omega)$ spectra overlap the trailing edges in the reflectivity, for instance, the peak of $L(\omega)$ for pure GaN is at 12.4 eV corresponding to the abrupt reduction of reflectivity, and the corresponding peak of $L(\omega)$ for 6.25% V-doped GaN appears at about 12.0 eV, in which the reflectivity for the 6.25% V-doped GaN is also sharply reduced.

## 4. Conclusion

In summary, using first-principles calculations, we have evaluated magnetic properties of V-doped GaN in different configurations and shown that the V introduces FM ground state in GaN. $T_c$ is predicted to be above RT. For V-doped GaN system, N-2p state and V-3d state hybridize weakly, and the FM property originates from a double exchange mechanism. The V doping also drastically affects optical properties of GaN such as absorption and reflectivity, which signifies that V-doped GaN is a promising materials and has potential applications in various optical and magneto-optical devices.
