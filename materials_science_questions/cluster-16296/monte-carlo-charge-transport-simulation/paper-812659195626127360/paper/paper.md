The effect of impurities placed in the wells of double-barrier resonant tunneling diodes on the current-voltage characteristics was experimentally determined. Four different double-barrier structures were grown by molecular beam epitaxy with $n$-type, $p$-type, undoped, and highly compensated doping in the center of the well. Resonant tunneling devices of various sizes were fabricated, and measured at 77 K. Systematic shifts in the peak position and peak to valley ratios were observed for the different dopant profiles. The shifts in peak position are correctly predicted by a ballistic model which includes the effects of band bending due to ionized impurities in the well. The doped devices showed a systematic decrease in the peak to valley ratio which is not predicted by the ballistic model. By scaling our results, it is apparent that in most cases unintentional background impurities are not sufficient to significantly degrade the current-voltage characteristics of resonant tunneling diodes.

Double-barrier resonant tunneling diodes (DBRTD's) are being fabricated and studied by many groups. $^{1,2}$ One of the most common methods of modeling the current-voltage ($I$-$V$) characteristics for these devices is to calculate the transmission coefficient through the structure and to integrate over the electron supply function. $^{3-6}$ Though these models predict the major features of the $I$-$V$ characteristics of these devices, there are still significant differences between the models and experiment. Specifically, the experimental valley current of the DBRTD is larger than predicted by the ballistic models based on the work of Tsu and Esaki. $^{3}$ Scattering events can alter the momentum conservation conditions upon which such models are based, and have therefore been investigated as a possible cause of the differences between theory and experiment. Inelastic scattering events should not change the resonant current of the device to first order, if the incoming electrons have an energy distribution which is broader than the width of the resonance $^{7,8}$; they may, however, alter the background nonresonant current of the device near room temperature. At lower temperatures phonon scattering becomes less important; however, there are still significant discrepancies in the valley current between experiment and theory. Several workers have investigated various aspects of the role of elastic scattering events in resonant tunneling devices. $^{5,9-11}$ In this letter, we demonstrate experimentally that elastic scattering centers in the well of a DBRTD reduce the peak to valley ratio of the device.

In order to understand the role of elastic scattering centers placed in a one-dimensional potential barrier system $V(1)$, we separate the problem into a longitudinal part (motion perpendicular to the quantum barriers) and a transverse part (motion parallel to the barriers). In the absence of scattering, transverse momentum is conserved; thus we can define the quantities $E_{t}\equiv\hbar^{2}k_{t}^{2}/(2m^{*})$ and $E_{l}\equiv E-E_{t}-V(1)$ which can be thought of as energies parallel and perpendicular to the wells, respectively, where $\hbar$ is the reduced Plank's constant, $m^{*}$ is the effective mass of the electron in GaAs, and $E$ is the sum of the kinetic and potential energies of the electron. Elastic scattering events which conserve transverse energy, as depicted in Fig. 1(a), will not change the $I$-$V$ characteristics of the device. $^{12}$ On the other hand, elastic scattering events which transfer momentum from the longitudinal to the transverse direction, or vice versa, break the condition of transverse energy conservation upon which ballistic transport models are based. This scattering allows transitions though the DBRTD which would otherwise be impossible or improbable. In order to measure the effect of scattering centers on the $I-V$ characteristics on DBRTD's, four double-barrier structures were grown with different dopant profiles using molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) in a modified Varian Gen II. The growths consisted of a $2500 \mathring{A}$ GaAs buffer layer with $10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ Si doping, an $80 \mathring{A}$ undoped GaAs spacer layer, an undoped $60 \mathring{A} \mathrm{Al}_{x} \mathrm{Ga}_{1-x} \mathrm{As} \ (x=0.3)$ barrier, a $50 \mathring{A}$ GaAs well with various doping profiles, a $40 \mathring{A}$ undoped $\mathrm{Al}_{x} \mathrm{Ga}_{1-x} \mathrm{As} \ (x=0.3)$ barrier layer, a $40 \mathring{A}$ undoped GaAs spacer layer, a $1500 \mathring{A}$ GaAs capping layer with $10^{18}$ $\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ Si doping, and a GaAs/InGaAs superlattice nonal- loyed ohmic contact. $^{13,14}$ Typical background doping densi ties for this chamber are approximately $2 \times 10^{14} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$. The barriers were intentionally grown to be asymmetric at zero bias so that, at the resonance bias condition, the reflections from the two barriers would be nearly equal, thus enhancing the peak resonance current. The control sample had an un- doped well region, the second sample had $16.7 \mathring{A}$ of $1.0 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ Si $n$-type doping centered in the well, the third was similarly doped with Be, and the fourth sample had $16.7 \mathring{A}$ of alternating Si and Be doping to create a highly compensated doping effect in the well's center. As the doped wells have $n$-type, $p$-type, and compensation doping, the ef fects of band bending on the $I-V$ characteristics should be separable from the effects of elastic scattering events in the well. A 20 s growth interruption was performed $20 \mathring{A}$ before the quantum barriers, in order to smooth the interface while minimizing the accumulation of any impurities at the inter- face itself.

The structures were processed by wet etching mesas to isolate the devices. The mesas were designed to be squares ranging from 2 to $20 \mu \mathrm{m}$ on a side with outward slopes along the direction of the interconnect metallization. A nickel- gold-germanium alloy was deposited to provide ohmic con- tact to the region surrounding the mesas. $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ was deposited by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) to passivate the structure. The alloyed ohmic contacts were subsequently annealed at $450{^\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 30 s. Buffered HF was used to etch openings for the interconnect metallization to connect the ohmic contacts and the bonding pads. Dice from each wafer were mounted and bonded onto 24 pin carriers which were submerged in liquid nitrogen for low-tempera- ture measurements.

The very high peak current densities achieved at negative bias resulted in large voltage drops across series resistances. As a result of this, the measurements under negative bias had a large statistical scatter. In addition, it can be seen from simu- lation of the band diagram $^{15}$ that the negative bias condition results in a large accumulation region near the $40 \mathring{A}$ barrier when the device is biased past resonance. Scattering in the accumulation layer may result in additional valley current due to a scattering assisted tunneling mechanism similar to the one we described for scattering in the well. With positive bias on the top contact, the bottom of the device is now the emitter; simulation of the band diagram shows that the $80 \mathring{A}$ undoped buffer layer minimizes the accumulation region. Therefore, in this work, we have only shown results with a positive bias on the top contact.

As shown in Fig. 2 and Table I, a systematic shift in the voltage of the peak current can be observed due to the raising and lowering of the confined state due to band bending in the well. Ionized $p$-type dopants in the well bend the conduction band such that the resonant state is raised, thus increasing the amount of bias necessary to achieve resonance. On the other hand, ionized $n$-type dopants lower the resonant state. Only the devices which were $2 \mu \mathrm{m}$ on a side were used to tabulate the peak and valley voltages. The peak to valley ratios of the devices from a given wafer were relatively consistent despite large variations in measured current densities. This is because factors such as variations in mesa size and monolayer fluctuations in barrier thickness can have large effects on current density with little effect on the shape of the $I$-$V$ curve. Due to this variation in current densities, it is not clear from this experiment whether the reduction in peak to valley ratios of the doped devices is due to a decrease in peak current density, or to an increase in valley current density.

The above results are compared with predictions from a ballistic model. The potential of the conduction band in the device is approximated as a series of steps⁶ where the conduction-band minimum is computed using a Poisson equation solver.¹⁵ We calculate the transmission coefficient through the structure and integrate over the electron supply function,³,⁶ a Fermi energy of 0.056 eV above the conduction band and a temperature of 77 K were assumed. The results of this calculation are shown in Table II, where we can see that the predicted peak and valley voltages show good agreement with the data in Table I and Fig. 2. The model predicts a higher peak to valley ratio for doped devices due to band bending effects. Thus, the experimentally observed reduction in peak to valley ratios in the doped devices can be attributed to scattering from the impurities which were intentionally placed in the well.¹⁶

The distribution of impurities in the devices with doped wells may have significant fluctuations. This would lead to broadening of the resonant peak due to the local raising and lowering of the resonant state. Some broadening of the peak should also result from elastic scattering effects. It is not clear from the experimental data, however, that a consistent pattern of peak broadening (as measured by the half-width at half-maximum on the low bias side) is occurring. The difficulty in observing such broadening is probably due to the fact that the transmission resonance is much narrower than the energy distribution of incident electrons for a degenerately doped emitter.

The increase in valley current due to impurities in the well does not account for all of the excess valley current that has been observed when comparing experimental results with ballistic models, as most resonant tunneling devices would not have such a high concentration of ionized impurities in the well of the device; but it does show that elastic scattering can make a significant contribution to this effect if large numbers of impurities are intentionally introduced or if the growth conditions allow Si surface riding or dopant diffusion. Other possible sources of additional valley current include scattering due to interface roughness, inelastic scattering events, scattering into satellite¹⁷ valleys, and scattering due to Coulomb potentials originating in the barriers or doped contact layers.

Elastic scattering centers in the well of a DBRTD can change the voltage and magnitude of features in the $I$-$V$ characteristics of resonant tunneling devices. The effects of band bending due to impurities in the well on the $I$-$V$ characteristics of the devices have been modeled, and a shift in resonant voltage is predicted which shows good agreement with experiment. The band bending model predicts an increase in the peak to valley ratio of the devices with doped wells, whereas the experimental results show a decrease in the peak to valley ratio for these devices. A normal background concentration of impurities in the well of a RTD, at least three orders of magnitude below the number of dopants intentionally introduced into the well, should not cause the discrepancies between the models and experiment which have been observed.
