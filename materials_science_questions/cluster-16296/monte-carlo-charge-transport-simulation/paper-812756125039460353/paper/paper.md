# Hall and drift mobility of polar p-type semiconductors:
## I. Theory†

D Kranzer

Institut für Physikalische Elektronik der Technischen, Hochschule and
Ludwig Boltzmann Institut für Festkörperphysik, Vienna, Austria

Received 7 February 1973, in final form 1 June 1973

Abstract. The contribution to the Hall and drift mobility from the polar scattering by optical modes of p-type semiconductors for low electric fields is investigated including the essential effect of light to heavy hole band intervalley scattering. It is shown that this mechanism reduces the light hole mobility and the Hall coefficient factor substantially whereas it hardly influences the heavy hole mobility. Polar scattering by optical modes is considered only. The importance of these effects in any real situation depends on the relative strength of the other scattering processes and this is discussed in several particular cases in a forthcoming paper.

## 1. Introduction

Two main differences between the properties of holes and electrons in polar semiconductors influence the p-type mobility: (a) the degenerate valence band structure and (b) the symmetry properties of the carrier wavefunctions. The effect caused by these properties on the effective hole mobility was originally discussed by Wiley (1971) who found an increase of the hole mobility roughly by a factor two due to the symmetry properties of the hole wavefunction. The approach was based on a relaxation time approximation. The result, however, was confirmed by an exact solution of the Boltzmann equation (Kranzer 1972a).

The validity of the work quoted above is seriously affected by the neglect of polar intervalley scattering. The drastic influence of this mechanism on the effective hole mobility was pointed out by Kranzer (1972b) and more recently confirmed by Monte Carlo calculations (Costato et al 1972). The polar intervalley scattering was discussed in detail in view of the hot carrier situation (Costato et al 1972). The low field mobility was also derived from the Monte Carlo result. This paper is concerned with detailed calculation of the polar optical low field mobility based on the difference equation method for the solution of the Boltzmann equation. For the cases calculated by Costato et al (1972) the results of both methods are in exact agreement. However, our method will be extended to a treatment of the weak field galvanomagnetic properties of p-type polar semiconductors. These are of special interest because the low hole mobility does not allow the experimental realization of large Hall angles which would be necessary for

From the result of Costato *et al* (1972) it is expected that acoustic and optical defor- mation potential scattering predominates in pure p-GaAs whereas it is less important for p-CdTe. In a subsequent paper the theory of this work is extended to include these scattering mechanisms and is applied to p-ZnTe, p-CdTe, and p-ZnSe.

The theory presented in § 3 accounts for the twofold degeneracy of the band and polar inter- and intravalley scattering by optical modes. The results are given in § 4. A detailed discussion is presented concerning the effect of polar intervalley scattering on the drift and Hall mobility. A summary of the results and conclusions is given in § 5.

## 2. Band structure and scattering mechanism

We approximate the warped surfaces by two parabolic spherical bands as shown in figure 1. The carrier wavefunctions have therefore the symmetry properties of atomic p-functions (Kane 1957).

![](./images/812756125039460353_1.jpg)

Figure 1. Valence band model. The bands are calculated using a heavy hole mass of $m_1 = 0.5m$ and a light hole mass of $m_2 = 0.068m$ ($m$ is the free electron mass).

The transition probability from state $\boldsymbol{k}$ to $\boldsymbol{k}'$ depends on the overlap between initial and final state wavefunction $G(\boldsymbol{k}, \boldsymbol{k}')$. This effect—usually ignored in s-like bands—must be taken into account. Since (Ehrenreich 1957)
$$
G(\boldsymbol{k}, \boldsymbol{k}') \leqslant 1 \tag{1}
$$
the transition probability becomes smaller and consequently the mobility increases. In our case $G(\boldsymbol{k}, \boldsymbol{k}')$ can be written as (Wiley 1971)
$$
G(\boldsymbol{k}, \boldsymbol{k}') = \frac{1}{4}(1 + 3 \cos^2 v), \tag{2}
$$

for intravalley scattering and
$$G(\boldsymbol{k}, \boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=\frac{3}{4} \sin ^{2} v,\qquad(3)$$
for intervalley scattering, where $v$ is the angle between $\boldsymbol{k}$ and $\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}$. The matrix element, originally calculated for carriers with wavefunctions of s-like symmetry (Fröhlich 1937) must be multiplied by $G(\boldsymbol{k}, \boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})$.

### 3. Boltzmann equation
The distribution function at low electric fields and arbitrary magnetic fields may be written as
$$f_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})=f_{0 i}(\epsilon)+\boldsymbol{p} \boldsymbol{c}_{i}(\epsilon),\qquad(4)$$
where $f_{0 i}(\epsilon)$ is the thermal equilibrium function of the $i$ th band, $\boldsymbol{p}$ is the crystal momentum, $\epsilon$ is the energy of the carriers and $\boldsymbol{c}_{i}(\epsilon)$ is an unknown vector which will be calculated below. $\dagger$ The distribution functions (4) satisfy the coupled Boltzmann equation symbolically written as
$$\left(\frac{\delta f_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})}{\delta t}\right)_{F}+\left(\frac{\delta f_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})}{\delta t}\right)_{i \rightarrow i}-\left(\frac{\delta f_{i}(\boldsymbol{p})}{\delta t}\right)_{i \rightarrow j}^{\prime}+\left(\frac{\delta f_{j}(\boldsymbol{p})}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}=0.\qquad(5)$$

The third and the fourth term on the left side of the equation system (5) accounts for the interband scattering that is the scattering out (third) and the scattering into (fourth) the band $i$ under consideration. The prime on these terms denotes $i \neq j$. These collision terms are given in more detail in the Appendix. The first two terms describe the situation for a single valley semiconductor. For this case a theory was already worked out (Kranzer 1973). In the present section we extend this theory to a two-band model. As far as possible we will use the notation of the previous paper (Kranzer 1973).

If the magnetic field $\boldsymbol{B}(0,0, B)$ is parallel and the electric field $\boldsymbol{E}(E_{x}, E_{y}, 0)$ is perpendicular to the $z$ direction the component $c_{z i}(\epsilon)$ of the unknown vector $\boldsymbol{c}_{i}(\epsilon)$ vanishes. Since the applied electric field is small we may write
$$\begin{aligned}
& c_{x i}=\alpha_{x i} E_{x}+\alpha_{y i} E_{y} \\
& c_{y i}=\beta_{x i} E_{x}+\beta_{y i} E_{y}.
\end{aligned}\qquad(6)$$

Inserting (4) and (6) into (5) and performing the integrations in the collision terms we obtain a coupled difference equation system consisting of eight equations. These, however, are not linearly independent. Using the well known relations
$$\begin{aligned}
& \alpha_{x i}=\beta_{y i} \\
& \alpha_{y i}=-\beta_{x i}
\end{aligned}\qquad(7)$$
we finally obtain four equations
$$\left(\frac{\delta p_{i} \alpha_{x i}}{\delta t}\right)_{i \rightarrow i}-\frac{p_{i} \alpha_{x i}}{\tau_{i \rightarrow j}^{\prime}}+D_{j}^{\prime} F\left(\alpha_{x j}^{\prime}\right)+\omega_{\mathrm{c} i} p_{i} \alpha_{y i}=b_{i}\qquad(8a)$$

$\dagger$ In this section the index $i$ appearing in the equations means that in fact two equations should be written referring to the heavy (1) and light (2) hole band.

$$
\left(\frac{\delta p_{i} \alpha_{y i}}{\delta t}\right)_{i \rightarrow i}-\frac{p_{i} \alpha_{y i}}{\tau_{i \rightarrow j}^{\prime}}+D_{j}^{\prime} F\left(\alpha_{y j}^{\prime}\right)-\omega_{\mathrm{c} i} p_{i} \alpha_{x i}=0
\tag{8b}
$$

which determine $\alpha_{x i}$ and $\alpha_{y i}$. The abbreviations appearing in (8) are explained in the Appendix. Again the prime signifies $i \neq j$. The equation system (8) reduces to equation (9) given by Kranzer (1973) if interband scattering is neglected. Using the method described by Kranzer (1971) numerical solutions of (8) can be easily obtained with the aid of a digital computer.

The calculation of the conductivity tensor is now straightforward. Following Kranzer (1973) we obtain

$$
\sigma_{x x}^{i}=e \int \frac{p_{x i}^{2}}{m_{i}} \alpha_{x i}(\epsilon) \mathrm{d}^{3} p_{i}
\tag{9a}
$$

$$
\sigma_{x y}^{i}=e \int \frac{p_{x i}^{2}}{m_{i}} \alpha_{y i}(\epsilon) \mathrm{d}^{3} p_{i},
\tag{9b}
$$

where the integrations have to be performed over the momentum space of the carriers under consideration. Because of (7) the conductivities become

$$
\sigma_{x x}^{i}=\sigma_{y y}^{i} \quad \sigma_{x y}^{i}=-\sigma_{y x}^{i}.
\tag{10}
$$

In the two-band model the Hall factor $R_{\mathrm{H}}$ is given by (Beer 1963)

$$
R_{\mathrm{H}}=\frac{1}{B} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{2} \sigma_{x y}^{i}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2} \sigma_{x y}^{i}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2} \sigma_{x x}^{i}\right)^{2}}.
\tag{11}
$$

It is convenient to discuss two limits: (a) high and (b) very low magnetic fields. In case (a) $\sigma_{x y}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2} \sigma_{x y}^{i}\right)^{2}$ is large as compared with $\sigma_{x x}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2} \sigma_{x x}^{i}\right)^{2}$ and $\alpha_{y i}$ is approximately given by

$$
\alpha_{y i}=\frac{1}{B} \frac{\mathrm{d} f_{0 i}(\epsilon)}{\mathrm{d} \epsilon}.
\tag{12}
$$

Inserting equation (12) into (9b) and performing the integrations yields

$$
\sigma_{x y}^{i}=\frac{e}{B} n_{i}
\tag{13}
$$

and hence

$$
R_{\mathrm{H}}(B \rightarrow \infty)=\frac{1}{e\left(n_{1}+n_{2}\right)},
\tag{14}
$$

where $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ is the carrier density of the heavy and light hole, respectively.

In case $(b) \sigma_{x y}^{2}$ is small as compared with $\sigma_{x x}^{2}$. The unknown functions $\alpha_{x i}$ and $\alpha_{y i}$ can be obtained from (8) by neglecting the fourth term on the left side of (8a). Inserting the solution of (8a) into (9a) and performing the integrations the effective drift mobility $\mu_{\text {eff }}$ of the holes

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sigma_{x x} R_{\mathrm{H}}(B \rightarrow \infty)
\tag{15}
$$

can be calculated. From (15) and $\sigma_{x x}=e\left(n_{1} \mu_{1}+n_{2} \mu_{2}\right)$ we derive

$$
\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mu_{1}}+\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mu_{1}}-1\right) \frac{n_{1}}{n_{2}}
\tag{16}
$$

which is the ratio of the light to heavy hole drift mobility. The ratio of $R_{\mathrm{H}}$ to $R_{\mathrm{H}}(B \rightarrow \infty)$ is denoted as $r_{\mathrm{H}}$

$$
r_{\mathrm{H}}=\frac{R_{\mathrm{H}}}{R_{\mathrm{H}}(B \rightarrow \infty)},
\tag{17}
$$

where $R_{\mathrm{H}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{H}}(B \rightarrow \infty)$ is given by (11) and (14), respectively. The effective Hall mobility is calculated according to (18)

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{H}}=\mu_{\text {eff }} r_{\mathrm{H}}.
\tag{18}
$$

Finally we want to point out that the theory presented here is limited to values of the magnetic fields

$$
k_{\mathrm{B}} T \gg \hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c} i},
\tag{19}
$$

where $T$ is the lattice temperature, $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the Boltzmann constant and $\omega_{\mathrm{c} i}$ is the cyclotron frequency of the carriers having an effective mass $m_{i}$.

### 4. Effect of intervalley scattering on the Hall and drift mobility

Because of the effective mass dependence proportional $m^{-3 / 2}$ of the polar optical mobility the effective drift mobility may be written as (Wiley 1971)

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\left(\frac{2 r^{3 / 2}}{1+r^{3 / 2}}\right) \mu_{1}
\tag{20}
$$

if intervalley scattering is neglected. Here $\mu_1$ is the mobility of the heavy holes, $r = m_1/m_2$ and the mobility of the light holes $\mu_2$ was substituted by $r^{-3/2}\mu_1$. The factor in parentheses lies in the range 1·8–2 for $r > 5$. This leads to an effective hole drift mobility of the heavy holes. The weakness of polar optical intervalley scattering is suggested by Wiley (1971) because of the pronounced maximum of the scattering rate at vanishing change of the wavevector exhibited by the factor $|\boldsymbol{k} - \boldsymbol{k}'|^{-2}$, where $\boldsymbol{k}$ and $\boldsymbol{k}'$ are the initial and final wavevectors, respectively. Thus transitions with large change of $\boldsymbol{k}$ (A' to A'' in figure 1) may be neglected. However, it should be noticed that vertical transitions (A' to A''' in figure 1) by emission of an optical phonon will frequently occur for which the singularity of $|\boldsymbol{k} - \boldsymbol{k}'|^{-2}$ at $\boldsymbol{k} = \boldsymbol{k}'$ is cancelled by the $\sin^2 v$-dependence of $G(k, k')$. Furthermore, because of the large density of states in the heavy hole band the com- paratively small number of light holes will frequently be scattered into the heavy hole band. These qualitative conclusions have been quantitatively established by Costato *et al* (1972) who calculated the corresponding scattering rates and proved the dominant rôle of this type of interband transition. Thus a pronounced decrease of the light hole mobility by intervalley scattering may be expected while the heavy hole mobility will remain nearly unaffected. Therefore (20) will not hold. This conclusion is confirmed by the results shown in figure 2 where $\mu_{\text{eff}}/\mu$ (full curve) and $\mu_2/\mu_1$ (broken curve) are plotted against $r$ for various values of $\gamma = \theta/T$, where $\theta$ is the optical Debye temperature. The curve $\mu_2/\mu_1 = r^{3/2}$ for negligible intervalley scattering is also shown in figure 2 (dotted curve). At a fixed value of $\gamma$ the effect of intervalley scattering is more pronounced if $r$ increases, resulting in a much lower value of $\mu_2/\mu_1$ as compared with $r^{3/2}$. This is primarily caused by a larger density of states in the heavy hole band. A smaller value of $\gamma$ decreases the value of $\mu_2/\mu_1$ too. This indicates that interband transitions from the light to the heavy hole band with small momentum change are enhanced by increasing temperature. The $\mu_{\text{eff}}/\mu_1$ curves show a maximum which becomes smaller as $\gamma$ is decreased. In the limit at infinite values of $r$, $\mu_{\text{eff}}$ equals $\mu_1$.

## 5. Conclusion
Two effects enlarge the polar mobility in p-type materials compared with a corresponding n-type compound: the different symmetry properties of the carrier wavefunction and the two-band model. It was shown recently (Wiley 1971) that the first effect increases the drift mobility approximately by a factor of two. The two-band model enlarges the effective drift mobility too. The effect depends on the magnitude of $m_{1} / m_{2}$ and the lattice temperature and does not exceed $26 \%$ for $\gamma \leqslant 5$. The low magnetic field galvanomagnetic properties are much more affected by the addition of light holes than by the symmetry properties of the carrier wavefunction. The factor $r_{\mathrm{H}}$ becomes large depending on the magnitude of $m_{1} / m_{2}$. A lower value of $\mu_{\text {eff }} \times B$ is necessary to decrease $r_{\mathrm{H}}$ with increasing magnetic fields compared with a single band model. The ratio $\mu_{2} / \mu_{1}$ is found to be larger than 1 but smaller than $\left(m_{1} / m_{2}\right)^{3 / 2}$ valid for the case of decoupled bands. The polar intervalley scattering reduces the light hole mobility and the factor $r_{\mathrm{H}}$ substantially whereas it does hardly influence the mobility of the heavy holes.

For intervalley scattering out of the $i$ th valley under consideration the collision term may be written as

$$
\left(\frac{\delta f_{i}}{\delta t}\right)_{i \rightarrow j}=\lambda_{j}(\epsilon) f_{i}(\boldsymbol{p}), \tag{A.1}
$$

where $\lambda_{j}(\epsilon)$ is the scattering rate $\lambda_{j}=\left(\tau_{i \rightarrow j}\right)^{\gamma-1}$ which is given by Costato *et al* (1972). The collision term describing the scattering into the band $i$ under consideration is given by (Conwell 1967)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{\delta f_{i}}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}= & \frac{2 \pi}{\hbar} \sum_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}} G(v)\left|\left\langle p|H| p^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\left(\delta\left(\epsilon(\boldsymbol{p})-\epsilon\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\right)+\hbar \omega_{1}\right)\left(N_{q}+1\right)\right. \\
& \left.+\delta\left(\epsilon(\boldsymbol{p})-\epsilon\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\right)-\hbar \omega_{1}\right) N_{q}\right) f_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}\right), \tag{A.2}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $N_{q}$ is the Planck distribution. $\hbar \omega_{1}$ is the energy of the optical phonon which is either emitted or absorbed by a carrier of the $j$ band. The meaning of the other symbols are discussed by Conwell (1967). Because of the Dirac delta function $\delta(x)$ and the known

angular dependence of the distribution function (4) the integrations involved in (A.2) can be easily performed. Since the collision operator is linear we may write

$$
\left(\frac{\delta f_{i}}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\delta f_{0 j}}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}+\frac{p_{x}}{p}\left(\frac{\delta p c_{i}}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}+\frac{p_{y}}{p}\left(\frac{\delta p c_{i}}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}. \tag{A.3}
$$

The term associated with $f_{0 j}$ is cancelled by $\lambda_{j} f_{0 i}$. The only term necessary to evaluate is $(\delta p c_{j} / \delta t)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}$ for which we obtain

$$
\left(\frac{\delta p c_{i j}}{\delta t}\right)_{j \rightarrow i}^{\prime}=D_{j}^{\prime}\left(F\left(\alpha_{x j}^{\prime}\right) E_{x}+F\left(\alpha_{y j}^{\prime}\right) E_{y}\right) \tag{A.4}
$$

where $D_{j}^{\prime}$ and $F(\alpha_{x j}^{\prime})$ are given by

$$
D_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{3}{4} \frac{e E_{j} N_{q}}{p_{i}(\epsilon)} \tag{A.5}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
F\left(\alpha_{x j}^{\prime}\right)= & {\left[\left(1-\left(a_{j}^{+}\right)^{2}\right) \Phi^{+} a_{j}^{+}+\left(a_{j}^{+}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\right] e^{\gamma} p_{j}^{+} \alpha_{x j}\left(\epsilon+\hbar \omega_{1}\right)+\sigma\left(\epsilon-\hbar \omega_{1}\right) } \\
& \times\left[\left(1-\left(a_{j}^{-}\right)^{2}\right) \Phi^{-} a_{j}^{-}+\left(a_{j}^{-}\right)^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\right] p_{j}^{-} \alpha_{x j}\left(\epsilon-\hbar \omega_{1}\right)
\end{aligned} \tag{A.6}
$$

$$
p_{j}^{ \pm}=p_{j}\left(\epsilon \pm \hbar \omega_{1}\right) \quad \gamma=\frac{\theta}{T}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{j}^{ \pm} & =\frac{\left(p_{j}^{ \pm}\right)^{2}+p_{i}^{2}}{2 p_{j}^{ \pm} p_{i}} \\
\Phi^{ \pm} & =\ln \left|\frac{a_{j}^{ \pm}+1}{a_{j}^{ \pm}-1}\right|
\end{aligned} \tag{A.7}
$$

$$
e E_{j}=\frac{m_{j} e^{2} \hbar \omega_{1}}{4 \pi \kappa \hbar^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_{\infty}}-\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}}\right)
$$

$\sigma(\epsilon-\hbar \omega_{j})$ is Heaviside's step function, $\kappa$ is the dielectric constant of the vacuum, $\kappa_{\infty}$ and $\kappa_{0}$ are the relative high and low frequency dielectric constants, respectively.

In (8) the cyclotron frequency $\omega_{c i}$ and $b_{i}$ appear which are given by

$$
\omega_{c i}=\frac{e B}{m_{i}} \tag{A.8}
$$

and

$$
b_{i}=e v_{i} \frac{\mathrm{d} f_{0 i}}{\mathrm{~d} \epsilon}, \tag{A.9}
$$

where $v_{i}$ is the group velocity of the carrier of the band $i$ under consideration. The collision term $(\delta p \alpha_{x i} / \delta t)_{i \rightarrow i}$ was given previously (Kranzer 1972b).
