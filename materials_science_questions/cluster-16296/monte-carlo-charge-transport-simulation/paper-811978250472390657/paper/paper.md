# The Photogradient E.M.F. of Hot Carriers

By
S. AŠMONTAS, J. POŽELA, and K. REPŠAS

Results are presented of the investigation of the hot carriers' photogradient e.m.f., $U_{\text{fg}}$, dependent on the electric field intensity in n- and p-type germanium and n-type silicon. The rate of change of the photogradient e.m.f. in n-Ge is found to decrease with an increase of the electric field intensity at fields higher than 5 kV/cm. This is explained by the influence of the higher $\langle 100 \rangle$ minima on heating of electrons. The measurements were carried out at room temperature by a microwave technique. The dependence of $U_{\text{fg}}$ on the electric field intensity is calculated in the electron and hole temperature approximation.

В работе представлены результаты исследования фотоградиентной ЭДС горячих носителей тока $U_{\text{fg}}$ в германии n- и p-типа и кремнии n-типа. В случае n-Ge экспериментально наблюдается уменьшение скорости роста фотогра- диентной ЭДС горячих носителей тока от напряженности электрического поля, начиная с полей $E > 5$ kV/cm, что объясняется влиянием высших долин $\langle 100 \rangle$ на разогрев электронов. Измерения проводились при комнатной тем- пературе с помощью СВЧ методики. Зависимость $U_{\text{fg}}$ от напряженности элек- трического поля рассчитывалась в приближении электронной и дырочной температур.

## 1. Introduction

If part of the homogeneous semiconductor filament is illuminated with light and a high electric field gradient $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ is created on one of the sides of illumination as shown in Fig. 1, an electromotive force arises between the ends of the sample which further will refered to as the photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers, $U_{\text{fg}}$ [1]. It is seen from Fig. 1 that diffusion conditions for the excess carriers excited by light on opposite sides of the light spot are different due to the high electric field. The conditions for diffusion of hot carriers, on the other hand, are different on both high electric field gradients due to excess carriers. The net result of these two effects is the e.m.f., $U_{\text{fg}}$.

## 2. Calculations of the Photogradient E.M.F. of Hot Carriers

The photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers, $U_{\text{fg}}$, can be expressed as follows:

$$
U_{\text{fg}} = \oint \boldsymbol{E}_{\text{fg}} \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{l} \,, \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{\text{fg}}$ is the photogradient electric field intensity. Assuming for simplicity that the relaxation processes are isotropic and quasi-elastic for non-degenerate semiconductors with spherical isoenergetic surfaces, one easily obtains

$$
\boldsymbol{E}_{\text{fg}}(\boldsymbol{r}) = \frac{\hbar}{e} \frac{\int \tau_{\text{n}} \boldsymbol{v}_{\text{n}}[\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{n}} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}} f_{0\text{n}}(\varepsilon, \boldsymbol{r})] \mathrm{d}^3 \boldsymbol{k} - \int \tau_{\text{p}} \boldsymbol{v}_{\text{p}}[\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{p}} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}} f_{0\text{p}}(\varepsilon, \boldsymbol{r})] \mathrm{d}^3 \boldsymbol{k}}{\int \tau_{\text{n}} \boldsymbol{v}_{\text{n}} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}} f_{0\text{n}}(\varepsilon, \boldsymbol{r}) \mathrm{d}^3 \boldsymbol{k} + \int \tau_{\text{p}} \boldsymbol{v}_{\text{p}} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}} f_{0\text{p}}(\varepsilon, \boldsymbol{r}) \mathrm{d}^3 \boldsymbol{k}} \,, \tag{2}
$$

where $\hbar$ is Planck's constant divided by $2\pi$, $e$ is the electronic charge, $\boldsymbol{v}$ is the velocity, $\boldsymbol{k}$ is the wave vector of carriers, $\tau$ is the momentum relaxation time,

![](./images/811978250472390657_1.jpg)

Fig. 1. Schematical view of the sample and distributions of excess carriers, $\Delta n$, (region CH) and electric field intensity (region BD) in the sample. At A and F are the metallic contact between which $U_{\text{fg}}$ is measured

$f_0$ is the symmetric part of the distribution function of hot carriers; the sub-scripts "n" and "p" label electrons and holes, $\boldsymbol{r}$ is the radius vector.

Let us assume that the distribution functions of hot carriers can be approx-imated by Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions with carrier temperatures $T_{\mathbf{n}}$ and $T_{\mathbf{p}}$. For the one-dimensional case equation (2) after integration over $\boldsymbol{k}$ can be written

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{fg}}(x) & =\frac{k_{\mathbf{0}}}{e} \frac{\left[\left(1-s_{\mathbf{n}}\right) n(x) \frac{\partial T_{\mathbf{n}}(x)}{\partial x}+T_{\mathbf{n}}(x) \frac{\partial n(x)}{\partial x}\right] \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(x)}{n(x) \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(x)+p(x) \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(x)}- \\
& -\frac{\left[\left(1-s_{\mathbf{p}}\right) p(x) \frac{\partial T_{\mathbf{p}}(x)}{\partial x}+T_{\mathbf{p}}(x) \frac{\partial p(x)}{\partial x}\right] \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(x)}{n(x) \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(x)+p(x) \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(x)},
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$

where $s_{\mathbf{n}}$ and $s_{\mathbf{p}}$ determine the momentum relaxation time dependence $(\tau \sim \varepsilon^{-s})$ on energy for electrons and holes, respectively, $k_{\mathbf{0}}$ is Boltzmann's constant, $n$ and $p$ are the concentrations of electrons and holes. $\mu_{\mathbf{n}}$ and $\mu_{\mathbf{p}}$ are the mobilities of electrons and holes, respectively.

To calculate the e.m.f. $U_{\text{fg}}$ we assume that
1. scattering mechanisms for electrons and holes do not depend upon the coordinate $x$;
2. the concentration of the excess carriers excited by light does not change in high electric fields.

With these assumptions and the aid of equation (3) and Fig. 1, the expression (1) becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
& U_{\mathrm{fg}}= \\
= & \frac{k_{\mathbf{0}}}{e}\left\{\left(n_{\mathbf{0}} \Delta p^{\prime}-p_{\mathbf{0}} \Delta n^{\prime}\right) \int_{0}^{E} \frac{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E) \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E)\left[\left(1-s_{\mathbf{n}}\right) \frac{\partial T_{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial E}+\left(1-s_{\mathbf{p}}\right) \frac{\partial T_{\mathbf{p}}}{\partial E}\right] \mathrm{d} E}{\left[n_{\mathbf{0}} \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E)+p_{\mathbf{0}} \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E)\right]\left[\left(n_{\mathbf{0}}+\Delta n^{\prime}\right) \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E)+\left(p_{\mathbf{0}}+\Delta p^{\prime}\right) \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E)\right]}+\right. \\
+ & \frac{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E) T_{\mathbf{n}} \Delta n-\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E) T_{\mathbf{p}} \Delta p}{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E) \Delta n+\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E) \Delta p} \ln \left[1+\frac{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E) \Delta n^{\prime}+\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E) \Delta p^{\prime}}{n_{\mathbf{0}} \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(E)+p_{\mathbf{0}} \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(E)}\right]- \\
- & \left.\frac{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(0) \Delta n-\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(0) \Delta p}{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(0) \Delta n+\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(0) \Delta p} T_{\mathbf{0}} \ln \left[1+\frac{\mu_{\mathbf{n}}(0) \Delta n^{\prime}+\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(0) \Delta p^{\prime}}{n_{\mathbf{0}} \mu_{\mathbf{n}}(0)+p_{\mathbf{0}} \mu_{\mathbf{p}}(0)}\right]\right\},
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

where $\Delta n'$ and $\Delta p'$ are the excess carrier concentrations at the boundary of the electric field gradient, $E$ is the intensity of the carrier-heating electric field, $T_0$ is the lattice temperature.

The first term of the expression (4) is determined by the hot-carrier diffusion due to a gradient of the carrier temperature in the sample. The second and third terms depend on the concentrations of the excess carriers and their diffusion coefficients, the last two terms further will be referred to as the "diffusion terms". When the equality $n_0 \Delta p' = p_0 \Delta n'$ is valid, the first term is equal to zero and the photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers is determined by the diffusion terms, i.e. a difference of the Dember electromotive forces with and without the high electric field. The Dember effect in high electric fields was investigated recently in paper [2].

### 3. Experimental Results and Discussion

The photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers has been measured by a microwave technique since a high dc electric field would extract the excess carriers and measurements will be very difficult to perform. The carriers were heated by $2\ \mu$s microwave pulses at a repetition rate of 10 Hz; the microwave frequency was 10 GHz.

The filament-shaped samples $22 \times 1 \times 0.5$ and $22 \times 1 \times 0.19\ \text{mm}^3$ were prepared from single crystal germanium and silicon with resistivities $\varrho_{\text{Ge}} = = 10\ \Omega\text{cm}$ and $\varrho_{\text{Si}} = 15\ \Omega\text{cm}$, respectively. They had such orientation that electrons in all equivalent valleys were equally heated by the microwave electric field $\boldsymbol{E}$, i.e., $\boldsymbol{E}$ was parallel to [100] and [111] directions for germanium and silicon, respectively. The photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers has been measured in germanium in the [010] direction and in silicon in [110]. The sample (1) was placed on the wide wall of the rectangular waveguide (4) as shown in Fig. 2a. In order to create the stepped form distribution of the high microwave field in the sample, as shown in Fig. 1, a rectangular semiconducting bar (2) was put on the sample in the centre of the waveguide. The amplitude of the microwave field $E_{\text{m}}$ in the sample was determined from the measured average dc resistance of a semiconductor sample subjected to microwave pulses [3].

The excess carriers were created by illuminating the sample with a tungsten-bulb radiation through an aperture in the wide wall of the waveguide. The carrier concentration in the sample was determined from measurements of the stationary photoconductivity. By measuring the diffusion length of the excess carriers their lifetime $\tau_{\text{e}}$ was established. For a Ge sample, $\tau_{\text{e}}$ was larger than $150\ \mu$s and for n-Si $\tau_{\text{e}} > > 50\ \mu$s.

The dependence of the photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers averaged over the microwave cycle, $\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\rangle_{T}$, upon the electric field intensity for an n-type germanium sample at room temperature is shown in Fig. 3. The calculation of $\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\rangle_{T}$ versus $E_{\mathrm{m}}$ was carried out with expression (4). The hot-carrier temperatures were calculated using the energy balance equation

$$
e \mu E^{2}+\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{d} t}\right\rangle=0, \tag{5}
$$

where $\langle\mathrm{d} \varepsilon / \mathrm{d} t\rangle$ is the average energy loss rate of hot carriers.

The dashed line of Fig. 3 represents the calculated curve obtained including both intravalley and equivalent intervalley optical and acoustical mode scattering for electrons and intravalley optical and acoustical mode scattering for holes. It is seen that the experimental data agree qualitatively with the calculated curve up to $E=5 \mathrm{kV} / \mathrm{cm}$ and deviate from it at higher electric fields. This deviation may be caused by a number of reasons:

1. In high electric field the ratio of the excess carrier concentration to the equilibrium concentration may be changed due to the variation with the electric field intensity of the bulk and surface recombination velocities [4 to 6]. The additional maesurements have shown that such concentration changes do not take place in our case.

2. The simple model of band structure was used in the calculations of the hot carrier photogradient e.m.f. $\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\rangle_{T}$. However, the conduction band of germanium has a many-valley structure. The distinctive feature of field transport in many-valley semiconductors is that the current may be unparallel to the electric field in each valley. This effect leads to an increase of the carrier diffusion lengths in electric fields. In this case the diffusion coefficient $D_{\mathrm{n}}^{*}$ is given by [7]

$$
D_{\mathrm{n}}^{*}=D_{\mathrm{n}}\left(1+\gamma^{2}\right), \tag{6}
$$

where $D_{\mathrm{n}}$ is the mean isotropic diffusion coefficient and $\gamma$ is the dimensionless electric field:

$$
\gamma^{2}=\frac{1}{\nu} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\nu} \frac{\tau^{\mathrm{int}}}{D_{\mathrm{n}}}\left(\mu_{y x}^{(\alpha)} E_{y}\right)^{2}. \tag{7}
$$

Here $\nu$ is the number of equivalent minima, $\mu_{y x}^{(\alpha)}$ is the mobility tensor for the $\alpha$-th valley, $\tau^{\text {int }}$ is the intervalley relaxation time. An estimation of this effect yields that the magnitude of $\gamma^{2}$ does not exceed 0.15 in our case.

3. The hole temperature calculated from the energy balance equation can differ from the actual one. In connection with this the photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers was calculated changing the hole temperature over a wide range: At first, the holes were assumed to have lattice temperature and, later, three times higher than that obtained from the energy balance equation. In both cases the deviation from the dashed line does not exceed 20%.

4. The calculated electron temperature $T_{\mathrm{n}}$ may be too high since scattering of electrons into higher valleys was not taken into account. Indeed, at electric fields higher than 5 kV/cm, the temperature of electrons exceeds 1800 °K while the equivalent temperatures corresponding to the energy separations between the $\langle 111\rangle$ and $\langle 000\rangle$ minima, $\Delta \varepsilon_{1}=0.14 \mathrm{eV}$, and between the $\langle 111\rangle$ and $\langle 100\rangle$ minima, $\Delta \varepsilon_{2}=0.18 \mathrm{eV}$, are about $T_{\Delta_{1}}=\Delta \varepsilon_{1} / k_{0} \approx 1580{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ [8] and $T_{\Delta_{2}} \approx 2090{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ [9], respectively. Therefore one may expect non-equivalent intervalley scattering of hot electrons to become effective in this electric field intensity range.

The coupling constants for intervalley scattering of electrons between $\langle 111\rangle$ and $\langle 000\rangle$ valleys and between $\langle 111\rangle$ and $\langle 100\rangle$ valleys are believed to have nearly the same values, but the density of states of the $\langle 000\rangle$ minimum is much smaller than that of the $\langle 100\rangle$ minima [10]. The effect of intervalley $\langle 111\rangle \leftrightarrow\langle 000\rangle$ scattering on the energy loss of electrons compared with $\langle 111\rangle \leftarrow$ $\rightarrow\langle 100\rangle$ scattering may, therefore, be neglected.

Assuming Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions for hot carriers and negligible populations of the $\langle 100\rangle$ valleys, the energy loss rate of the electrons due to non-equivalent intervalley (n-i) $\langle 111\rangle \leftrightarrow\langle 100\rangle$ scattering can be written as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{d} t}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{ni}}= & \frac{\sqrt{2} N D_{\mathrm{ni}}^{2} m_{\|}^{1 / 2} m_{\perp}\left(k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}\right)^{1 / 2}}{\pi^{3 / 2} h^{2} \varrho\left[\exp \left(\frac{\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{k_{0} T_{0}}\right)-1\right] \exp \left(\frac{\Delta \varepsilon-\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{2 k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)} \times \\
& \times\left[\frac{\Delta \varepsilon-\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{2 k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}} \mathrm{K}_{1}\left(\frac{\Delta \varepsilon-\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{2 k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)-\right. \\
& \left.-\frac{\Delta \varepsilon+\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{2 k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}} \mathrm{K}_{1}\left(\frac{\Delta \varepsilon+\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{2 k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}}\right) \exp \left(\frac{\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{k_{0} T_{0}}-\frac{\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}}{k_{0} T_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
$$
where $N$ is the number of higher $\langle 100\rangle$ minima, $\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{ni}}=k_{0} \theta_{\mathrm{ni}}$ is the n-i phonon energy, $D_{\mathrm{ni}}$ is the coupling constant for n-i scattering, $\Delta \varepsilon$ is the energy separation between the $\langle 111\rangle$ and $\langle 100\rangle$ minima, $T_{\mathrm{n}}$ is the temperature of the electrons in the $\langle 111\rangle$ minima, $\varrho$ is the density of the material, and $\mathrm{K}_{1}$ is a Bessel function of the second kind with imaginary argument.

The solid line in Fig. 3 represents the calculated curve $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T}$ obtained by including the additional n-i phonon scattering for electrons in high electric fields. It is seen that the agreement between experimental data and calculation is better when n-i phonon scattering for hot electrons is taken into account.

The calculations for n-Ge were carried out with the following parameter values:

For the $\langle 111\rangle$ valleys, $m_{\|}=1.58\ m_{0}$, $m_{\perp}=0.082\ m_{0}$, $\theta_{0}=430\ ^{\circ}\text{K}$, $D_{0}==5 \times 10^{8}\ \text{eV/cm}$, $\theta_{1}=320\ ^{\circ}\text{K}$, $D_{\mathrm{i}}=-1.4 \times 10^{8}\ \text{eV/cm}$, $E_{1}=11\ \text{eV}$.

The dependence of the photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers upon the electric field intensity for p-Ge and n-Si at room temperature is shown in Fig. 6. Since the discrepancy between experimental data and calculations is large (for n-Si it is about 18 times at $E_{\mathrm{m}}=16 \mathrm{kV} / \mathrm{cm}$ and for p-Ge about 2.5 times), the relative $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T} /\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T \text { max }}$ values are plotted against the ordinate axis. Here $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T \text { max }}$ is the maximum value of the photogradient e.m.f. of hot carriers at $E=16 \mathrm{kV} / \mathrm{cm}$. For n-Si $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T \text { max }}$ equals $25 \mathrm{mV}$ at $\Delta n^{\prime} / n_{0}=0.2$ and for p-Ge $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T \text { max }}=27 \mathrm{mV}$ at $\Delta p^{\prime} / p_{0}=0.34$.

Calculating the temperature of the electrons in n-Si we assumed that they lose their energy to intervalley phonons with the characteristic temperatures $\theta_{1}=720{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{K}, \theta_{2}=220{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ and that the deformation potential constants are $D_{1}=1.6 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{eV} / \mathrm{cm}, D_{2}=1.5 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{eV} / \mathrm{cm}$ [12]. In Ge the holes are supposed to lose their energy to optical phonons with $\theta_{0}=430{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ and $D_{0}=1.2 \times$ $\times 10^{9} \mathrm{eV} / \mathrm{cm}$. Because we did not know the constants necessary for a calculation of the temperature of hot holes in n-Si, we calculated the dependence of $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T}$ upon $E_{\mathrm{m}}$ for two extreme cases: At first, the holes were assumed to have lattice temperature and, later, the hole temperature was assumed to be equal to the electron temperature. In both cases the calculation does not agree with experimental data within an order of magnitude. In Fig. 6, curve 1 represents the calculation of $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T}$ in n-Si when the holes have lattice temperature. The discrepancy between calculated and experimental data may be caused either by the form of the distribution function of hot carriers or their underestimated temperatures obtained from the energy balance equation.

An analogous situation is observed in p-Ge, i.e., the calculation of $\left\langle U_{\mathrm{fg}}\right\rangle_{T}$ does not match the experimental data, and for $E_{\mathrm{m}}$ less than $9 \mathrm{kV} / \mathrm{cm}$ the calculated curve is even different in sign in comparison with the observation.

The distribution function of hot holes in p-Ge is known to be non-Maxwellian at fields up to $3 \mathrm{kV} / \mathrm{cm}$ [13] and the temperatures obtained from the energy balance equation are smaller than noise temperatures [14]. Apparently, the discrepancy between calculation and experimental data in p-Ge as well as in n-Si may be explained both by the form of the distribution function of the hot carriers and the underestimated mean energy of the holes in Ge and electrons in n-Si.
