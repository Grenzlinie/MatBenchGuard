# Novel approaches for highly selective, room-temperature gas sensors based on atomically dispersed non-precious metals

Renbing Tian¹, Shiyan Wang², Xuefeng Hu³, Jian-Guo Zheng⁴, Peng Ji¹, Jun Lin⁵, Jing Zhang⁶, Mingjie Xu⁴, Jun Bao⁷, Shouwei Zuo⁸, Hui Zhang⁹, Wei Zhang³*, Jinlan Wang²* and Liandong Yu³*

Atomically dispersed (AD) materials have incredible catalytic ability and offer atom economy with 100% metal utilization during catalytic reactions. Herein, we report the first attempt to synthesize AD FeNC materials for use as highly selective, room-temperature gas sensors. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM), extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy, and Mössbauer spectroscopy characterizations confirm the existence of atomically dispersed Fe with a FeN₄ coordination. We demonstrate a room temperature, sensitive, and selective NO₂ gas sensor technology using this platform, offering significant advantage over existing technology. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations verify that electrons are transferred to NO₂ from FeN₄ during NO₂ adsorption. Both DFT calculations and experiments also reveal that the barrier for NO₂ decomposition by AD FeNC is 0.73 eV, which is significantly lower than previously reported barriers (2.60-3.54 eV) in other materials. Such unique catalytic properties combined with a high surface affinity for NO₂ molecules enable AD FeNC gas sensors to have excellent selective NO₂ detection at room temperature. The method proposed here can inspire the use of AD materials to detect other gases and catalyze similar novel developments in the gas sensor industry.

## 1 Introduction

Single metal atom dispersed on a solid support have emerged as promising new area of catalytic research due to their exceptional catalytic performance.¹ Atomically dispersed (AD) materials share some of the advantages of both heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysts.² Compared to traditional catalysts, AD materials have unique electronic structures and unsaturated coordination environments that lead to high catalytic activity and efficiency.³ The single catalytic active site (i.e., the single metal atom) in AD catalysts (ADCs) causes these materials to have high selectivity in catalytic reactions.⁴ The simplicity and homogeneity of the AD structure also leads to high catalyst stability. It is possible to intelligently design a catalyst for the target reaction at the atomic level.⁵ Particularly, AD materials utilize 100% of the catalytic atoms they contain. Recently developed AD materials have been shown to have excellent performance in the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR),⁶⁻⁸ hydrogen evolution reaction (HER),⁹ ¹⁰ oxygen evolution reaction (OER),¹¹⁻¹³ and carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO₂RR).¹⁴⁻¹⁶

Different metals (e.g., Au, Rh, Pt, Pd, Fe, Co and Ni) have been used to prepare AD materials on solid supports including carbon, graphene, metal oxides and metal organic frameworks (MOFs).¹⁷⁻²² Despite the great potential of AD materials for electrochemical catalytic reactions, to the best of our knowledge, AD materials have not yet been implemented in any practical industrial application. One of the main limitations that hinders the practical application of AD materials is that catalytic applications generally require more than 20% catalyst mass loading,²³ while previously reported AD materials only have up to 7.5% coverage.²⁴ Continuing to increase the loading of AD materials leads to problems with aggregation which destroy the atomic-dispersed nature of the AD materials. However, the field of gas sensing has the potential to provide the first industrial application of AD materials. The outstanding catalytic performance of AD materials could overcome the problems of poor selectivity and high operating temperature for the sensors based on metallic oxide semiconductors (MOS). It is particularly worth noting that low catalyst coverage, which is a problem in other fields, is not a barrier to applications in gas sensing, particularly in the detection of gases at low concentrations.²⁵⁻²⁷

Nitrogen oxides emitted by vehicle engines and industrial combustion processes are the main source of anthropogenic $NO_x$ emissions. $^{28}$ Nitrogen oxides also play a decisive role in the formation of airborne smoke upon reaction with surface ozone. $^{29}$ In particular, nitrogen oxides alter the radiation balance of the atmosphere, severely affecting crop growth. $^{30, 31}$ More seriously, people exposed to even very low $NO_x$ concentrations (< 3 ppm) can develop symptoms including a sore throat, eye irritation, and brief coughing, $^{32}$ while long-lasting exposure can result in fatal illnesses. $^{33}$ Controlling $NO_x$ emissions first requires the development of highly reliable and accurate $NO_x$ gas detection technologies. $^{34}$

Considering the low concentration of nitrogen oxides, an ideal $NO_x$ detector requires the susceptibility to capture oxidative decomposition and a large area traceability. $^{35}$ To be useful in real-world applications, $NO_x$ gas sensors must combine fast response times with accurate and reliable measurements, the possibility of miniaturization, low cost, and compatibility with signal processing chips. Although the gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (GC-MS), $^{36}$ optical sensors $^{37}$ and electrochemical sensors $^{38}$ have their own characteristics, and overall considerations, metal oxide-based gas sensors best meet all requirements for nitrogen oxide gas detection. However, MOS sensors have primarily suffered the problems with poor selectivity and with requirements for high temperature operation. Selectivity is the core requirement for accurate gas identification, while a low operating temperature is essential to achieve a low power consumption, which facilitates miniaturization of the sensor and networked sensor deployment.

In recent decades, countless efforts have been paid to the synthesis of MOS composite materials, $^{39-43}$ nanostructures $^{44-45}$, and heterostructures $^{46}$ to enhance $NO_2$ gas sensing performance. In addition, low-dimensional materials like single-walled carbon nanotubes, $^{47}$ graphene nanomeshes, $^{48}$ graphene aerogels, $^{49}$ $MoS_2$$^{50}$, and their complexes have also been extensively scrutinized for improving gas sensor selectivity and reducing operating temperature. Vanalakar et al. reported a $NO_2$ gas sensor based on ZnO nanorod arrays prepared by the wet chemical route, and the sensitivity was 3100% at $NO_2$ gas concentration of 100 ppm. $^{51}$ By preparing rGO/$MoS_2$ composites, Zhou et al. realized a detection limit of $NO_2$ of 5.7 ppb. $^{52}$ Tai et al. reported a flexible $NO_2$ gas sensor based on poly(3-hexylthiophene) films with the sensitivity up to 0.169 ppm⁻¹.⁵³ Lee et al. fabricated an organic conductive nanocomposite paste composed of cheap $Fe_2O_3$ hollow nanoparticles and polystyrene sulfonic acid-doped polyaniline, which showed the detectable sensitive limit down to 0.5 ppm $NO_2$ gas concentration. $^{54}$ Although these studies have made significant progress, utilizing the catalytic properties of precious metal (such as Pd, Pt, Au, Rh, and Ag) nanoparticles and nanoclusters has long been considered the most effective path to lift the performance of MOS sensors due to their unique properties arising from their size, shape and composition. $^{55}$ However, precious metals are very expensive and their reserves are decreasing. $^{56}$ The catalytic materials containing non-precious metals have emerged as alternatives to precious metals. $^{57, 58}$ In specific field of $NO_x$ gas sensors, Zhang et al. have recently synthesized $WO_3$ hollow nanospheres doped with Fe, which are able to detect 10 ppb $NO_2$ at 120°C. $^{59}$ Gawali et al. have prepared rare-earth cerium-doped nickel oxide nanoparticles using a cost-effective sol-gel synthesis method. $^{60}$ The material displayed a selective response towards gaseous $NO_2$ at the moderately low temperature of 150°C. Overall, in the case of catalysis using metallic nanoparticles or clusters, only the metal atoms positioned at the apices, edges, steps and corners participate in the catalytic process, while internal atoms do not contribute to the catalytic reaction. Therefore, scaling down the size of metal particles to improve the catalytic performance and to increase material utilization is a striking approach.

In this contribution, an atomically dispersed iron-nitrogen-doped carbon catalyst (AD FeNC) was successfully synthesized and for the first time applied as the basic-sensing material for the construction of a novel $NO_2$ gas sensor. An AD FeNC material was chosen for this study because they exhibit superior performance compared to non-metallic catalysts. $^{61}$ Nitrogen doping plays an important role in improving and optimizing N-C performance, but the single-atom iron is sited at the center of the catalytic reaction. $^{62}$ More importantly, iron is not a precious metal and has abundant storage on earth, which can greatly reduce the cost of catalysts. Different characterization techniques including X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FETEM), aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM), extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy, Mössbauer spectroscopy and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) were used to prove the successful preparation of the AD FeNC and determine the Fe-N coordination of the catalyst. AD FeNC-based gas sensor exhibited a highly sensitive and excellent selective detection to $NO_2$ at room temperature. The observation of a peculiar response curve reveals that a two-step decomposition reaction occurs during the sensing process. The density functional theory (DFT) is used to calculate the electronic structure of the materials, the adsorption energy of the various gases and the electron transfer process during $NO_2$ gas adsorption. As DFT calculations confirmed, the larger adsorption energy and stronger catalytic reaction between $NO_2$ and $FeN_4$ associated with more transferred electrons from the surface of the AD FeNC to $NO_2$ the gas are responsible for highly sensitive to $NO_2$. DFT is also utilized to calculate the activation energy required to decompose $NO_2$ and to offer some theoretical explanations for the observed experimental results. This study both prepares a novel AD catalytic material using earth-abundant metals and demonstrates the first practical application of AD material in the field of gas sensing.

## 2 Experimental section
### 2.1 Preparation of AD FeNC.
The AD FeNC material was prepared using the methodology reported in a previous work. $^{63}$ All reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, Shanghai, China and used

without further purification. Typically, 140 mg of $FeSO_4{\cdot}7H_2O$ and 330 mg of 1,10-phenanthroline monohydrate were dissolved in 50 mL of ethanol to form 1,10-phenanthroline-Fe(Ⅱ). After sonication for 10 min, 3 g of nano MgO were added to the mixture and sonicated for an additional 10 min. The mixture was refluxed at 60°C overnight, followed by evaporation of the solvent using a rotary evaporator. The solid product was heated to 700°C in a nitrogen atmosphere for 2 h. Finally, the product was treated with $HNO_3$ to remove the MgO support.

### 2.2 Characterization.
XRD was carried out using a D8-Advance diffractometer (Bruker) operating in a $2\theta$ range of 20–80° to characterize the phase and crystallinity of AD FeNC. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was carried out on a JEOL grand ARM TEM instrument operated at 300 kV in STEM mode to observe the morphology of AD FeNC. SEM (Ultra55 FE-SEM) was also utilized to observe the morphology of the product. A Raman spectrometer (Horiba Aramis) was used to record the Raman spectra. XPS was performed using an electron spectrometer (PHI QUANIERA II) with 300 W Al Kα radiation. The Mössbauer spectra (Analysis and Testing Center for Nuclear Science, Shanghai Institute of Applied Physics) were measured using a constant acceleration transmission mode with a 57 Co/Rh source at room temperature. The velocity was calibrated using 25 μm α-Fe foil, and the IS was calibrated to the center of α-Fe at room temperature. The EPR spectrum (Bruker) was measured on an X-band JOEL-FA200 spectrometer at room temperature. Fe K-edge EXAFS of the sample were obtained at the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) at the 1W1B station in transmission mode at room temperature. The data were analyzed using the Athena software package and fitted using Artemis. The Hall effect measurements were conducted on a Swin Hall 8800VM. FeNC powder was first treated with a tablet machine and heated at 600°C for 3 h under nitrogen atmosphere to form a dense coating. UV-Vis (HITECHI, U-3900) was used to characterize the structure information before and after sensing process.

### 2.3 Fabrication of the AD gas sensor and gas sensing experiments.
AD FeNC was first ground into a powder and then mixed with ethanol to form a paste. A capillary was subsequently used to coat the sample on a four-bottom interdigitated Au electrode (two heating cross-finger electrodes were not used in the testing process, and the distance between the two Au electrodes is about 0.5 mm). The experiments were performed at room temperature and the concentration of $NO_2$ varied from 20 to 300 ppm. The carrier gas was dry air with 99.999% purity. The gas flow control and resistance acquisition were performed using a computer (LW001, Six-dimension Tech Co, Ltd., Fuyang, China). The other gases (500 ppm $H_2$, 500 ppm $NH_3$, 500 ppm $CH_4$ and 500 ppm $CO_2$) were tested using a similar method to determine the designated gas concentrations. The resistance of the gas sensor was measured in second intervals. The sample response, S, was defined as the ratio of $R_g/R_a$, where $R_g$ is the resistance of the gas sensor in the detected gas with a given concentration and $R_a$ is the resistance in air.

### 2.4 Computational methods.
All the first-principles spin-polarized calculations were performed using plane-wave density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP).⁶⁴ The electronic exchange-correlation energy was calculated using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA).⁶⁵ The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was used to describe the ionic cores. The kinetic energy cutoff chosen for the plane-wave basis set was 500 eV. The vacuum space was 20 Å, which is large enough to avoid interactions between periodic images. The convergence criteria were set to $10^{-5}$ eV and -0.01 eV/Å for the energy and the forces on each atom, respectively. The 5 × 5 × 1 supercell of AD FeNC used is shown in Fig. 4a and included 44 C atoms, 4 N atoms and 1 Fe atom. The atomic charge transfer was evaluated using Bader's charge analysis.⁶⁶ In order to investigate the kinetics of the $NO + NO^* \rightarrow O_2 + N_2$ reaction on AD FeNC, the climbing image nudged elastic band method (CI-NEB)⁶⁷ was employed to identify the minimum energy paths, and the TS structure was further confirmed by vibrational analysis.

### 3.2 Gas sensing properties of the AD FeNC sensor.
Before measuring the sensing properties of the AD FeNC sensor, the Hall-effect method was used to verify the semiconductor properties of the AD FeNC material. The results showed that the $FeN_4$-coordinated FeNC material is an n-type semiconductor. Fig. 3a shows the sensing performance of the AD FeNC material towards different concentrations of $NO_2$ at room temperature from 1 to 300 ppm. When the AD FeNC gas sensor was exposed

to NO₂, the resistance of the sensor initially increased quickly and then reached a maximum value, which is the normal response of n-type semiconductors to gaseous oxides. Subsequently, the resistance of the sensor slowly and peculiarly decreased before leveling off. After exposing the gas sensor to air again, the resistance of the sensor returned to the original equilibrium value. Red asterisks were marked in Fig. 3a to show the balanced resistances which were used to calculate the response in Fig. 3b. As shown in Fig. 3b, the response of the gas sensor, defined as 100% × (Rg-Ra)/Rg (where Rg and Ra refer to the sensing resistance with and without exposure to the target gas, respectively), increased from 5.5 to 72% as the concentration of NO₂ increased from 1 to 200 ppm in air. However, the sensing responses no longer increased and actually remained constant at NO₂ concentrations greater than 200 ppm (two dot points in Fig. 3b). This result is directly related to the single atomic limited loading concentration. When the available AD catalytic sites are fully occupied by adsorbed NOₓ, no additional sites are available to bind surplus NOₓ molecules, resulting in saturation of the sensor response. Since the resistance of the gas sensor first increased and then decreased, ΔR ((Rg-Ra)) here is equal to the resistance of the gas sensor before exposure to NO₂ minus the stable resistance in the NO₂ environment. The sensing response showed two different linear relationship to the NO₂ concentration from 1 to 20 ppm and 20 to 200 ppm range (Fig. 3b) with high correlation coefficient of 0.996 and 0.991. In order to evaluate the repeatability of the sensor, six consecutive measurement cycles were conducted in 20 ppm NO₂ using the AD FeNC gas sensor. As revealed in Fig. 3c, the response-recovery curves maintained identical amplitude, showing that the sensing response had good stability and repeatability. The selectivity of the AD FeNC gas sensor was evaluated using five common inferring gases (20 ppm NO, 500 ppm H₂, 500 ppm NH₃, 500 ppm CH₄ and 500 ppm CO₂) and the sensing response was measured at room temperature. As shown in Fig. 3d, the AD FeNC sensor demonstrated a superb selective response to NO₂ compared to NO, H₂, NH₃, CH₄ and CO₂. It can be found in Fig. 3e, the sensing responses and recovery times of the AD FeNC sensors towards 20 ppm of NO₂ are recorded around 1270 and 1300 s, respectively. In order to calculate the limit of detection, the sensing response in the linear period of 1 to 20 ppm was used. The root-mean-square value of response noise is measured to be 0.39%, and the slope is approximate 4.14 for the gas concentration range of 1 to 20 ppm, suggesting a limit of detection of 0.28 ppm. Furthermore, the AD FeNC sensors were measured towards 20 ppm NO₂ once a day for continuous 10-days to evaluate the long-term stability. As shown in Fig. 3f, the response declines tenderly from 31 to 29 at the first 4 days and then maintains the stable values at rest six days, showing a good long-term stability of the AD FeNC sensor. Besides, UV-Vis and SEM were utilized to study the morphological stability of the AD FeNC sensor after gas sensing process, which was discussed in Fig. S3 and S4. The influence to gas sensing performance from the thickness of carbon support or the coating film in fabricating gas sensor were also studied, which was analyzed in Fig. S5.

Most metal oxide gas-sensitive materials naturally require to operate at high temperatures. In order to evaluate the gas-sensitive properties of AD FeNC materials, recent emerging gas-sensitive materials operating at room temperature or low temperature such as two-dimensional (2D) materials, 2D /nano-metal oxides heterojunctions and 2D hybrid with carbon nanotubes were selected for comparison. As shown in Table 2, the current AD FeNC materials do exhibit the good characteristics in sensing performance, which sufficiently meets the requirements of practical gas sensor applications. In addition, the low cost of non-precious metal single-atom sensitive materials, simple synthesis method and high reliability add more advantages of AD FeNC. Moreover, AD FeNC is a brand-new material that are still in evolution and optimization. This work can catalyze the development of other non-precious metal monoatomic materials that extensively improve the gas-sensitive performance.

The direct decomposition reaction of NO₂ to N₂ and O₂ is spontaneous even at room temperature, because the Gibbs free energy value is less than zero (-51.86 kJ·mol⁻¹ at 298.15 K).⁸³ However, this type of decomposition of NO₂ and NO is thermodynamically favourable but kinetically limited.⁸⁴ The NO₂ decomposition rate can be notably accelerated because the single Fe atom catalyzes the reaction by significantly reducing the activation energy of the decomposition of NO₂. Moreover, the nature of the electron gain from AD FeNC during the NO₂ decomposition into N₂ and O₂ would decrease the electrical resistance of the sensor.⁸⁵ Therefore, neither the NO₂ adsorption nor the direct NO₂ decomposition reaction to form N₂ and O₂ are solely responsible for the unusual observed sensing response profile.

One possible mechanism to explain this peculiar sensing response is considering the synergic contributions from the NO₂ gas adsorption and decomposition. When the concentration of NO₂ is less than 20 ppm, there are two chemical channels occurred simultaneously on the surface of the AD FeNC. The first one is the increase in sensing resistance owning to ordinary chemical absorption of NO₂,⁷⁰, ⁸⁶ which can be explained by gaining the electrons from the AD FeNC due to the nature of n-type semiconductor AD FeNC. Such the electrons transfer from AD FeNC to the absorbed gases were confirmed by DFT calculation as shown in Fig. S6. The second reaction channel is the subsequent decomposition of absorbed NO₂ into N₂ and O₂, which leads to the drop-in sensing resistance. The detailed mechanism of NO₂ decomposition will be discussed in the next paragraph. In the case of low gas concentration, as shown in Fig. S7a, the gas adsorption is far greater than the decomposition due to the large number of available FeN₄ adsorption sites, leading to the maximum sensing resistance value increasing rapidly. This explains the sensor's responses possessing a steep slope at low gas concentrations. However, in the case of high gas concentration (20-250 ppm), the sensor resistances tend to be saturated with NO₂ gas concentration because of the limited FeN₄ adsorption sites being quickly occupied by NO₂ gas adsorption. In addition, NO₂ gases that do not occupy AD FeN₄ sites may be chemically adsorbed at C sites on the surface of AD FeNC. Due to the strong oxidation nature of the C site, such gas adsorption is difficulty to be decomposed and will result in the increase in the sensing resistance macroscopically, as shown in Fig. S7b. The added sensor resistance by NO₂ gases absorbed on the C site can partially offset the decrease in sensor resistance caused by the decomposition of the adsorbed gas on the FeN₄ site. The synergistic effect of the three processes leads to an increase and stabilization of the equilibrium resistance to higher values. Experimentally, for the concentration exceeding 250 ppm, the maximum and equilibrium resistance of the sensor are saturated. Therefore, combining the above three processes, as shown in Fig. S7a, can basically explain the overall peculiar sensing resistance behavior.

Fig. S8 represents the free energies of formation (in units of kilocalories per mole) of NO and NO₂ and some comparison compounds, such as H₂, CO₂, CH₄ and NH₃, all at a temperature of 25°C and a pressure of one atmosphere.⁸⁷ The free energy of formation of a chemical compound may be likened to the potential energy of a dynamic system. Thus, a high free energy of formation means that the compound is thermodynamically unstable. Then it is obvious from the diagram that NO (nitric oxide) and NO₂ are not stable. However, this does not mean that NO and NO₂ will automatically decompose into N₂ and O₂. In fact, the reason for the stability (at low temperatures) of these and other chemical compounds with positive free energies of formation becomes clear if the molecules do not undergo a chemical reaction unless they are activated to a higher than normal energy state (denoted by an asterisk). As shown in Fig. S8, the direct NO₂ decomposition reaction to N₂ and O₂ is unfavorable because it requires a very high activation energy. At room temperature, the thermal energy available is not large enough to activate the dissociation of NO₂ to form N₂ and O₂; therefore, no chemical reaction occurs. Fig. S8 also shows that NO₂ can first be activated to the NO₂* state, then decomposed into NO and oxygen, and finally NO is decomposed to form N₂ and O₂, because the activation energy required for each step is much smaller than the direct decomposition of NO₂ to N₂ and O₂. In the case of the dissociation of NO₂ to NO and O₂, the activation energy is about 30,000 calories per mole, or 1.3 eV per molecule.⁸⁸ This high value renders NO₂ stable at temperatures below 370 K, but it starts to dissociate with an appreciable rate at a temperature of 450 K. In the case of the dissociation of NO to form N₂ and O₂, the activation energy for the dissociation process is even higher, approximately 1000K. When the temperature is high enough for the dissociation of NO into N₂ and O₂, other reactions also occur. Thus, a non-thermal activation mechanism, such as catalytic activation, is necessary. The strong catalytic effect of AD FeNC may support the hypothesis that NO₂ decomposes into NO and then continues to decompose into N₂ and O₂. The electron transfer pathway in adsorption and decomposition reactions is a significant parameter that affects the electrical properties of materials. To elucidate the electron transfer process during the interaction between NO₂ and AD FeNC, DFT was used to calculate the net electron gain and loss. As shown in Fig. S9, the yellow areas show zones in which the charge density increased, while the blue areas reveal a loss of charge. Overall, it can be clearly seen that there is a net charge transfer from AD FeNC to NO₂, which agrees well with the experimental results. The charge transfer from AD FeNC to NO₂ may change the atomic charge density and chemical bond strengths in the NO₂ molecule. To reveal these properties, DFT calculations were operated to assess the change in the charge and bond lengths, as summarized in Tables S1-2. When NO₂ adsorbed on AD FeNC, electrons transferred (0.13 and 0.09 e) from the Fe in FeN₄ and the N in NO₂ to O in NO₂. Simultaneously, the N-O bond length increased to 1.265 Å from the free NO₂ bond length of 1.243 Å. The increased N-O bond length and the concentrated electron density on O in NO₂ facilitate N-O bond breaking, separating one O atom from NO₂ and finally decomposing NO₂ into NO and O₂. NO is subsequently excited to NO*, and the NO + NO* reaction causes NO to further decompose into N₂ and O₂.

To further reveal the mechanism of NO decomposition, we plot the reaction diagrams for NO + NO*→O₂ + N₂. The minimum energy profiles and the configuration of different states involved in this reaction, including the initial state (IS), transition state (TS) and final state (FS) were shown in Fig. 4c. DFT demonstrates that the activation energy barrier of the NO + NO* reaction on AD FeNC is about 0.73 eV, which is significantly lower than that reported in previous studies, which report values in the energy range of 2.60-3.54 eV without ADCs.⁸⁹ These experimental results and DFT simulations strongly support the superior catalytic performance of AD FeNC and its decisive role in reducing the activation energy of the adsorption and decomposition reactions. This explains the decrease in resistance in the sensor response curve (Fig. 3a) mentioned above, since the decomposition of NO to form N₂ and O₂ decreases the sensor resistance, though the NO₂ decomposition to form NO and O₂, catalyzed by AD Fe, obtains electrons from the AD FeNC resulting in an increase in the resistance.⁸⁵, ⁹⁰⁻⁹² The theoretical calculation confirms the proposed two-step decomposition mechanism that the resistance of AD FeNC materials to NO₂ increases first and then decreases in the process of gas sensing.

There is generally a one-to-one correspondence between the adsorption energy and the selectivity without regard to chemical reactions, that is, a high adsorption energy normally leads to good gas response selectivity⁸². To clarify the selective response of the AD FeNC gas sensor towards NOₓ, the adsorption structures and energies of the various experimentally measured gases were further calculated by using DFT based on a FeN₄ coordination. As shown in Fig. 4d and S6, the adsorption energies of NO₂, NO, NH₃, H₂, CO₂, and CH₄ on AD FeNC are -1.82, -2.12, -0.55, -0.18, -0.21, and -0.24 eV, respectively. However, the response to 20 ppm NO₂ is larger than that of 20 ppm NO, which does not follow the general trend mentioned above, even though their resistance profiles in the gas-sensing experiments are similar, as shown schematically in Fig. S10. This inconsistency in the relationship between the sensor selectivity and the absorption energy suggests that other factors may play a more important role in the selectivity. As discussed above, the decomposition of NO₂ to NO is actually an electron-obtained procedure on the AD FeNC so that it can further increase the resistance before the starting of the decomposition of NO to N₂ and O₂. As a result, NO₂ would show a better sensing response compared to NO. In the case of H₂ and NH₃, they also show opposite trends between sensing selectivity and absorption energy. As previously discussed, the response of AD-based FeNC sensors to gases also depends on the free energy of the gas molecules. The relative chemical energies of H₂, NH₃, CH₄ and CO₂, recorded in Fig. S8, are -0.2, -2.7, -12.5 and -93 kJ/mole, respectively. This chemical energy corresponds to the activation energy required to decompose these molecules. Obviously, the activation energy required to decompose H₂ is less than that required for NH₃, and the highest activation energy is required to decompose CO₂. Therefore, the magnitude orders of the selective sensing responses can be arranged as NO₂ > NO > H₂ > NH₃ > CH₄ > CO₂, which highly agrees with the observed experimental result shown in Fig. 3d and explains a superb selective response to NO₂ and NO using the AD FeNC sensor.

Most conventional MOS gas sensors require high operating temperatures, normally above 300°C. A high operating temperature mainly provides enough thermal energy to overcome the activation energy of the adsorption process and the chemical reaction, and speeds up the catalytic reaction rate. In the case of the AD FeNC material, the NO₂ gas sensor can be operated at room temperature without heating. There may exist three factors which may affect the AD FeNC sensors detection of NO₂. First, as characterized from the Hall-effect measurement, the carrier density of AD FeNC (~10¹⁴ cm⁻³) is much lower than that of popular metal oxides such as ZnO and SnO₂. It implied that the change in carrier density due to either NO₂ gas adsorption or decomposition will have a more significant impact on the overall conductivity of AD FeNC. In fact, many graphene-like materials show that the gas responses of the material at low temperature or room temperature is better than the responses at high temperature. Second, as discussed in the DFT simulation, the absorption energy between NO₂ and FeN₄ is -1.82 eV, which is much less than that of NO₂ and ZnO (surficial structure contingent, normally less than -1 eV, for instance -0.57 eV⁹³) and graphene (sites reliant on, normally -55 meV ~ -67 meV⁹⁴). Therefore, the excellent catalytic effect of AD FeNC lowers the activation energy to decompose NO₂ to NO and N₂ and further enhances the sensing response to NO₂ at room temperature. Finally, the porous structure (Fig.1c-d) formed during the chemical synthesis of AD FeNC helps to expand the area of AD FeNC to expose to NO₂ gases, leading to an extra increase in the gas sensing response. Therefore, the nature of low carrier density, low absorption energy, highly catalytic activity, and the porous structure of AD FeNC synergistically contribute a highly sensitive detection of NO₂ at room temperature.

## Conclusions
This article reports the first attempt to utilize AD FeNC materials for application as highly selective, room-temperature gas sensors. SEM, Raman, XRD, XPS, Mössbauer, HAADF-STEM, and EXAFS characterizations confirm that a monoatomic Fe catalyst with a FeN₄ coordination structure was successfully synthesized on the N-C support. The band structure calculations by DFT and the Hall effect experiment show that the AD FeNC material is an n-type semiconductor with a band gap of 0.5 eV. This AD FeNC-based NO₂ gas sensor produces a sensing response to 1 ppm NO₂ at room temperature of up to 5.5% and a linear response range of 1-20 ppm and 20-200 ppm with excellent selectivity. The limit of detection was calculated to be 0.28 ppm. The DFT's results reveal that charge transfer from Fe in FeN₄ and N in NO₂ to O in NO₂ occurs during the NO₂ adsorption process. The barrier for NO₂ decomposition by AD FeNC is calculated to be 0.73 eV. This is significantly lower than the barriers that have been previously reported (2.60-3.54 eV)⁸⁹ for NO₂ decomposition using other materials, which, in turn, explains AD FeNC materials' effectiveness at room temperature. High selectivity of the sensor for NOₓ is due to the higher adsorption

affinity for NO₂ compared to other gases. Finally, the sensing mechanism that the adsorption and initial decomposition of NO₂ to NO and O₂ first and then dissociation of NO to form N₂ and O₂ is proposed. Particularly meaningful, this work overcomes the limitations of the current low mass load in AD materials, first realizes the industrial application of AD materials.
