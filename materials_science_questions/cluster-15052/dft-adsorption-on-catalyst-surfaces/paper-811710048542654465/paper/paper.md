# Interaction of $\text{O}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{N}_2$, and $\text{O}_3$ with stoichiometric and reduced $\text{ZnO(10}\overline{1}\text{0)}$ surface

H. Xu, $^{1}$ R. Q. Zhang, $^{1}$ and S. Y. Tong $^{2,3,*}$

$^{1}$Department of Physics and Materials Science, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong
$^{2}$Department of Physics, The University of Hong Kong, Pokfulam Road, Hong Kong
$^{3}$Hefei National Laboratory for Physical Sciences at Microscale, University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei, Anhui 230026, People's Republic of China

(Received 30 August 2010; published 27 October 2010)

Density-functional theory (DFT) is used to study the interaction of stoichiometric and reduced $\text{ZnO(10}\overline{1}\text{0)}$ surfaces with ambient gases ($\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and oxygen plasmas ($\text{O}_3$ and atomic O). Results of adsorption geometry, formation energy, and reactive barrier are reported. Vacancy-vacancy interaction on the reduced surface is also investigated. The study reveals that $\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, and $\text{O}_3$ only interact weakly with stoichiometric $\text{ZnO(10}\overline{1}\text{0)}$ while $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and atomic O interact strongly. At oxygen vacancies on a reduced $\text{ZnO(10}\overline{1}\text{0)}$ surface, $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ do not interact while atomic O, $\text{O}_2$, and $\text{O}_3$ readily bind to or strongly react with the vacancy. The study also finds that O-O complexes are formed on the surface.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The properties of ZnO as an effective photocatalyst and gas sensor have attracted considerable attention in recent years. $^{1-4}$ As these applications occur under ambient conditions, it is important to first understand how ZnO surfaces interact with gases present in the ambient. The most abundant molecules in the atmosphere are $\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. In addition, oxygen plasma in the form of atomic O or $\text{O}_3$ molecules is commonly used to treat oxide surfaces. Thus, one also would like to know how atomic O or $\text{O}_3$ interact with ZnO surfaces. The applications of ZnO in photocatalysis and gas sensing usually use nanoparticles or nanostructures having different surface facets. $^{4,5}$ Of the different facets, the $(10\overline{1}0)$ surface is the most stable and abundant for ZnO. $^{6}$ Thus, our study focuses on this surface. One also needs to study reduced surfaces because of the ubiquitous nature of oxygen vacancies on the surface. $^{7}$

We report detailed studies on the interaction of $\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{O}_3$ with stoichiometric and reduced $\text{ZnO(10}\overline{1}\text{0)}$ surfaces. Results of adsorption geometry, formation energy, and reactive barrier are reported based on DFT calculations. The projected augmented wave $^{8}$ potentials is employed as implemented in the Vienna $ab$ initio simulation package (VASP). $^{9,10}$ The Perdew and Wang (PW91) form $^{11}$ of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) is adopted and spin-polarized calculations are performed. The Zn $3d$ electrons are treated as valence electrons. A cutoff energy of 400 eV is used throughout the calculations. The $(16\times 10\times 1)$, $(8\times 5\times 1)$, and $(4\times 5\times 1)$ $k$-point Monkhorst-Pack sampling $^{12}$ are used for the structural relaxation of $p(1\times 1)$, $p(2\times 2)$, and $p(4\times 2)$ surfaces, respectively. Optimized lattice parameters are used to construct the slabs and the slab thickness is tested to make sure the results using these parameters have converged. All surfaces are separated by a vacuum region of $10$ Å to ensure negligible intersurface interactions. The conjugate gradient method is used to optimize structural geometries until the Hellmann-Feynman forces on each atom are less than $0.01$ eV/Å. The energy barriers and pathways are calculated using the nudged elastic band method. $^{13}$ Although DFT-GGA calculations are known to underestimate energy band gaps, it has been well documented that these calculations can give reliable results on adsorption geometry, formation energy, and reactive barrier, the focus of this study.

## II. DISTRIBUTION OF OXYGEN VACANCIES

Oxygen vacancies are introduced during sample preparation. $^{7,14}$ The vacancies have a lower formation energy at the surface than in the bulk. $^{15,16}$ It is also important to consider the distribution of oxygen vacancies on the surface, e.g., do vacancies tend to cluster together or form at isolated locations away from each other? To study the interaction between oxygen vacancies, we consider a $p(4\times 2)$ unit cell containing two oxygen vacancies in three configurations shown in Fig. 1. The formation energy per oxygen vacancy ($E_{\text{formation}}$) is calculated as

$$
E_{\text{formation}}=\left(E_{\text{reduced}}-E_{\text{bare}}+\frac{n}{2}E_{\text{O}_2}\right)/n, \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{\text{reduced}}$ is the energy of the reduced surface, $E_{\text{bare}}$ is the energy of the stoichiometric surface, $E_{\text{O}_2}$ is the energy of one oxygen molecule, and $n$ is the number of oxygen vacancies in a $p(4\times 2)$ unit cell.

### III. FORMATION OF O-O COMPLEXES ON THE SURFACE

Molecular oxygen only physisorbs on a stoichiometric ${\rm{ZnO}}(10\overline{1}0)$ surface. $^{15,17,18}$ To check this result, we consider different adsorption configurations with one molecular oxygen in a $p(4\times 2)$ unit cell on the ${\rm{ZnO}}(10\overline{1}0)$ surface. The adsorption energy is calculated as
$$
E_{\text{adsorption}} = E_{\text{bare+O}_{2}} - E_{\text{bare}} - E_{\text{O}_{2}}, \tag{2}
$$
where $E_{\text{bare+O}_{2}}$ is the energy of the surface with one adsorbed molecular oxygen in the unit cell, $E_{\text{bare}}$ is the energy of the stoichiometric surface, and $E_{\text{O}_{2}}$ is the energy of one molecular oxygen.

Therefore, it is easy to produce O-O complexes on either reduced ZnO surfaces with the adsorption of molecular oxygen or stoichiometric surfaces with the adsorption of atomic oxygen.

Similar to $O_2$ adsorption on a stoichiometric $ZnO(10\overline{1}0)$ surface, molecular ozone only weakly binds to such a surface. The adsorption energy is only $-0.05$ eV per $O_3$ molecule. Therefore, at moderate temperatures, the $O_3$ molecule is likely to desorb from a stoichiometric surface as an intact molecule, instead of climbing over the energy barrier and dissociate.

Nitrogen is the most abundant gas in the atmosphere and we explore the interaction of $N_2$ molecules with the $ZnO(10\overline{1}0)$ surface. On a stoichiometric surface, the $N_2$ molecule only interacts weakly. $^{20}$ In Fig. 7, two stable structures are shown. The adsorption energies per $N_2$ molecule for structures 7a and 7b are $-0.03$ eV and $-0.12$ eV, respectively. In structure 7a, the distance between a nitrogen atom of the $N_2$ molecule and the nearest surface Zn atom is $3.15$ Å. In structure 7b, the shortest distance between an N atom of the $N_2$ molecule and the nearest surface Zn atom is $2.57$ Å. Both 7a and 7b are weak physisorbed structures on the stoichiometric surface.

The interaction of an $N_2$ molecule with an oxygen vacancy is studied using a $p(2\times 2)$ unit cell containing one oxygen vacancy per unit cell. Four representative structures are shown in Fig. 8. Figures 8(a) and 8(b) show dissociative adsorption configurations where the oxygen vacancy is filled by a nitrogen atom while the other nitrogen atom is bonded, respectively, to one or two surface Zn atoms. In structure 8c, the $N_2$ molecule adsorbs at the oxygen vacancy site. Structure 8d has the $N_2$ molecule floating away from a reduced surface and this configuration has the lowest energy. The side view of structure 8d is shown in Fig. 8(e). Compared to the most stable structure, 8d, the adsorption energies per $N_2$ molecule for 8a, 8b, and 8c are respectively 6.16 eV, 6.15 eV, and 1.82 eV higher. Thus, the $N_2$ molecule does not adsorb at an oxygen vacancy site. We further investigate the interaction of $N_2$ with an O-O complex on the surface. After structural relaxation, the $N_2$ molecule is found to float away from the O-O complex. These results reveal that the $N_2$ molecule only weakly physisorbs on a stoichiometric $ZnO(10\overline{1}0)$ surface and it does not interact with either an oxygen vacancy or O-O complex.

It is known that water dissociates at an oxygen vacancy site on reduced rutile TiO₂(110).²⁶⁻²⁸ The oxygen in the water molecule fills the vacancy while the two hydrogen atoms form a pair of hydroxyl groups by adsorbing to surface oxygen atoms. On reduced ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface, the behavior is markedly different. The vacancy does not get filled. Again, considering a $p(2\times 2)$ unit cell with one oxygen vacancy per unit cell, the adsorption energy per water molecule is calculated as

$$
E_{\text{adsorption}}=(E_{\text{total}}-E_{\text{reduced}}-nE_{\text{H}_2\text{O}})/n, \tag{5}
$$

where $E_{\text{total}}$ is the energy of the system, $E_{\text{reduced}}$ is the energy of the reduced surface, $E_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$ is the energy of one water molecule, and $n$ is the number of water molecules in the unit cell. The results show that at 1/4 ML coverage, water prefers to adsorb in molecular form on surface Zn sites far away from the oxygen vacancy (see Fig. 11 and Table III). The most stable adsorption configurations are structures 11d and 11e, which are essentially degenerate in energy where the water adsorbs on surface Zn atoms in rows not containing oxygen vacancies.

## VI. COMBINED ADSORPTION OF WATER AND OXYGEN ON REDUCED ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface

When exposing a reduced ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface to the atmosphere, it is necessary to consider the combined adsorption of oxygen and water on the surface. Consider a water molecule arriving at a $p(2\times 2)$ ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface with one oxygen vacancy per unit cell that is preadsorbed with an O₂ molecule. We know from Fig. 3(e) that an O-O complex is formed at the vacancy site. Calculating the adsorption energy for 1/4 ML water on this surface, we find that water adsorbs in molecular form and several adsorption configurations and energies are shown, respectively, in Fig. 13 and Table IV. The most stable adsorption configurations are 13a and 13b whose energies are almost degenerate. If water molecules land on the reduced surface first, they adsorb in rows not containing the vacancies according to structures 11d and 11e. Subsequent arrival of O₂ molecules then form O-O complexes at vacancy sites and we recover structures 13a and 13b as before. Therefore, the sequence of water and O₂ arrival is not important, and structures 13a and 13b are formed when reduced ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) is coadsorbed with water and O₂.

### VII. CONCLUSION
The properties of ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface are strongly influenced by the interaction of ambient gases and presence of oxygen vacancies on the surface. The study reveals that $O_2$, $N_2$, and $O_3$ only interact weakly with the stoichiometric ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface while $H_2O$ and atomic O interact strongly, forming interesting chemisorbed species on the surface. On reduced ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0), the vacancies tend to form as point defects away from each other. At oxygen vacancies on a reduced ZnO(10$\overline{1}$0) surface, $N_2$ and $H_2O$ do not interact while the other species studied (i.e., atomic O, $O_2$, and $O_3$) readily bind to or strongly react with the vacancies. It is also found that O-O complexes are formed on either reduced ZnO surfaces with the adsorption of molecular oxygen or stoichiometric surfaces with the adsorption of atomic oxygen.
