# Adsorption and Activation of Methane on the (110) Surface of Rutile-type Metal Dioxides

Yuta Tsuji and Kazunari Yoshizawa*

Institute for Materials Chemistry and Engineering and IRCCS, Kyushu University, Nishi-ku, Fukuoka 819-0395, Japan

Supporting Information

**ABSTRACT:** Methane strongly adsorbs on the (110) surface of $IrO_2$, a rutile-type metal dioxide. Its C−H bond is facilely dissociated even below room temperature, as predicted in a few theoretical works and actually observed in a recent experimental study. Thence, three questions are posed and answered in this paper: First, why does methane adsorb on the $IrO_2$ surface so strongly? Second, why is the surface so active for the C−H bond breaking reaction? Third, is there any other rutile-type metal dioxide that is more active than $IrO_2$? A second-order perturbation theoretic approach is successfully applied to the analysis of the electronic structure of methane, which is found to be significantly distorted on the surface. Regarding the first point, it is clarified that an attractive orbital interaction between the surface Ir $5d_{z^2}$ orbital and the distorted methane's highest occupied molecular orbital leads to the strong adsorption. As for the second point, the bond strength between the surface metal atom and the $CH_3$ fragment generated after the C−H bond scission of methane is correlated well with the activation barrier. A substantial bonding interaction between $CH_3$'s nonbonding orbital and the $d_{z^2}$ orbital hints at the strong $Ir−CH_3$ bond and hence high catalytic activity ensues. Last but not least, $\beta$-$PtO_2$, a distorted rutile-type dioxide, is identified as a more active catalyst than $IrO_2$. Here again, a perturbation theoretic line of explanation is found to be of tremendous help. This paper is at the intersection of theoretical, catalytic, inorganic, and physical chemistry. Also, it should serve as a model for the design and study of metal-oxide catalysts for the C−H bond activation of methane.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Methane, the primary component of natural gas, could play an important role as feedstock to produce value-added chemicals. Since we enjoy abundant supply of natural gas, the last few decades have witnessed a growing requirement for an effective scheme to transform methane to fine commodities. A significant problem chemical industry faces is how one could activate methane. The following are the major causes why the activation of methane is so intricate. First, the $sp^3$ C−H bond is too strong as exemplified by its large bond dissociation energy (104 kcal/mol). Second, its nonpolar nature makes it difficult to be trapped in solvents or on surfaces that may catalyze the C−H bond scission. Finally, we need to take care of a problem of overoxidation because the products of the methane functionalization often include a C−H bond, which is more reactive than that of methane. For starters, in this study, we will focus on the first problem, namely, the C−H bond dissociation, because nothing would happen without the C−H bond breaking. In the reactions of methane on most heterogeneous catalysts, this process is regarded as the rate-determining step and researchers are trying very hard to lower the activation barrier for that process.¹

A currently available industrial process, steam reforming, can convert methane to synthesis gas, a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen. This process is followed by a catalytic reaction to synthesize methanol or the Fischer− Tropsch reaction to produce higher hydrocarbons.²³ Once one looks into the process of steam reforming, one can realize how high the reaction temperature is needed. This no doubt reflects the difficulty of the C−H bond breaking in methane. Also, it would be nice if one could develop a process in which methane is directly converted into other higher-value chemicals so that the expensive and environmentally harmful syngas step can be evaded.¹⁴

Nature had achieved what we wish through a certain kind of enzyme that includes copper or iron complexes in its active site for the C−H bond cleavage, namely, methane monooxygenase.⁵⁷ This enzymatic function in methanotropic bacteria happens at ambient temperature and pressure. Actually, human beings have achieved some sort of success in the direct conversion of methane to methanol by developing metal-exchanged zeolites, such as Fe-ZSM-5,⁸⁹ and, more recently, Cu-ZSM-5.¹⁰¹¹ Still, this process generally requires higher temperature, although some attempts to lower the reaction temperature have been reported.⁹

A reasonable last resort might be found through surface inspection.¹² Methane, although only weakly, adsorbs on transition-metal surfaces, where the C−H bond can be activated.⁴,¹³,¹⁴ Owing to high-temperature conditions required for the reaction, various competing processes may occur simultaneously on the surfaces, resulting in poor selectivity. Continuing efforts have been made to lower the temperature required for the surface reaction so that one can have control over the reaction more or less.¹⁵

On transition-metal surfaces, methane undergoes C−H bond rupture followed by a formation of bonds to the surface, i.e., dissociative chemisorption, which can be described by either of two mechanisms: direct mechanism or trapping-mediated mechanism.¹⁶ In the direct mechanism, the collision of methane with the surface leads to C−H bond dissociation when the kinetic energy of the molecule surmounts the activation barrier. On the other hand, in the trapping-mediated mechanism, the kinetic energy of methane has to be dissipated so that the molecule can be trapped by the surface and a competition between dissociation and desorption ensues. In this regime, the lower the kinetic energy of gas-phase methane is, the more probably its adsorption happens. Thus, low gas temperatures result in dissociative chemisorption of methane dominated by the trapping-mediated mechanism, whereas high gas temperatures lead to the direct mechanism.¹⁷,¹⁸

As far as the trapping-mediated mechanism goes, strong affinity of the surface to methane plays a key role in determining the branching probability for the competing desorption versus C−H bond dissociation processes.¹⁶ We note that the desorption barrier can be gauged by the adsorption energy, although the two quantities have the same magnitude but opposite sign. If the magnitude of the adsorption energy of methane on the surface is lower than the C−H dissociation barrier, C−H bond activation is favored over desorption, resulting in a relatively high probability for the dissociative chemisorption of methane.

A legitimate strategy for the C−H bond activation of methane via the trapping-mediated mechanism would rely on how one can effectively enhance the affinity of a surface to methane. Yet, that is not so simple because methane can only weakly physisorb on transition-metal surfaces, such as the (111) surfaces of Rh, Ni, Pd, and Pt, with adsorption energy varying from 0.02 to 0.03 eV.¹⁹⁻²¹ By contrast, a variety of metal oxides can effectively catalyze the process of the activation of C−H bonds;²² metal-oxide facets, including the face (100) of MgO, (101) of PdO, and (110) of TiO₂, provide a larger adsorption energy for methane, ranging from 0.13 to 0.36 eV.²³⁻²⁵

To begin, let us examine what is known about the C−H bond dissociation of methane on the surface of such heterogeneous metal-oxide catalysts as PdO and MgO. Owing to in situ measurements, one can observe methane oxidation with high rates over the PdO surface under steady-state conditions at elevated pressure.²⁶ It is reported that specific facets of the metal oxide, e.g., the (101) face of PdO, can promote alkane C−H bond cleavage.²⁷ The existence of coordinatively unsaturated metal atoms on the surface plays a crucial role in forming adsorbed methane σ complexes on the surface, making C−H bond cleavage more facile.²⁷ The coordinatively unsaturated metals also play an important role in the C−H bond activation using metal complexes.²⁸ On the other hand, for the case of MgO, the edges and steps provide acid−base pairs involving Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions, which facilitate the C−H bond activation in a heterolytic fashion.²⁹ In addition, it should be noted that the O atoms on the metal-oxide surfaces can synergically enhance the H-atom abstraction from methane as with those at the coordination sphere of metal complexes, namely, metal-oxo groups.³⁰ A similar feature can also be found in gas-phase experiments using metal-oxide clusters, where the thermal activation of methane at room temperature is attained.³¹,³²

Researchers have been active in exploring how the interaction between methane and a coordinatively unsaturated metal benefits the catalytic reaction of methane. Wu et al. pointed out that the unscreened electrostatic effect due to the coordinatively unsaturated metal ions should lead to a strong binding of methane.³³ Weaver et al. clarified the dative interaction of methane with a coordinatively unsaturated metal site, which results in strengthening of the alkane−surface binding as well as weakening of the metal-coordinated C−H bonds.²⁷

On the basis of a theoretical study using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, Jiang and co-workers³⁴,³⁵ found a surprisingly large adsorption energy of methane (ca. 0.4 eV) on the stoichiometric IrO₂(110) surface, which includes onefold coordinatively unsaturated Ir atoms (Ir_cus) and bridged O atoms (O_br) (see Figure 1). Their analyses on the electron density difference contour plot of CH₄ adsorption and the density of states (DOS) difference in the d_z² state of the Ir_cus atom hint at a significant interaction between the C−H bonding orbital and the d_z² state of the Ir_cus atom, where cus means “coordinatively unsaturated”. This unique d−σ interaction seems indicative of an agostic interaction,³⁶ which is familiar in organometallic chemistry. Also, an O_br atom is found to be important for the formation of a hydrogen bond between methane and the surface. Both Ir_cus and O_br play a key

Recently, using temperature-programmed reaction spectros- copy, Weaver and co-workers have experimentally observed that methane readily undergoes C−H bond cleavage on the stoichiometric IrO₂(110) surface at temperatures as low as 150 K.³⁷ Such an active, yet persistent, catalytic surface had not been reported experimentally. Since the activation energy for the initial C−H bond breaking is expected to be lower than the desorption barrier or the negative of the adsorption energy, the reaction is expected to occur through the trapping-mediated mechanism. Actually, it is observed that the dissociation probability of methane decreases with increasing temperature. This observation can be a hallmark of the occurrence of the trapping-mediated mechanism.

Methane activation and following conversion at low temperature should be of great scientific as well as industrial importance because low-temperature process may provide a way of circumventing undesired side reactions, allowing selective oxidation. For example, when one makes a comparison between CH₃OH and CO formations from CH₄, one can realize that the CO formation is preferred over the CH₃OH formation at high temperatures (above around 600 K) from a thermodynamic viewpoint.³⁸ Lowering the activation temperature has been achieved, to some extent, by using admetal/CeO₂ surfaces³⁹ and H-galloaluminosilicate ZSM-5-type zeolites.⁴⁰ But these temperatures are not as low as those reported in the work of Weaver and co-workers.

The Ir atom per se may play an important role for the C−H bond scission, and a series of studies on Ir-complex-catalyzed borylation of methane have been reported recently.⁴¹,⁴² Also, Ir metal surfaces are reactive for methane compared to other transition-metal surfaces,¹⁸,⁴³⁻⁴⁵ although they are found to be less reactive compared to the oxide surfaces. This implies that, whether an Ir atom is incorporated in a metal complex or an oxide surface, an unsaturated Ir atom in the ligand-field environment is likely to be indispensable for the C−H bond cleavage of methane.

There are three main objectives in this paper. The first objective is to trace back the origin of the strong affinity of the Ir atom to methane, although previous calculations by Jiang and co-workers³⁴,³⁵ and Weaver and co-workers³⁷ exist. We aim to shed new light on the methane−oxide surface interaction from the perspective of molecular orbitals (MOs), bands, and their interaction. The second objective is to ponder whether such an effective catalytic reaction might be seen in other metal-oxide surfaces. Our third objective is to provide a design principle for effective heterogeneous metal-oxide catalysts for the C−H bond activation of methane.

### 2.3. Perturbation Theoretic Analysis of Orbital
Mixing. In Scheme 3, we did not yet allow the three orbitals to interact with each other. In this sense, one can regard those as diabatic states. Now let us use simple but powerful consequences of the perturbation theory for orbital interactions.⁵⁰,⁵¹ The first guiding principle for the orbital interaction is that only orbitals of like symmetry can interact with each other. Thus, in the C_s structure, the two a′ orbitals can interact, but the leftover a″ orbital cannot.

The second principle is that when two levels interact, the lower level is stabilized while the upper level is destabilized. This energy change is caused by the second-order perturbation energy correction,⁵⁰ which reads as

$$
\Delta \varepsilon_{i}=\frac{\left|H_{i j}^{\prime}\right|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}}
\tag{1}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}$ is an unperturbed (zeroth order in the terminology of perturbation theory) orbital energy before a perturbation or interaction is turned on. The numerator of eq 1 is the absolute squared value of the matrix element of the perturbation, which can be written as

$$
H_{i j}^{\prime}=\left\langle\psi_{i}^{(0)}\left|H^{\prime}\right| \psi_{j}^{(0)}\right\rangle
\tag{2}
$$

where $\psi_{i}^{(0)}$ denotes an unperturbed (or zeroth order) wave function and H′ represents a Hamiltonian operator for perturbation. The unperturbed wave functions $\psi_{i}^{(0)}$ and their eigenvalues $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}$ correspond to the ones depicted in Scheme 3. We note that the numerator of eq 1 is positive definite. If $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)} < \varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}$, then $\Delta \varepsilon_{i} < 0$. By contrast, if $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)} > \varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}$, then $\Delta \varepsilon_{i} > 0$. This is the reason why the two interacting orbitals repel each other. When the orbitals in Scheme 3 are allowed to interact with each other, one can anticipate that the upper a′ orbital is further pushed up but the lower one is pushed down, whereas the a″ orbital stays at a constant energy.

The third principle of orbital interaction is about the shape of the wave function after the perturbation is turned on. Suppose that two orbitals interact with each other. The upper- level orbital $\psi_{j}^{(0)}$ mixes into itself the lower-level one $\psi_{i}^{(0)}$ in the first order, leading to a perturbed lower-level orbital $\psi_{i}$, and vice versa. The construction of the new wave function can be expressed as

$$
\psi_{i}=\psi_{i}^{(0)}+c_{j i} \psi_{j}^{(0)}
\tag{3}
$$

where $c_{j i}$ is the first-order mixing coefficient, which measures how much orbital j mixes into orbital i. By switching i and j in eq 3, one can readily obtain an expression for the new wave function of the jth level. When one predicts the shape of the perturbed wave function on the basis of eq 3, the sign of the mixing coefficient is of significant importance and can be assessed from

$$
c_{j i}=\frac{H_{i j}^{\prime}}{\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}} \propto \frac{-S_{i j}}{\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}}
\tag{4}
$$

where $S_{i j}$ denotes the overlap between the unperturbed orbitals and has the opposite sign to $H_{i j}^{\prime}$. Although the sign of the overlap between the two a′ orbitals shown in Scheme 3 is not so easy to evaluate at first glance, the sign can be assessed to be negative. A graphical scheme for the qualitative assessment of the sign is presented in the Supporting Information (SI).

Now that we know the sign of the overlap, we can qualitatively predict that the upper a′ orbital in Scheme 3 mixes into the lower a′ orbital with a negative coefficient, whereas the lower one mixes into the upper one with a positive coefficient. The orbital energy change mentioned above and orbital mixing associated with the orbital interaction are summarized in Scheme 4.

### 2.4. Electronic Structure of the IrO₂(110) Surface.
Before we move on to scrutiny of the interaction between the methane orbitals and the IrO₂ surface states, it would be instructive to briefly review IrO₂ itself and its surfaces. IrO₂ is a Pauli paramagnetic⁵⁴⁻⁵⁶ transition-metal dioxide with metallic conductivity⁵⁷,⁵⁸ that crystallizes in a tetragonal rutile structure (see Figure 1a).⁵⁹ Over and co-workers found that the IrO₂ film can be predominantly oriented along (110) with good epitaxial relationship to the Ir(111) substrate.⁶⁰ The (110) surface is one of the dominant surfaces of IrO₂, having been well characterized by using both experimental and theoretical methods.⁶¹,⁶²

As can be seen from Figure 1a, in the rutile IrO₂ structure, each Ir⁴⁺ center is in an octahedral six-coordinate environment. The d orbitals of the Ir metal centers split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ sets due to a strong octahedral ligand field, where one can observe a significant hybridization between Ir 5d and O 2p states.⁵⁵ Since some important links between organometallic chemistry and surface science have been found,¹²,⁶³⁻⁶⁷ one can understand the electronic structure of not only metal-oxide bulks but also their surfaces, on the basis of knowledge of the MOs of the corresponding metal complexes. Surfaces can be viewed as increasing nuclearity of a complex.⁶³

The (110) surface of IrO₂ includes five-coordinate unsaturated $Ir_{cus}$ sites. The interactions labeled as A and B are two-orbital two- electron interactions, which are called a charge-transfer interaction. Both of them result in the weakening of the $\sigma$ bond. Thus, in terms of the C−H bond activation of methane, we need to consider how one can promote these interactions.

Interaction C is a two-orbital four-electron one, leading to destabilization and a repulsive force between the adsorbate molecule and the surface. $^{69}$ To enhance the affinity of methane to the surface, one needs to suppress this interaction. This comes from the highly localized nature of metal-oxide orbitals compared to that of metals, where the orbitals are well delocalized. $^{71,72}$ In $IrO_{2}$, the $e_{g}$ states (or $a_{1}$ and $b_{1}$ states) generate the CB, whereas the $t_{2g}$ states (or $e + b_{2}$ states) lead to the VB, which is partially filled. When one applies the interaction diagram to the $IrO_{2}$ surface−methane interaction, one needs to assume that the Fermi level cuts through the VB.

To enhance the interaction, a small energy difference between the surface state and the adsorbate MO and a substantial overlap between them are needed. In the diagram shown in Scheme 6b, the energy differences associated with the attractive interactions A and B look larger than those associated with the repulsive interaction C, although this is just a conceptual scheme. Thus, the MO interactions in the depicted system are expected to be dominated by the repulsive one. This is not good for the bonding of methane to the surface. However, in general, it would be difficult to exert an influence on the MO level location of methane itself so that a desirable energy-level alignment between methane’s MO levels and CB or VB levels of a surface can be achieved. Instead, one may have a choice for the surface that possesses CB and VB levels favorably located relative to the methane MOs.

2.6. Orbital Interactions between a Distorted Meth- ane and the $IrO_{2}(110)$ Surface. In the previous sections, we have conducted a thorough scrutiny of the MO levels of

Two scenarios have been presented in Scheme 7. To determine which scenario applies to the methane-$IrO_{2}$ interaction, we need to inspect the energy-level alignment between the methane MOs and the $IrO_{2}$ surface state levels. One could do it by looking at the PDOS plots for methane. An analysis of the crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP), $^{74-77}$ however, would be an alternative way and more informative because the COHP itself includes information about DOS as well as that about the bonding or antibonding state of a pair of atoms. The COHP can be regarded as DOS weighted by the Hamiltonian matrix elements for a pair of atoms, dating back to the crystal orbital overlap population (COOP) proposed by Hoffmann and co-workers. $^{12,78}$ The COHP between atoms $i$ and $j$ in the same unit cell can be expressed as

$$
\operatorname{COHP}_{i, j}(E)=H_{i j} \sum_{n, \vec{k}} C_{i, n}^{*}(\vec{k}) C_{j, n}(\vec{k}) \delta\left(\varepsilon_{n}(\vec{k})-E\right)
\tag{5}
$$

where $H_{i j}$ denotes the corresponding Hamiltonian matrix element for the atom pair, and $C_{j, n}(\vec{k})$ and $\varepsilon_{n}(\vec{k})$ are the expansion coefficient of atom $j$ in the $n$th wave function at $k$-point $\vec{k}$ and its eigenvalue, respectively. The concept of COHP is inherently based on the formalism of linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAOs), but VASP uses planewave basis sets, so a projection technique which transfers the wave function obtained from a planewave computation to an LCAO picture has to be used. Thus, COHP obtained through the projection scheme is called projected COHP (pCOHP).

The molecular analogue of COOP or COHP is referred to as molecular orbital overlap population (MOOP) or molecular orbital Hamilton population. $^{79}$ One of the authors (Yoshizawa) and co-workers, conducted MOOP analyses to understand $C-H$ bond dissociation of methane by a diiron model of soluble methane monooxygenase $^{80}$ as well as transition-metaloxide ions. $^{81}$ It is about time we applied the concept to surfaces.

Figure 6 shows negative pCOHP curves for the $C-H$ bond of methane adsorbed on the $IrO_{2}(110)$ surface and that optimized in vacuo. Even when we calculate the COHP curve of methane optimized in vacuo, the $IrO_{2}$ surface slab is also kept included in the unit cell well below methane so that the surface and methane cannot interact with each other but the Fermi levels and vacuum levels of the whole system can be compared to those of the methane-adsorbed system.

Since bonding interactions are characterized by a positive overlap matrix element, which leads to a negative Hamiltonian matrix element, as we have seen in eq 4, negative pCOHP values indicate a bonding character. In Figure 6, however, the pCOHP values have already been multiplied by $-1$ so that positive -pCOHP values can be viewed as a bonding interaction. At first glance, one can realize that the $C-H$ bond of distorted methane on the surface and the one relaxed in vacuo have a bonding character in the energy range below the Fermi level, whereas it has generally an antibonding character above the Fermi level. Thus, the bonding states are all occupied, whereas the antibonding ones are all unoccupied.

### 2.7. What about Other Metal Dioxides?
A key guiding principle to facilitate the C−H bond activation of methane as well as enhance the affinity of methane to the surface is to strengthen the interaction between the metal d orbitals and the $\sigma_{\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}}$ orbital, which can be measured by the second-order perturbation energy, i.e., eq 1. The smaller the energy difference between the metal and methane orbitals is, the larger interaction between them is expected. The Ir $\mathrm{d}_{z}{ }^{2}$ orbital is located at around $E = 1$ eV (see Figure 5), while the HOMO of the fully optimized methane in vacuo is located at around $E = -3$ eV (see Figure 6). Hence, the energy difference is about4 eV in the case of the interaction between methane and $\mathrm{IrO}_{2}$ surface. If one could reduce this energy difference, a more active catalyst for the C−H bond dissociation of methane would be realized. A reasonable strategy is to lower the metal d level.

For consistency, here, let us adhere the (110) surface of rutile-type metal dioxides and seek for a suitable metal. As Scheme 5 shows, since the $\mathrm{t}_{2g}$ orbitals are nonbonding, their energy levels are anticipated to be close to those of the d orbitals of noninteracting metal atoms. By contrast, the energy-level location of the $\mathrm{d}_{z}{ }^{2}\left(\mathrm{a}_{1}\right)$ orbital is expected to be higher than the metal-atom d level due to the antibonding interaction with the $\mathrm{O}^{2-}$ ligands. If one deals with the interaction within the framework of the second-order perturbation theory, $^{50}$ one can obtain an expression for the $\mathrm{d}_{z}{ }^{2}$ orbital level $\varepsilon_{i}$ as follows

$$
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}+\Delta \varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}+\frac{\left|H_{i j}^{\prime}\right|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}} \tag{6}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}$ is the metal-atomic d-orbital energy and $\Delta \varepsilon_{i}$ is the second-order energy correction or the energy difference between the $\mathrm{d}_{z}{ }^{2}$ orbital and the noninteracting metal d level. This energy correction can be regarded as the same form as eq 1, but in this case, $H_{i j}^{\prime}$ can be interpreted as the strength of the interaction between the $\mathrm{d}_{z}{ }^{2}$ orbital and the $\mathrm{O}^{2-}$ ligand orbitals and $\varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}$ denotes the $\mathrm{O}^{2-}$ ligand orbital energy.

To make $\varepsilon_{i}$ lower, one generally needs to make $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}$ lower. This, in turn, however, leads to an increase in $\Delta \varepsilon_{i}$ because $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)} - \varepsilon_{j}^{(0)}$ becomes smaller. $\Delta \varepsilon_{i}$ may counteract the lowering of $\varepsilon_{i}$. We conducted an extended Hückel molecular orbital (eHMO) calculation to clarify the effect of $\varepsilon_{i}^{(0)}$ on $\varepsilon_{i}$. On the basis of the structure of an $\left[\mathrm{IrO}_{5}\right]^{6-}$ cluster cut out of the optimized surface slab of $\mathrm{IrO}_{2}$, we carried out a fragment molecular orbital (FMO) analysis implemented in the YAEHMOP program, $^{82}$ where the MOs for the $\left[\mathrm{IrO}_{5}\right]^{6-}$ cluster are divided into contributions from the MOs of an $\left(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\right)_{5}$ fragment and the atomic orbitals (AOs) of the $\mathrm{Ir}^{4+}$ fragment (see Figure 8a). A standard parameter set for the Ir and O atoms $^{83}$ used is tabulated in the SI.

A strength of the eHMO method is that it can allow one to study general trends which are not readily approached by sophisticated ab initio calculations. $^{84}$ One such example follows below. Here, to see the effect of the energy of Ir 5d AOs on the MO levels of the $\left[\mathrm{IrO}_{5}\right]^{6-}$ cluster, we tune the $H_{i i}$ value, which is the diagonal Hamiltonian matrix element or Coulomb integral for the Ir 5d orbital. A standard $H_{i i}$ value for the Ir 5d orbital is $-12.17$ eV. $^{83}$ And this value was artificially reduced to $-14.5$ eV, where the $\left(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\right)_{5}$ fragment orbitals reside, and another FMO calculation was conducted (see Figure 8b).

### 2.9. Geometrical and Electronic Structures of $\mathrm{CrO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$.
Chromium(IV) dioxide, $\mathrm{CrO}_{2}$, has a tetragonal rutile-type crystal structure (see Figure 11a) and shows ferromagnetic and metallic properties. $^{89}$ $\mathrm{CrO}_{2}$ is metastable in air but has been widely used in the manufacture of magnetic tapes, $^{90}$ although such a form of data storage enjoys less and less popularity and may eventually disappear.

Platinum(IV) dioxide, $\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, is a reagent known as Adams' catalyst, which is named after its discoverer Roger Adams. $^{91}$ Although it is used in hydrogenations of alkenes to alkanes, the actual chemical species, which is thought to act as a catalyst, is not the oxide but platinum black, which is formed during the hydrogenation reaction. $^{92}$ Also, it has been reported that the catalytic oxidation of CO and NO can be enhanced on the $\mathrm{PtO}_{2}(110)$ surface. $^{93,94}$

Several structures have been identified for $\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, for example, $\alpha$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, and $\beta'$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$. $^{95}$ $\alpha$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$ has a $\mathrm{CdI}_{2}$-type layered structure with space group $P\overline{3}m1$ and nothing to do with the rutile structure. $^{96}$ $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$ with an orthorhombic $\mathrm{CaCl}_{2}$-type structure and space group $Pnnm$ can be perceived as a distorted rutile structure (see Figure 11b). $^{97}$ $\beta'$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$ has a tetragonal rutile-type structure with space group $P4_{2}/mnm$. $^{98}$ Judging from their heats of formation, the distorted rutile structure, $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, is found to be the most stable of the three forms of $\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$. $^{95}$ We adopt $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$ in the following calculations.

Owing to the rotation of the $\mathrm{PtO}_{6}$ octahedra about the $c$ axis in $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, an energy gap is opened at the Fermi level, stabilizing the distorted structure. $^{99}$ Although there is no experimental data available for determining the band gap of $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, its resistivity measurement implies a semiconductive nature. $^{100}$ In a theoretical study conducted by Yang, Sugino, and Ohno, $^{101}$ the band gap of $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$ is determined to be 1.25 eV by using the single-shot GW $(G_{0}W_{0})$ approach, $^{102}$ which is one of the most reliable ways to predict the band gap although it requires huge computational cost. $^{103}$ They verified that the DFT plus Hubbard $U$ (DFT + $U$) method, $^{104}$ which is a more practical method in terms of computational effort, can reproduce the $G_{0}W_{0}$-calculated band gap if the effective on-site Coulomb repulsion parameter $U_{\text{eff}}$ is set to be 7.5 eV for Pt 5d states. If the normal DFT method is used, the band gap will be severely underestimated. In the following sections, we will adopt a DFT + $U$ method with $U_{\text{eff}} = 7.5$ eV for the calculation of the $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}(110)$ surface (for more details, see the Theoretical Methods section). In our calculation, the method of choice gives a band gap of 1.2 eV for bulk $\beta$-$\mathrm{PtO}_{2}$, which shows a good agreement with Yang et al.'s study.

To begin with, it would be instructive to examine the MOs of the $CH_3$ fragment. $CH_3$-adsorbed metal-dioxide surfaces can be regarded as an organometallic compound of a certain kind, where the $CH_3$ fragment serves as a Lewis base ligand and has a formal charge of $-1.^{121}$ Scheme 10 illustrates

This results in a scanty overlap between the upper lobe and the 1s orbitals of the H atoms, making it a nonbonding orbital. This is why we call it the $n$ orbital. When we think about the interaction of methyl with a surface, the most important orbital should obviously be the $n$ orbital because it can lead to the great overlap with any surface orbitals. $^{12,122}$ Its partner of the interaction is no doubt the metal's $d_{z^2}$ orbital.

Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the strength of the $M-CH_3$ bond is dominated by the bonding interaction between the $n$ orbital of $CH_3^-$ and the metals' $d_{z^2}$ orbital.

On the basis of the second-order perturbation expression (eq 1), we can determine to what extent an orbital interaction between the orbitals of two fragments results in stabilization. But it provides us with important information about the denominator of eq 1, i.e., $\varepsilon_i^{(0)} - \varepsilon_j^{(0)}$, which corresponds to the energy difference between the $n$ and $d_{z^2}$ orbitals. Pt's $d_{z^2}$ is found to be the closest to the $n$ orbital of $CH_3^-$, whereas Cr's one is the furthest. And Ir's $d_{z^2}$ falls in between Pt's and Cr's. The smaller the energy difference is, the larger the stabilization energy is expected. Thus, the most significant stabilization can be found in the $Pt-CH_3$ system, whereas the $Cr-CH_3$ system is the least stabilized one. The $Ir-CH_3$ system is a moderately stabilized. This trend qualitatively agrees with how much the energy of the $n$ orbital is lowered as a result of the interaction in Figure 19.

As we have seen in Figure 19, the interaction between the $n$ and $d_{z^2}$ orbitals can be viewed as a charge-transfer interaction, where there occurs an electron density shift from $CH_3^-$ to the metal atom. Thus, the charge of the $CH_3$ fragment can be a good measure of interaction. Such charge-transfer features have been found in $CH_3$-adsorbed pure metal surfaces. $^{122}$ We will apply this concept to the metal-dioxide surfaces. Only the $CH_3$ fragment adsorbed on the $PtO_2$ surface has a positive charge, suggesting a significant charge transfer from $CH_3$ to Pt. These DFT results validate the speculation based on the qualitative eHMO interaction diagrams.

### 2.14. Adsorption vs Activation.
In the early stage of this paper, we have inspected the orbital interaction, which dominates the methane adsorption on the $IrO_2$ surface. ### 3. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Stimulated by a recent experimental work regarding the C−H bond activation of methane on the stoichiometric (110) surface of the rutile-type IrO₂ below room temperature, we started first-principles calculations of a methane adsorption structure. A significantly distorted methane structure was observed. The second-order perturbation theory was found to be helpful for understanding why the distortion enhances the affinity of methane to the surface. That is to say, the distortion increases the amplitude of the HOMO around the activated C−H bond, resulting in a good overlap with the metal $d_{z^2}$ orbital on the surface. In this analysis, we realized how helpful a coordination chemistry perspective is in surface science.

We then proceeded to the investigation of the process of the C−H bond dissociation on the surface. For a comparative purpose, we also inspected the surfaces of a rutile-type dioxide CrO₂ and a distorted rutile-type dioxide $\beta$-PtO₂. On the basis of the Bell−Evans−Polanyi principle, we found that the bond strength between the metal atom of the dioxide surfaces and the $CH_3$ fragment generated after the dissociation reaction is correlated well with the activation barrier of the reaction. The energetic proximity of the surface metal $d_{z^2}$ orbital to the $n$ orbital of the $CH_3$ fragment is a determining factor of the bond strength. The second-order perturbation theory was again successfully applied to the explanation of the interaction.

The HOMO of distorted $CH_4$ is lower in energy than the $n$ orbital of $CH_3^-$, and the $d_{z^2}$ orbital of the surface metal atom cannot be located below them. Thus, a rational design principle for active catalysts for the C−H bond cracking of $CH_4$ can be summarized as follows: The surface metal $d_{z^2}$ orbital should be located immediately above the $n$ orbital of $CH_3^-$. Once this condition is met, the energy difference between the $d_{z^2}$ orbital and the HOMO of $CH_4$ also gets smaller. $\beta$-PtO₂ is found to satisfy this condition well. Therefore, we believe that the catalytic activity of the $\beta$-PtO₂ surface is worth being investigated experimentally.

### 4. THEORETICAL METHODS

All periodic DFT calculations in this study were done by using the Vienna ab inito simulation package (VASP 5.4.1).¹²³⁻¹²⁶ The Kohn−Sham equations were solved with a planewave basis set using the projector-augmented wave method.¹²⁷,¹²⁸ Spin-polarization calculations were performed for all systems.

The generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange–correlation functional was adopted. $^{129}$ For $\beta$-PtO$_2$, we carried out Dudarev's method of DFT + $U$ calculations, $^{130}$ in which a value of $U_{\text{eff}} = 7.5$ eV was adopted for Pt's 5d states. A planewave basis set cutoff of 450 eV, self-consistent field tolerance of $10^{-5}$ eV, and $2 \times 10^{-2}$ eV/Å threshold of forces on atoms guaranteed good convergence. Brillouin zone sampling on a grid of spacing $2\pi \times 0.05$ Å$^{-1}$ was used for the crystal structure optimizations of IrO$_2$, $\beta$-PtO$_2$, and CrO$_2$.

The (110) surfaces of the metal dioxides were modeled as a periodic slab model constructed by cleaving the optimized bulk crystal. The unit cell includes a $p(3 \times 1)$ surface and a vacuum space of 15 Å above the surface. The slab models have four O–M–O repeating units in the direction perpendicular to the surface. This corresponds to 12 atomic layers, whose thickness is about 12 Å. The lower six atomic layers were kept fixed during geometry optimization, where a conjugate gradient algorithm $^{131}$ was employed. Graphics of all of the periodic structures and their isosurfaces were produced using VESTA. $^{132}$

To calculate the minimum energy paths and find the transition-state structures for the C–H bond breaking reactions of methane on the metal-dioxide surfaces, the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method$^{44,114,115}$ as implemented in VASP through the VTST-Tools $^{133}$ was used. The spring constant between adjacent images was set to 5.0 eV/Å. A quasi-Newton algorithm $^{134}$ implemented in VASP was adopted for the geometry optimization of all of the NEB images.

pCOHP and PDOS curves are generated with LOBSTER $2.2.0^{75-77}$ and visualized with the help of wxDragon 2.1.3. $^{135}$ For the purpose of the projection of wave functions, we used the basis set given by Bunge et al. $^{136}$ for CrO$_2$ and the pbeVASPFit2015 basis set $^{77}$ for IrO$_2$ and $\beta$-PtO$_2$. We confirmed that the charge spilling value is always lower than 1%, which suggests that LOBSTER can transfer more than 99% of the charge density from the VASP-generated plane waves into the local orbitals.

All molecular DFT calculations were done by using the Gaussian 09 software, $^{137}$ with the B3LYP functional $^{138-140}$ and the 6-31G(d) basis sets. $^{141}$ ChemCraft $^{142}$ software was used for visualization of the Gaussian-calculated Kohn–Sham orbitals.

Extended Hückel calculations for the cluster models cut out from the surfaces were carried out with the YAeHMOP program. $^{82}$ The isosurfaces of extended Hückel MOs were calculated by using Atomistix ToolKit 11.8.2. $^{143}$ FMO diagrams were drawn with Viewkel. $^{144}$ The parameters used for the extended Hückel calculations are tabulated in the SI.
