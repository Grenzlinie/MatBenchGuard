## ABSTRACT

In this paper, a composite oxide catalyst consisting of NiO and ZnO was prepared by homogeneous precipitation method, which was used to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) to remove bisphenol A (BPA) in water. 95.26% BPA removal and 67.11% total organic carbon (TOC) removal were obtained under the optimum condition. Characterization and theoretical calculations (DFT) of the catalyst proved that electron rearrangement occurred in oxygen vacancy modified heterojunction catalysts. It resulted in the formation of acidic sites (NiO) and alkaline sites (oxygen vacancies) on the surface of the catalyst, thus promoting the adsorption and activation of PMS. According to the quenching experiments, a typical surface-bound radical reaction system was proposed. The reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the system were surface-bond $SO_{4}^{--}$, surface-bond $^{\bullet}OH$ and $^{1}O_{2}$. Finally, the BPA degradation pathways and the activation mechanism of PMS were analyzed in detail.

### 1. Introduction

A large number of environmental hormones are released into the environment during the production and use of pesticides, hormone drugs, additives and plastic products. Bisphenol A (BPA), an important environmental hormone, is widely used in the manufacture of epoxy resin, polycarbonate, polyphenol oxygen and plastic products, which leads to the widespread distribution of BPA in surface water and groundwater environment [1]. Studies have shown that BPA has certain embryotoxicity and teratogenicity which can significantly increase the incidence of cancer [2,3]. In addition, BPA can cause endocrine disorder and precocious puberty in children [4]. Therefore, the removal of BPA has drawn more attraction from researchers in the field of environmental protection.

To solve this problem, some conventional methods have been used to remove BPA in water, such as adsorption [5], biodegradation [6], flocculation [7]. However, the difficult regeneration treatment of BPA regeneration solution limits the application of adsorption [8]. Meanwhile, the weak interaction between flocculant and BPA weakens the removal efficiency of flocculation [9]. Besides, the biodegradation is hindered by the refractory properties of BPA [10]. Based on these reasons, it is very important to develop an effective process for BPA removal from water.

Compared with the former methods, Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have faster and better degradation efficiency for water pollutants [11-15]. Therefore, AOPs have attracted considerable attention from researchers. Moreover, due to the asymmetric structure of PMS, some radicals with high redox potential are easy to be produced, such as sulfate radical $(SO_{4}^{\cdot-})$ and hydroxyl radical $(^{\circ} OH)$. These radicals have the advantages of non-selectivity and wide pH application range, which has great potential in wastewater treatment [16,17]. Based on the above reasons, PMS-based advanced oxidation processes have been widely concerned by researchers. Furthermore, people have paid increasing attention to environmental and friendly activation methods of PMS for organic pollutant elimination. Compared with homogeneous catalytic process, the application of heterogeneous catalysts would produce less secondary pollution [18,19]. Thereby, the development of heterogeneous catalysts for efficient activation of PMS has become the focus of current researchers.

NiO, a transition metal oxide, has been used to activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) to degrade organic pollutants in the aquatic environment [20]. Compared with the reported cobalt oxide catalysts, the reported NiO catalysts do not have a strong advantage in activating PMS. Therefore, it is of great significance to prepare NiO catalyst with high efficiency which can match the typical Co-based catalyst. ZnO, an-type semiconductor material with excellent optical, physical, chemical and electrical properties, can promote electron transfer and enhance the performance of the catalyst [21,22]. Besides, ZnO is easy to generate oxygen vacancy to promote charge redistribution [23]. The above advantages imply that ZnO is a promising electronic assistant. Therefore, it might be an effective way to use ZnO to regulate the charge distribution and promote the electron transfer between NiO and PMS.

In this work, NiO-ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized by homogeneous precipitation method. The catalytic performance of NiO-ZnO nanoparticles was evaluated by BPA degradation test. First, the mole ratio of Ni to Zn in composites and the calcination temperature were optimized. Then, the optimal parameters, including catalyst dosage, PMS concentration, initial solution pH, initial concentration of BPA and reaction temperature, were also obtained by single factor control method. The effects of co-existing inorganic ions $(Cl^{-}, H_{2}PO_{4}^{-}, NO_{3}^{-}$ and $HCO_{3}^{-})$ were discussed. Meanwhile, cycle test and degradation test of trace BPA in real water were carried out. Besides, the major reactive oxygen species (ROS) were identified by quenching test and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). The degradation pathways of BPA were also elucidated by the analysis of intermediates. Finally, the activation mechanism of PMS and the characteristics of the catalyst were clarified according to the characterization and DFT calculations.

## 2. Experimental

### 2.1. Chemicals and reagents

Chemicals and reagents required for the study are listed in the supporting documents (Supporting Information (SI) TEXT S1.1).

### 2.2. Preparation of the catalyst

Homogeneous precipitation method was used to prepare NiO-ZnO nanoparticle catalysts [24]. In brief, 0.02 mol $NiSO_{4}\cdot6H_{2}O$, 0.02 mol $ZnSO_{4}\cdot7H_{2}O$ and 0.80 mol urea were dissolved in 500 mL deionized water (the molar ratio of urea to metal salt was 20). Then, the salt solution was placed in a beaker sealed with plastic film, stirred and refluxed in a water bath at $90\ ^{\circ}C$ for 13 h. After that, pale green precipitation, obtained through filtration and drying, was calcined at a specified temperature for 3 h in a muffle furnace. Finally, the nanoparticle catalyst was synthesized (Fig. 1). Additionally, the pure NiO or pure ZnO catalyst involved in the control experiment was prepared according to the above methods, only without the addition of $ZnSO_{4}\cdot7H_{2}O$ or $NiSO_{4}\cdot6H_{2}O$.

### 2.3. Experiment procedure

The batch experiment was carried out in a glass beaker (250 mL) in a water bath (constant temperature $30\ \pm\ 1\ ^{\circ}C$). The process of each test was as follows: 200 mL of BPA stock solution $(10\ mg\cdot L^{-1}$ and the initial pH is 6.5) prepared in advance was added into the glass beaker. Then, the required amount of catalyst and PMS was added into the solution in turn and reacted in continuous stirring (300 rpm) for 30 min. During the reaction, 2 mL sample was taken out and filtered through a PTFE syringe filter disc $(0.45\ \mu m)$ at a given time interval. Also, 100 uL sodium thiosulfate $(Na_{2}S_{2}O_{3}, 0.10\ mM)$ was injected into the samples to scavenge free radicals. All the experiment was carried out three times. The data in the figure was average, and the error bar represented the standard deviation of the average.

### 2.4. Characterization and analytical method

Characterization and analytical method are listed in the supporting documents (SI TEXT S1.2-S1.3).

### 2.5. DFT calculations

To further understand the properties of catalyst and the activation mechanism of PMS, DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4). The projector-augmented wave (PAW) was used to describe the electron-ion interactions, and the generalized gradient approximation functional (GGA-PBE) was applied to describe the electron exchange and correlation [25,26]. The cutoff energy and Gaussian smearing width were set to 400 eV and 0.10 eV, respectively. The Brillouin zone was sampled with a $3\ \times\ 3\ \times\ 1$ K points. All structures and energy were allowed to relax below $0.01\ eV\cdot\AA^{-1}$. Considering the antiferromagnetic properties of NiO, spin polarization was applied. To better describe the strong electron correlation in the localized Ni 3d states, strong on-site Coulomb repulsion parameter U and the screened exchange interaction parameter J (U = 6.30 eV and J = 1.00 eV) were introduced [27]. In addition, when discussing the adsorption of PMS on the surface of catalyst, the adsorption energy $(E_{ads})$ was defined as follows [13]:

$$
E_{ads}=E_{sum}-(E_{Ni15Zn85-500}+E_{PMS}) \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{sum}$, $E_{Ni15Zn85-500}$ and $E_{PMS}$ denote the total energy of the adsorption complex, pure $E_{Ni15Zn85-500}$ and the isolated molecule (PMS), respectively.

## 3. Results and discussion

In the early stage of the experiment, the preparation parameters of the catalyst, including the molar ratio of Ni/Zn and calcination temperature, were optimized (SI Text S2). Finally, the catalyst with Ni/Zn molar ratio of 15:85 and calcined at $500\ ^{\circ}C$ was selected as the research object to carry out the following experiments $(Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500)$.

### 3.1. Characteristics of the prepared $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$

#### 3.1.1. The phase, morphology and structure of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$

X-ray diffraction was used to analyze the phase of the prepared $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$. According to the analysis of XRD data (Fig. 2(a)), the diffraction peaks at $31.8^{\circ}, 34.4^{\circ}, 36.3^{\circ}, 47.5^{\circ}, 56.6^{\circ}, 62.9^{\circ}, 66.4^{\circ}$ and $67.9^{\circ}$ correspond to (1 1 0), (0 0 2), (1 0 1), (1 0 2), (1 1 0), (1 0 3), (2 0 0) and (1 1 2) crystal planes of ZnO (JCPDS card No.36-1451), respectively. It implies that ZnO with wurtzite structure has been detected in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$. Meanwhile, the diffraction peak at $43.2^{\circ}$, detected in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$, belongs to the (2 0 0) crystal plane of cubic NiO (JCPDS card No.78-0643). In addition, no other phase can be found.

To further confirm the structure and phase of the catalyst, the SEM

Furthermore, the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 has a larger specific surface area ($S_{BET}$, 62.70 $m^2·g^{-1}$), which is instrumental in providing more adsorption sites for PMS and promoting the catalytic reaction (Fig. S1(f)).

In addition, the EDS-mapping of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 was measured to study the distribution of elements in nano-catalysts (Fig. 2(i)). Moreover, the morphologies of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 after reaction (the used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500) were also analyzed by SEM (Fig. 2(e-f)). As observed, the morphology of the used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 is still a sheet formed by the sintering of nanoparticles. Combined with the metal leaching data shown in Fig. S1(c), it illustrates that the overall structure of the catalyst remains heterojunction structure due to less metal leaching, suggesting the good stability and reusability of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500.

### 3.1.2. The XPS of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used for the analysis of surface atomic composition and chemical state. The XPS survey spectra of the fresh and used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 reveal the presence of Ni 2p, Zn 2p, O 1s (Fig. 3(a)). The C 1s peak (binding energy relative to 284.60 eV) was used to obtain accurate spectra. Meanwhile, the core level spectra of Ni 2p, Zn 2p and O 1s were scanned in more detail to determine the charge states of elements in the fresh and used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. All XPS core level spectra were fitted using Shirley background. Fig. 3(b) displays the high resolution XPS scan of Zn 2p for the fresh and used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. The doublet spectral lines of Zn 2p are observed at the binding energies of 1021.20 eV (Zn 2p$_{3/2}$) and 1044.30 eV (Zn 2p$_{1/2}$) in the fresh and used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, which is consistent with $Zn^{2+}$ in ZnO [28]. In addition, as shown in Fig. 3(c), the detected O 1s peaks in the fresh $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 can be fitted into three components, which correspond to the lattice oxygen in NiO and ZnO (at 528.70 eV), the molecular oxygen adsorbed on surficial oxygen vacancies (at 531.20 eV), and the loosely bonded surface oxygen species on the surface (at 530.00 eV) [29-31]. Compared with the fresh $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, a new signal peak (at 533.20 eV) appears in O 1s core level of the used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, which belongs to the water molecules adsorbed on the surface of catalyst [32]. Additionally, Ni 2p high resolution XPS spectra of the fresh and used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 were obtained (Fig. 3(d)). The results reveal that Ni 2p peaks can be fixed into six peaks. The peaks at 853.20, 854.90 and 860.40 eV can be attributed to Ni 2p$_{3/2}$ and its satellite peaks, while the peaks at 870.60, 872.50 and 878.50 eV belong to Ni 2p$_{1/2}$ and its satellite peaks, indicating the existence of $Ni^{2+}$ (853.20 eV, 860.40 eV, 872.50 eV and 870.60 eV) and $Ni^{3+}$ (at 854.90 eV and 873.50 eV) in the fresh and used $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 [31,33,34]. The above results infer that the presence of oxygen vacancies leads to charge rearrangement in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, which causes the formation of $Ni^{3+}$ ions. It might be beneficial to the activation of PMS [35].

### 3.1.3. The FTIR of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to distinguish the surface groups of catalysts (NiO, ZnO and $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500). As observed in Fig. 4(a), the absorption bands locating at 3450 $cm^{-1}$ and 1629 $cm^{-1}$ might correspond to the stretching mode and bending vibration of the hydroxyl groups in the adsorbed water molecules, respectively [36]. The absorption band at 500-800 $cm^{-1}$ could be attributed to the vibration mode of metal oxygen bond (Zn-O or Ni-O). The peak appearing at 1386 $cm^{-1}$ could be identified as the C═O vibration of polydentate carbonate species formed via the interaction of $CO_2$ with oxygen vacancy [37,38]. Furthermore, comparing with NiO and ZnO, there is a unique absorption peak in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 at 1119.7 $cm^{-1}$, which is due to the C═O stretching modes arising from the absorption of atmospheric $CO_2$ on the surface of the nanocrystals [39]. To sum up, it further proves the existence of oxygen vacancies on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, which can lead to the rearrangement of the charge distribution of the catalyst. The change might be beneficial to the activation of PMS [35].

### 3.1.4. The TPD of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500

To further analyze the adsorptive properties of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, $CO_2$-TPD and $NH_3$-TPD were carried out. As shown in Fig. 4(b), it can be seen from $CO_2$-TPD that with the increase of temperature, there are some $CO_2$ desorption peaks in 300-400 °C, 400-650 °C and 650-850 °C, which belong to the $CO_2$ signal peaks adsorbed by weak alkaline sites, moderate alkaline sites and strong alkaline sites on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, respectively [40]. It proves the existence of alkaline sites on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. According to FTIR results, oxygen vacancies on the surface of the catalyst are the main sites for $CO_2$ adsorption. In other words, oxygen vacancies act as a major basic site on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. Meanwhile, the $NH_3$-TPD profiles of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 show that a series of $NH_3$ desorption peaks appear in the temperature ranges of 200-350 °C, 350-650 °C and 650-830 °C, respectively, corresponding to the $NH_3$ desorption signals adsorbed at different acidic sites on the catalyst surface (Fig. 4(c)). Therefore, both acidic and alkaline sites exist on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500.

### 3.1.5. The Tafel scans of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500
The electron transfer activity of catalysts (NiO, ZnO and $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500) were obtained by measuring their free corrosion potential with Tafel polarization diagrams. As observed in Fig. 4(d), Tafel diagrams illustrate that the free corrosion potential of the pure NiO, the pure ZnO and the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 are 0.25 V, $-0.18$ V and $-0.17$ V, respectively. Normally, a more negative free corrosion potential corresponds to a higher electron transfer rate [41]. The data shows that both the pure ZnO and the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 have high electron transfer rates. In other words, the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 inherit the excellent electron transfer properties of ZnO. The excellent electron transport property might be one of the important reasons why the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 had a strong ability to activate PMS.

According to the above characterization results, it can be found that the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 is a type of mesoporous heterojunction catalyst, which is mainly composed of 20-nm-sized NiO and ZnO particles. TPD characterizations further confirm the presence of both alkaline and acidic sites on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. ### 3.2. Effects of operational parameters

#### 3.2.1. Effects of catalyst dosage
Fig. 5(a) displays the effect of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 dosage (0-60 mg$\cdot$L$^{-1}$) on BPA removal efficiency in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system. The data shows that BPA is difficult to be degraded by PMS alone (0.10 mM). However, after introducing the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, the residue rate of BPA decreases rapidly. When the catalyst dosage increases from 10 mg$\cdot$L$^{-1}$ to 60 mg$\cdot$L$^{-1}$, the BPA residue decreases from 69.53% to 7.73%. The results indicate that in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system, the ability of BPA removal in the presence of PMS alone can be neglected, and the rapid degradation of BPA is due to the excellent activation of PMS by the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. From Fig. S2(a), all experimental data could be fitted as pseudo-first order kinetic ($R^{2}>0.990$). With the increase of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 dosage, the $k_{obs}$ for BPA removal at 60 mg$\cdot$L$^{-1}$ $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 (0.128 min$^{-1}$) is almost 6.70 times than that at 10 mg$\cdot$L$^{-1}$ $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 (0.019 min$^{-1}$). The phenomenon could be explained that more catalyst dosage leads to more active sites in the system, which provides more sites for the adsorption and activation of PMS. It means that more PMS can be quickly converted into ROS to remove BPA [45].

#### 3.2.2. Effect of PMS dosage
The effect of PMS dosage (0-0.13 mM) on the removal efficiency of BPA was studied. As shown in Fig. 5(b), BPA removal is not obvious when only the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 was added, suggesting that the physical adsorption of BPA by $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 can be neglected. On the other hand, at a specific dosage of 50 mg$\cdot$L$^{-1}$ $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, the residue of BPA decreases from 99.88% to 3.32% with PMS dosage increasing from 0 to 0.13 mM, which infers that PMS is transformed to ROS under the action of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. When the total amount of PMS is less than 0.07 mM, the trend of data is parallel to abscissa after 20 min of treatment, suggesting that BPA is no longer degraded due to the complete consumption of PMS. Meanwhile, the $k_{obs}$ of BPA removal increases evidently from 0.045 to 0.074, 0.101, 0.117 and 0.136 min$^{-1}$ with PMS dose increasing from 0.03 to 0.05, 0.07, 0.10 and 0.13 mM (Fig. S2(b)). It can be understood that the dosage of PMS is the limiting factor for the production of ROS, and the increase of PMS dosage is beneficial to the interaction between $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 and PMS, which leads to more ROS to remove BPA [46].

### 3.2.4. Effect of initial BPA concentration
To investigate the effect of initial concentration of pollutants on removal efficiency, removal data of different BPA concentrations are shown in Fig. 5(d). The results illustrate that the removal efficiency of BPA decreases with the increase of initial concentration of BPA. When the initial concentration of BPA is $2.5\ \text{mg·L}^{-1}$, the removal efficiency of BPA is 99.54% in 10 min, while with the initial concentration of BPA increasing to $20\ \text{mg·L}^{-1}$, only 72.59% BPA can be removed in 30 min. It is attributed to that the limited amount of PMS and catalysts result in a limited number of ROS to remove BPA [50].

As shown in Fig. 5(e), with the increase of system temperature, the removal efficiency of BPA is accelerated. To compare the effect of reaction temperature on the removal efficiency of BPA more intuitively, $k_{\text{obs}}$ values were obtained by pseudo-first-order kinetics fitting of BPA degradation data (Fig. S2(d)). It can be seen that the $k_{\text{obs}}$ value increases from $0.080\ \text{min}^{-1}$ to $0.146\ \text{min}^{-1}$ as the system temperature increases from 20 °C to 50 °C. In other words, the elevated temperatures substantially facilitated PMS activation by the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. The result is consistent with the literature reports [51]. Besides, to eliminate the effect of thermal activation of PMS, a series of control experiments without catalyst were carried out. The $E_{a}$ value for BPA degradation is evaluated as 14.89 kJ·mol⁻¹ (Fig. 5f), which is close to the $E_{a}$ value of diffusion-controlled reactions (in the range of 10–13 kJ·mol⁻¹), indicating that the apparent reaction rate of BPA degradation is mainly dominated by the rate of mass transfer [52].

Through the discussion of the above experimental results, the optimum conditions for 10 mg·L⁻¹ BPA removal in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system are as follows: PMS = 0.10 mM, Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 = 30 mg·L⁻¹, the initial pH = 6.5, temperature = 30 °C.

### 3.3. Effect of co-exciting ions for BPA removal

Because there are various anions in the actual water, the effects of several typical anions (Cl⁻; H₂PO₄⁻; HCO₃⁻; NO₃⁻) on the degradation of BPA in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system were investigated ((SI) TEXT S3). As shown in the results, the presence of excessive Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻ and NO₃⁻ (≥5 mM) reduces the removal efficiency of BPA, because excessive anions can react with PMS to produce some low-activity species [53]. However, the removal efficiency of BPA will be reduced at lower concentration of $H_{2}PO_{4}{}^{-}$ (≤0.5 mM), because $H_{2}PO_{4}{}^{-}$ can complex with Ni (II) on the surface of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500, which inhibits the contact between PMS and Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500, resulting in deactivation of catalyst [54].

### 3.4. Control experiments

To further clarify the catalytic effect of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500, seven control experiments including (a) the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 alone system, (b) the PMS alone system, (c) the NiO/PMS system, (d) the ZnO/PMS system, (e) the Ni²⁺/PMS system, (f) the Zn²⁺/PMS system, and (g) the alkali-activated PMS system (PMS-pH-11 system) were carried out. In homogeneous contrast system, the amount of Ni²⁺ and Zn²⁺ ions added was equal to the amount of Ni²⁺ and Zn²⁺ leached from Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 under the optimum conditions, respectively. In addition, the initial pH of the solution was adjusted to 11 by NaOH in the alkali-activated PMS system. As shown in Fig. 6(a), BPA could hardly be removed in the systems of Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 alone, PMS alone, ZnO/PMS, Ni²⁺/PMS and Zn²⁺/PMS, while the residual rate of BPA after 30 min is 83.19% and 90.26% in the NiO/PMS and PMS-pH-11 system, respectively. It is far from the BPA residual rate (4.74%) in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system. The experimental results are analyzed as follows: (i) The physical adsorption of BPA by the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 can be neglected. Since the little dosage of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 leads to less BPA adsorption. Meanwhile, according to the dissociation coefficient of BPA (pKa₁ = 9.6; pKa₂ = 10.2), BPA is in molecular state under acidic condition. However, the surface of catalyst is positively charged at pH < 7. It means that there is no strong electrostatic adsorption between BPA and catalyst. Besides, the weak selective adsorption of BPA by the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 may also be the reason for the phenomenon [55]. (ii) BPA is hardly oxidized by PMS alone, but BPA is slightly degraded in the alkali-activated PMS system. It can be seen that even though alkali can activate PMS to produce ROS, the removal efficiency of BPA is much less than that of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500. The result further proves that the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 has excellent catalytic performance in a wide range of initial pH values (5-11). (iii) PMS can't be activated by Ni²⁺ and Zn²⁺ in homogeneous system to remove BPA. (iv) ZnO could hardly catalyze the decomposition of PMS to produce ROS, but NiO has a weak ability to activate PMS, suggesting that the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 activates PMS to produce ROS through the valence change of Ni element. It is consistent with the enhancement of BPA degradation performance with the increase of NiO content (Fig S1(a)). Therefore, the above experimental results infer that the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 has a good ability to activate PMS. Compared with the catalysts studied in other literature, the turnover frequency (TOF) of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system is 2.34 min⁻¹ (Table S3), which proves that the degradation of BPA by PMS activated with the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 is better than that of most catalysts [56]. In addition, the stability and reusability of the catalysts are shown in Fig. S5, after four cycles, the removal efficiency of BPA is 80.66%. Combined with the results of SEM and XPS, it can be concluded that the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 has excellent stability and catalytic activity.

### 3.5. Mineralization efficiency and PMS residues

TOC and PMS residues in heterogeneous catalytic systems are discussed (Fig. 6(b-c)). The results illustrate that the PMS residue and TOC data of each system could match well. The PMS residual rates of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS, the NiO/PMS, the ZnO/PMS and the PMS alone systems were 14.54%, 81.40%, 93.08% and 100%, respectively. Their corresponding mineralization efficiencies were 67.11%, 11.43%, 0.28% and 0.00%, respectively. It is also consistent with the trend of BPA degradation data in the control experiment. The results infer that the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 has an excellent activation ability for PMS, while NiO or ZnO has a limited activation ability for PMS. Thereby, 67.11% TOC removal was obtained under the optimum condition in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system.

From the above discussion, the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 has excellent PMS activation ability, and the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system has high BPA mineralization efficiency.

### 3.6. Practical application

To understand the application of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system in practical water, a series of experiments were carried out to study the degradation of BPA with low concentration ($1\ \text{mg·L}^{-1}$) in river water, lake water and tap water. The samples of lake water and tap water were taken from Sichuan University, and river water are taken from Fuhe River in Chengdu. The characteristics of various water matrices are shown in Table S4. Trace BPA in the samples is detected by high performance liquid chromatography equipped with fluorescence detector. The mobile phase for the analysis of BPA consisted of a mixture of acetonitrile (C) and ultrapure water (A) in a certain volume ratio (A:C = 50%:50%). The fluorescence detector is set at 276/305 nm (excitation wavelength/emission wavelength) [57]. Compared with the degradation of BPA in deionized water, the removal rate of BPA in river water and lake water is slower. It might be due to the fact that there are amount of other organic substances in practical water, which could compete with BPA for ROS (Fig. S6). But BPA in different water bodies can be completely removed in 30 min. It infers that $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 can effectively catalyze the decomposition of PMS to remove BPA in practical water.

### 3.7. Mechanism discussion of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system

#### 3.7.1. Discussion of DFT calculations

According to the above material characterization, the prepared $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 is a type of heterojunction catalyst composed of NiO and ZnO, and its surface contains a large number of oxygen vacancies. It has been reported in the literature that ZnO is prone to generate abundant oxygen vacancies [23]. Meanwhile, the content of Ni in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 is low, and NiO is highly dispersed on the surface of ZnO. Therefore, a smaller NiO slab model loads on a larger ZnO slab model with oxygen vacancies to simulate the prepared $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 in this study. At same time, the crystal plane with the strongest signal in XRD was chosen to discuss the catalytic properties. Thereby, $1\times1\times2$ supercell of NiO (2 0 0) and $3\times5\times2$ supercell of ZnO (1 0 1) in 15 Å vacuum layer were established to do the following research. Generally, an oxygen atom was removed from the surface of the ZnO slab model to simulate the existence of oxygen vacancies (the oxygen vacancy density is 0.74%) [58]. After that, the loading sites of NiO on oxygen vacancy modified ZnO slab model were optimized, and the slab model with the lowest binding energy was selected for charge density difference analysis and bader charge analysis.

The results of charge density difference of the slab models show that the formation of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 causes the rearrangement of charge between ZnO and NiO (Fig. 7a) [59,60]. It can be seen that a part of electrons accumulates at the interface between NiO and ZnO, while Ni atoms are almost in the state of electron depletion, and the average bond length of Ni-O bond decreases from 2.06 Å to 1.92 Å. It indicates that the interface interaction between NiO and ZnO is strong, which leads to obvious electron transfer and bond length changes. To further explore the specific quantities of charge transfer, the bader charge of the system was analyzed [61]. The results are shown in Table S5. All Ni atoms lose electrons, with the number of electrons lost being 1.03, 1.13, 1.11 and 1.11, respectively. It is consistent with the charge difference distribution that Ni atoms are in the state of electron depletion. Besides, the total number of electrons gain and loss for all atoms in NiO is $-0.61$. In other words, the strong interaction between NiO and ZnO causes the transfer of some electrons from NiO to ZnO. Combining the charge density difference and the bader charge results, the lost electrons in NiO can be transferred to O atoms around oxygen vacancies in ZnO at contact interface, leading to the formation of electron cloud around those O atoms. Therefore, DFT calculations of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 show that oxygen vacancy modified ZnO interacts strongly with the NiO loaded near the oxygen vacancy, which causes some electrons in NiO to transfer to the O atom of ZnO. It results in the formation of the electron dissipation centers (NiO) and electron accumulation centers (O atom around oxygen vacancy on ZnO). It is consistent with the previous characterizations that the electron dissipation centers and the electron accumulation centers correspond to the acidic and alkaline sites on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500, respectively. It is well known that the acidic sites are in the state of electron dissipation and easy to adsorb electronegative $\text{HSO}_5^-$, which is good for the adsorption and activation of PMS [62].

Furthermore, to discuss the activation mechanism of PMS on the prepared catalyst, the adsorption model of PMS based on the above slab model was established. The adsorption energy ($E_{ads}$) is $-1.57$ eV, indicating that there is a strong adsorption between PMS and the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500. The bond length of peroxide O-O bond in PMS molecule increases from 1.35 Å to 1.44 Å, which implies that the peroxide O-O bond is easier to break when PMS contacts with the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 (Fig. 7(b)). Meanwhile, the charge difference distribution between PMS and the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 is shown in Fig. 7(c). It can be found that there is electron cloud around O atoms in PMS, which indicates that these O atoms are in the state of charge accumulation, while the Ni atoms at the adsorption interface are in the state of charge depletion. Moreover, the results of bader charge show that the overall charge gain and loss of PMS is 0.52. In other words, the adsorption of PMS on the catalyst surface could obtain a part of electrons, which makes the peroxide O-O bond longer and easier broken [63].

From the above DFT calculations, the formation mechanism of radicals is further elucidated: when PMS is adsorbed on the catalyst surface, some electrons are easily transferred to PMS, which makes the length of the peroxide O-O bond longer and is beneficial for the formation of $^\text{·}\text{OH}$ or $\text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}$.

#### 3.7.2. Radical scavenging experiment

To determine the main ROS of BPA degradation in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system, a series of quenching experiments were carried out. Because MeOH has fast reaction rate constants with $^\text{·}\text{OH}$ or $\text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}$ ($k(\text{MeOH}, ^\text{·}\text{OH}) = 9.70\times10^8\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$; $k(\text{MeOH}, \text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}) = 3.20\times10^7\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$), it is often used as probe compound for the total flux of $^\text{·}\text{OH}$ and $\text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}$ [64]. At same time, literature has reported that TBA has the different reaction rate constants with $^\text{·}\text{OH}$ or $\text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}$ ($k(\text{TBA}, ^\text{·}\text{OH}) = (3.80-7.60)\times10^8\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$; $k(\text{TBA}, \text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}) = (4.00-9.10)\times10^5\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$), which could be used to distinguish $^\text{·}\text{OH}$ from $\text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}$ [65]. In addition, $p$-BQ is chosen as the scavenger for $\text{O}_2^{^\text{·}-}$ ($k(p\text{-BQ}, \text{O}_2^{^\text{·}-}) = (0.90-1.00)\times10^8\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$) [66]. FFA and $\text{NaN}_3$ are chosen as the scavenger for $^1\text{O}_2$ ($k(\text{FFA}, ^1\text{O}_2) = 1.20\times10^8\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$; $k(\text{NaN}_3, ^1\text{O}_2) = 1\times10^9\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$), respectively [66,67]. From Fig. 8(a), the quenching efficiency of MeOH and TBA for the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system is not obvious even if the concentration of MeOH and TBA increases from 50 mM to 500 mM. But $p$-BQ has remarkable quenching effect. When the concentration of $p$-BQ is 5 mM, only 5.13% of BPA is removed. Meanwhile, BPA degradation is inhibited with the addition of FFA or $\text{NaN}_3$. Therefore, the results of the quenching experiments infer that the main ROS in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system is $\text{O}_2^{^\text{·}-}$ and $^1\text{O}_2$.

To further verify the main ROS in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system, EPR spectroscopy was conducted with DMPO and TEMP as the spin trap agents. From Fig. 8(b), the signals of DMPO-OH adduct, DMPO-$\text{SO}_4$ adducts and DMPO-$\text{O}_2^-$ adducts are not detected. But a typical three-line EPR spectrum with equal intensities is detected, which represents the adduct signal of TEMP-$^1\text{O}_2$ [68]. It indicates that $^1\text{O}_2$ existed in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system, but $^\text{·}\text{OH}$, $\text{SO}_4^{^\text{·}-}$ and $\text{O}_2^{^\text{·}-}$ don't exist in the system. Besides, the EPR signal of TEMP-$^1\text{O}_2$ is very weak. Therefore, to further confirm whether the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system is dominated by $^1\text{O}_2$, the degradation experiment of BPA in $\text{D}_2\text{O}$ was carried out [69]. The results imply that the degradation of BPA in $\text{D}_2\text{O}$ is not much different from that in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Fig. 8(f)). Based on the above discussion, it can be concluded that only $^1\text{O}_2$ exists in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500/PMS system, but it is not dominant. The result is different from the previous quenching results.

Therefore, Nitro Blue Tetrazolium (NBT) was used as reagent to qualitatively analyze the presence of O₂˙⁻ in the solution [70]. The results display that there is no absorption peak at 560 nm of NBT reaction product, which means that the reaction product have not been detected (Fig. 8(c)). Generally, the EPR detection of O₂˙⁻ is in the condition of pure methanol. In other words, O₂˙⁻ can exist in the pure methanol system, thus leading to the degradation of organic contaminants [71]. Therefore, BPA degradation experiment was carried out in pure methanol system to determine the existence of O₂˙⁻. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the results indicate that BPA is hardly degraded in pure methanol system. Combined with the results of NBT, it can be concluded that there is no O₂˙⁻ in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system. Moreover, Atrazine (ATZ) and benzoic acid (BA) are probes for ˙OH and SO₄˙⁻, respectively. In other words, ATZ and BA could not be degraded by ¹O₂ [72]. From Fig. 8(d), after 60 min of treatment, the residual rate of ATZ and BA is 17.37% and 36.22%, respectively. Their physical adsorption effect could be negligible. The results suggest that there are ˙OH and SO₄˙⁻ besides ¹O₂ in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system. Moreover, the residual PMS was detected after 30 min of treatment in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system without BPA. Fig. 8(e) shows that after 30 min of coexistence with the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500, PMS almost completely decomposed ([PMS]₃₀ min/[PMS]₀ < 0.07). It indicates that the decomposition of PMS to produce ROS does not require contaminants to provide electrons. Thereby, mediated electron transfer system, which gets electrons from contaminants, is excluded.

In the light of literature, surface-bound active species might be the reason why scavengers do not inhibit the reaction between free radicals and substrates very well. It has been reported that surface-bound active species could only react with substrates adsorbed on or near the surface of the catalysts [73,74]. Phenol, as a hydrophobic radical scavenger, is often used to quench the surface-bound radicals [75]. From Fig. 8(a), the presence of 10 mM phenol results in a drop on BPA removal by 66.07%. Meanwhile, with the increase of phenol concentration, the removal efficiency of BPA is further reduced. When the concentration of phenol is 50 mM, the removal efficiency of BPA is only 9.51%. It infers that the main ROS in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system is surface-bound active radicals, and BPA oxidation occurs on the catalyst surface. In other words, surface mass transfer rate will be the key step to determine the apparent reaction rate. It is consistent with the result that the system has a smaller activation energy ($E_a = 14.89\ \text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$).

All above, the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system is a typical surface-bound radical system. It might be attributed to the strong adsorption capacity of acidic sites on the surface of the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500 for PMS ($E_{ads} = -$ 1.57 eV). Therefore, the ROS in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system could be summarized as follows: (i) The existence of surface-bound SO₄˙⁻ and surface-bound ˙OH could be demonstrated by degradation of probe substances (ATZ and BA). (ii) EPR analysis infer the existence of a small amount of ¹O₂.

Based on the above research, the possible catalytic pathway is proposed: First, the redox reaction in the Ni₁₅Zn₈₅-500/PMS system is initiated by adding PMS. Subsequently, electrons generated by the valence change of Ni (Ni(II) → Ni(III)) rapidly transfer to HSO₅⁻ adsorbed on the surface of NiO, resulting in the production of surface-bound active radicals and ¹O₂. Meanwhile, BPA adsorbed on or near the catalyst surface is oxidized by these ROS. The relevant radical reactions and the mechanism can be proposed as shown in Eqs. (2-6) [48,76,77]:

$$\text{Ni(II)} \rightarrow \text{Ni(III)} + \text{e}^- \tag{2}$$

$$\text{HSO}_5^- + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{SO}_4^{\cdot -} + \text{HO}^- \tag{3}$$

$$\text{SO}_4^{\cdot -} + \text{HO}^- \rightarrow \cdot\text{OH} + \text{SO}_4^{2-} \tag{4}$$

$$\text{Ni(III)} + \text{HSO}_5^- \rightarrow \text{Ni(II)} + \text{H}^+ + 2\text{SO}_5^{\cdot -} \tag{5}$$

$$\text{SO}_5^{\cdot -} + 2\text{H}_2\text{ O} \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4\text{SO}_4^{2-} + 3^1\text{O}_2 \tag{6}$$

## 4. Conclusion

In this study, a kind of mesoporous heterojunction catalyst $(Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500)$, composed of 20-nm-sized NiO and ZnO composite oxide particles, was prepared by homogeneous precipitation method. Characterizations (XPS, FTIR, $CO_2$-TPD, $NH_3$-TPD, etc.) and theoretical calculations prove that the oxygen vacancy modified heterojunction catalyst produces electron rearrangement, thus forming the acid center on the surface of the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$. It is beneficial to the adsorption and activation of PMS. Moreover, when PMS is adsorbed on the catalyst surface, some electrons are easily transferred to PMS, which is beneficial to the formation of $SO_4^{.-}$. Due to the large adsorption energy $(E_{ads} = -1.57$ eV) and the small activation energy $(E_a = 14.89\ \text{kJ·mol}^{-1})$, a typical surface-bound radical system has been proposed. Moreover, the experimental results indicated that the ROS in the system is surface-bound $SO_4^{.-}$, surface-bound $^·OH$ and $^1O_2$. The BPA degradation (95.26%) and total organic carbon (TOC) removal (67.11%) are obtained in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$/PMS system under the operating condition: BPA = $10\ \text{mg·L}^{-1}$, PMS = 0.10 mM, $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}$-500 = $30\ \text{mg·L}^{-1}$, the initial pH = 6.5, the temperature = $30\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Finally, according to the detected results of LC-QTOF-MS/MS, BPA can be degraded through three degradation pathways in the $Ni_{15}Zn_{85}-500$/PMS system. This work provides an efficient and stable catalyst for the removal of pollutants in water.
