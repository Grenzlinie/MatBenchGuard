## ABSTRACT

The electronic metal-support interaction (EMSI) has a very profound impact on the catalytic activity of supported catalysts. In this paper, density functional theory calculations were utilized to understand the effect of EMSI between Ni cluster (Ni₄) and three substrates (CeO₂, TiO₂, and BNO) on the catalytic hydrogenation of ethylene. When Ni cluster was anchored on the surface of support, visibly electron transfer from Ni to substrate was observed, leading to positively charged Ni clusters with the sequence of Ni₄/TiO₂ > Ni₄/CeO₂ > Ni₄/BNO. During the ethylene hydrogenation reaction, the electron rich Ni₄/BNO exhibits superior performance in activating of both H₂ and ethylene molecules, while the electron deficient Ni₄/TiO₂ shows the worst activation among the three catalysts. As a consequence, the activation energies of ethylene hydrogenation follow the same sequence of the charge state on the supported Ni clusters at both low and high H coverages. Our results suggest that the EMSI between metal nanoparticles and support materials is capable of tuning the charge state and electron density of supported metals, which further determines the activity of the catalyst. This work provides a potential approach for design new catalysts with desired activity by utilizing appropriate support materials.

## 1. Introduction

Transition metal nanoparticles (TMNPs) are widely utilized as active catalysts to promote the hydrogenation reactions in petroleum and chemical industry [1-3]. However, the TMNPs suffer from irreversible agglomeration due to their small particle size and high surface energy, leading to severe performance degradation [4-6]. Anchoring TMNPs on the surface of porous support materials is one of the most effective approach to handle the aggregation issue [7,8].

Generally, the support materials should contain several active sites to interact with metal particles via strong metal-support interactions (SMSI). As an inert component, support materials often do not directly participate in the catalytic reaction, but they can significantly affect the catalytic performance of the supported metal particles via SMSI [9-12]. For instance, Kim et al. [13] studied the support effect on the catalytic performance of Pd. Their results suggest that Pd supported on CeO₂, SnO₂ and TiO₂ surface could deliver much higher catalytic performance than that of Co₃O₄ and Mn₂O₃. Besides the component, the structure variation of the support materials may also change the performance of catalyst. Panpanot et al. [14,15] employed a series of TiO₂ substrate with different particle size to serve as support of Pd catalyst for phenylacetylene hydrogenation. As the size of TiO₂ increases, the catalytic activity of Pd increases first and then decreases. Ewing et al. [16] studied the adsorption behavior of Pt clusters on silica substrate by density functional theory (DFT) simulation. It was found that the electron density of Pt could be affected by the Pt-O bonds formed between Pt particles and the silica surface, revealing that there are electronic interactions between metal and support. Obviously, with the variation of electronic structure of metal particles, their catalytic performance would be inevitably changed accordingly [17,18]. In fact, a combined experimental and theoretical study by Bruix et al. [19] clearly confirmed the existence of electronic metal-support interaction (EMSI) between Pt and ceria, which could change the charge distribution of Pt and significantly enhances its activity for water dissociation.

Recently, a number of studies have shown that the metal catalytic activity is highly correlated to their electronic state [20-22]. Gates et al.

[24] synthesized a series of Pt based single-atom catalysts with various supporting materials (i.e. Co₃O₄, CeO₂, ZrO₂, graphene). Qu [25] reported that the EMSI interaction between Pt nanorods and CeO₂ can enhance the electron density of Pt, which exhibits superior hydrogenation activity for quinoline even at room temperature.

Obviously, systematic understanding of the EMSI between metal and support is of great importance for design and develop of new catalyst with suitable metal-support combination for a certain catalytic process. In this paper, we conducted a series of theoretical studies to address the EMSI between Ni cluster, a typical active metal in hydrogenation process, and various supports. Here, the BNO, a recently reported two-dimensional substrate [26-28], and two representative oxides [29-31], CeO₂ and TiO₂, were employed as support materials. In order to address the substrate effect on the catalytic performance of Ni cluster, the hydrogenation of ethylene was selected as a model reaction, which has been widely studied as a probe to evaluate the hydrogenation activity of various metal catalysts [32-35]. Our results show that the hydrogenation activity of the three supported Ni catalysts follow the trend Ni₄/BNO > Ni₄/CeO₂ > Ni₄/TiO₂, which is highly correlated to the charge transfer between metal and substrate. It was found that the BNO substrate could retain the highest electron density of Ni, which promotes both the dissociation of H₂ and the activation of ethylene during the hydrogenation reaction.

## 2. Models and computational details

The BNO substrate was modeled as a monolayer BN with one N atom replaced by O atom, as in our previous work [26,36]. Since the π-π interaction is relatively weak between BNO layers, the thickness effect of the substrate is almost negligible (Table S1). The TiO₂(101) substrate [37,38] was cleaved as slab model from the optimized crystal structure of anatase TiO₂ with the most commonly used (101) facet. Similar procedure was applied to build CeO₂(111) substrate [39]. The optimized structure of the three substrates and the main geometric parameters were listed in the supporting information (Figure S1 and Table S2).

All calculations were conducted with a periodic DFT method as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [40]. A spin-polarized generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was integrated to deal with the exchange-correlation effects [41]. During the calculation, the valence electrons were treated by a plane-wave basis set with a cutoff energy of 400 eV, while the core-electron interaction was described by the projector augmented wave (PAW) method [42]. A 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh was utilized for sampling the Brillouin zone integration [43]. Calculations with higher k-point mesh of 4 × 4 × 1 were also performed as benchmarks, which shows only negligible changes in both energetics (less than 0.004 eV) and geometrics (less than 0.007 Å). Structural optimizations were performed using a self-consistent-field (SCF) with an energy tolerance of 10⁻⁴ eV. The nudged-elastic-band (NEB) algorithm was employed to locate the transition state structure along the prescribed reaction pathways. Typically, 6 images were calculated in between the initial and final states with a tolerance of 0.003 eV. The Bader division scheme of charge density was utilized to calculate atomic charges of the systems.

## 3. Result and discussion

### 3.1. Ni₄ cluster adsorption on substrate

According to our previous work [44], the smallest Ni₄ cluster with 3D configuration was selected as the active component in our simulations to simplify our calculation. The optimized adsorption structures of Ni₄ cluster on the surface of CeO₂(111), TiO₂(101) and BNO are summarized in Fig. 1. The main geometric parameters are also collected in Table S3. Generally, the Ni₄ cluster prefers the tetrahedral configuration on all the involving surfaces. In particular, three Ni atoms of the Ni₄ cluster were directly attached to the substrate, leading to strong anchoring strength between metal and support. For CeO₂(111) and TiO₂(101) substrates, the atop sites of oxygen on the surface were identified to be the most active sites for Ni atom to form Ni-O covalent bond. Since the O atoms on CeO₂(111) surface are highly exposed, the Ni₄ anchoring strength on CeO₂(111) surface (5.584 eV) is much stronger than that on TiO₂(101) (4.326 eV). In contrast, the O atoms of BNO substrate are somewhat embedded, leading to the low accessibility of O atoms. As a result, a slightly weak anchoring strength between BNO and Ni₄ was observed with the calculated adsorption energy of 4.211 eV. It is interesting to note that the most stable adsorption site for Ni₄ on BNO surface is not the atop site of O atom. Instead, the Ni atoms are attracted by the nearby B and N atoms, forming three Ni-B bonds and three Ni-N bonds. As reported in our previous work [36], such a special adsorption configuration could be ascribed to the O heteroatom on the substrate, which destroys the strong conjugation of BN plane. As a consequence, the involving B and N atoms could adopt sp³ hybridization to facilitate the bond formation with Ni atoms. The average bond length between Ni₄ and substrate is 1.820 Å, 1.903 Å, 2.018 Å for CeO₂(111), TiO₂(101), BNO, respectively, in line with the adsorption strength. Here after, we directly use CeO₂ and TiO₂ to represent CeO₂(111) and TiO₂(101), respectively.

Bader charge analysis was then carried out to understand the charge transfer upon Ni adsorption. As shown in Fig. 1, the Ni₄ cluster would donate moderate electrons to the CeO₂ (0.177 e) and TiO₂ (0.210 e) substrate via the Ni-O bond interactions. In contrast, a much lower electron transfer of 0.031 e was observed between Ni₄ and BNO. Based on the calculated secondary differential charge distribution (Figure S2), the entire Ni cluster would donate its electrons to CeO₂ and TiO₂. However, only the three Ni atoms at the bottom lose electrons to BNO substrate, while the Ni atom at the top remains charge neutral. As a result, the Ni₄ cluster anchored on BNO surface preserves more electrons, which should be beneficial to the catalytic hydrogenation reaction [45-47].

### 3.2. H₂ and C₂H₄ adsorption on the supported Ni₄ cluster

We now consider the dissociative chemisorption of H₂ on the supported Ni₄ cluster, which is recognized as the starting step of C₂H₄ hydrogenation. The structures along the dissociation process of H₂ on Ni₄/CeO₂, Ni₄/TiO₂ and Ni₄/BNO catalysts were displayed in Fig. 2, and the energetics along the reaction were projected in Fig. 3. For Ni₄/CeO₂ (Fig. 2a), the H₂ molecule initially attaches to the topmost Ni atom with a weak physisorption energy of 0.417 eV (Fig. 3), leading to slight activation of H₂ molecule as evidenced by the elongated H—H distance. Subsequently, the anti-bonding orbital of H₂ receives the electrons from d-orbital of Ni to break the H—H bond. As a result, the H—H bond length increases to 1.398 Å at transition state and finally reaches to 3.164 Å at the product state. ### 3.3. $C_2H_4$ hydrogenation

Generally, the hydrogenation of $C_2H_4$ could undergo two possible reaction pathways, the Eley-Rideal (ER) route [51,52] or the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (LH) route [53]. Since the adsorption energy of $H_2$ is comparable with $C_2H_4$, the two molecules should be both adsorbed on the catalyst, indicating that the hydrogenation reaction should follow the LH route. Fig. 6 displays the structures of the reactant (R), transition state (TS) and product (P) of the hydrogenation reaction over the three involved catalysts. The hydrogenation process is virtually identical on all the three catalysts. Before the hydrogenation reaction, the ethylene molecule should be firstly adsorbed on the catalyst with two H atoms been attached. During the reaction, the two H atoms simultaneously migrate from $Ni_4$ cluster to the C atoms of ethylene, forming two new C—H bonds to break the original C—Ni bonds. In the meantime, the $C=C$ double bond of ethylene is gradually converted to C—C single bond, leading to the formation of ethane. For instance, when the ethylene is adsorbed on $Ni_4/CeO_2$ surface (Fig. 6a), the $C=C$ bond is slightly increased to 1.396 Å, which completely converted to C—C at the final state with the bond length of 1.547 Å. Two new C—H bonds are formed with the calculated average bond distance of 1.158 Å, in accordance with the typical C—H bond length in ethane. At the transition state, the $C=C$ bond length is elongated by 0.177 Å with respect to the value of pristine ethylene (1.333 Å), revealing the fact that the $C_2H_4$ molecule is significantly activated by the $Ni_4/CeO_2$ catalyst (Fig. 6a). Interestingly, the two migrating H atoms are not symmetric at the transition state, where one H atom is closer to $C_2H_4$ than another. Similar activation could also be observed for $Ni_4/TiO_2$ and $Ni_4/BNO$ with the $C=C$ bond length increases to 1.510 Å and 1.513 Å, respectively. Our results imply that a strong interaction between $C_2H_4$ and the catalyst would significantly activate the $C=C$ bond of ethylene, leading to a lower hydrogenation barrier. Indeed, the calculated activation barriers follow a sequence of $Ni_4/TiO_2$ (1.037 eV) $> Ni_4/CeO_2$ (0.824 eV) $> Ni_4/BNO$ (0.798 eV), which confirms that the activation of $C=C$ bond is the key step in the hydrogenation reaction (Fig. 7). However, the hydrogenation of ethylene is an endothermic process with the reaction energy of 0.623 eV, 0.417 eV and 0.560 eV for $Ni_4/TiO_2$, $Ni_4/CeO_2$ and $Ni_4/BNO$, respectively. Here, the high reaction energy could be ascribed to the strong interaction between H and $Ni_4$ (Fig. 3), which leads to additional energy consumption in breaking the H—Ni bonds. After the hydrogenation reaction, the generated ethane should undergo a facile desorption process, which needs relatively low desorption energy ranging 0.21~0.22 eV, to release the active sites of catalysts for other ethylene molecules.

Since the ethylene hydrogenation process is virtually identical on all the three catalysts, we only describe the reaction on $Ni_4$/BNO catalyst, which exhibits the lowest reaction barrier at low H coverage (i.e. 2 H atoms). Although most active sites of $Ni_4$ have been occupied by the H atoms, the ethylene molecule could still be anchored and be activated on the $Ni_4H_6$ species via $\pi$-d interaction (Fig. 8c). After ethylene adsorption, the $C=C$ bond length of $C_2H_4$ molecule was elongated to $1.401\ Å$, which is slightly shorter than the value at low H coverage (Fig. 6c), implying the interaction between ethylene and the $Ni_4$/BNO should be somewhat weakened at high H coverage. Indeed, the adsorption energy of ethylene on $Ni_4$/BNO is decreased by 0.117 eV at high H coverage (Fig. 8). Similar to the low H coverage, the transition state structure at high H coverage also exhibits an asymmetric pattern. In particular, one fast migrating H atom breaks the Ni-H bond $(l_{Ni-H}=2.795\ Å)$ and approaches to the C atom of $C_2H_4$ with a short C—H distance of $1.194\ Å$, while another H atom moves somewhat slower with a relatively long C—H distance of $1.758\ Å$. Comparing with low H coverage, a relatively low activation barrier of 0.320 eV with a highly exothermic reaction energy of $-0.481$ eV was observed (Fig. 9) for the hydrogenation process, suggesting that the hydrogenation reaction is energetically more favorable at high H coverage. Such a low activation energy could be attributed to the low H adsorption strength at high H coverage, where the average H adsorption energy drops from 1.217 eV at 2 H coverage to 0.630 eV at 6 H converge (Figure S4). As a consequence, the desorption of H should be significantly promoted, leading to fast H migration from Ni cluster to ethylene. Similar phenomena were observed for $Ni_4/CeO_2$ and $Ni_4/TiO_2$. Considering the great influence of H coverage on both kinetics and thermodynamics of the hydrogenation reaction, we suggest that the appropriate H coverage should be employed according to the actual conditions when calculating a hydrogenation process. It is worth noting that the activation energy at high H coverage also follows the trend of $Ni_4/TiO_2$ $(0.475\ eV) > Ni_4/CeO_2$ $(0.352\ eV) > Ni_4$/BNO $(0.320\ eV)$, in line with the sequence at low H coverage (Figure S5). Our results show that the metal-support effect on the catalytic performance of the supported catalyst is highly related to the charge transfer between the substrate surface and metal particles, regardless of the H coverage.

## 4. Conclusions
In summary, the influences of EMSI on the catalytic hydrogenation of ethylene on three supported Ni catalysts have been studied systematically via DFT simulations. The Ni4 cluster could be stably anchored on all the involving supports with strong adsorption energies ranging from 4.211 eV to 5.584 eV. Besides the anchoring effect, evident electronic interactions between Ni and substrate were observed. It was found that the electron loss of Ni cluster follows a trend of $Ni_4/TiO_2 > Ni_4/CeO_2 > Ni_4$/BNO. Such a charge variation would significantly affect the adsorption behavior of $H_2$ and $C_2H_4$. In particular, the adsorption energy of ethylene shows approximately linear relationship with both the charges on Ni cluster and the charge transfer from Ni to ethylene. The hydrogenation of ethylene over three catalysts were conducted to address the kinetics of the reaction. It was found that the activation of $C=C$ bond of ethylene on the Ni cluster is a key step of the reaction, which could be significantly promoted by the $Ni_4$/BNO due to its relatively high electron density. However, the electron deficient $Ni_4/TiO_2$ exhibits a poor kinetics for ethylene hydrogenation with a high energy barrier of 1.037 eV. The H coverage is also considered as an important issue that affects the hydrogenation process. At high H coverage, the anchoring strength of H atoms on Ni cluster decreases visibly, leading to facile H migration from Ni cluster to ethylene molecule. Indeed, the activation barriers of ethylene hydrogenation drop significantly at high H coverage. Considering the fact that the amount of hydrogen is always excessive in an actual hydrogenation process, it is rational to conduct simulations at high H coverage. Our results show that the activation energies of ethylene hydrogenation follow a sequence of $Ni_4/TiO_2 > Ni_4/CeO_2 > Ni_4$/BNO at both low and high H coverages, in line with the amount of electron transfer from Ni to substrate. Therefore, we could rationally conclude that an electron abundant substrate would enrich the electron density of the supported metal nanoparticles, resulting in superior hydrogenation activity.
