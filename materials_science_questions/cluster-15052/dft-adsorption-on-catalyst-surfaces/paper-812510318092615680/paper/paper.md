# Deciphering $NH_3$ Adsorption Kinetics in Ternary Ni-Cu-Fe Oxyhydroxide toward Efficient Ammonia Oxidation Reaction

Mingke Zhu, Yi Yang, Shibo Xi, Caozheng Diao, Zhigen Yu,* Wee Siang Vincent Lee,* and Junmin Xue*

Developing efficient catalysts for the ammonia oxidation reaction (AOR) is crucial for $NH_3$ utilization as a large-scale energy carrier. This work reports a promising Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH material for ammonia oxidation, and density functional theory is used to investigate the AOR mechanism. It is revealed that the oxygen-atoms bonded with the metal-atom on the surface of electrode play an important role in AOR. By codoping Cu and Fe, the electron distribution around the oxygen-atom is affected, which helps to promote the occurrence of ammonia oxidation. The Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH material delivers one of the highest ammonia removal efficiency to date of ≈90% after 12 h. In addition, ≈55% of the initial ammonia is successfully degraded after 24 h in high ammonia concentration. Thus, this work reveals the mechanism of AOR that can provide new ideas to tailor more powerful and updated catalysts in the future.

## 1. Introduction

Ammonia oxidation reaction (AOR) has gradually gained significant research attention as it is considered as one of the measures in addressing the growing ammonia pollutants generated in the society.[1,2] The concept of utilizing the undesirable ammonia pollutants into useful resources for practical applications is particularly exciting, especially for energy storage application.[3,4] This is mainly due to the high theoretical energy density of ammonia ($≈3000$ Wh kg⁻¹), which is significantly higher as compared to conventional fuels such as methanol, ethanol, and formic acid.[5] Hence, it is unsurprising that much effort has been devoted toward the development of efficient AOR catalysts in order to take advantage of this practical utilization of ammonia through AOR. While there are significant strides in AOR catalysts in the recent years, one of the major challenges in AOR is the sluggish $NH_3$ adsorption kinetic on the material which ultimately limits the efficiency of these catalysts.[6-8] To address this issue, understanding the interaction between the material and $NH_3$ is vital to approach this challenge with relevant strategies.

Non-precious metal based catalysts such as Ni, Cu, Co, $Ni(OH)_2$, graphite, and organic material have gained rapid recognition over Pt as an attempt to increase the practicality of AOR catalyst by lowering the cost.[9-12] Among these materials, Ni-based compound has been widely studied due to its high ammonia removal efficiency of ≈40-70%, which poise it as an appealing high performing AOR catalyst candidate. Even though various types of Ni-based compound have been used as AOR catalysts, these compounds are eventually converted to NiOOH during the electrochemical process.[13-19] Thus, due to this phenomenon, it is generally accepted that NiOOH is the key material that can contribute toward ammonia oxidation. In the recent years, modification of NiOOH with Cu doping to form $Ni_{(1-x)}Cu_xOOH$ has shown enhanced AOR activity as compared to its undoped counterpart.[12,20] To provide a deeper insight into the enhancement mechanism, density functional theory (DFT) simulation was employed in this work. As a $NH_3$ molecule approaches the material, it was discovered that $NH_3$ tends to adsorb onto the $O_{O-Ni}$ by forming weak electrostatic attraction. Such adsorption process is thermodynamically unfavorable due to its high Gibbs free energy of adsorption, which is one of the main factors toward the sluggish AOR kinetics.

According to the simulation result, it is identified that by manipulating the electron density at $O_{O-Ni}$, Gibbs free energy of $NH_3$ adsorption can be altered. Thus, by doping $Cu^{2+}$ into the $NiO_6$ framework, two $O_{O-Ni}$ with dangling bonds are formed as $Cu^{2+}$ dopants tend to coordinate to four $O^{2-}$. These dangling bonds on $O_{O-Ni}$ are particularly favorable for $NH_3$ adsorption due to its high electron density, which may be the rationale behind the enhanced AOR performance. It is shown in the DFT simulation result that the $\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}$ with lower electronegativity is able to polarize the electron cloud to a large extent as compared to $\mathrm{Ni}^{3+}$. As such, the electron density at $\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{Ni}}$ could be further enhanced which leads to a lower Gibbs free energy of adsorption as compared to $\mathrm{Ni}_{(1-x)} \mathrm{Cu}_{x} \mathrm{OOH}$.

In addition to the enhanced AOR activity, the as-synthesized NiCuFe electrode shows excellent stability of current density after 24 h testing. Thus, this work not only reports a high performing AOR catalyst, it also reveals the $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ adsorption kinetics in AOR which can provide constructive strategies toward high performing AOR catalysts development in the future.

## 2. Results and Discussion
To understand the mechanisms of AOR, the DFT calculation is first conducted to study the ammonia oxidization process. According to the Gerisher and Mauerer mechanism (G-M mechanism) proposed by Herron et al., $^{[10]}$ there are two possible reaction paths that AOR can follow. One of the reaction path is the " $\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{N}$ " mechanism, $\mathrm{NH}_{3} \rightarrow \mathrm{NH}_{2}{ }^{*} \rightarrow \mathrm{NH}^{*} \rightarrow \mathrm{N}^{*}$ $+\mathrm{N}^{*} \rightarrow \mathrm{N}_{2}$, in which the adsorbed $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ dehydrogenates first and then form $\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}$ bond between $\mathrm{N}^{*}$. The other reaction path is the "G-M" mechanism, $\mathrm{NH}_{3} \rightarrow \mathrm{NH}_{2}{ }^{*} \rightarrow \mathrm{H}_{x} \mathrm{NNH}_{y}{ }^{*} \rightarrow \ldots \rightarrow \mathrm{N}_{2}$, in which the $\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}$ bond form between intermediates and then dehydrogenate to $\mathrm{N}_{2}$. The bifurcation of the two reaction paths depend on whether the adsorbed $\mathrm{NH}_{x}^{*}$ is more inclined to dehydrogenate to $\mathrm{NH}_{x-1}^{*}$ or to form $\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}$ bond with another intermediate. To determine the reaction path, the energy barrier of the bifurcation step of the G-M mechanism and the $\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{N}$ mechanism in $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{OOH}$ system is calculated first, as shown in the inset figure in Figure 1b. Figure 1a shows the optimized Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH model, it is found that Fe-atom prefers to stay near to Cu-atom to form a cluster in order to achieve lower formation energy (shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information). It should be noted that the atomic ratio in the computational model is divergent from the experimental samples. The expanded supercell ensures the unneglectable interaction between chemical dopant and its adjacent images in the periodic boundary conditions adopted in our DFT calculations. The energy landscapes of the intermediates on the surface of Ni-Cu-OOH and Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH are calculated comprehensively using the nudged elastic band (NEB) method (shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information). Figure 1b shows the energy barriers of the different reaction steps of the N + N mechanism measured from the NEB results of Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH and Ni-Cu-OOH systems. During AOR process, ammonia is firstly adsorbed onto the O-atom that is bonded to dopant atoms (Step 1). The adsorbed ammonia then deprotonates to form $NH_{2}^\ast$ (Step 2), NH* (Step 3), and N* (Step 4). Finally, the two N* bond with each other to form $N_2$ and is later desorbed from the surface of electrode (Step 5). Based on the simulation result, among the five steps during AOR process, Step 1 is considered to be the most limiting step due to the high energy barrier for $NH_3$ adsorption. As Cu-Fe are codoped in NiOOH, the energy barrier in Step 1 is significantly decreased by ≈4.5-fold (from 4.91 to 1.08 eV), as shown in Figure 1b, which greatly improves the reaction rate of ammonia adsorption in the first step of AOR. In addition, the results indicate that Fe-dopant would significantly decrease the thermodynamic energy and lower the kinetic energy (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The formation of $N_2$ process would be fast and an exothermic process that leads to a more efficiency ammonia oxidation process. In contrast, the same process is an endothermic process on Ni-Cu-OOH electrode (see in Figure S2, Supporting Information). Based on these results, the preferred pathway for ammonia electrooxidation on the surface of Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH is determined as $NH_3 \to NH_{2}^\ast \to NH^\ast \to N^\ast + N^\ast \to N_2$. By codoping Cu-Fe in NiOOH, it would significantly reduce the thermodynamic and kinetic energies required during AOR and change some steps to an exothermic one leading to faster reaction rate. Therefore, Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH is expected to deliver promising AOR activity due to the significantly lowered energy in Step 1.

To gain an in-depth understanding of the reaction mechanism, the chemical bonding is analyzed through computing the charge difference between dopants and the host matrix. For Cu doped NiOOH, the charge difference can be calculated using the formula $\Delta \rho = \rho_{(Cu + NiOOH)} - (\rho_{Cu} + \rho_{NiOOH})$, where $\rho_{(Cu + NiOOH)}$, $\rho_{Cu}$, and $\rho_{NiOOH}$ represent the charge density of the Cu doped NiOOH, isolated Cu, and isolated NiOOH, respectively; for Cu-Fe codoped NiOOH, the charge difference is calculated by using the formula of $\Delta \rho = \rho_{(Cu + Fe + NiOOH)} - (\rho_{Cu} + \rho_{Fe} + \rho_{NiOOH})$, where, $\rho_{Fe}$ is the charge density of the isolated Fe. The calculated results are shown in Figure 1c,d for Ni-Cu-OOH and Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH respectively, in which the charge accumulated around O-ion that is bonded with Cu-ion and Fe-ion. Comparing to Ni-Cu-OOH, Ni-Cu-Fe-OOH shows more free electrons distributing around the O-ion due to the synergistic effects between the dopants that helps to adsorb ammonia on to the surface. The results indicate that by codoping Cu and Fe in NiOOH, the electrons near the Fermi level of O will become more delocalized, which leads to the increased availability of empty orbitals to accept electrons during AOR process.

The NiCuFe ternary electrode is synthesized by twofold: 1) the Ni-Cu-Fe ternary metal is first grown on commercial carbon paper (denoted as NiCuFe/CP) using a controlled potential electrodeposition process at −1.15 V versus SCE in a prepared precursor solution (more information in the Experimental Section); 2) To exhibit AOR activity, the NiCuFe/CP is activated by electrochemical treatment that is carried out in 1 м KOH with a controlled current density of $20 mA cm^{-2}$ for 10 h (Figure S3, Supporting Information). In this paper, the activated NiCuFe/CP is denoted as a-NiCuFe. During the activation process, the loaded metal on the surface of the NiCuFe/CP electrode gradually dissolved into the electrolyte, whereby forming a metal oxides layer on the surface of a-NiCuFe.

Figure 2a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the carbon paper substrate, NiCu/CP, NiCuFe/CP, and NiCuFe after activation (denoted as a-NiCuFe). Comparing to NiCu bimetal electrode prepared as a control sample, similar peaks are observed for Ni-Cu-Fe ternary electrode. The magnified XRD spectrums in the range of $42^\circ$ to $52^\circ$ shows the shifting of (111) and (200) peaks highlighting the effect of Fe-doping on the metallic structure. In addition, the element ratio on the surface of the sample was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and the result shows Ni:Cu:Fe = 1.72:2.76:1. The results showed that the content of copper and iron on the surface of the material increased slightly, indicating that Ni tends to deposit on the concave, while Cu and Fe tend to deposit on the convex. Due to the inevitable overlap between ammonia oxidation and oxygen evolution, which contributes toward a combined current density reading, ammonia removal efficiency can be used to accurately determine the AOR electrocatalytic perfor- mance of a-NiCuFe electrode. The ammonia concentration in the electrolyte before and after AOR testing is determined using an indophenol blue method (Figure 3c). The parameters that can affect the competition between AOR and OER, such as applied voltage, pH value, and ammonia concentration in the electrolyte, are studied in this work. After electrolysis for 12 h, 89.4% of the ini- tial ammonia can be decomposed under 0.55 V applied voltage, while as much as 95.1% of initial ammonia is removed after 24 h. The overall Faradaic efficiency is around 15%. To the best of our knowledge, this result shows one of the highest electrocata- lytic performance reported so far. Notably, there is no obvious improvement on ammonia removal efficiency when the applied voltage further increases to 0.6 V, even though the current den- sity obtained from 0.6 V is much higher than that from 0.55 V (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The possible reason is that oxygen evolution becomes more intensive under higher applied voltage that results in high energy consumption. Furthermore, the poor Faradaic efficiency at 0.6 V indicates a large portion of generated current is due to OER. Thus, ammonia electrol- ysis conducted at lower applied voltage would lead to a higher Faradaic efficiency and, therefore, leading to a more energy-effi- cient way to ammonia electrooxidation. To provide a more com- prehensive result, ion chromatography (IC) test was conducted to detect the concentration of nitrate in the electrolyte after AOR testing. It is worth noting that nitrite ion is not detected, as nitrite ion can be rapidly oxidized to nitrate ion in the presence of dissolved oxygen in the electrolyte. $^{[23]}$ Based on the IC result (Figure S10, Supporting Information), only $0.528$ ppm $NO_{3}^{-}$ is detected as by-product after prolonged AOR process (24 h).

Thus, this result suggests that, NiCuFe electrode exhibits a high selectivity toward $N_2$, and hence suitable as AOR catalyst.

Moreover, the performance of a-NiCuFe electrode at pH = 8, 10, and 12 in 50 mL initial electrolyte containing $55 \times 10^{-3}$ м $NH_4Cl$ under 0.55 V (vs SCE) is investigated. Figure 4a shows the ammonia removal efficiency under different pH values for 12, 15, 18, and 24 h. Higher initial pH value of electrolyte is observed to have a higher removal efficiency, leading to a faster reaction rate. The ammonia removal efficiency can reach ≈96% after 24 h. The phenomenon can be explained according to the Nernst equation: $E = -0.77 + 0.0592\ (14 - pH)$, whereby a high pH value would lead to a negative potential ($E$) and make it easier to process AOR in terms of thermodynamics.

In addition, the performance of a-NiCuFe electrode at high ammonia concentration is tested. The average concentration of ammonia in agricultural or industrial wastewater can range from 20 to 100 mg L⁻¹ (ppm). Thus, in this work, $100 \times 10^{-3}$ м ($≈5350$ mg L⁻¹) $NH_4Cl$ and $500 \times 10^{-3}$ м ($≈26\ 750$ mg L⁻¹) $NH_4Cl$ are added into 0.5 м NaOH as electrolyte to test the AOR performance of NiCuFe electrode at extreme concentration. The ammonia removal efficiency is shown in Figure 4b. The a-NiCuFe electrode is still able to exhibit good ammonia oxi- dation property in high concentrated ammonia conditions. At $100 \times 10^{-3}$ м $NH_4Cl$ electrolyte, around 55% of initial ammonia is successfully degraded after 24 h. The result indicates that the a-NiCuFe electrode is a promising catalyst that can operate in harsh condition. Thus, it not only can be used for wastewater treatment, it can also generate hydrogen directly from high concentrated ammonia. However, in $500 \times 10^{-3}$ м $NH_4Cl$ electrolyte, nearly 10% ammonia is decomposed after 12 h and the surface material is later deactivated due to poisoning. In general, higher ammonia content in the electrolyte beyond a certain range can lead to a higher coverage of surface of the catalyst with ammonia. Thus, this aid the ammonia to compete with other adsorbed species such as $OH^-$, which in turn enhances the AOR process. However, as the active sites on the catalyst are fully saturated, more ammonia can result in the poisoning of the surface of the catalyst.

## 3. Conclusions
The as-prepared NiCuFe electrode shows superior AOR catalytic activity as compared to NiCu electrode and other noble-metal free catalysts. This improvement in AOR activity is mainly attributed to the formation of abundant active surface of as-prepared NiCuFe electrode and the synergistic effect of $\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Fe}$ codopant in $\mathrm{NiOOH}$. The catalyst reported in this study exhibits excellent ammonia oxidation properties which is one of the most efficient catalysts to date. Nearly $95 \%$ ammonia can be removed after $12 \mathrm{~h}$. Also, the catalyst demonstrates good performance in high ammonia concentration condition which makes it appealing in harsh operating conditions. With these properties, the asprepared NiCuFe material could be a promising stable and lowcost catalyst for efficient electrooxidation of ammonia. Besides, the investigation in this work would provide inspiration on the design and development of ammonia electrocatalyst.

## 4. Experimental Section
### Synthesis of $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Fe}$ Ternary Electrode:
The $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Fe}$ ternary electrode was prepared through two steps. i) The $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Fe}$ ternary metal was first electrodeposited on carbon paper. ii) The electrode material was activated via electrochemical treatment. Before electrodeposition, the carbon paper $(1 \mathrm{~cm} \times 1.5 \mathrm{~cm})$ was rinsed with $\mathrm{HCl}(1 \%)$, deionized water, and acetone. The electrodeposition was carried out with a standard three-electrode electrochemical cell, in which the washed carbon paper works as the working electrode, a platinum foil $(2 \mathrm{~cm} \times 2 \mathrm{~cm})$ works as counter electrode, and an SCE electrode works as reference electrode. The precursor solution consisted of $80 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{NiSO}_{4} \cdot 6 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}, 40 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~m}$ $\mathrm{CuSO}_{4} \cdot 5 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}, 0 \times 10^{-3}-40 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{Fe}_{2}\left(\mathrm{SO}_{4}\right)_{3} \cdot 7 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}, 0.2 \mathrm{~m}$ sodium citrate tribasic dehydrate $\left(\mathrm{Na}_{3} \mathrm{C}_{6} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{O}_{7} \cdot 2 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right), 0.45 \mathrm{~m}$ ammonium chloride $\left(\mathrm{NH}_{4} \mathrm{Cl}\right)$, and $0.55 \mathrm{~m}$ sodium hypophosphite monohydrate $\left(\mathrm{NaPO}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2} \cdot \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)$. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without any further purification. Then, the $\mathrm{pH}$ value of the precursor solution was adjusted to 10.0 by adding $50 \mathrm{wt} \% \mathrm{NaOH}$ solution. Typically, the $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Fe}$ ternary metal was deposited through controlled potential electrolysis at $-1.15 \mathrm{~V}$ versus SCE for $1 \mathrm{~h}$ under room temperature using an electrochemical station (Bio-logic VMP3). After electrodeposition, the sample was carefully rinsed with deionized water. For the activation part, the electrochemical treatment was carried out in $1 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{KOH}$ with a controlled current density of $20 \mathrm{~mA} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$ for $10 \mathrm{~h}$. After activation, the sample was withdraw carefully from the electrolyte, rinsed with deionized water, and then allowed to dry in air.

Characterization: X-ray diffraction analysis (Bruker D8 Advanced Diffractometer System) was used to examine the phases of the samples. XRD patterns were collected on using Cu K-edge radiation (40 kV, $40 \mathrm{~mA}$ ), the diffractometer were recorded in the $2 \theta$ range of $10^{\circ}-100^{\circ}$ with a step size of $1^{\circ}$ and step time of $10 \mathrm{~s}$. XPS(Kratos Analytical Axis UltraDLD UHV spectrometer) equipped with an Al K-edge X-ray source $(1486.69 \mathrm{eV})$ was used to analyzed surface composition. $\mathrm{Ni}, \mathrm{Fe}$, and $\mathrm{Cu}$ $\mathrm{L}_{2,3}$-edge and O K-edge soft-XAS of samples were performed at beamline of Singapore Synchrotron. All samples were attached to an aluminum sample holder using double-sided conductive carbon tape. The $\mathrm{L}_{2,3}$-edge spectra were collected at room temperature under a spherical grating monochromator with $1100 \mathrm{~mm}^{-1}$ ruling, where the exit and entrance slits were set to an intermedia resolution of $0.3 \mathrm{eV}$. All XAS spectra were collected in the TEY method, obtained through the drain current, and corresponding to probing depths of $3 \mathrm{~nm}$. The $\mathrm{NiO}, \mathrm{CuO}, \mathrm{Fe}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$, and $\mathrm{Fe}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ standard powders were applied as ordered directly onto the carbon tape. The spectra were normalized to the incident photo flux measured by a gold grid upstream via a linear background subtraction followed by an intensity normalization (maximum set to 1) for visual comparison. The microstructure and morphology were examined using a SEM (ZEISS SEM Supra 40). TEM was conducted by a JEOL-3010 (300 kV acceleration voltage) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer that allowed elemental composition analysis.

The electrocatalytic properties of NiCuFe ternary electrode were measured in a three-electrode system by CV and CA techniques using a VMP3 electrochemical workstation (Biologic Inc.). A platinum foil $(2 \mathrm{~cm}$ $\times 2 \mathrm{~cm}$ ) worked as counter electrode, and an SCE electrode worked as reference electrode. The CV measurements, from -0.2 to $0.7 \mathrm{~V}$ with a scan rate of $10 \mathrm{mV} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$, were recorded after at least 5 cycles until stable result.

Quantification of Ammonia: The amount of ammonia presented in the electrolyte was quantified using the indophenol blue method. ${ }^{[23-25]}$ The following stock solutions were prepared for the quantification: $0.32 \mathrm{M}$ $\mathrm{NaOH}$ and $0.4 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{C}_{7} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{O}_{3} \mathrm{Na}$ as coloring reagent; $0.75 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{NaOH}$ and $4 \%$ $\mathrm{NaClO}$ as oxidizing reagent; $1 \% \mathrm{Na}_{2}[\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN}){ }_{5} \mathrm{NO}]$ as catalyzing reagent. In a typical setup, $2 \mathrm{~mL}$ of standard solution or sample solution was required each time. $1 \mathrm{~mL}$ of coloring reagent, $0.4 \mathrm{~mL}$ of oxidizing reagent, and $0.2 \mathrm{~mL}$ of catalyzing reagent were added to the standard solution or sample solution. The resulting solution was thoroughly mixed and was left to stand for $1 \mathrm{~h}$ at room temperature. The UV-vis absorption spectrum (UV-vis spectrometer Shimadzu UV-1800) was used to measure the wavelength at $655 \mathrm{~nm}$. The concentration-absorbance curves are calibrated using standard $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ solution with a series of concentrations (Figure S13, Supporting Information). The fitting curve shows a good linear relationship between the absorbance value and $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ concentration, according to three independent calibrations.

Calculation of Faradaic Efficiency: Faradaic efficiency of ammonia oxidation was defined as the amount of electric charge used for electrooxidizing $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ divided the total charge passed through the electrodes during the electrolysis. The total amount of decomposed ammonia was measured using colorimetric methods.

Assuming three electrons are needed to produce one $\mathrm{NH}_{3}$ molecule, the Faradaic efficiency can be calculated as follows

$$
\mathrm{FE}=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{e}}}{Q_{\mathrm{t}}}=\frac{n \times F \times V \times\left(C_{0}-C_{\mathrm{t}}\right) / 17000}{\int \mathrm{Idt}} \tag{1}
$$

where $Q_{\mathrm{t}}$ is the total consumed electric quantity calculated from the integration of the discharge current-time curve, and $Q_{\mathrm{e}}$ is the theoretical electric charge quantity transferred based on ammonia oxidation reaction, $C_{0}$ and $C_{\mathrm{t}}$ are initial and final ammonia concentration $\left(\mathrm{mg} \mathrm{L}^{-1}\right)$, Faraday constant $\left(96485 \mathrm{C} \mathrm{mol}^{-1}\right)$, the volume of electrolyte for AOR

testing (V), and the transferred electron number ($n=3$) in per mole of ammonia oxidation reaction.

DFT Calculation: All calculations were carried out using the DFT with the generalized Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) and DFT+U (Ueff=U-J = 5.3), $^{[26]}$ and the projector augmented-wave (PAW) pseudopotential plane-wave method was implemented in the VASP code. $^{[27,28]}$ For the PAW pseudopotential, d$^8$4s$^2$, 2s$^2$2p$^4$, 3d$^7$4s$^1$, 3d$^{10}$4p$^1$, and 1s$^1$ as valence electrons for Ni, O, Fe, Cu, and H atoms were included, respectively. $\beta$-Ni(OH)$_2$ has a layered structure, with OH$^-$ anions between nickel ions, and layers stack together by hydrogen bonds. A $12 \times 12 \times 10$ Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid was used for Ni(OH)$_2$ unitcell geometry optimization calculations. Good convergence was obtained with these parameters, and the total energy was converged to $0.1 \times 10^{-6}$ eV per atom. Energy convergence with respect to the plane wave cutoff was tested by varying this setting between 300 and 600 eV considering the electron spin polarization. Convergence to within 10 meV was achieved with a cutoff energy of 500 eV for Ni(OH)$_2$ unitcell. Lattice constants of the optimized unitcell of Ni(OH)$_2$ are, $a = b = 3.04$ Å and $c = 4.61$ Å, which is very close to the experimental values of $a = b = 3.12$ Å and $c = 4.66$ Å. $^{[29]}$ One side H atoms were removed from the optimized Ni(OH)$_2$ surface to form NiOOH. The optimized supercell model of NiOOH containing 60 Ni, 120 O, and 60 H atoms, which was adopted to study Cu and Fe doping and the oxidation of ammonia. The calculation were carried out with the van der Waals (vdW) correction by employing optPBE-vdW functional, $^{[28]}$ and the reaction paths of the oxidation of ammonia steps were calculated by means of the NEB method. $^{[30]}$
