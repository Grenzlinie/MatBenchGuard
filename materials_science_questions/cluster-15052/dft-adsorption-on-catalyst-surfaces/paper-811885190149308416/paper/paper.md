# Enthalpy-change driven synthesis of high-entropy perovskite nanoparticles

Siyang Nie, Liang Wu, Lingci Zhao, and Pengfei Zhang (⊗)

School of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai 200240, China

© Tsinghua University Press and Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2021
Received: 19 May 2021 / Revised: 15 July 2021 / Accepted: 10 August 2021

## ABSTRACT
Due to their diverse and tunable composition, distorted lattice and excellent stability, high-entropy ceramics (HECs) hold great promise for catalysis, especially when they present as nanoparticles (NPs). However, current HECs are typically limited to bulky materials with none or fewer defects, because high synthetic temperature (e.g., 1,000–1,200 °C) is usually required to highlight the entropic contribution ($T\Delta S$) in $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. Being different with previous strategies, a negative Gibbs free energy for HECs crystallization is obtained by dramatically decreasing the mixing enthalpy ($\Delta H$). Guided by this principle, single-phase high-entropy $\text{La(Ni}_{0.2}\text{Mn}_{0.2}\text{Cu}_{0.2}\text{Fe}_{0.2}\text{Co}_{0.2}\text{)O}_{3-\delta}$ perovskite (HE-LMO) NPs were prepared at a relatively low temperature (≤ 500 °C). Interestingly, abundant oxygen vacancies were directly created within HE-LMO NPs, which exhibited good activity in catalytic oxidation. Meanwhile, the high-entropy structure endows as-made HE-LMO with robust stability even with 5 vol.% water in feeding gas. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the defective sites in HE-LMO NPs facilitated the charge transfer from HE-LMO to CO, thus activating the adsorbed CO gas. The current work may inspire future design and synthesis of HECs NPs.

enthalpy-change, high-entropy ceramics, mechanochemical synthesis, perovskite, CO oxidation

## 1 Introduction
In 2015, Rost group proposed a new kind of high-entropy ceramics (HECs): entropy-stabilized oxides, $\text{(NiMgCuZnCo)O}_{5}$ [1]. Since then, HECs have been widely studied because they possess excellent stability beyond their unary and binary counterparts [2]. Besides, metal cations in uncommon coordination environments often endow HECs a number of unexpected physico-chemical properties [3,4]. The design of single high-entropy phase is guided by Gibbs free energy ($\Delta G$), $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, where entropic contribution ($T\Delta S$) could act as the dominant role [5–7]. Based on this principle, the family of HECs was greatly enriched, including different kinds of oxides from rocksalts to perovskites and spinels, as well as sulfides, carbides, nitride and so on [8]. However, the incorporated multiple elements in HECs are usually in large proportions and the intrinsic complexities between them (e.g., difference in atomic radii and high mixing enthalpy for formation of solid solution) make them prone to phase separation or element segregation [9]. In order to force the metal cations to uniformly and randomly occupy similar sites, high reaction temperature is usually required [10]. For instance, in the works of Rost group, a temperature of 1,273 K was considered because CuO and ZnO were not expected to crystallize in rocksalt structure [1,10]. Unfortunately, the high temperature usually causes the collapse of pores and the disappearing of defects, which thus inhibit the application of HECs in catalysis community [11].

Actually, scientists paid insufficient attention on the role of mixing enthalpy ($\Delta H_{\text{mix}}$), which has been perceived as main quantity in $\Delta G$ [9]. During previous studies, the elevated sintering temperature ($T$) and configurational entropy ($\Delta S_{\text{config}}$) collectively increased entropic contribution ($T\Delta S_{\text{config}}$) to a similar magnitude of $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}$. For example, for five equimolar elements oxides with 1.61R for $\Delta S_{\text{config}}$, a reaction temperature higher than 1,000 K is needed to compensate the heat of 13.4 kJ/mol [10]. Through the density functional theory (DFT) estimation, Hu’s group chose the optimized constituent elements to achieve the shallow enthalpy of mixing and thereby synthesizing high-entropy alloys with a decreased formation temperature [12]. Therefore, decreasing the $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}$ would benefit the formation of single-phase HECs at a lower temperature and maintain the porous and defect-rich structure [11,13].

$\text{ABO}_{3}$-type perovskite oxides have emerged as one of the most studied oxides because of their exceptional performance and alterable compositions [14]. In the field of heterogeneous catalysis, the intriguing property of La-based perovskites comes from their tunable composition in B site. For example, by substituting Mn site with reducible 3d transition metals (e.g. Fe, Co and Cr), the redox capacity of $\text{LaMnO}_{3}$ would be highly enhanced [15]. Although high-entropy $\text{LaMnO}_{3}$ perovskite has been successfully synthesized, the high calcination temperature (1,300–1,500 °C) would result in bulky materials, which limits their performance in various fields [13].

To decrease $\Delta G$ and prepare single-phase high-entropy $\text{LaMnO}_{3}$-type perovskite (HE-LMO), conventional works emphasized the role of $T\Delta S$ (Fig. 1(b)) [5,10,12,16]. In contrast, the key concept of this work is finding a way to decrease the $\Delta H$

## 2 Results and discussion
The preparation process of HE-LMO NPs could be divided into two steps. Firstly, the mechanochemical redox process *in situ* produces high-entropy hydroxide ($La_5(NiMnCuFeCo)(OH)_{30-\delta}$ HE-hydroxide) [17]. Secondly, the subsequent calcination at $500\ ^\circ\text{C}$ would result in high-entropy $LaMnO_3$-type perovskite. The whole process was shown as Route A in Fig. 1(a) and the obtained high-entropy $LaMnO_3$ powder was denoted as Redox-HE-LMO. For comparison, a conventional mechanochemical process with no addition of $KMnO_4$ powder was performed. Typically, no redox reaction proceeded and a mixture of hydroxides were obtained ($La(OH)_3$, $Ni(OH)_2$, $Mn(OH)_2$, $Cu(OH)_2$, $FeOOH$, $Co(OH)_2$, multi-hydroxides) (Route B in Fig. 1(a)). It should be noted that the breakage and mixing of particles during the ball milling of Route B were similar with Route A. Unfortunately, at least $800\ ^\circ\text{C}$ was required to achieve the single HE-LMO in Route B, the obtained high-entropy $LaMnO_3$ in this process was denoted as Bulk-HE-LMO. The synthetic details and redox reaction equation during the ball milling were summarized in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

The crystalline structure of HE-hydroxide (Route A, Fig. 1(a)) was initially investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD). Figure S1 in the ESM shows a series of peaks which could be assigned to $La(OH)_3$ ($P63/m$ #176) and no second phases could be found. Then, scanning electron microscope (SEM) was carried out to investigate the morphology of HE-hydroxide. Accumulation of irregular NPs was presented (Fig. S2 in the ESM). Besides, the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images showed the uniform distribution of these five transition metals and La atoms (Figs. S3 and S4 in the ESM). The optimized crystal structures of HE-hydroxide and different hydroxides were predicted by Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) program, the internal energies ($U$) of them are summarized in Table S1 in the ESM. Then, we calculated the $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}$ and configurational entropy change ($\Delta S_{\text{config}}$) for the formation of HE-LMO. The temperature-dependent Gibbs free energies from HE-hydroxide or multi-hydroxides to HE-LMO by Routes A and B derived from DFT calculations are showed in Fig. 1(c) (the calculation details are shown in the ESM). It should be noted the surface energy, vibrational and electronic entropy were not taken into account in the calculated thermodynamic parameters [12]. Besides, the transitional entropy derived from gas (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) evolution during calcination was also not included in the calculations (Eqs. (S3) and (S4) in the ESM). Obviously, the $\Delta S_{\text{config}}$ plays a positive role in decreasing the transformation temperature ($T_{\text{formation}}$), namely, configurational entropy showed contributions towards phase stability. Although $\Delta S_{\text{config}}$ in Route B dramatically decreased, $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}$ acted as a promoter for decreasing $\Delta G$ in Route A to a negative value, which meant the reaction from HE-hydroxide to HE-LMO was spontaneous [18].

Guided by the DFT calculations, the synthesis of HE-LMO was thermodynamically favorable, and we treated HE-hydroxide with repeated thermal processes at $400\ ^\circ\text{C}$ (see the ESM). Interestingly, several peaks could be assigned to $\text{LaMnO}_3$-type perovskite appeared (Fig. S6 in the ESM), which meant the formation of perovskite crystalline at a low temperature of $400\ ^\circ\text{C}$ yet with slow reaction rates. In order to synthesize HE-LMO in a facile way, the calcination temperature of $500\ ^\circ\text{C}$ was chosen. Rietveld refinement revealed the obtained Redox-HE-LMO showed single cubic perovskite phase and lattice parameter decreased from 3.908 to $3.854\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ (Fig. 1(d)) [19,20]. Besides, the inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) results revealed the atomic ratio of Co/Cu/Fe/Mn/Ni/La was 1:1.1:0.97:1.1:0.92:4.98, in agreement with the theoretical value. As shown in the scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) image of Redox-HE-LMO, perovskite NPs indeed formed, with sizes of ~ 10–100 nm (Fig. S7 in the ESM). High revolution TEM image showed the lattice spacing of $2.73\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, which belongs to the (110) crystal plane of $\text{LaMnO}_3$ (Fig. S8 in the ESM) [21,22]. No lattice spacing for other oxides could be found. Besides, EDS elemental mapping images (Fig. 2 and Fig. S9 in the ESM) illustrated the well distribution of different metal cations. Therefore, the results above suggested that single-phase HE-LMO was synthesized at a mild calcination temperature ($500\ ^\circ\text{C}$). On the contrary, multi-hydroxides prepared in Route B should be sintered at least $800\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to obtain the pure $\text{LaMnO}_3$ perovskite phase (Fig. S10 in the ESM).

In order to monitor the change of heat during the sintering process, simultaneous thermal analysis (STA) was performed. Though HE-hydroxide and multi-hydroxides depicted almost similar exothermic and endothermic profiles in Fig. S11 in the ESM, the HE-hydroxide powder released more energy, especially at $285\ ^\circ\text{C}$, which was probably caused by dehydration of hydroxides. Therefore, we proposed the higher internal energy of HE-hydroxide than multi-hydroxides. Thermodynamically, the decreased $\Delta H_{\text{mix}}$ favors a lower crystalline temperature and the growth or aggregation of initial particles could be avoided in some degree.

To inquire the porosity of HE-LMO, $\text{N}_2$ adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K. The HE-LMO powder gave a type II isotherm and significant $\text{N}_2$ uptake especially under high relative pressure (e.g., $P/P_0\ >\ 0.9$) (Fig. 3(a)), which meant the Redox-HE-LMO possessed macropores [23]. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area of HE-LMO catalyst under relative pressure $P/P_0$ between 0.05–0.20 was calculated and estimated to $20.4\ \text{m}^2/\text{g}$ (Fig. S12 in the ESM). It is well known that the oxygen vacancies on oxides were closely related to their catalytic performance. Therefore, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out to investigate the surface oxygen defects and chemical states of different transition metals of HE-LMO samples. Figure 3(b) shows the O 1s spectra of Redox-HE-LMO and Bulk-HE-LMO samples. Four characterization peaks could be divided from O 1s spectra, namely, lattice oxygen (528.9 eV), defect oxygen sites adjacent to the oxygen vacancies (530.3 eV), hydroxyl groups or surface-adsorbed oxygen (531.5 eV), and surface adsorbed molecular moisture (532.7 eV) [24,25]. The concentration of defective oxygen species, which could be obtained from the integration of peaks from O 1s spectra, was 15.9% for Redox-HE-LMO, 2.84 times than Bulk-HE-LMO of 5.6% (Table S2 in the ESM).

The high concentration of oxygen vacancies in Redox-HE-LMO powder could be attributed to the following two aspects: a) the introduction and random distribution of bivalent cations (e.g., $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ni}^{2+}$) [11]; b) the decreased $T_{\text{formation}}$ dramatically reduced the particle size to nanoscale level, which possessed a lower oxygen vacancy formation energy [11,21,26]. Besides, the $\text{Cu}\ 2\text{p}_{3/2}$ spectrum can be decomposed into two peaks belonging to $\text{Cu}^+/\text{Cu}^0$ (932.7 eV) and $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ (934.6 eV) [25,27]. As shown in Fig. S14 and Table S3 in the ESM, the relative content of $\text{Cu}^+/\text{Cu}^0$ of Redox-HE-LMO was higher than Bulk-HE-LMO, 68.2% versus 36.1%, respectively. The similar phenomenon could also be obtained for $\text{Ni}\ 2\text{p}_{3/2}$ and Co 2p spectra that reductive transition metals in Redox-HE-LMO were slightly reduced (Figs. S15 and S16, and Table S3 in the ESM), because of the enhancement of oxygen vacancies.

Then, $\text{H}_2$-temperature programmed reduction ($\text{H}_2$-TPR) was performed to investigate the reducibility of Redox- and Bulk-HE-LMO samples. As shown in Fig. 3(c), Redox-HE-LMO showed three main hydrogen consumption peaks at 285, 326 and $345\ ^\circ\text{C}$. According to the literatures, the former peak could be attributed to reduction of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ to $\text{Cu}^+$, the latter two peaks could be ascribed to reduction of $\text{Ni}^{3+}$ to $\text{Ni}^{2+}$, $\text{Cu}^+$ to $\text{Cu}^0$ and $\text{Ni}^{2+}$ to $\text{Ni}^0$, $\text{Co}^{3+}$ to $\text{Co}^{2+}$, respectively [11,27,28]. However, these three peaks of Bulk-HE-LMO shifted to higher temperatures at 333, 440 and $472\ ^\circ\text{C}$, which suggested the lower reductive temperature and lattice-oxygen mobility of Redox-HE-LMO.

Next, the activity of as-made oxides was evaluated by CO oxidation, a model reaction to inquire their intrinsic catalytic behavior. The light-off curves for CO oxidation over Redox-HE-LMO, Bulk-HE-LMO and bulk unitary perovskites including $\text{LaCoO}_3$, $\text{LaMnO}_3$, $\text{LaNiO}_3$ and $\text{LaFeO}_3$ were investigated. As shown in Fig. 4(a) and Fig. S17 in the ESM, the Redox-HE-LMO catalyst performed better than bulk-HE-LMO and unitary perovskites. Furthermore, the corresponding Arrhenius plot shown in Fig. 4(b) demonstrated that Redox-HE-LMO exhibited lower apparent activation energy during CO oxidation than Bulk-HE-LMO. We proposed that the superior catalytic activity of Redox-HE-LMO may derive from the abundant oxygen defects. Then, the DFT calculation was performed to shed light on the CO oxidation over Redox-HE-LMO.

As characterized and discussed above, Redox-HE-LMO possesses more oxygen vacancies and reduced transition metals. Accordingly, three oxygen atoms adjacent to Co, Cu and Ni atoms were removed (see computational details in the ESM), and the optimized crystal structures together with ionic positions were shown in Figs. S18-S21 in the ESM. After these three oxygen atoms were removed, Bader charge analysis revealed that the chemical states of five transition metals were consistent with XPS results. In detail, the Bader charges of Cu, Co and Ni atoms in first layer of the Redox-HE-LMO (110) slab were slightly increased (Figs. S20 and S21 in the ESM), which meant these three cations were reduced. It has been reported and validated that the CO oxidation over $\text{LaMnO}_3$-type perovskite followed the Mars-van Krevelen mechanism [29]. In this work, CO preferred to adsorb atop reduced Co in Redox-HE-LMO with adsorbed energy ($E_{\text{ads}}$) of -2.47 eV (Fig. 4(c) and Figs. S22 and S23 in the ESM). Besides, the C-O length prolonged to $1.207\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, as compared with C-O distance of $1.128\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ for CO gas, which meant the CO was activated. Similarly, CO was prone to adsorb atop Co in Bulk-HE-LMO. However, the $E_{\text{ads}}$ was -0.33 eV and the bond length of CO almost maintained ($1.158\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, Fig. 4(c)). Then, the differential charge plot in Fig. 4(d) and Fig. S24 in the ESM clearly revealed lone-pair $\sigma$ orbital of CO donated electrons to $\text{e}_\text{g}$ orbital of Co in Redox-HE-LMO and $\pi^*$ received electrons from the $\text{t}_{2\text{g}}$ orbital [30]. Besides, Bader Charge analysis demonstrated the charges of reduced Co decreased from $8.77|e|$ to $8.42|e|$ after the CO adsorbed, which meant the valence of Co increased from +2 to +3 (Bader charges of $\text{CoO}$ and $\text{Co}_2\text{O}_3$ are $8.8|e|$ and $8.5|e|$). On the contrary, the differential charge plot (Fig. 4(e)) revealed almost no charge transfer from Co in Bulk-HE-LMO to CO molecule because the Bader charges changed from $7.94|e|$ to $7.88|e|$ after the CO adsorbed. The electron localization function maps of CO adsorbed on the slabs were presented in Figs. 4(f) and 4(g). Obviously, the $\sigma$ orbital of CO was dramatically weakened after adsorbed on reduced Co of Redox-HE-LMO, which further verified that the oxygen vacancies around Co strengthened the adsorption of CO on Redox-HE-LMO surface.

Actually, moisture is ubiquitous in real world catalysis, which could often poison transition metal-based catalysts, while high-entropy LMOs in principle have a more stable structure [3,31]. Then, the stability of Redox-HE-LMO and binary $\text{LaCoO}_3$ catalysts was studied. We performed CO oxidation test under $270\ ^\circ\text{C}$ with 5 vol.% water in feeding gas over 100 h and no noticeable decrease of activity was observed by Redox-HE-LMO (Fig. 4(h)). After the stability test, the recycled Redox-HE-LMO sample was denoted as Redox-HE-LMO-T. However, the $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in feeding gas afforded a corrosive effect on $\text{LaCoO}_3$ and the conversion of CO significantly decreased over 50 h (Fig. S25 in the ESM). At last, the crystalline structure and chemical environments of transition metals and oxygen atoms were investigated by XRD and XPS analysis. As shown in Fig. S26 in the ESM, XRD patterns of Redox-HE-LMO-T afforded a series of peaks at $2\theta=23.2^\circ$, $33^\circ$, $40.6^\circ$ and $47^\circ$, which could be assigned to the (100), (110), (111) and (200) characteristic reflection planes of $\text{LaMnO}_3$-type perovskite. The surface oxygen defects and chemical states of Cu, Co and Ni were shown in Fig. S27 in the ESM and summarized in Tables S4 and S5 in the ESM. After stability test under $270\ ^\circ\text{C}$ in moist condition for 100 h, the surface oxygen defects and particle reduced Cu, Co and Ni maintained. We considered that the well stability of Redox-HE-LMO in moisture-containing conditions could be the result of the high-entropy stabilized structure [3,31,32].

## 3 Conclusion
In summary, enthalpy-change driven synthesis of HE-LMO ($\text{LaNi}_{0.2}\text{Mn}_{0.2}\text{Cu}_{0.2}\text{Fe}_{0.2}\text{Co}_{0.2}\text{O}_{3-\delta}$) was put forward and validated, which enables Gibbs free energy to be negative without high temperature calcination (e.g., $1,000$-$1,200\ ^\circ\text{C}$). The high entropy oxide-related catalysis has been unveiled in around 2018, while their unexpected performance for catalysis is a young topic, yet full of promise. The idea of lowering crystallization temperature by enthalpy-change strategy may inspire more high entropy catalysts in the near future.
