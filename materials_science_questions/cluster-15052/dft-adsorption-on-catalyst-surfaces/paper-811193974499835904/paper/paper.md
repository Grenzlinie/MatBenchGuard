# ELECTROCATALYSIS BY AD-ATOMS

## PART XXII. $S_{hole}$ CONTROL BY AD-ATOMS ON HCOOH OXIDATION

MASAMI SHIBATA, OSAMU TAKAHASHI and SATOSHI MOTOO

Department of Applied Chemistry, Faculty of Engineering, Yamanashi University, Takeda-4, Kofu (Japan)
(Received 3rd July 1987; in revised form 18th April 1988)

## ABSTRACT

The catalytic effect of various ad-atoms (Cu, Ag, Hg, Ge, Sn, Pb, As, Sb, Bi, S, Se and Te) on formic acid oxidation was examined on a Pt electrode. It was found that the catalytic effect depends on the number of sites occupied by an ad-atom ($S_M$), which is typical of each ad-atom species. The greater the value of $S_M$ of an ad-atom species, the greater the enhancement effect of the ad-atoms. The catalytic effect is the greatest for Bi ad-atoms for which $S_M$ is 3. The enhancement effect of these ad-atoms will be explained by introducing the new concepts of "$S_{hole}$" and "$S_R$".

## (I) INTRODUCTION

Angerstein-Kozlowska and co-workers studied the catalytic effect by ad-atoms, pioneering the role of geometrical control of surface sites by ad-atoms, in electro- catalysis, and suggested a theory of the "third body" effect [1,2]. The poisoning species is formed by the dimerization of formic acid species ($\underset{\sim}{\text{C}}\text{OOH}$) on a Pt electrode. But Hg ad-atoms block the sites necessary for this dimerization of ($\underset{\sim}{\text{C}}\text{OOH}$) on the Hg-Pt (subs) electrode. Thus the activity of a Pt electrode does not decay. This catalytic effect is not an enhancement, but the suppression of the poison formation reaction. Poison formation in formic acid oxidation is suppressed by the site blocking by Hg ad-atoms.

Subsequently, Adzic et al. examined the effects of Pb, Bi, Tl, Cd, Cu and Ag ad-atoms on formic acid oxidation [3-5]. The enhancement was explained by the "third body" effect. They assumed that an ad-atom occupies one Pt site. When ad-atoms deposit to a coverage of 0.5 on a Pt electrode, $\underset{\sim\sim}{\text{C}}\text{OH}$ and $\underset{\sim}{\text{C}}(\text{OH})_2$, which are poisoning species, cannot be adsorbed on the electrode [4]. Examining the effects of Pb, Bi, Tl, Cd, Cu and Ag ad-atoms on formic acid oxidation, Adzic et al.

## (II) CONCEPTS OF "$S_{\text{hole}}$" AND "$S_{\text{R}}$" [14-17]

### (II.1) "$S_{R}$", the lowest number of active sites required for a molecule to react on

When a reaction occurs on an electrode surface, the reaction needs some vacant sites. The number of sites needed for a reactant molecule to react on is denoted by "$S_{\text{R}}$". The value of $S_{\text{R}}$ is not always the same as the number of sites occupied by a reaction intermediate to be adsorbed.

Taking hydrogen evolution as the simplest example, we shall explain the value of $S_{\text{R}}$. Hydrogen evolution on a Au electrode occurs via an atom + ion step which is a reaction of an adsorbed hydrogen atom with a hydronium ion. The number of sites needed for this reaction is 1, i.e. $S_{\text{R}} = 1$. Hydrogen evolution on a Pt electrode occurs via the surface reaction of neighbouring adsorbed hydrogen atoms with each other, i.e. an atom + atom step. The number of sites needed for this reaction is 2, i.e. $S_{\text{R}} = 2$.

### (II.2) "$S_{\text{hole}}$", the number of neighbouring sites active for a reaction

The concept of "$S_{\text{hole}}$" is defined for the electrode side.

Suppose an electrode surface is covered by a inactive material, except for a part (A). The part (A) is a "hole", which is an isolated group of vacant sites. We call it a "reactive domain". The number of sites in a reactive domain is denoted by "$S_{\text{hole}}$". This reactive domain provides a place for a reactant molecule to react.

On a clean electrode, the value of $S_{\text{hole}}$ is infinite. However, when there are ad-atoms on an electrode, the value of $S_{\text{hole}}$ cannot be infinite.

### (II.3) Selectivity by $S_{\text{hole}}$ control

The relation between $S_{\text{R}}$ and $S_{\text{hole}}$ is described in this section.

If the value of $S_{\text{R}}$ of a reaction exceeds the value of $S_{\text{hole}}$ of a reactive domain ($S_{\text{R}} > S_{\text{hole}}$), this reaction cannot occur in this reactive domain. Conversely, if the value of $S_{\text{R}}$ of a reaction does not exceed the value of $S_{\text{hole}}$ of a reactive domain ($S_{\text{R}} \leqslant S_{\text{hole}}$), this reaction can occur in this reactive domain.

In this way, we can make the latter reaction occur but not the former by $S_{\text{hole}}$ control.

### (III) $S_{\text{hole}}$ CONTROL BY AD-ATOMS (GEOMETRICAL CONTROL OF SITE ARRANGEMENT)

A method is available for the preparation of an electrode having reactive domains with a determined value of $S_{\text{hole}}$. A detailed explanation is given in the following.

Consider an electrode surface having a monolayer of orderly dispersed ad-atoms. When an ad-atom is desorbed from this surface, a group of vacant sites appears on the electrode surface. Then the number of vacant sites ($S_{\text{hole}}$) is the same as that of sites ($S_{\text{M}}$) occupied by the ad-atom. The values of $S_{\text{M}}$ for various ad-atoms species were determined by Furuya and Motoo [18]. The values for Cu, Ag, Tl, Cd, Hg, Ge, Sn, Pb, As, Sb, Bi, Se and Te are 1, 1.12, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2.5, 2.6, 3, 2.4 and 2.5, respectively. For example, when a Cu ad-atom having $S_{\text{M}} = 1$ is desorbed from a monolayer of Cu ad-atoms deposited on a Pt electrode, a reactive domain appears having $S_{\text{hole}} = 1$. When a Hg ad-atom having $S_{\text{M}} = 2$ is desorbed from a monolayer of Hg ad-atoms deposited on a Pt electrode, a reactive domain appears having $S_{\text{hole}} = 2$. When a Bi ad-atom having $S_{\text{M}} = 3$ is desorbed from a monolayer of Bi ad-atoms deposited on a Pt electrode, a reactive domain appears having $S_{\text{hole}} = 3$.

When some ad-atoms are desorbed in an orderly way from a monolayer of ad-atoms, the number of reactive domains, being $S_{\text{hole}} = S_{\text{M}}$, increases with decreas- ing ad-atom coverage, and is a maximum at a coverage of 0.5 on the fcc (100) plane, as described previously [14]. On the fcc (111) plane, the number of reactive domains is a maximum at a coverage of 0.67 or 0.65, as shown in refs. 14 and 1. The isolated reactive domains are adjacent to each other at lower coverages of ad-atoms. Hence the reactive domains having $S_{\text{hole}} > S_{\text{M}}$ increase on the electrode, but those having $S_{\text{hole}} = S_{\text{M}}$ decrease.

Figure 1 shows the relation between the number of reactive domains and the coverage of Bi ad-atoms. The number of reactive domains is a maximum at $\theta_{\text{Bi}}^{\text{Pt}} = 0.5-0.67$.

### (IV) EXPERIMENTAL

First, an ad-electrode of known coverage was prepared in an ad-atom deposition cell before the kinetic measurement. Second, the ad-electrode was transferred to another cell for the kinetic measurement under the protection of an argon atmo- sphere in a jacket described previously [7]. The loss of ad-atoms during the transfer and the kinetic measurement was confirmed to be less than 3% of the deposited ad-atoms, which is virtually negligible for the evaluation of the catalytic effect [21].

The ad-electrode was prepared in a solution containing salt of the ad-atom species but no formic acid. Kinetic measurements were carried out on pre-prepared ad-electrodes in a solution containing formic acid but no salt of the ad-atom species. The reasons for this were: (1) reactant adsorption affects ad-atom deposition, and (2) in situ determination of ad-atom coverage is impossible when the experimental solution contains both a salt of the ad-atom species and the reactant [21]. Thus, exact information about the relation between the surface composition and the kinetics of formic acid oxidation is obtained in this case. What is necessary for the kinetic investigation is not the relation between the concentration of ions of the ad-atom species but that between the surface composition and the kinetics. This is so because the kinetics are determined not by the ion concentration but by the surface concentration of the ad-atoms, or even by their arrangement.

### (IV.1) Electrolyte

Water was purified using a "NANOPURE" system. Reagent grade sulphuric acid was dissolved in this water and then pre-electrolysed to an extreme purity [19].

### (IV.2) Preparation of the ad-electrode

A platinum wire ($d=0.3$ mm, $l=20$ mm) was used as the working electrode.

Ad-electrodes were prepared by underpotential deposition from $0.5\ M\ \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ solutions containing a salt of the ad-atom species at a concentration of about $5\times10^{-6}\ M$ but no formic acid. The ad-atom species examined were Cu, Ag, Hg, Ge, Sn, Pb, As, Sb, Bi, S, Se and Te. The coverage was determined by the single pulse ($20\ \text{s}^{-1}$) method described previously [18].

### (IV.3) Kinetic measurement

The measurements were carried out at $40^{\circ}\text{C}$ using the potential step method. The potential was stepped in 0.05 V increments from 0.05 to 0.6 V (vs. RHE) at 30 s intervals. The current was measured at the end of each step.

### (V) RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

It has been found that formic acid oxidation is inhibited by self-poisoning on a Pt electrode [22-25]. The main reaction (R1) and poison formation (R2) of formic acid oxidation can be summarized as follows:

R1 and R2 occur on a Pt electrode simultaneously. R1 does not occur on the Pt sites occupied by a poisoning species formed in R2. If the poisoning species cannot form on a Pt electrode, i.e. R2 cannot occur, the striking decay of activity of the electrode is suppressed. At the same time, if R1 can occur, the electrode is active. When this selectivity is added to the nature of the Pt electrode, the high activity on the electrode can be maintained.

Various reactive domains having $S_{\text{hole}}$ controlled by geometrical site arrangement by various ad-atoms can be obtained as described in Sect. (III). In the following section, the concepts of "$S_{\text{hole}}$" and "$S_{\text{R}}$" are applied to understand the effect of ad-atoms on formic acid oxidation.

### (V.1) Suppression of poison formation

Current decay curves for formic acid oxidation on Pt(subs), Ag-Pt(subs) and Bi-Pt(subs) electrodes at 0.35 V are shown in Fig. 2. The current on the Pt electrode decays very rapidly with time. This tells us that the poison formation reaction occurs and that the poisoning species accumulate rapidly.

On the other hand, the current on Bi-Pt(subs) decays slightly with time. Also, the current on Ag-Pt(subs) decays slightly, though the initial current on Ag-Pt(subs) is small. Poison formation is suppressed on both ad-electrodes.

A measuring rule for suppression of the poison formation reaction is denoted by "SP". The value of SP is obtained from the ratio of the oxidation current after 30 s to the extrapolated initial oxidation current. The values of SP on a Pt electrode, Ag-Pt(subs) and Bi-Pt(subs) are 0.09, 0.43 and 0.53, respectively. The value of SP on a Pt electrode is small, so the poison formation reaction occurs rapidly. On the other hand, Ag and Bi ad-atoms inhibit the poison formation reaction.

The origin of the different conclusions concerning the catalytic effect of these ad-atoms results from the evaluation of the catalytic effect using different experimental methods [21]. In summary, the catalytic effect is greater on Pb-Pt(subs) than on Bi-Pt(subs) when the kinetic measurement is carried out in a solution containing both HCOOH and the salt of the ad-atom species. On the other hand, the catalytic effect is greater on Bi-Pt(subs) than on Pb-Pt(subs) when the kinetic measurement is carried out in a solution containing no salt of the ad-atom species on an electrode having pre-deposited ad-atoms. In the former case, ad-atoms are deposited in competition with the adsorption of HCOOH species or the formation of poisoning species. Therefore the real effects of these ad-atoms cannot be evaluated by the former method. A discussion of this was given in detail in a previous paper [21].

### (V.3) Selectivity by $S_{hole}$ control on formic acid oxidation

The main oxidation is slow on reactive domains having $S_{hole} < 3$, but is fast on those having $S_{hole} \geqslant 3$. On the other hand, poison formation is slow on reactive domains having $S_{hole} \leqslant 3$.

The above results tell us the following: On reactive domains having $S_{hole} = 1$ and 2, neither the main oxidation nor poison formation can occur. On reactive domains having $S_{hole} = 3$, the main oxidation can occur, but poison formation cannot occur. On reactive domains having $S_{hole} \geqslant 4$, both the main oxidation and poison formation can occur. But the rate of the main oxidation decays very rapidly with time due to the accumulation of poisoning species.

As described above, it is found that the value of $S_{hole}$ works selectively on formic acid oxidation.

### (V.4) Is there any relation with other physical and chemical properties?

In this section, we will discuss whether there is any relation with other physical and chemical properties, and whether there is another explanation for the enhancement effect of various ad-atoms on formic acid oxidation.

#### (V.4.a) Radius of the ad-atom

Shabrang et al. studied the catalytic effects of Bi, Pb and Ag ad-atoms on formic acid oxidation [11]. They explained the order of the catalytic effect of these ad-atoms by means of the radius of the ad-atom [11].

We examined the catalytic effects of twelve kinds of ad-atom and elucidated whether there is a relation between the catalytic effect and the atomic radius. These results are shown in Fig. 8. The magnitude of the enhancement effects is not found to increase with the size of the atom, as supported by Shabrang et al. It is made clear that there is no relation between the catalytic effect and the atomic radius.

### (V.4.b) Stability of the ad-atoms
Adzic and Podlavicky explained the order of the enhancement effect of Pb and Cd ad-atoms on formic acid oxidation by means of the stability of the ad-atoms [26].

In this section, the stability of ad-atoms on a Pt electrode is evaluated by the equilibrium potential at an ad-atom coverage of 0.5 [27]. The higher the equilibrium potential, the more stable are the ad-atoms.

Oxidation currents on various ad-electrodes are plotted against the equilibrium potential in Fig. 9. According to Adzic et al. [26], it is predicted that the magnitude of the enhancement effect increases with increasing stability of the ad-atom. But no relation between the catalytic effect and the stability of the ad-atom is found for the different ad-atoms, as is made clear in this figure.

### (V.4.c) Adsorption of oxygen species on ad-atoms
CO oxidation requires oxygen donation as follows [25];
$$CO+[O]\to CO_{2}$$

CO oxidation is enhanced strikingly by oxygen-adsorbing ad-atoms [28,29]. The lower the oxygen-adsorbing potential of the ad-atoms, the larger the enhancement effects [29].

On the other hand, formic acid oxidation is a dehydrogenation, as follows [25];
$$HCOOH\to CO_{2}+2\ H^{+}+2\ e^{-}$$

Figure 10 shows the relation between the oxygen-adsorbing potential of the ad-atoms and the current of formic acid oxidation on the ad-electrode. We obtain a contradic- tory conclusion in that the catalytic effect increases with increasing potential of oxygen adsorption of the ad-atoms. Hence the catalytic effect does not depend on the adsorption of oxygen species on ad-atoms.

![](./images/811193974499835904_10.jpg)

Fig. 10. Correlation between current density at 0.25 V (vs. RHE) and oxygen-adsorbing potential of the ad-atoms.

The catalytic effect of Bi ad-atoms, which do not adsorb oxygen species [17,18], on formic acid oxidation is greater than that of oxygen-adsorbing ad-atoms. As formic acid oxidation does not require oxygen donation, the striking enhancement effect by oxygen-adsorbing ad-atoms is not found.

## (VI) CONCLUSION

Formic acid oxidation is enhanced by geometrical control of sites by ad-atoms. The order of the enhancement effects is explained by the concepts of "$S_{\text{hole}}$" and "$S_{\text{R}}$".

There is no relation between the enhancement effect and other physical or chemical properties of the ad-atoms, i.e. the size, stability, Pauling's electronegativity, work function or adsorption of oxygen species.
