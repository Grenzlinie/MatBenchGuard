# Support promotion effect on the SO₂ and K⁺ co-poisoning resistance of MnO₂/TiO₂ for NH₃-SCR of NO

## ABSTRACT

Mn-based catalysts are expected to be applied for removing NOₓ due to its excellent low-temperature activity. However, the practical use of these catalysts is extremely restricted with the co-poisoning of alkali metal and SO₂ in the flue gas. Here the MnO₂/TiO₂ catalyst was employed to elucidate the co-poisoning mechanisms of K and SO₂ for the low temperature selective catalytic reduction (SCR) of NO. The physicochemical properties of catalysts under different toxicity conditions were studied by experiments. The adsorption of NH₃, SO₂, NO, and K on active component (MnO₂) and support (TiO₂) was studied by density functional theory. This work unravels a promotion effect of support on the alkali and sulfur resistance. The SO₂&K co-poisoning catalyst had higher SCR activity than the SO₂-poisoned and K-poisoned catalyst alone. For a fully toxic condition: (1) SO₂ was preferentially bonded with the terminated O site of MnO₂ inhibiting the dehydrogenation of NH₃ and redox cycle. (2) The presence of Lewis base (K atom) on the catalyst decreased the binding energy of a Lewis base (NH₃) and hindered the adsorption of NH₃. For the synergistic effect of K and SO₂, the majority of K adsorbed on the support (TiO₂) lead to increase alkalinity, which could promote the adsorption of SO₂ on the TiO₂ and reduce the toxicity of the active component (MnO₂).

### 1. Introduction

NOₓ emitted from power plants and industrial kilns not only damage ecological environment but also have a harmful impact on human health. How to reduce NOₓ emission is a hot issue in the international environmental field. The selective catalytic reduction of NOₓ with NH₃ (NH₃-SCR) is widely used in the NOₓ removal at stationary sources (Paolucci et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021; Han et al., 2019a; Liu et al., 2020). The design and preparation of catalyst with excellent performance is the key of this technique. The commercial V₂O₅-WO₃(-MoO₃)/TiO₂ catalysts, which has been successfully applied in thermal power plants, exhibit excellent activity in the temperature range of 300–400 °C (Jaegers et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al. 2016). However, vanadium based catalyst is not suitable for cement and glass plants because the exhaust temperature of these industrial kilns is usually less than 250 °C, and high SO₂ oxidation efficiency at the high temperature stage causing secondary pollution (Wu et al., 2020; Youn et al., 2018). Hence, it is essential to focus on the development of low-temperature catalyst with high activity and resistance to SO₂ and alkali. Many researchers have fully studied the composite oxide type, supported type and special configuration catalysts with MnOₓ, FeOₓ, CeOₓ and CuOₓ as active components (Xiang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019a; Wang et al., 2019; Mou et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2019b; Zhang et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019; Ali et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2018). Manganese based catalysts exhibit excellent low temperature SCR activity due to its variable valence state and redox ability. It indeed turns out that the MnOₓ/TiO₂ catalyst exhibits SCR activity in the low temperature (90–180 °C) (Zhang et al., 2012). However, there is still a small amount of SO₂ and alkali metal in the exhaust gas even after desulfurization device and particle controller, which lead to manganese based catalysts poisoning, seriously limiting their practical application.

Amounts of alkali is generated from the raw materials or coal such as in the cement production process, which was contained in fine fly ash and persisted in the downstream of the flue gas after dedusting (Zhang et al. 2017). Many researchers have investigated different poisoning mechanism of SO₂, alkali, and heavy metals under a single toxic condition. The deactivation of SO₂ can be classified into two categories: (1) deposition of ammonium sulfates blocking the active site, and (2) permanent and irreversible inactivation caused by sulfuration of active sites (Han et al., 2019b). The amount of catalysts' surface chemisorbed oxygen under the influence of different alkali metals and alkaline earth ions were compared, which indicated that the downward trend of toxicity was K > Na > Ca > Mg (Chen et al., 2011). The main causes of alkali metal poisoning are the decrease in acid strength and the loss of reducibility (Zha et al., 2018). Several studies have been carried out on co-poisoning of alkali metal and sulfur dioxide, demonstrating that the decrease in the metal atomic concentration, the changes of their oxidative states, and the variation in acidic properties of catalysts could be the reasons for the fluctuant changes of the catalytic activity in in- fluence of alkali metal and sulfur dioxide (Zhou et al., 2016). It should be noted that SO₂ was preferentially bonded with the surface heavy metal oxide in the co-existence of heavy metal (Cd) and SO₂ while active sites were released. The unexpected offset reserved the activity of catalyst (Yan et al., 2020). The deactivation of V₂O₅/TiO₂ catalysts increased due to the co-presence of SO₂ and KCl. The generated K₂S₂O₇ would be interacted with V₂O₅ to produce a eutectic. The generated eutectic significantly decreased NH₃ adsorption, particularly those on the Brønsted acid sites, and weakened the oxidation ability of the catalyst, thus significantly decreasing the SCR activity (Li et al., 2015). As is known, NH₃ and alkali metals are both alkaline, and SO₂ is acidic. When the active site of catalyst is acidic, it is beneficial to improve the ability to resist sulfur poisoning, while the ability to resist alkali poisoning is weakened. When the active site of the catalyst is alkaline, it is beneficial to improve the resistance to alkali metal and weaken the resistance to sulfur, in the meanwhile, it is not conducive to NH₃ adsorption leading to the decrease of reaction activity. The mechanisms of copoisoning of SO₂ and K⁺ over manganese based catalyst have not been clearly elucidated.

In this work, the synergistic effects of SO₂ and K⁺ deactivation over the MnO₂/TiO₂ catalyst for NH₃-SCR were unraveled. Combined with experiments and density functional theory calculation, the causes of poisoning and the regulation of the support's ability to resist poisoning were revealed at the atomic scale. It provides theoretical and experi- mental guidance for the design of the catalyst with outstanding resis- tance to poisoning, and is expected to realize the practical application of manganese based catalyst in the field of low temperature SCR.

## 2. Experiment

The MnO₂/TiO₂ catalyst was synthesized by an impregnation method, and labeled as "Fresh". The "SO₂-poi" catalyst was the fresh catalyst after being treated in a flow of SO₂ (100 ppm), balanced with N₂ at 150 °C for 10 h. The "K-poi catalyst" was prepared by impregnating the fresh MnO₂/TiO₂ catalyst in the potassium nitrate solution with the K/Mn atomic ratio of 0.5. The "SO₂&K-poi" catalyst was the "K-poi" catalyst after being treated in a flow of SO₂ (100 ppm), balanced with N₂ at 150 °C for 10 h. All the catalysts were characterized by various techniques. The typical reaction conditions were: 500 ppm NH₃, 500 ppm NO, 100 ppm SO₂, 3% O₂, 5% H₂O and N₂ balance, with a total flow rate of 200 ml min⁻¹, and the corresponding gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was 40,000 h⁻¹. Note that the adsorption energy data were the absolute value of the calculated value, and the larger value suggested a stronger interaction between adsorption substrate and gas. The detailed preparation process, characterization procedure, catalytic performance evaluation procedure, and computational methodology and model for DFT calculation were described in the Supporting Information.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. SCR performance

Fig. 1A showed the NOₓ conversion and N₂ selectivity of the fresh and poisoned catalysts at 150-300 °C under the typical reaction con- ditions. The fresh catalyst displayed 87% NO conversion and 96% N₂ selectivity at 150 °C. Furthermore, NO conversion at 180 °C remained stable at 90% within 30 h on-stream reaction time, indicating the catalyst possessed good hydrothermal stability (Fig. S1). As the tem- perature increased, the NO conversion rose to more than 90%, while the N₂ selectivity decreased gradually. The SO₂-poi catalyst exhibited lower NO conversion than the fresh catalyst, and N₂ selectivity was consistent with the fresh catalyst. The K-poi catalyst showed the lowest NO con- version, indicating that the toxicity of alkali metal to catalyst activity was greater than that of sulfur. When the temperature was lower than 175 °C, the influence of potassium element on catalyst activity was higher than that of sulfur dioxide, resulting in the NO conversion over the SO₂&K-poi catalyst was lower than that over the SO₂-poi catalyst. Interestingly, when the temperature was higher than 175 °C, the SO₂&K- poi catalyst exhibited higher SCR activity than the SO₂-poi and K-poi catalyst alone, which indicated further sulfation could alleviate the ef- fect of K poisoning to the catalyst, and there was a mutually restrictive relationship between SO₂ and K poisoning. When the temperature risen above 300 °C, the activity of the fresh and poi catalyst decreased, which was due to temperature rise lead to the direct oxidation of ammonia, and an increase of N₂O and other by-products (Wei et al., 2018). The oxidation of NO to NO₂ could greatly promote the SCR reaction through the "fast SCR" reaction. Fig. 1B showed the NO oxidation of the fresh and poi catalysts. It could be seen that the SO₂-poi and K-poi catalyst exhibited a noticeable drop of NO oxidation in comparison to the fresh and SO₂&K-poi catalyst, which was in good accordance with the results of NO conversion. It is very necessary to investigate the mechanism of mutual inhibition between K and SO₂ poisoning over the MnO₂/TiO₂ catalysts.

### 3.2. Morphology and texture of the catalyst

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the fresh and poisoned catalyst were shown in Fig. 2A. All of the samples exhibited character- istic peaks attributed to the anatase TiO₂. In addition, no characteristic peak was observed for α-MnO₂, indicating that MnO₂ was existed in amorphous phases on the catalyst surface or highly dispersed in the form of microcrystals. For the SO₂-poi and K-poi samples, the intensity and position of peaks did not change after being poisoned, indicating that the SO₂ or K poisoning had little effect on the crystal textures of catalyst. For the SO₂&K-poi sample, the characteristic peak at 30.8° and 29.8° were corresponded to the first and second diffraction peak of sarcanite K₂SO₄, and these diffraction peaks were not found for the K-poi sample, indi- cating that pre-deposited K⁺ on the catalyst surface reacted with gaseous SO₂ to form K₂SO₄ during SCR reaction.

The fresh catalyst presented high mesoporosity (Fig. 2B and Table S1). The specific surface area and pore volume of catalyst decreased in different degrees when the catalyst was poisoned by SO₂, K or SO₂&K. The SO₂&K-poi sample showed the greatest decrease in the surface area and pore volume in comparison to others due to the con- verage of K₂SO₄ on the catalyst, and this result was in consist with XRD. After SO₂ poisoning, there was no significant change in the specific surface area and pore volume of catalyst, indicating SO₂ poisoning had few influence on the texture of catalyst. In our previous work, the K-poi catalyst was also prepared by the same method, and EDS studies indi- cated the dispersion of potassium was lower than that of manganese and oxygen (Zhang et al., 2015). K poisoning changed the catalyst specific surface area significantly due to the uneven dispersion of K and its high loading (Peng et al., 2017).

### 3.3. Surface acidity and reducibility

In order to evaluate the acidity of catalysts before and after poisoned, NH₃-TPD in the temperature range of 100-700 °C was shown in the Fig. 2C. The desorption peaks at 100-400 °C and 400-700 °C could be due to the weak/medium-strong and strong acid sites, respectively (Yan et al., 2018). For the fresh catalyst, the desorption peak before 200 °C and in the region of 200-400 °C could be assigned to weak and strong acidity of the catalyst, respectively (Peng et al., 2017). After K poisoning, the amount of the desorbed NH₃ decreased, indicating the reduction of acidity of catalyst in comparison to the fresh catalyst. After SO₂ poisoning, the amount of absorbed NH₃ was even more than the fresh catalyst, indicating SO₂ could provide additional acid sites on the catalyst surface. After SO₂&K copoisoning, the acidity of the catalyst was maintained, and the desorption temperature increased slightly in com- parison to the fresh catalyst. This result suggested that the existence of K could reduce the binding of SO₂ to the surface lattice oxygen, while SO₂ could increase the acidity of the K-poi catalyst. The amount of NH₃ desorption calculated from the NH₃-TPD profiles indicated that the adsorption capacity of the SO₂&K-poi catalyst to NH₃ was the same as that of the fresh catalyst, and the K-poi catalyst had the weakest adsorption capacity for NH₃ (Table S1).

H₂-TPR were performed to analysis the effect of K and SO₂ on the redox properties of the fresh and poisoned catalysts, shown in Fig. 2D. Over the fresh catalyst, there were two reduction peaks at 386 and 564 °C, due to the reduction of MnO₂ to Mn₃O₄, and Mn₃O₄ to MnO, respectively (Tang et al., 2010). When K was added into the fresh catalyst, a new reduction peak appeared at 495 °C, due to the reduction of K₂O or the reduction peaks of Mn₃O₄ to MnO shifted to lower temperatures. It was indicated that K₂O enhanced the reduction of Mn³⁺ leading to the decrease of Mn⁴⁺, which was disadvantageous to the catalytic reaction. For the SO₂-poi catalyst, upshift of both the reduction peaks was found (20 °C), indicating the loss reducibility of catalyst was blocked by SO₂. For the SO₂&K copoisoning catalyst, the potassium ion of K₂SO₄ was easily reduced by hydrogen, which resulted in the reduction of SO₂&K-poi catalyst moved to lower temperature compared with the SO₂-poi and K-poi sample.

The corresponding O1s and Mn2p XPS spectra of the fresh and poisoned catalysts were presented in Fig. 3A and B to further elucidate the redox properties. Two kinds of surface oxygen species could be clearly observed in all the samples. The banding energy at 531.1 and 529.7 eV were corresponding to surface adsorbed oxygen (O_α) and lattice oxygen (O_β), respectively (Kang et al., 2019; Boningari et al., 2015). It has been suggested that the mobility of O_α was higher than O_β, which was critical to the circulation of active intermediates (Kang et al., 2019). The ratio of O_α calculated by O_α/(O_α + O_β) ranged from high to low of 34%, 19%, 17%, 16% for the fresh, SO₂&K-poi, K-poi, and SO₂-poi catalysts, respectively (Table S2). When the catalyst was poisoned with K or SO₂, the O_α ratio decreased obviously, due to K or SO₂ was bonded with surface oxygen yielding stable potassium oxide or sulfate species, and reducing the surface-activated oxygen and surface oxygen vacancies (Gu et al., 2010). Similarly, the O_α ratio of catalyst after the SO₂&K copoisoning was lower than the fresh catalyst, which also demonstrated the existence of the interaction between K and SO₂. The chemical environment might differ by the type or number of elements bound to the atom, or by the different chemical valence states of the atom. O1s spectra had shifted to lower binding energy, which was due to that K and SO₂ were adsorbed on the surface of the catalyst, and electrons were transferred to the catalyst, reduction reduced the electron binding energy of inner layer. The deconvoluted Mn 2p₃/₂ spectra showed two peaks with different binding energies. The banding energy at 642.8 and 641.6 eV were attributed to Mn⁴⁺ and Mn³⁺ species, respectively (Xiong et al., 2015). The ratio of Mn³⁺ calculated by Mn³⁺/(Mn⁴⁺ + Mn³⁺) was 35%, 39%, and 42% for the fresh, SO₂&K-poi, and K-poi catalysts, and then increased to 60% for the SO₂-poi catalyst (Table S2). To confirm the presence of S and K species on the poisoned catalyst, S 2p and K 2p spectra were measured (Fig. 3C and D). The peaks at 168.1 eV were attributed to S⁶⁺ species, indicating the presence of SO₄²⁻ on the catalyst surface. The peaks at 292.8 eV were assigned to K⁺ species for the K-poi and SO₂&K-poi catalysts. These results indicated the poisoning molecule could be adsorbed on the surface of catalyst during the SCR reaction, and Mn⁴⁺ was converted into Mn³⁺ in the presence of SO₂, and K also caused some Mn⁴⁺ to be reduced. It has been demonstrated that SO₂ could be oxidized to SO₄²⁻ by the surface lattice oxygen in MnO₂, which lead to the partial reduction of Mn⁴⁺ (Peng et al., 2017). The Mn³⁺ ratio of SO₂&K-poi catalyst was close to that of fresh catalyst, which also confirmed the offset effects of SO₂ and K copoisoning.

### 3.5. Insight into the promotion effect of support on the alkali and sulfur
resistance

To understand the synergistic effects of alkali metals and sulfur di-
oxide on the catalyst surface and SCR reaction process from a molecular
level view, the adsorption energy, population analysis and partial den-
sity of states (PDOS) were calculated based on density function theory
(DFT). According to the above crystal phase analysis, the catalyst was a
mixed oxide composed of α-MnO₂ and anatase-TiO₂. Our previous work
had reported that α-MnO₂ played the role of the catalytic component,
while anatase-TiO₂ played the role of support with high specific surface
area providing the necessary space for the reaction (Wei et al., 2018). In
consequence, the plate model of manganese oxide and titanium oxide
was established to illustrate the effects of SO₂ and K poisoning on the
active components, and the promotion effects of the support on the
anti-toxic ability. The values of the adsorption energies were summa-
rized in Fig. 5.

Both type and coordination number of atom on the surface of catalyst
had an impact on the adsorption property of active site responsible for
SCR catalytic performance (Sun et al., 2011). The different molecules
involved in the SCR reaction show different adsorptive tendency to the
active sites on the catalyst surface. For the optimization structure of
α-MnO₂(100), there were two structurally inequivalent oxygen sites.
The single coordinated oxygen O(1) was beneficial for ion adsorption,
such as H⁺, and the bridging oxygen O(2) with only 2-fold Mn ions
coordination (Fig. S2), was likely to be a site of facile oxygen vacancy
formation. The (100) surface Mn(1) and Mn(2) ions had 5 and 6 fold
coordination, respectively. Comparing the adsorption energy of

Indeed, Mn(2) was less electrophilic, similar to that occurred in the octahedra of the bulk α-MnO₂ crystal. On the contrary, Mn(1) was Lewis acidic because of its inadequate coordination number. Result of the mulliken charge analysis presented the charge difference of adsorbents and active sites upon adsorption to the catalyst (Table S3) (Sanville et al., 2007). NH₃ molecule was oxidized by transferring electrons to the Mn(1) Lewis acid site and adjacent oxygen atoms. One H atom of activated NH₃ molecule was likely trapped by the surrounding single coordinated O(1), and this oxidation process lead to the formation of [NH₂], which was a key step in the SCR reaction cycle.

Considering stability of adsorption structure and electronegativity of each element, the SO₂ molecule was more like to adsorb at single coordinated oxygen O(1) in the way of S–O double bond, and the adsorption energy was −0.79 eV (Fig. S4). The mulliken population analysis indicated that S was oxidized by donation electrons not only to the O(1) atom on the surface but also to the O atom of SO₂, which meant the formation of sulfites (Table S4). The presence of sulfites on the surface of catalyst resulted in the loss of oxidizability of O(1), which blocked the dehydrogenation of NH₃ adsorbed on the Mn(1) Lewis acid site. To further elucidate the electron changes of the adsorbents and active sites during the adsorption process, PDOS of related atom was calculated. Alkali metals, as impurity components of the catalyst, participated in the gas-solid reaction with SO₂, NO, and/or NH₃. Therefore, the interaction of K⁺ and catalyst had not been analyzed through PDOS. Fig. 6A and B showed the projected density of s/p/d state of NH₃/Mn(1) and SO₂/O(1) before and after adsorption. The electron densities of s and p orbitals of NH₃ molecule corresponded to the sum of electron densities of s and p orbitals of N atom and three H atoms, respectively, and so did SO₂. The s/p state of adsorbed NH₃ shifted to the low energy significantly, which meant that energy of electron in NH₃ adsorbed on the surface of MnO₂(100) decreased, and NH₃ was more stable in the adsorbed state than in the gas phase. For the Mn(1) Lewis acid site, its s and p state was strongly hybridized with the s and p state of NH₃ as inferred from the characteristic overlap at −8.1 eV. The new peak of d state at −1.6 eV was obtained demonstrating Mn(1) acquired electron. This result indicated the existence of unoccupied d at the bottom of conduction bands was the main reason for its Lewis acidity of Mn(1). S and O(1) of MnO₂(100) were held together strongly through covalent bonding, and this interaction was indicated by the overlapping O(1) p state and SO₂ p state in the region of −6.5 to −0.3 eV. Compared with free SO₂, the s state of adsorbed SO₂ broadened and moved further away from the Fermi level (located at 0 eV). This shift indicated that SO₂ in the gas phase could spontaneously adsorb on the O(1) of MnO₂(100). Comparing the distribution of the O(1) p state at the top of valence band, it was clear to see that after SO₂ adsorption, the position of p state were at lower energies and further away to the Fermi level than those before SO₂ adsorption. The top position of valence band away from the Fermi level indicated a lower reactivity (Mcfarland and Metiu, 2013). These results confirmed that the oxidation of O(1) decreased after the formation of sulfites.

NO was more inclined to adsorb on the top sites of Mn(1) Lewis site in the way of O-down orientation, and the adsorption energy was −0.46 eV (Fig. S4). The mulliken population analysis indicated that after adsorption on the catalyst the overall charge of NO rose to 0.275 e, and electrons were transferred from adsorbents to the catalyst (Table S5). The bonding strength between adspecies and surface atoms could be analyzed by comparing the degree of overlapping between the density peaks and their position with respect to the Fermi level (Negreira and Wilcox, 2013). The result of the adsorption energies on MnO₂(100) indicated NO had the lowest adsorption energies compared with SO₂ and NH₃ (Fig. 5). The s, p and d state of Mn(1) did not change significantly before and after adsorption (Fig. 6C). In addition, the state density of NO did not show distinct hybridization with Mn(1). The above results showed that although there were electrons transfer between NO and Mn, the relationship between them was weak physisorption. The adsorption energy of SO₂ and NH₃ on catalyst was higher than that of NO, and presented obvious overlap of the state density peaks, corresponding to strong chemisorption. NH₃ and NO adsorbed on the same active site belonged to a competitive adsorption relationship, and NH₃ was more prone to occupy Mn(1) Lewis acid site preferentially. The SCR reaction occurred between adsorbed NH₃ and gaseous NO, following the Eley-Rideal mechanism. SO₂ was more likely to be adsorbed at the terminated O(1) site, had little influence on the adsorption process of NH₃ and NO, and mainly affected the SCR reaction by inhibiting the dehydrogenation of NH₃.

The optimized structure, population analysis and PDOS for anatase TiO₂(001) were shown in the supporting information. There were two different adsorption sites on the TiO₂(001), namely bridging oxygen O, and 5 fold coordination Ti (Fig. S3). According to the minimum energy principle, NH₃, SO₂ and NO tended to be adsorbed at Ti site, and their adsorption energies were −1.28, −1.11, and −1.10 eV, respectively (Fig. 5). PDOS of NH₃ after adsorption on the surface of TiO₂(001) was similar to that of MnO₂(100), which indicated Ti was Lewis acid site (Fig. S6). Comparing the contribution of d state to the conduct band, it was clear to see that d state distribution of Mn site on MnO₂(100) was wider than that of Ti on TiO₂(001), which demonstrated the Lewis acidity of Mn(1) was stronger than that of Ti (Blake et al., 1966). The activity sequence was consistent with the adsorption energy of NH₃ on these two sites. In the case of SO₂ adsorption, the absolute value of adsorption energy on TiO₂(001) was greater than that on MnO₂(100), and s and p state of SO₂ was strongly hybridized with the s and p state of Ti at −2.1, −5.2, and −9.3 eV (Fig. S6). These results indicated that SO₂ preferentially adsorbed on Ti site. Therefore, TiO₂ as a support could weaken the toxic influence of SO₂ on the active O(1) of MnO₂(100). In the case of NO adsorption, the hybridization between Ti and NO could be found, corresponding to strong chemisorption.

To understand the nature of the poison effect of potassium ions on catalyst, structure of K⁺ doped catalyst was established and optimized initially. Because the atomic radius of potassium (2.34 Å) was much larger than that of manganese (1.32 Å) and oxygen (0.66 Å), the deposition of potassium atoms on the surface of manganese oxide was unstable. Tang et al. reported K⁺ coordinated by eight lattice oxygen of α-MnO₂ on the tunnel walls arrived at a steady state (Chen et al., 2016).

The optimization of $K^{+}$ located at the central vacancy on $\alpha$-MnO₂(001) with single-walled structure was shown in the Fig. 6G. The adsorption energy was $-6.28$ eV. In order to make a contrast with the above content, the influence of $K^{+}$ atom on the electronic structure of the active site on $\alpha$-MnO₂(001), namely the 5 fold coordinated Mn(1) and the single coordinated oxygen O(1), was studied mainly.

Topsoe had indicated that the SCR reaction over heterogeneous catalysts, followed Eley-Rideal mechanism, and included both acid and redox reactions (Kumar et al., 2015). The electronic states of Lewis acid sites determined acid reaction rates. The ability of electron transport of catalyst was associated with the electron exchanges between reactants/products and catalyst in redox reactions. As a Lewis base, NH₃ tended to adsorb at Lewis acid sites with lower electron density, and then the lower electron density of Mn(1) site had stronger ability to accept electron. When $K^{+}$ was doped on MnO₂, the Mulliken charge of Mn(1) decreased from 1.15 to 1.09, indicating that Mn(1) gained electron and the electron density increased. Fig. 6G showed the differential charge density of K/MnO₂. It presented the change of local electron density caused by K adsorption on MnO₂ surface, in which red indicated the increase of electron density, while blue indicated the opposite. The results indicated that potassium is Lewis alkaline. Furthermore, the d state of Mn(1) had shift towards the Fermi level after $K^{+}$ doping, indicating tight $K^{+}$ could lower the acidity of Mn(1). Band gap, calculated from the results of band structure, for $K^{+}$ doped MnO₂ (0.33 eV) was slightly wider than that for MnO₂ (0.24 eV), indicating the electron transfer was blocked in the $K^{+}$ doped MnO₂. As known, band gap was usually related to the surface reactivity (Calatayud et al., 2005; Sayle et al., 2005). The redox reactions were more likely to occur over catalyst with smaller band gap or no gap than the catalyst with a larger one.

In the experiment, $K^{+}$ ions were deposited on the surface of catalyst by impregnation initially, and then the effect of $K^{+}$ poisoning on the catalyst was studied. Hence, in the part of theoretical calculation, the influence of $K^{+}$ on the activity of catalyst had been examined by comparing the adsorption energies and electron structure of reactants on $K^{+}$ doped model, as shown in the Figs. S5 and S6. The preferred orientation for SO₂ on the $K^{+}$ doped MnO₂ was similar to that on the undoped one (Fig. S4). The mulliken population analysis indicated that the S atom of SO₂ was oxidized by O(1) site (Table S4). The adsorption energy of SO₂ on $K^{+}$ doped MnO₂ ($-1.21$ eV) was larger than that on undoped MnO₂ ($-0.79$ eV). Comparing the distribution of p state of SO₂ to the

Fermi level, it could be seen that energy distribution of p state of $SO_2$ on $K^+/MnO_2$ was lower than that on $MnO_2$. Similarly, the adsorption energy of $SO_2$ on the surface of $K^+/MnO_2$ was lower than that on $MnO_2$. More importantly, after adsorption of $SO_2$, the left shift of p state peaks of O(1) on $K^+/MnO_2$ was greater than that on the fresh $MnO_2$, which indicated the decrease of O(1) oxidizability could be attributed to the synergistic toxicity of $SO_2$ and $K^+$. The existence of $K^+$ promoted the adsorption process of $SO_2$. Hence, more O(1) active sites were occupied by $SO_2$, and then lost oxidative activity, causing the formation of $[NH_2]$ intermediate was inhibited severely.

The effect of $K^+$ on $NH_3$ adsorption was also discussed. The adsorption energy of $NH_3$ on $K^+$ doped $MnO_2$ ($-0.77$ eV) was much less than that on undoped $MnO_2$ ($-1.59$ eV). The same trend could be seen in Table S3. The mulliken charge of $NH_3$ adsorbed on $K^+/MnO_2$ ($0.29$ e) was smaller than that on undoped $MnO_2$ ($0.36$ e), indicating the presence of $K^+$ reduced the number of electron transferred from $NH_3$ to the catalyst upon adsorption. The PDOS result indicated that the s and p state of $NH_3$ in the $K^+/MnO_2$ model was closer to the Fermi level than that in the $MnO_2$ model, and the d state of Mn(1) remained consistent before and after adsorption, confirming the adsorption energy of $NH_3$ on Mn(1) site in the $MnO_2$ model was bigger than that in the $K^+/MnO_2$ model. These results indicated that $K^+$ reduced the Lewis acidity of Mn (1), resulting in a decrease in the adsorption amount of $NH_3$. It also could be deduced from the adsorption energy and electron transfer result that $K^+$ inhibited the adsorption of $NH_3$. In other words, the presence of Lewis base (K atom) on the catalyst decreased the binding energy of Lewis base ($NH_3$). Since NO usually participated in SCR reaction in gaseous form, the decrease of NO adsorption had little influence on SCR reaction.

In the case of $TiO_2(001)$, the adsorption energy of $K^+$ was $-4.34$ eV less than that on $MnO_2(100)$. This result could be attributed to the surface acidity of $MnO_2(100)$ greater than that of $TiO_2(001)$. Our previous work reported that the catalyst with the molar ratio of Mn/Ti being 0.25 presented the best activity in the low temperature SCR. Although $K^+$ ions were more likely to deposit on the surface of $MnO_2$, the content of $TiO_2$ as a supporter in actual catalyst was much higher than that of $MnO_2$ as the active component, and $TiO_2$ had a high specific surface area, so the majority of $K^+$ should adsorb on the surface of $TiO_2$. The adsorption energy and electron transfer of $NH_3$ and NO adsorbed on $K^+/TiO_2$ were less than that on $TiO_2$. Besides, there was no overlapping of PDOS between the reactants and active sites. This result, consistent with that for the $MnO_2$, indicated that deposition of $K^+$ on the catalyst weakened the activity of acid sites. For the $K^+/TiO_2$ model, the p state of $SO_2$ was strongly hybridized, with the s and p state of Ti(1) as inferred from the characteristic overlap in the region of $-7$ to $-1.8$ eV. It is worth noting that the p state of $SO_2$ adsorbed on the $K^+/TiO_2$ showed stronger delocalization than that on the $TiO_2$ without $K^+$ deposition, indicating stronger chemical bonds formed between adsorbed molecules and surface atom of catalyst, thus the adsorption process released more energy.

The schematic diagram of synergistic effects of $SO_2$ and $K^+$ on the $MnO_2/TiO_2$ catalyst in the SCR reaction was shown in Fig. 7. In the absence of $K^+$ and $SO_2$, two separate catalytic reactions, that is acid and redox reactions, occurred on the $MnO_2/TiO_2$ catalyst. As an initial reaction, $NH_3$ adsorbed on a $Mn^{4+}$ Lewis acid site, and then the adsorbed $NH_3$ was activated by the transfer of a H to the $Mn^{4+}-O$ site, which became partly reduced. In the second step, the activated $NH_2$ reacted with the gaseous NO, and the obtained intermediate undergone rapid decomposition to yield $H_2O$ and $N_2$. In the last step, $Mn^{4+}$ Lewis acid site was regenerated, and the $Mn^{3+}-O$ site would be oxidized to $Mn^{4+}-O$ by the gaseous $O_2$ oxidation. In the presence of $K^+$ and $SO_2$, $K^+$ acted as Lewis base, reducing activity of $Mn^{4+}$ Lewis acid site on the catalyst and hindering adsorption of $NH_3$ and acid reactions. In the meanwhile, S atoms of $SO_2$ were easily oxidized by terminated O(1) sites to form sulfate, which inhibited the transfer of H atom and redox reactions. Furthermore, $K^+$ promoted the adsorption of $SO_2$ for both $MnO_2$ and $TiO_2$, and the synergistic effect of the two factors had a greater impact on the activity of catalyst. Since $MnO_2$ was more acidic than $TiO_2$, $NH_3$ and $K^+$ tended to adsorb on $MnO_2$ while $SO_2$ tended to adsorb on the surface of $TiO_2$. Under practical conditions, $K^+$ was usually deposited in catalysts in the way of solid particles, and majority of $K^+$ adsorbed on $TiO_2$. Therefore, more $SO_2$ could be adsorbed on $TiO_2$ under the influence of $K^+$, reducing the toxicity of the active component $MnO_2$.

## 4. Conclusions

The co-poisoning mechanisms of $K^{+}$ and $SO_{2}$ over $MnO_{2}/TiO_{2}$ for low temperature SCR was investigated through experiment and DFT calculation. The experimental results show that the $SO_{2}\&K^{+}$ co-poisoning catalyst had higher SCR activity than the $SO_{2}$ poisoned and $K^{+}$ poisoned catalyst alone. Comparing the adsorption process of $NH_{3}$, $SO_{2}$, $NO$, and $K^{+}$ on active component ($MnO_{2}$) and support ($TiO_{2}$), we found that $SO_{2}$ was preferentially bonded with the terminated O site of $MnO_{2}$ inhibiting the dehydrogenation of $NH_{3}$ and redox cycle, and the presence of Lewis base ($K^{+}$) on the catalyst decreased the binding energy of a Lewis base ($NH_{3}$) and hindered the adsorption of $NH_{3}$. For the synergistic effect of $K^{+}$ and $SO_{2}$, the majority of $K^{+}$ adsorbed on the support ($TiO_{2}$) lead to increase alkalinity, which could promote the adsorption of $SO_{2}$ on the $TiO_{2}$ and reduce the toxicity of the active component ($MnO_{2}$).
