## ABSTRACT:
Electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) to high-value-added products is a promising strategy for mitigating the greenhouse effect and energy shortage. Designing a high-performance electrocatalyst with a low limit potential and tunable reaction path is a critical challenge for $CO_2$ reduction. Two-dimensional (2D) nanostructured materials are considered as competitive catalysts for electrochemical reduction due to their large specific surface area and rich active sites. The current work theoretically evaluates four 2D MBene nanosheets as potential catalysts for $CO_2$ reduction. It is found that $Mo_2B_2$ and $Cr_2B_2$ show good catalytic selectivity due to their poor hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) performance and low limit potential for $CO_2$ reduction. We found that the Gibbs energy increase for CHO formation is the highest on all MBenes. Among them, $Mo_2B_2$ and $Cr_2B_2$ maintain a lower limit potential with values of $-0.45$ and $-0.5$ eV, respectively. The electronic structure analysis demonstrates that the electron migration from MBene substrates to the antibonding states of adsorbates can lower the Gibbs free energy of hydrogenation reactions of intermediate products.

## 1. INTRODUCTION
In past years, carbon emissions have been more massive than before due to the extensive consumption of fossil energy. $^{1}$ On the other hand, carbon dioxide, which is the major product of fossil combustion and abundant in the atmosphere, can be the raw material for producing hydrocarbons and alcohols. $^{2}$ Moreover, capturing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere can reduce the greenhouse effect and improve people's living environment. Many transition metals have been investigated for catalyzing $CO_2$ reduction, $^{3,4}$ and copper and Cu-based alloys exhibit high selectivity for producing hydrocarbons. $^{5-7}$ According to previous theoretical calculations, the equilibrium potential for reducing $CO_2$ to methane is $0.17$ V, $^{8}$ and the $CO_2$ reduction reaction ($CO_2RR$) will compete with the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). $^{9}$ In an aqueous electrolyte, the equilibrium potential of HER on Cu is comparable to the values of many $CO_2RR$ products. $^{10}$ It is worth noting that a low HER onset potential can suppress the Faraday efficiency (FE) of $CO_2RR$. Therefore, the HER performance of a $CO_2RR$ catalyst should be weak.

In addition to traditional metal and alloy materials, there are some studies of $CO_2RR$ on two-dimensional (2D) materials. Compared with the traditional metal materials, two-dimensional materials like 2D transition-metal carbides (MXenes) have an outstanding catalytic ability due to their numerous active sites and stronger atomic conductivity. $^{11-13}$ Qiao et al. confirmed in experiments that $Ti_3C_2$ is a highly efficient photocatalyst for hydrogen production. $^{14}$ Sun et al. theoretically evaluated several MXenes as $CO_2RR$ catalysts. $^{15}$ Similar to MXenes, 2D layered transition-metal boride (MBene) nanosheets are produced by chemically etching Al atoms in the metal aluminum boride (MAB) phase. $^{16}$ In the theoretical part, Huang's simulation of nitrogen reduction on MBenes illustrates the excellent stability of MBenes. $^{17}$ Jiang et al. predicted twelve stable MBenes using a density functional theory (DFT) simulation, and some phases have a high Curie temperature. $^{18}$ A few efforts have been made to evaluate MBenes as catalysts for boosting electrochemical reactions. Guo et al. studied HER on $Mo_2B_2$ and $Fe_2B_2$. They found that these materials exhibited an outstanding catalytic capacity. $^{19}$ Yang et al. theoretically investigated $N_2$ electrochemical reduction on other ten MBenes and reported that B atoms could act as active sites due to the partially occupied p orbitals. $^{20}$ These studies demonstrated that MBenes could be competitive candidates as electrochemical catalysts.

In this study, $CO_2RR$ for producing methane on four MBenes ($Cr_2B_2$, $Mn_2B_2$, $Fe_2B_2$, $Mo_2B_2$) is systematically studied and compared with that on the Cu(111) surface.

First-principles calculations show that the electrocatalytic performance of some MBenes is higher than that of the Cu(111) surface, which indicates that MBenes have advantages over the traditional metallic catalyst. Bader charge and the local density of states (LDOS) are analyzed for understanding the catalytic mechanism at the electronic structure level. The results show that the adjustment of the boron layer of MBenes to the charge environment on the metal surface is the reason for the unique electrocatalytic performance. In general, the electrocatalytic $CO_2$RR reaction of the MBene material was evaluated by a theoretical calculation. Furthermore, we provide some suggestions for the design of the material in the field of MBene electrocatalysis.

## 2. COMPUTATIONAL METHODS

All calculations in this work are performed by the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) $^{21,22}$ based on density functional theory (DFT). $^{23}$ Blöchl all-electron-like projector-augmented wave (PAW) method deals with the interaction between electrons and ion cores. $^{24,25}$ The exchange−correlation functional is the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE). $^{26}$ The highest cutoff energy is set as 450 eV. The Fermi scheme is used to determine the electron occupancies, and smearing energy is set as 0.05 eV. All structures are optimized until the force is less than 0.02 eV/Å, and the energy is less than $10^{-5}$ eV. K-points use the Monkhorst−Pack method and are set to 5 × 5 × 1 for surface optimization and 10 × 10 × 10 for molecule optimization. $^{27}$ The spin polarization is considered for magnetic atoms in all simulations. The DFT-D3 correction is used to deal with van der Waals interactions between adsorbents and surfaces. $^{28}$ Solvation effects in adsorption models are expressed in powers of the implicit solvent model. $^{29,30}$ The Vaspkit package and VESTA package are used for data processing. $^{31,32}$ The Bader charge analysis is performed to calculate the charge occupancy of each atom. $^{33}$ Four MBene materials are chosen for this work, namely, $Cr_2B_2$, $Mo_2B_2$, $Fe_2B_2$, and $Mn_2B_2$. As shown in Figure 1, all MBene models have four layers: the upper and lower layers are metals and the middle two are boron atoms. In all simulations, the upper two layers are allowed to relax and the bottom two layers are fixed. A vacuum layer as large as 20 Å is added to avoid the interaction between two consecutive slabs along the thickness direction. A 3 × 3 supercell is used for $CO_2$ adsorption and subsequent hydrogenation calculations for mitigating interactions between adsorbates induced by a periodic boundary condition. The energies of isolated $CO_2$, $H_2O$, and $H_2$ molecules are calculated in a box of $20 × 20 × 20$ $\mathring{A}^3$.

During the electrochemical reduction process, the Gibbs energy profile plays a key role in evaluating the performance of the catalyst. The following formula was used for calculating the Gibbs free energy change from the initial state to the final state in the proposed reaction path

$$
\Delta G = \Delta \text{DFT} + \Delta \text{ZPE} - T \Delta S \tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta \text{DFT}$ is the energy change between two states, $\Delta \text{ZPE}$ is the zero-point energy change between two states, $T$ is the operating temperature, set as 298.15 K, and $\Delta S$ is the entropy change.

The computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) proposed by Nørskov in 2004 was used to deal with the hydrogen electrode. $^{34}$ The following formula expresses the potential of the relative hydrogen electrode

$$
\mu(\text{H}^+) + \mu(\text{e}^-) = 1/2\mu(\text{H}_2) - eU \tag{2}
$$

The free energy of the proton−electron pair is as follows

$$
G(\text{H}^+ + \text{e}^-) = 1/2G(\text{H}_2) \tag{3}
$$

A reaction with the proton−electron pair has charge transfer, and it is affected by free energy. The free energy calculation for this reaction is shown in the following formulas

$$
^*\text{R} + \text{H}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow ^*\text{RH} \tag{4}
$$

$$
\Delta G = G(^*\text{RH}) - G(^*\text{R}) - 1/2G(\text{H}_2) \tag{5}
$$

where R indicates the reactant and * indicates the active site on the catalyst surface.

The reaction path proposed in this study is shown in Figure 2. $^{8,35}$ The whole mechanism has six steps, and there are five proton−electron pair transfers, resulting in five carbon-related intermediates. According to the Gibbs free energy change between two consecutive steps, the limit potential can be predicted.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### 3.1. $CO_2$ to $COOH$.
For $CO_2$RR on a Cu catalyst, the Cu(111) surface has the highest Faraday efficiency for $CH_4$ formation,³⁶ so we choose the Cu(111) surface as the baseline for evaluating the $CO_2$RR performance of MBenes. First, $CO_2$ adsorption on the surface of Cu and MBenes is investigated. As shown in Figure 3, MBenes provide a stronger affinity to $CO_2$ than the Cu(111) surface. Among the four MBenes considered in this study, $Mo_2B_2$ has the strongest affinity to $CO_2$ with an adsorption energy of $-1.97$ eV, which is followed by $Cr_2B_2$ with an adsorption energy of $-1.15$ eV and $Mn_2B_2$ with an adsorption energy of $-0.40$ eV. $Fe_2B_2$ exhibits the weakest interaction with $CO_2$, and the adsorption energy is only $-0.33$ eV. C−O bond lengths and O−C−O bond angles are analyzed in detail to explore the geometry structure of $CO_2$ on MBenes and Cu(111) (shown in Table 1). The Cu(111) surface does not affect the geometric properties of $CO_2$. It is found that the $C\overset{=}{-}O$ bonds lengths are $1.18$ Å, and the $O\overset{=}{-}C\overset{=}{-}O$ angle remains at $180^\circ$. Compared with $CO_2$ on the Cu(111) surface, the configuration of $CO_2$ adsorbed on the MBene surface is significantly changed. As shown in Figure S1, the bridge site on MBenes is the most energetically preferred site for capturing a $CO_2$ molecule. The $C\overset{=}{-}O$ bonds of $CO_2$ on MBenes are longer than $1.23$ Å, and the $O\overset{=}{-}C\overset{=}{-}O$ angle on MBenes is around $140^\circ$. For example, the bond angle of $CO_2$ on $Cr_2B_2$ is $135.76^\circ$.

The electronic structure is analyzed to understand the interaction between $CO_2$ and the substrate. The Bader charge analysis shows that electrons tend to move to the adsorbed $CO_2$. The net charge of $CO_2$ molecules on $Mo_2B_2$, $Cr_2B_2$, $Mn_2B_2$, and $Fe_2B_2$ is $-0.92|e|$, $-0.80|e|$, $-0.60|e|$, and $-0.51|e|$, respectively. To further clarify the charge environment of $CO_2$ on different surfaces, the local density of states (LDOS) is calculated, and the result is shown in Figure 4. We suppose that these electrons are filled into the LUMO of $CO_2$ and activate $CO_2$, which can facilitate the hydrogenation of $CO_2$ to $COOH$.

For the subsequent hydrogenation process, the proton−electron pair bonds with oxygen in carbon dioxide to form $COOH$. Moreover, we also consider the possibility of HCOO- formic formation. The calculation results can be seen in Figure S2, and we find uphill free energy for formic formation on MBenes with values of $+0.74$ to $+1.13$ eV. From these results, we do not think that HCOOH is a key product on MBenes. For the Cu(111) surface, referring to the previous work, we hypothesize that gaseous $CO_2$ combines with the proton−electron pair to generate $COOH$. $COOH$ is adsorbed on the surface, and the free energy variation of the whole process is $+0.53$ eV. Among MBene materials, $Mo_2B_2$ exhibits the Gibbs free energy change of $+0.44$ eV and the other MBenes show a significantly lower Gibbs free energy variation ranging from $-0.22$ to $+0.21$ eV. As discussed above, MBenes considered in this study perform much better than the Cu(111) surface for producing $COOH$. Although these MBene materials have extraordinary ability to capture $CO_2$, too high adsorption energy can hinder the further hydrogenation of $CO_2$ to $COOH$. As shown in Figure 3, $Mo_2B_2$ exhibits the strongest adsorption interaction with $CO_2$, but the following hydrogenation reaction also suffers from the most significant increase of Gibbs free energy. It can be inferred that the $CO_2$ adsorption energy should be maintained at a moderate value, in which case both $CO_2$ adsorption and hydrogenation can be facilitated.

### 3.2. $COOH$ to $CHO$.
$COOH$ can either be reduced to $*CO$ by releasing an $H_2O$ molecule or be reduced to formic acid (HCOOH). Experimental studies reported that a small amount of HCOOH could be observed during the reduction process.³⁶ The result, which is shown in Figure S3, suggests that the free energy of formic acid formation on MBenes is higher than free energy of CO formation. In this theoretical study, the reaction Gibbs free energy for reducing $*COOH$ to $*CO$ is exothermic. As shown in Figure 3, all surfaces have a negative Gibbs free energy variation, which means $*COOH$ can be spontaneously converted to $*CO$ in the electrochemical reduction condition. $Fe_2B_2$ performs best for converting

*COOH to *CO with a Gibbs energy drop of 0.64 eV, followed by $Mn_2B_2$ ($\Delta G = -0.56$ eV), $Cr_2B_2$ ($\Delta G = -0.32$ eV), and $Mo_2B_2$ ($\Delta G = -0.11$ eV). Converting *COOH to *CO on the Cu(111) surface is also exothermic, and the energy drop is 0.6 eV. Numerous experiments and calculations prove that CO is one of the most important intermediates in $CO_2RR.^{37,38}$ As discussed above, MBenes are competitive candidates for electrochemically producing CO from $CO_2$.

CO can be reduced to COH or CHO at the next hydrogenation step. Figure S4 shows that formation of COH encounters a higher Gibbs free energy increase than CHO. Hence, the former one and the corresponding reaction path are not considered in this study. Previous studies reported that CHO formation suffers from a high Gibbs free energy increase on most surfaces of catalysts. $^{39,40}$ Therefore, reducing the Gibbs free energy of CHO formation will benefit methane formation. After full optimization, we get a series of stable adsorption sites in Figure S1. CHO on MBenes and Cu(111) presents a state where the C$-$O bond is located on the bridge site, while the C$-$H bond is above the C atom. According to Figure 3, reducing CO to CHO has the highest energy for synthesizing $CH_4$ on the Cu(111) surface due to the corresponding Gibbs energy increase of 0.82 eV. The energy increase of reducing CO to CHO is 0.79 eV on $Fe_2B_2$, 0.74 eV on $Mn_2B_2$, and 0.50 eV on $Cr_2B_2$. In all of the MBene catalysts, $Mo_2B_2$ has the lowest Gibbs free energy increase of 0.45 eV. These results demonstrate that $Fe_2B_2$ and $Mn_2B_2$ have no apparent advantages over Cu(111) in CHO formation because of the high Gibbs free energy increase. $Cr_2B_2$ and $Mo_2B_2$ are easier to get CHO than the other three catalysts, and these two catalysts also have a lower Gibbs free energy increase for COOH formation.

The Bader charge analysis is performed for providing electronic structure insight toward the CO hydrogenation reaction. It is found that the net charge of CO molecules on $Mo_2B_2$ and $Cr_2B_2$ is $-1.38|e|$ and $-1.33|e|$, respectively, and these two MBenes exhibit a better CO hydrogenation performance than the other two. For CO molecules adsorbed on $Mn_2B_2$ and $Fe_2B_2$, the adsorbates only gain $-0.30|e|$ and $-0.24|e|$, respectively. It can be inferred that electrons transfer from MBenes to the antibonding $\pi$ orbitals of the adsorbed CO molecule. The occupation of antibonding states can weaken the $C\equiv O$ triple bond and facilitate CHO formation. It is also found that the electron migration is dependent on the CO adsorption configuration. On $Mo_2B_2$ and $Cr_2B_2$, CO preferred to be adsorbed on the hollow site, and in this configuration the C atom coordinates with four transition- metal atoms from the substrate, while on $Mn_2B_2$ and $Fe_2B_2$, CO can only coordinate with one transition-metal atom.

3.3. CHO to $CH_4$. After CHO formation, proton$-$electron pairs are continuously added to the C atom until $CH_4$ is formed and leaves the surface. From Figure S5, we found that $CH_2O$ is adsorbed on bridge site C close to the surface, while $CH_3O$ tends to be adsorbed on the bridge site as O is close to the surface. All of the free energy changes in these steps are shown in the reactions of Figure 3e$-$g. $CO_2$RR on Cu(111) shows a downtrend, while some steps have a tiny Gibbs free energy increase on MBenes. These results also confirm that the previous CHO formation step was the highest Gibbs free energy increase. For $CH_2O$ formation, $Cr_2B_2$ and $Mo_2B_2$ show a downhill energy change, while $Fe_2B_2$ and $Mn_2B_2$ show an uphill energy change. However, to be clear, all of the steps do not have a higher Gibbs free energy increase than the previous steps shown in Figure 3.

On the hydrogenation process after CHO, methanol as a possible byproduct is also considered. Here, two reaction paths are proposed implicitly considering the solvent environment, and the results are shown in Figures S6 and S7 in the Supporting Information. From Figure S6, we can find that there are uphill reactions with a value of +0.14 to +0.92 eV. However, compared to the formation of $CH_3O$, $Fe_2B_2$ tends to get $CH_2OH$ and further generates methanol. However, in Figure S7, we can see that the hydrogenation of $CH_3O$ encounters a large energy increase on all MBenes, which means that $CH_3O$ on MBenes is not easily converted to $CH_3OH$.

3.4. O to $H_2O$. After $CH_4$ dissociation, atomic oxygen is adsorbed on the surface. According to the total reaction, atomic oxygen will continue to combine with hydrogen until $H_2O$ is formed and separated from the surface. We simulated the adsorption of these molecules, and their geometry is shown

in Figure S8. On MBenes, OH is adsorbed on the bridge site, except for the top site for $Fe_2B_2$, while OH on Cu(111) is adsorbed on the hollow site. In adsorption of $H_2O$, all MBenes and Cu(111) have adsorption on the top site. In terms of free energy, as shown in Figure 5, OH formation on MBenes shows

3.5. HER Performance. To achieve high $CO_2RR$ efficiency, HER should be suppressed. Figure S9 shows that the bridge site is the most energetically favored site for H adsorption on MBenes, while Cu(111) has hollow site adsorption. The Gibbs free energy of hydrogen adsorption is a descriptor of the HER activity. The HER activity increases as the Gibbs energy of H adsorption approaches zero. In general, when the absolute value of free energy is less than 0.2 eV, we can consider that the HER on the catalyst is active. $^{41,42}$ As shown in Figure 6, $Fe_2B_2$ and $Mn_2B_2$ have near-zero free energy with values of $-0.12$ and $-0.06$ eV, which show a high HER activity. The free energy of H absorbed on the Cu(111) surface is $-0.15$ eV. Comparing with these three catalysts discussed above, $Cr_2B_2$ and $Mo_2B_2$ show a relatively low HER activity with a free energy of $-0.34$ and $-0.76$ eV. In these five catalysts, the ultra-high activity of HER on $Fe_2B_2$ and $Mn_2B_2$ may result in poor selectivity of $CO_2RR$. In addition, we also considered the influence of hydrogen coverage on HER. The coverage varies from 1/9 to 1 ML, and the corresponding Gibbs free energy is shown in Table S1 in the Supporting Information. It can be concluded from the results that under different coverages, $Mo_2B_2$ ($\Delta G = -0.76$ to $-0.45$ eV) and $Cr_2B_2$($\Delta G = -0.42$ to $-0.34$ eV) maintain the same low HER

3.6. Comprehensive Evaluation of the Limit Potential and Selectivity. After obtaining the limit potentials on all MBenes, we combined the HER performance on each MBene to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the $CO_2RR$ catalytic ability. In the DFT study of $CH_4$ formation on MBenes, all MBenes have a high limit potential for CHO formation. After converting the highest Gibbs energy increase into the limit potential ($U_L$), we get the $CO_2RR$ catalytic ability on each MBene material. The present theoretical results show that the $U_L$ on $Cr_2B_2$ and $Mo_2B_2$ has a satisfactory drop, while that on $Fe_2B_2$ and $Mn_2B_2$ has not significantly changed. In the previous section, we introduced the HER absolute value of Gibbs free energy for quantifying the HER activity of the catalyst. In this section, we measure the comprehensive catalytic properties of these materials from the difference between the $U_L$ of $CO_2RR$ and $\Delta G_{H^*}$. The results are shown in Figure 7. We suggest that a good carbon material should have a low limit potential of $CO_2RR$ and a weak capability of HER, and these materials will

## 4. CONCLUSIONS
This work theoretically investigates MBenes as potential catalysts for CO₂RR. The present computational results demonstrate that MBenes can provide a decent affinity to CO₂. However, it is worth noting that too strong adsorption interaction can adversely affect the hydrogenation of CO to COOH. In the subsequent hydrogenation process from COOH to CH₄, the hydrogenation of CO to CHO has a tremendous increase of Gibbs free energy. In this context, Mo₂B₂ has a lower Gibbs free energy increase for CHO formation on MBenes. In addition, we also evaluated the possibility of methanol formation on Fe₂B₂. In the selectivity benchmark, Cr₂B₂ and Mo₂B₂ show a poor HER catalytic performance, which benefits the selectivity of CO₂RR. However, MBenes did not perform well in the final formation and desorption of H₂O. We hope to solve this problem in our future work. Above all, we can foresee that MBenes have broad catalytic applications and are worth further studies from the results of this work.
