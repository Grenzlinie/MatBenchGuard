**Abstract**—Using a semianalytical solution to the problem of wave excitation in an elastically supported one-dimensional waveguide with a system of point obstacles, we analyze the relation between the observed effects of blocking–transmission and the discrete spectrum point distribution in the complex frequency plane. We check the assumption, stemming from earlier numerical analysis for two-dimensional elastic waveguides with extended obstacles, on whether the occurrence of transmission frequencies in the blocking range is related to the presence of close-to-the-real-axis points of a discrete spectrum. The number of such spectral points increases in proportion to the number of obstacles, giving in the limit continuous transmission bands described by the Bloch–Floquet theory for infinite periodic structures.

In preceding papers [14–16], resonance blocking of symmetric and antisymmetric normal modes in a strip elastic waveguide with obstacles in the form of cracks was considered. Analysis of the position of resonance poles $\omega_n$ in the lower half-plane $\text{Im}\,\omega < 0$ of complex plane $\omega$ has shown that, depending on the change in crack dimensions, they either move away from the real axis $\text{Im}\,\omega = 0$ or approach and touch it, at certain values of the waveguide and obstacle parameters. As well, there arises the situation of a mixed spectrum (a point of discrete spectrum $\omega_n$ lying on the continuous spectrum $\omega > \omega_*$) with full waveguide blocking and localization of the wave energy in the form of energy vortices [14, 16].

The position of spectral points for a waveguide with obstacles changes with the change in distance between them [15]; therefore, it can significantly differ from that as a simple combination of spectra found for each of the obstacles separately. Of most interest here was the change in the role of poles $\omega_n$ close to the real axis from the viewpoint of the blocking properties of obstacles. Whereas for a single crack or inclusion, the approach of $\omega_n$ to the real axis leads to sharp screening of incoming waves at frequencies $\omega \approx \text{Re}\,\omega_n$, for system of obstacles, the transmission coefficient $\kappa^+$ is close to unity at these frequencies, i.e., vice versa, a sharp unlocking of the waveguide takes place! As well, the number of poles $\omega_n$ located near the real axis in a certain frequency range increases in proportion to the number of obstacles, and in the $\kappa^+(\omega)$ plot, the unlocking effect manifests itself in the form of bunch of sharp peaks with maxima at closely located frequencies $\omega \approx \text{Re}\,\omega_n$ [17].

The aim of the present work is to analyze this effect on a simpler one-dimensional model of an elastic waveguide, for which an elastically supported string with point obstacles was chosen. This choice follows from the fact that, on the one hand, resonance and trapping effects are inherent to such waveguides [4, 5], and on the other hand, due to the relative simplicity of the model, their analysis is possible on the basis of explicit analytic representations. The derivation of solution for this model as well as effects observed not only for periodic obstacles but also for non-periodic systems are described in Ref. [18].

# MATHEMATICAL MODEL

We consider an infinite string stretched with force $S$, infinite string supported from below by spring linkage with a stiffness coefficient $c$ (Fig. 1). Under the action of dynamic loading $p(x,t)$, the string points exhibit vibrations $w(x,t)$, which are governed by the equation [19]:
$$
\rho A w_{,tt} + cw - S w_{,xx} = p(x,t),\quad -\infty < x < \infty,\quad t \geq 0\,(1)
$$
with zero initial conditions $w(x,0) = 0$ and $w_{,t}(x,0) = 0$. Here, $\rho$ and $A$ are the material density of the string and its cross-sectional area.

The waveguide has $N$ point defects, which are simulated by pointwise jumps in mass of cross section $\rho A$ and/or the stiffness coefficient $c$ of an elastic foundation:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho A(x) &= \rho_0 A_0\left[1 + \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_j \delta(x - x_j)\right], \\
c(x) &= c_0\left[1 + \sum_{j=1}^{N} \varepsilon_j \delta(x - x_j)\right].
\end{aligned}\tag{2}
$$

Here, $\delta(x)$ is the Dirac delta function, $\rho_0$, $A_0$, and $c_0$ are the waveguide parameters without defects, $x_j$ are points of defect locations (for definiteness, $0 < x_1 < x_2 < ... < x_N$), and $\alpha_j$ and $\varepsilon_j$ are their dimensionless parameters. Coefficients $\alpha_j$ specify the inhomogeneity of the string itself: at $\alpha_j > 0$, this is an additional point mass, and at $\alpha_j < 0$, this is a transverse-crack-type defect. Since at $\alpha_j = -1$ the cross section at point $x_j$ becomes zero, the value of $\alpha_j < -1$ has no physical sense in the given model. Similarly, $\varepsilon_j > 0$ and $\varepsilon_j < 0$ describe additional point stiffness or, correspondingly, weakening of the elastic base. On the whole, the values of these coefficients change within the limits $-1 < \varepsilon_j, \alpha_j < \infty$. Transient vibrations $w(x,t)$ can be represented in terms of the frequency spectrum $w(x,\omega)$ via the inverse Fourier transform $\mathscr{F}_t^{-1}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
w(x,t) &= \mathscr{F}_t^{-1}[w(x,\omega)] \equiv \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathscr{L}_\omega} w(x,\omega)e^{-i\omega t} d\omega \\
&\equiv \frac{1}{\pi} \text{Re} \int_{\mathscr{L}_\omega^+} w(x,\omega)e^{-i\omega t} d\omega.
\end{aligned}\tag{3}
$$

The integration contour $\mathscr{L}_\omega$ goes in the complex plane $\omega$ along the real axis from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, deviating from it into the upper half-plane $\text{Im}\,\omega > 0$ as it rounds real singularities of the spectrum $w(x,\omega)$; $\mathscr{L}_\omega^+$ is the part of the contour located in the right half-plane $\text{Re}\,\omega > 0$. From here on, the time functions of time and their frequency spectra are denoted by the same symbols, differing only in argument $t$ or $\omega$. For brevity, the latter

may be omitted when they are obvious from the context.

The rest condition $w(x,t) \equiv 0, t \leq 0$ implies that the frequency spectrum $w(x, \omega)$, analytically extended from the real axis into the entire complex plane $\omega$, has no singularities in the upper half-plane $\text{Re}\,\omega > 0$ and posses the property
$$
w(x, \tilde{\omega})=w^{*}(x, \omega), \quad \tilde{\omega}=-\omega^{*}. \tag{4}
$$

The asterisk here denotes a complex conjugation, and the tilde, a reflection into a point of the complex plane symmetric relative to the imaginary axis; for real values, the latter is equivalent to a change in sign: $\tilde{\omega}=-\omega$. Property (4) ensures the reality of the transient solution $w(x,t)$, in particular, the equality between the integrals over $\mathscr{L}_{\omega}$ and $\mathscr{L}_{\omega}^{+}$ in representation (3). In addition, if point $\omega_{n}$ is an isolated pole of function $w(\omega)$, then $\tilde{\omega}_{n}$ is also its pole and the residuals from these poles possess the same symmetry.
$$
r_{n}=\left.\operatorname{res} w(\omega)\right|_{\omega=\omega_{n}} \left.\rightarrow \operatorname{res} w(\omega)\right|_{\omega=\tilde{\omega}_{n}}=\tilde{r}_{n}. \tag{5}
$$

In closing the $\mathscr{L}_{\omega}$ contour into the lower half-plane (in fulfilling the condition of the Jordan lemma), such pair of residues yields a real contribution in accordance with the Cauchy theorem:
$$
w_{n}(x, t)=-i\left(r_{n} e^{-i \omega_{n} t}+\tilde{r}_{n} e^{-i \tilde{\omega}_{n} t}\right)=2 \operatorname{Im}\left[r_{n}(x) e^{-i \omega_{n} t}\right].
$$

At complex $\omega_{n}$, these components of solution $w(x,t)$ describe attenuating vibrations with the circular frequency $v_{n}=\operatorname{Re} \omega_{n}$, the phase shift $\varphi_{n}: r_{n}=\left|r_{n}\right| e^{i \varphi_{n}}$, and the logarithmic decrement $\theta_{n}=\left|\operatorname{Im} \omega_{n}\right|$:
$$
w_{n}(x, t)=2\left|r_{n}(x)\right| \sin \left(\varphi_{n}-v_{n} t\right) e^{-\theta_{n} t}. \tag{6}
$$

At real $\omega_{n}$, vibrations $w_{n}(x,t)$ become nonattenuating. For purely imaginary poles $\omega_{n}=-i \theta_{n}$, residues $r_{n}$ are also purely imaginary quantities.

Further, for simplicity, we assume that loading $p$ is point loading applied at the origin:
$$
p(x, t)=p_{0} \delta(x) f(t), \quad f(t) \equiv 0 \text { as } t<0. \tag{7}
$$

In this case, frequency spectrum $w(x, \omega)$ satisfies the ordinary differential equation
$$
S w_{, x x}+\left(\omega^{2} \rho A-c\right) w=-p_{0} F(\omega) \delta(x), \tag{8}
$$
which is derived from Eq. (1) after applying the time Fourier transform $\mathscr{F}_{t}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr{F}_{t}[w(x, t)] & \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} w(x, t) e^{i \omega t} d t \\
& =\int_{0}^{\infty} w(x, t) e^{i \omega t} d t=w(x, \omega),
\end{aligned} \tag{9}
$$

$F(\omega)=\mathscr{F}_{t}[f]$ is the frequency spectrum of function $f(t)$ specifying the shape of the driving pulse $p$. Due to the general property of the $\delta$ function $w(x) \delta\left(x-x_{j}\right)=$ $w\left(x_{j}\right) \delta\left(x-x_{j}\right)$, the components describing the presence of defects can be extracted and transferred into the right-hand side. It leads to an equation with constant coefficients with unknown complex vibration amplitudes of defects $w_{j}=w\left(x_{j}, \omega\right)$ in the right-hand side:
$$
\begin{gathered}
S w_{, x x}+\left(\omega^{2} \rho_{0} A_{0}-c_{0}\right) w \\
=-p_{0} F(\omega) \delta(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{j}\left(c_{0} \varepsilon_{j}-\omega^{2} \rho_{0} A_{0} \alpha_{j}\right) \delta\left(x-x_{j}\right).
\end{gathered} \tag{10}
$$

To reduce the number of input parameters, further analysis is performed in dimensionless form:
$$
\begin{gathered}
\bar{x}=x / h, \quad \bar{x}_{j}=x_{j} / h, \quad \bar{w}(\bar{x}, \bar{\omega})=w /\left(h t_{0}\right), \\
\bar{\omega}=\omega / f_{0}=h \sqrt{\rho_{0} A_{0} / S} \omega, \quad \bar{c}^{2}=h^{2} c_{0} / S ;
\end{gathered}
$$
here, $h$ is some characteristic length, and $t_{0}=$ $h \sqrt{\rho_{0} A_{0} / S}$ and $f_{0}=1 / t_{0}$ are units of time and frequency. Taking into account that solution $w$ is directly proportional to the amplitude of applied loading $p_{0} F$, it suffices for us to consider the dimensionless equation
$$
\bar{w}^{\prime \prime}+k_{0}^{2} \bar{w}=\delta(\bar{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{N} \bar{w}_{j}\left(\bar{c}^{2} \varepsilon_{j}-\bar{w}^{2} \alpha_{j}\right) \delta\left(\bar{x}-\bar{x}_{j}\right), \quad(11)
$$
in which
$$
k_{0}^{2}=\bar{\omega}^{2}-\bar{c}^{2}=\frac{h^{2} \rho_{0} A_{0}}{S} \omega^{2}-\frac{h^{2}}{S} c_{0}.
$$

The dimensional solution is expressed via the solution to Eq. (11) in accordance with the relation
$$
w(x, \omega)=-\frac{h^{2}}{S} p_{0} F(\omega) \bar{w}\left(x / h, \omega / f_{0}\right).
$$

In further calculations, bars over dimensionless quantities are omitted.

$$

The partial solution to Eq. (12), which with real $k_{0}(\omega)$ satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation conditions at infinity and is restricted with complex $k_{0}(\omega)$, has the form

$$
g(x)=\frac{e^{i k_{0}|x|}}{2 i k_{0}}, \quad k_{0}(\omega)= \begin{cases}\sqrt{\omega^{2}-c^{2}}, & \omega>c \\ i \sqrt{c^{2}-\omega^{2}}, & |\omega| \leq c \\ -\sqrt{\omega^{2}-c^{2}}, & \omega<-c\end{cases} \quad(13)
$$

The choice of square-root branches for the wave number $k_{0}$ agrees with property (4). Under analytical continuation into a complex plane, these branches are fixed by cuts going from the branch points $\pm c$ down to infinity $\pm c-i \infty$.

The continuous spectrum of the waveguide coincides with the rays $-\infty<\omega<-c$ and $c<\omega<\infty$ lying on the real axis. At these frequencies, wave number $k_{0}$ is real and harmonic solution $g e^{-i \omega t}$ describes nonattenuating waves $e^{i\left(k_{0}|x|-\omega t\right)}$ propagating to the right and to the left from the source with a phase velocity of $v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\omega / \sqrt{\omega^{2}-c^{2}}$ and a group velocity of $v_{\mathrm{gr}}=1 / v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-c^{2}} / \omega$. With increasing frequency, the group velocity monotonically increases from $v_{\mathrm{gr}}=0$ at the cutoff frequency $\omega_{*}=c$ to $v_{\mathrm{gr}}=1$ as $\omega \rightarrow \infty$. Beyond the continuous spectrum at frequencies $-c<\omega<c$, there are no propagating waves and $|g| \rightarrow 0$ as $|x| \rightarrow \infty$; i.e., the waveguide is locked.

Using the Green's function, we can write the general solution to Eq. (11) in the form
$$
\begin{gathered}
w(x, \omega)=g(x, \omega)+\sum_{j=1}^{N} f_{j}(\omega) g\left(x-x_{j}, \omega\right), \\
f_{j}=w_{j}\left(c^{2} \varepsilon_{j}-\omega^{2} \alpha_{j}\right).
\end{gathered}\quad(14)
$$

It is determined with an accuracy up to $N$ unknown constants $w_{j}$, which in turn are determined from a linear algebraic system with respect to the vector of unknown coefficients $\mathbf{w}=\left[w_{1}, w_{2}, \ldots, w_{N}\right]^{T}$ :
$$
A \mathbf{w}=\mathbf{g}, \quad(15)
$$
$A=2 i k_{0} I-B, \quad I$ is the unit matrix, $B=\left[b_{i j}\right]_{i, j=1}^{N}, b_{i j}=d_{j} e_{i j}, d_{j}=c^{2} \varepsilon_{j}-\omega^{2} \alpha_{j}, e_{i j}=e^{i k_{0}\left|x_{i}-x_{j}\right|}, \mathbf{g}=$ $\left[e_{1}, e_{2}, \ldots, e_{N}\right]^{T}, e_{i}=e^{i k_{0}\left|x_{i}\right|}$. System (15) arises upon substitution of $w(x)$ of form (14) into $N$ conditions for the vibration amplitude of defect points: $w\left(x_{j}\right)=w_{j}$, $j=1,2, \ldots, N$.

The points of spectrum $\omega_{n}$ (poles of function $w(x, \omega)$ in the complex plane $\omega$ ) coincide with the roots of the characteristic equation
$$
\Delta(\omega) \equiv \operatorname{det} A(\omega)=0, \quad(16)
$$
where $A$ is the matrix of system (15). The eigenvibrations of string $w_{n}(x)$ corresponding to them are described by the same representation (14), in which $\omega=\omega_{n}$ and the eigenvector components $\mathbf{w}_{n}$ : $A\left(\omega_{n}\right) \mathbf{w}_{n}=0$ are taken as coefficients $w_{j}$.

Expansion (14) determining the dynamic reaction of a string with defects can be interpreted as a superposition of the frequency spectra of vibrations caused in the defectless string by an initial concentrated pulse $p=\delta(x) \delta(t)$ (first summand) and conditional loadings $p=\delta\left(x-x_{j}\right) f_{j}(t)$ applied at the defect point $x_{j}$. In other words, defects work as point sources, the pulse shape of which $f_{j}(t)=\mathscr{F}_{t}^{-1}\left[f_{j}(\omega)\right]$ is determined via expansion coefficients (14). Correspondingly, the transient reaction of the string $w(x, t)=\mathscr{F}_{t}^{-1}[w(x, \omega)]$ can be represented in the form of the sum of the initial signal $g(x, t)=\mathscr{F}_{t}^{-1}[g(x, \omega)]$ and the wave packets $u_{j}(x, t)$ excited by vibrating defects:
$$
\begin{gathered}
w(x, t)=g(x, t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N} u_{j}(x, t), \\
u_{j}(x, t)=f_{j} * g_{j} \equiv \int_{\left|x-x_{j}\right|}^{t-x_{j}} f_{j}(t-\tau) g\left(x-x_{j}, \tau\right) d \tau.
\end{gathered}\quad(17)
$$

The values of the lower limits $\left|x-x_{j}\right|$ of the integral convolutions are determined by the resting property of the fundamental solution
$$
g(x, t) \equiv 0 \quad \text { at } \quad t<|x|, \quad(18)
$$
which is a consequence of the fact that
$$
\begin{gathered}
k_{0}(\omega) \sim \omega, \text { and } e^{i k_{0}|x|} e^{-i \omega t} \sim e^{i \omega(|x|-t)} \\
\text { as }|\omega| \rightarrow \infty, \operatorname{Im} \omega \geq 0.
\end{gathered}\quad(19)
$$

In the integral representation of form (3), but for $g(x, t)$, the exponential behavior (19) of the spectrum $g(x, \omega)$ makes it possible to close the contour $\mathscr{L}_{\omega}$ into the upper half-plane $\operatorname{Im} \omega>0$ for $|x|-t>0$. The absence of singularities above $\mathscr{L}_{\omega}$ leads to condition (18), meaning that point $x$ of the defectless string is at rest up to the moment of arrival of the leading wave front with the maximal velocity $v_{\mathrm{gr}}=1$.

The upper limits $t-x_{j}$ also results from the resting property, but already of additional sources: $f_{j}(t) \equiv 0$ and $t<x_{j}$. Physically, this also means that the defect is at rest up to the time of the arrival of the leading front moving with unit velocity from the source located at the origin. Mathematically it is determined by the exponential behavior of the frequency spectrum,
$$
f_{j}(\omega) \sim O\left(e^{i \omega x_{j}}\right), \quad|\omega| \rightarrow \infty, \quad \operatorname{Im} \omega \geq 0, \quad(20)
$$
making it possible in representation $f_{j}(t)$ to close the contour $\mathscr{L}_{\omega}$ upward at $t<x_{j}$. Coefficients $f_{j}(\omega)$ entering into $w_{j}(\omega)$ possess behavior (20) due to the fact

## SPECTRAL PROPERTIES

In the case of one defect,

$$
w(x, \omega)=\frac{1}{2 i k_{0}}\left[e^{i k_{0}|x|}+\frac{c^{2} \varepsilon-\omega^{2} \alpha}{\Delta(\omega)} e^{i k_{0}\left(x_{1}+\left|x-x_{1}\right|\right)}\right], \tag{22}
$$

$$
\Delta(\omega)=2 i k_{0}-c^{2} \varepsilon+\omega^{2} \alpha\left(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}, \alpha=\alpha_{1}\right),
$$

and the roots of characteristic equation $\Delta(\omega)=0$ can be written in explicit form. Relatively simple analysis shows that it cannot have real roots at $\omega > c$ (on the continuous spectrum), but in the blocking range $\omega < c$, there is a point of the discrete spectrum

$$
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{\alpha \varepsilon c^{2}-2+2 \sqrt{1+\alpha c^{2}(\alpha-\varepsilon)}} / \alpha,
$$

if $\max [-2 / c ;-1]<\varepsilon<\alpha<\infty$. At $\alpha=0, \quad \omega_{1}=$ $c \sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{2} c^{2}} / 4,|\varepsilon| c<2$, and at $\alpha<0$ along with $\omega_{1}$, a second real root can appear,

$$
\omega_{2}=\sqrt{\alpha \varepsilon c^{2}-2-2 \sqrt{1+\alpha c^{2}(\alpha-\varepsilon)}} / \alpha
$$

at those $\alpha, \varepsilon$, and $c$ for which both radicands are positive.

Figures 2 and 3 show examples of the dependence of real poles $\omega_n$ on parameters $\alpha_j = \alpha$ and $\varepsilon_j = \varepsilon$ for $N$ identical defects and on the distance between them $a = |x_{j+1} - x_j|$ at $c = 4$ and $N = 1, 2$, and 5. In the case of one defect (Fig. 2 for $N = 1$), the plots of dependences $\alpha = -0.5, 0, 1, 2$, and 5 have the form of a bush, the branches of which begin from point $\varepsilon_0 = -2/c = -0.5$, at which $\omega_n = 0$. With increasing (at $\alpha > \varepsilon_0$) or decreasing (at $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$) parameter $\varepsilon$, they achieve the limit value $\omega_n = c$ at $\varepsilon = \alpha$. Further at $\varepsilon > \alpha$, the root enters into the complex plane through point $\omega = c$. A negative inclination of the tangent to the curve for $\alpha = -0.5$ shows that, in this case, at fixed $\varepsilon$, there are two real roots $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$.

The number of such bushes with branches $\omega_n(\varepsilon)$ beginning from the first root located to the right and left of $\varepsilon_0$ increases in proportion to the number of defects $N$ (Fig. 2 for $N = 5$). The distance between the points where branches emerge increases with increasing distance between defects $a$ (see the series of plots in Fig. 3 for $N = 2$ and $a = 1, 0.5, 0.2$, and $0.1$). At comparatively large $a$, when the mutual influence of defects is not large, roots $\omega_n(\varepsilon)$ follow along trajectories close to the corresponding branches for the case of $N = 1$ (Fig. 3, $a = 1$), and with decreasing $a$, they begin to diverge (Fig. 3, $a = 0.5$); as well, the right bush is more conservative, remaining approximately the same at all $a \to 0$, whereas the second one moves, acceler-

ating to the left (Fig. 3, $a = 0.2$ and $a = 0.1$), leaving the admissible limits $\varepsilon > -1$.

As concerns the zone of traveling waves $\omega > c$, for $N \geq 2$, as in the case of $N = 1$, the real poles here are not found. In the case of two defects, their absence is established based on the explicit form of the characteristic equation

$$
\Delta = (2ik_0 - d_1)(2ik_0 - d_2) - d_1d_2e^{2ik_0a} = 0. \tag{23}
$$

With real $\omega > c$, it resolves into two equations

$$
\begin{cases}
\cos 2k_0a = 1 - 4k_0^2/(d_1d_2) \\
\sin 2k_0a = -2k_0(d_1 + d_2)/(d_1d_2),
\end{cases} \tag{24}
$$

squaring and adding them, we arrive at the relation $d_1^2 + d_2^2 + 4k_0^2 = 0$, which does not admit real solutions.

On the other hand, at $\alpha \neq 0$, in the right-hand side of system (24), there are summands tending to zero as $\omega \to \infty$:

$$
\begin{cases}
\cos 2k_0a = 1 + O(\omega^{-2}) \\
\sin 2k_0a = O(\omega^{-1})
\end{cases} \quad \text{as} \quad \omega \to \infty. \tag{25}
$$

From here, it follows that Eq. (23) should have an infinite set of roots close to the real axis. Their allocation is described by the asymptotics

$$
\begin{gathered}
\omega_n = \sqrt{c^2 + (\pi n/a)^2} + O(\omega^{-1}), \quad \omega \to \infty, \\
n = 1,2,..., \alpha \neq 0.
\end{gathered} \tag{26}
$$

The imaginary parts of these roots are negative quantities tending to zero; i.e., with increasing number $n$, the poles get up to the real axis.

At $\alpha = 0$, the asymptotics of the poles going along the real axis is derived directly from Eq. (23):

$$
\omega_n \sim \left[ \pi(n+1/2) - i\ln\left| \frac{2\omega_n}{c^2\sqrt{\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2}} \right| \right]/a, \quad n \to \infty. \tag{27}
$$

Whereas $\text{Re}\omega_n$ go with the same step $\pi/a$ as in asymptotics (26), the imaginary part of these poles logarithmically increases; i.e., they gradually deviate downward from the real axis.

The presence of periodic trigonometric functions $e_{ij} = \cos k_0a_{ij} + i\sin k_0a_{ij}$, $a_{ij} = |x_i - x_j|$ in characteristic equations at $N \geq 2$ indicates that with a number of defects larger than two, there should exist similar periodically repeating (with increasing $\omega$) sets of complex roots. The structure of the set of real roots (Figs. 2, 3) hints that they should be periodically repeating groups of poles located approximately in the same place as for two defects, with the number of poles in the group proportional to the number of defects.

# TRANSMISSION OF WAVES THROUGH A DEFECT ZONE

Traditionally, transmission coefficient $\kappa^+$ is introduced via the ratio of the time averaged energy of traveling waves propagating behind an obstacle to the energy of incoming waves (see, e.g., [14, 15]). In the

![](./images/811657008489955329_6.jpg)

Fig. 6. Frequency dependence of transmission coefficient $\kappa^{+}$ at different distances $a$ between two defects with parameters $\alpha=-0.5$ and $\varepsilon=0$.

considered problem, it is convenient to introduce it in the form of the squared ratio of the amplitude string vibrations at any point $x^{+}>x_{N}$ behind obstacles to the amplitude of defectless string vibrations at the same point $x^{+}$:

$$
\kappa^{+}(\omega)=\left|w\left(x^{+}, \omega\right)\right|^{2} /\left|g\left(x^{+}, \omega\right)\right|^{2}, \quad \omega>c. \tag{28}
$$

In the case of one defect, when there are no complex points of spectrum $\omega_{n}$ adjacent to the continuous spectrum, the dependences of the transmission coefficient on frequency have the form of smooth curves with a monotonic tendency to zero or unity at $\omega \rightarrow \infty$. However, already at $N=2$, the character of the dependency changes sharply: periodic peaks appear, the tops of which in the case of two identical defects achieve unity (complete transmission), and the distance between each (period of repetition along the frequency axis) is inversely proportional to the distance between defects $a$ (Fig. 6 for two defects in the form of transverse cracks $(\alpha=-0.5, \varepsilon=0)$; the dotted line here and further shows the dependence of $\kappa^{+}(\omega)$ for the corresponding single defect). The central frequencies of peaks $\omega_{p}$ are located above complex poles $\omega_{n}: \omega_{p} \approx \operatorname{Re} \omega_{n}$, i.e., at points described by asymptotics (26) and (27). Since with an increasing number of identical defects, the number of almost real poles in each group increases proportionally (Figs. 4, 5), the same number of new peaks, i.e., complete transmission frequencies $\omega_{p}$ correspondingly appears (Figs. 7, 8). With increasing $N$, the transmission frequencies densely fill the range to which the corresponding group of poles $\omega_{n}$ is adjacent, and the peaks practically merge into the dark bands above these ranges (see Fig. 7 for $N=20$ and $N P=50$). In the same way, at $N \rightarrow \infty$, the given frequency ranges become complete transmission ranges for waveguides with an infinite periodic microstructure. In the frequency intervals that divide them, on the contrary, $\kappa^{+}(\omega) \rightarrow 0$ at $N \rightarrow \infty$; i.e., they degenerate into complete blocking ranges.

Dependences $\kappa^{+}(\omega)$, shown by the dotted line, for a single defect manifest themselves in the case of $N$ defects, limiting from above minimal values $\kappa^{+}$ between peaks in the transmission range being formed. The given minima are the smaller, the closer the corresponding poles $\omega_{n}$ to the real axis; therefore, the location of the latter in the complex plane in the form of sagging chains (Figs. 4, 5, right) are manifested in Figs. 7 and 8 in the form of light arcs limiting from below the dark transmission bands. In the second example, when the string itself is not damaged $(\alpha=0$, Fig. 8), for a single defect, $\kappa^{+} \rightarrow 1$ at $\omega \rightarrow \infty$ and, as a consequence, for large $N$, nearly complete transmission is observed at high frequencies, alternating with decreasing (for increasing $\omega$) blocking bands.

It is noteworthy that the peaks in Fig. 7 are similar not only to the earlier discovered peaks for the elastic strip with several cracks or inclusions [15, 17], but also to the peaks of resonance transmission of acoustic waves through a finite number of layers [9], Fig. 2; the transmission bands in Fig. 8 look like those in the $\kappa^{+}$ plots for finite phonon lattices [7], Figs. 5 and 6.

It is naturally to assume that the transmission and blocking ranges forming at $N \rightarrow \infty$ coincide with the ranges obtained for infinite periodic structures in the framework of the Bloch–Floquet theory. In accordance with the given theory, the wave solution for a structure with period $a$ is sought in the form

$w(x,\omega)=e^{i\beta x}v(x,\omega)$, where $v$ is a periodic function: $v(x+a)=v(x)$. From the equations describing the behavior of periodic cells and from the boundary conditions between them, we derive the characteristic equation $\Delta(\beta,\omega)=0$ relating wave number $\beta$ and frequency: $\omega=\omega_n(\beta)$ or $\beta=\beta_n(\omega)$. Propagating waves correspond to the real branches of these dependences; i.e., the corresponding frequency ranges are transmission bands ($\kappa^+=1$), while blocking frequencies ($\kappa^+=0$) correspond to complex values $\beta_n(\omega)$. For the considered waveguide, the characteristic equation has the form
$$
\begin{gathered}
\Delta(\beta, \omega) \equiv \cos a k_{0}+\frac{d a}{k_{0}} \sin a k_{0}-\cos a \beta=0, \\
d=c^{2} \varepsilon-\omega^{2} \alpha.
\end{gathered}\tag{29}
$$

Since at real $\beta$ $|\cos a\beta|<1$, this equation can have real roots only if
$$
\left|\cos a k_{0}+\frac{d a}{k_{0}} \sin a k_{0}\right|<1.\tag{30}
$$

Real $\omega$ satisfying this inequality also determines the transmission ranges. Numerical analysis confirms their coincidence with the pass bands obtained at $N\to\infty$, i.e., with those regions of the real axis along which poles $\omega_n$ close to the real axis are concentrated.

In particular, at high frequencies, their boundaries can be estimated analytically based on the asymptotic behavior of the coefficient at the sine function
$$
\frac{d a}{k_{0}} \sim \begin{cases}O(\omega), & \alpha \neq 0 \\ O\left(\omega^{-1}\right), \alpha=0\end{cases} \quad \text { at } \quad \omega \rightarrow \infty.
$$

As a consequence, at $\alpha\neq0$,
$$
\begin{gathered}
\left|\sin a k_{0}\right| \sim O\left(\omega^{-1}\right) \Rightarrow\left|a k_{0}+\pi n\right|<\delta_{n} \rightarrow 0, \\
\text { at } \quad \omega \rightarrow \infty,
\end{gathered}
$$
or, solving with respect to $\omega$, we arrive at the estimate
$$
\begin{gathered}
\left|\omega-\omega_{p n}\right|<O\left(\delta_{n}\right), \omega_{p n}=\sqrt{c^{2}+(\pi n / a)^{2}}, \\
\delta_{n} \rightarrow 0, n \rightarrow \infty.
\end{gathered}
$$

It shows that in the first example (Fig. 7), with increasing $\omega$, the transmission bands contract toward points

At $\alpha = 0$, inequality (30) can be written in the form

|\cos a k_{0}| < 1-O(\omega^{-1}),\ \omega \to \infty,

The strict picture of pass and gap band formation is sharply violated with a change in the properties of even one of the obstacles. As well, if only the defect parameters change, and the distance remains the same, then the periodicity of peaks is retained, but their height can change substantially. If there is a fluctuation in the distances between obstacles (in cell size), then there appear periodic sets (corresponding to new dimensions) of nearly real poles, manifesting themselves in the form of additional peaks.

## CONCLUSIONS

We have constructed a convenient for wave analysis, mathematical model of forced vibrations of an elastically supported string with point defects with the use of which we have analyzed the relation between the spectral properties of the problem considered and the effect of resonance waveguide unlocking. We have shown that obstacles become acoustically transparent at those frequencies close to which nearly-real complex resonance poles $\omega_{n}$ appear; as well, their number in a limited frequency ranges increases with an increasing number of obstacles; in the limit, this leads to the formation of continuous transmission bands, i.e., the ranges where traveling waves exist in a waveguide with a periodic microstructure.
