# Mean-square displacements of an $F^-$ interstitial and its neighbours in $\text{CaF}_2$

T M Haridasan†, J Govindarajan‡, M A H Nerenberg§ and P W M Jacobs∥

University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada

Received 8 May 1979

**Abstract** The mean-square displacement of an $F^-$ interstitial and its first and second neighbours are studied using the lattice Green functions and the scattering matrix formalism. The interstitial is treated in the most general way, following the method of Maradudin *et al.* The mean-square displacement of the interstitial is found to be three to four times larger than those of the host atoms. The consequences of these results are discussed in the light of the experiments of Willis and the anomalous values of the mean-square displacements in fluorites at high temperatures, as observed by Thomas.

## 1. Introduction
Mean-square displacements of atoms in crystals are helpful in understanding many aspects of the binding in the crystal, as well as explaining the phenomena of melting and phase transitions. A study of the mean-square displacements of the atoms in $\text{CaF}_2$ by Willis (1965) showed that the results based on the Debye-Waller factors of ions at normal lattice sites do not reproduce the observed structure factors for neutron scattering in $\text{CaF}_2$. Willis was able to account for the discrepancy by attributing anharmonic vibrations to $F^-$ ions, which were assumed to move from their normal positions towards the interstices, while retaining the overall symmetry. However, when one of the interstices is occupied by a foreign atom, say, an $F^-$ ion, then the host atoms surrounding this interstitial will relax from their normal positions. Therefore it is instructive to study how the mean-square displacements of the host atoms change when an interstitial is present. The mean-square displacement of the interstitial itself merits investigation because of the important role played by the interstitial in transport properties such as diffusion.

Recent results reported by Thomas (1976) and Achmeazhanov *et al* (1978) show an unusual increase of the mean-square displacement of $F^-$ ions at high temperatures. It is possible that at high temperatures the interstitial $F^-$ ions also contribute to this anomalous increase. With these experimental observations in mind, we have studied the mean-square displacement of the $F^-$ interstitial along with those of its first and

### 2. Outline of the theory
We employ the general method as suggested by Maradudin *et al* (1971) for a molecular impurity in a crystal, generalised from the works of Wagner (1963, 1964). The inter- stitial, unlike a substitutional impurity, adds three additional degrees of freedom to the crystal. The equation of motion of a crystal containing $rN$ atoms ($r$ is the number of atoms in the unit cell and $N$ the number of cells), can be written in the matrix form,
$$
\mathbf{L} \boldsymbol{u}^{0}=0 \tag{1}
$$
where $\mathbf{L}$ is the $(3rN \times 3rN)$ dynamical matrix and $\boldsymbol{u}^{0}$ a $(3rN)$ column vector describing the displacements of the $rN$ atoms.

The introduction of an atom into an interstitial site, a site which is normally un- occupied in the host crystal, increases the number of degrees of freedom to $(3rN+3)$. The time-independent equations of motion of the coupled system of the lattice and the interstitial can be written as,
$$
(\mathbf{L}-\delta \mathbf{L}) \boldsymbol{u}+\mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\xi}=0 \tag{2}
$$

$$
\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}} \boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{\xi}=0. \tag{3}
$$

Here $\delta \mathbf{L}$ describes the modifications in $\mathbf{L}$ due to the interstitial. $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ is a $(3 \times 3)$ matrix describing the vibrations of the interstitial in the host crystal with all the host lattice ions 'frozen' in their equilibrium positions. $\mathbf{A}$ describes the $(3rN \times 3)$ matrix which couples the interstitial with the host lattice and $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is a three-dimensional column vector describing the displacements of the interstitial.

We shall now define $\gamma(\omega^{2})$, the interstitital Green function, as
$$
\gamma(\omega^{2})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}=\left[m_{\mathrm{I}}(\omega^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{I}}^{2})\right]^{-1} \mathbf{I} \tag{4}
$$
where $m_{\mathrm{I}}$ is the interstitial mass and $\omega_{\mathrm{I}}$ is the frequency of vibration of an interstitial if it were in the frozen host lattice. Using equation (4) in equation (3) allows us to express $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ in terms of $\boldsymbol{u}$ and $\gamma$, which, when substituted in equation (2), yields the form
$$
(\mathbf{L}-\delta \mathbf{L}-\mathbf{A} \gamma \mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}}) \boldsymbol{u}=0. \tag{5}
$$

Multiplying equation (5) by $\mathbf{G}$ (the Green function of the host lattice) which is the

inverse of $\mathbf{L}$, gives

$$
\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{G}\left(\delta \mathbf{L}+\mathbf{A} \gamma \mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\right) \boldsymbol{u}. \tag{6}
$$

If the interstitital is assumed to interact only with a few near neighbours of the host lattice (say, $n$) then the matrices $\mathbf{G}, \delta \mathbf{L}, \mathbf{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{u}$ can be partitioned as

$$
\mathbf{G}=\left(\begin{array}{c|c}
\mathbf{g} & \mathbf{G}_{12} \\
\hline \mathbf{G}_{21} & \mathbf{G}_{22}
\end{array}\right) ; \quad \delta \mathbf{L}=\left(\begin{array}{c|c}
\delta \boldsymbol{l} & 0 \\
\hline 0 & 0
\end{array}\right) ; \quad \mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{array}{c}
\mathbf{a} \\
\hline 0
\end{array}\right) ; \quad \boldsymbol{u}=\left(\begin{array}{c}
\boldsymbol{u}_{1} \\
\hline \boldsymbol{u}_{2}
\end{array}\right) \tag{7}
$$

$\mathbf{g}$ and $\delta \boldsymbol{l}$ are $(3n \times 3n)$ matrices and $\mathbf{a}$ is a $(3n \times 3)$ matrix. If we can obtain $\boldsymbol{u}_{1}$, which contains the $3n$ displacement component of the host atoms affected by the interstititial, then the displacement of the interstitial $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ can be obtained using equation (7) in (3):

$$
\boldsymbol{\xi}=-\gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}} \boldsymbol{u}_{1}. \tag{8}
$$

We shall now see how $\boldsymbol{u}_{1}$, and hence $\boldsymbol{\xi}$, can be obtained from the lattice Green function $\mathbf{g}$ and the $(\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}})$ matrices.

Substituting (7) in (5) gives $u$ as the solution of the equation

$$
\left(\boldsymbol{l}-\delta \boldsymbol{l}-\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\right) \boldsymbol{u}_{1}=0. \tag{9}
$$

This can be written as

$$
\left(\boldsymbol{l}-\delta \boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}\right) \boldsymbol{u}_{1}=0, \tag{10}
$$

where

$$
\delta \boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}=\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}. \tag{11}
$$

Using the method of Maradudin *et al* (1971), we shall consider this as a scattering problem. $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ is the matrix which gives the displacement of the scattered wave and can be expressed as,

$$
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}+\boldsymbol{w}_{1} \tag{12}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}$ describes the displacements of the atoms for the incident wave. $\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}$, a solution of

$$
\boldsymbol{l}_{1} \boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}=0 \tag{13}
$$

corresponds to a perfect lattice and can be expressed as a plane wave

$$
\left[u_{1}^{0}\left(l \kappa, \boldsymbol{k}_{0}, j_{0}\right)\right]_{\alpha}=\left[\hbar /\left(2 N M_{k} w_{j}\left(k_{0}\right)\right)\right]^{1 / 2} \omega_{\alpha}\left(\kappa \mid{ }_{j_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}\right) \exp \left[\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{k}_{0} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}_{(k)}^{(\iota)}\right] \tag{14}
$$

where $\omega_{j_{0}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{0})$ is the frequency of the phonon $(j_{0}, \boldsymbol{k}_{0})$ and $w_{\alpha}(\kappa|_{j_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}})$ the $\alpha$th eigenvector component.

We shall now define the scattering matrix $\mathbf{t}$ which is the solution of the equation

$$
\mathbf{t}=\delta \boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}+\mathbf{t g} \delta \boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}
$$

or

$$
\mathbf{t}=\delta \boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{l}-\mathbf{g} \delta \boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}. \tag{15}
$$

When we substitute equation (12) in equation (9) and use equation (15), we get

$$
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}=\mathbf{g t} \boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}. \tag{16}
$$

Thus the displacements of the host atoms in the defect space are given in matrix notation

by
$$
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=(\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{g t}) \boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}. \tag{17}
$$

In expanded form this is
$$
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\left\{\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{g}\left(\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\left[\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{g}\left(\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\} \boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0} \tag{18}
$$
and by using equation (18) and equation (8) this becomes,
$$
\boldsymbol{\xi}=-\gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\left\{\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{g}\left(\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\left[\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{g}\left(\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\} \boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}. \tag{19}
$$

Equations (18) and (19) are used to compute $\boldsymbol{u}_{1}$ and $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ for the normal mode frequencies and hence to compute the mean-square amplitudes.

### 3. Application to $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ lattice

A $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ lattice can be viewed as three interpenetrating FCC sublattices of $\mathrm{Ca}, \mathrm{F}_{1}$ and $\mathrm{F}_{2}$. The interstitial sits at the body centre formed by the cube of the fluorines and hence is surrounded by eight $\mathrm{F}^{-}$ions as first neighbours and six $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ ions as second neighbours. The coordinates of these 14 atoms surrounding the interstitial, which is taken to be at the origin, are given below in units of the $\mathrm{F}_{1}^{-}-\mathrm{F}_{2}^{-}$distance which is half the lattice constant.

1. $\frac{\overline{1} \overline{1} 1}{222}$
2. $\frac{\overline{1} 11}{222}$
3. $\frac{1 \overline{1} 1}{222}$
4. $\frac{11 \overline{1}}{222}$
5. 100
6. 010
7. 001
8. $\frac{11 \overline{1}}{222}$
9. $\frac{1 \overline{1} \overline{1}}{222}$
10. $\frac{\overline{1} \overline{1} \overline{1}}{222}$
11. $\frac{\overline{1} 1 \overline{1}}{222}$
12. $\overline{1} 00$
13. $0 \overline{1} 1$
14. $00 \overline{1}$.

Using the HADES program (Norgett 1974) and the interaction potential of Catlow and Norgett (1973) for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$, we found that the relaxations of these $\mathrm{F}^{-}$ions and $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ ions are significant when the interstitial is present. We chose the defect space to comprise the interstitial and the 14 atoms given above. This choice is supported by the fact that the relaxations of more distant neighbours were found to be negligible. Accordingly, the $\mathbf{g}$ and $\delta \boldsymbol{l}$ are of dimensions $(42 \times 42)$ whereas $\mathbf{a}$ is $(42 \times 3)$.

The relevant Green function submatrices appearing in the $\mathbf{g}$ matrix were computed from shell-model phonon data which were fitted to the experimental phonon dispersion relations of Elcombe and Pryor (1970). There are 23 Green functions. The elements of $\delta \boldsymbol{l}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ were computed from the Catlow and Norgett potential using the relaxations of the ions obtained from the HADES program (Norgett 1974). To be consistent, the same shell model data should have been used for the Green functions calculation as for the evaluation of $\delta \boldsymbol{l}$. In fact, the Catlow and Norgett potential reproduces the phonon dispersion quite adequately as has been pointed out by Haridasan *et al* (1979). Neverthe- less, the Green functions evaluated by both models yield values to within an accuracy of $0.5 \%$. The Green functions of $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ based on the shell model described by Elcombe and Pryor were available and we chose to use these.

The details of the Green function matrix elements and those of the $\delta \boldsymbol{l}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ matrices are given in an earlier publication by Haridasan *et al* (1979) on the impurity modes due to interstitials in $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$. The parameters of model I of that paper are used in the present calculations. If we look at equations (18) and (19) we find that $\mathbf{g}$ and $\gamma$ are complex matrices of Green functions and $\boldsymbol{u}_{1}^{0}$ is also complex for the $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ lattice. The $\mathbf{g}$ and $\gamma$ matrices were computed for $(\omega / \omega_{\text {max }}=x)$ ranging from $0.01$ to $0.99$ at intervals of $0.02$. The inverse of $\left[\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{g}\left(\delta \boldsymbol{l}+\mathbf{a} \gamma \mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\right]$ was computed using the routines from the IMSL

Library in the Cyber 73 computer at the University of Western Ontario. Since the matrices involved are (42 × 42), the complex matrices in the square brackets of equations (18) and (19), computed for x ranging from 0·01 to 0·99 at intervals of 0·02, were stored on disc. $u_{1}^{0}$ was furnished from a program in which the incoming phonon frequencies and eigenvectors were computed using the parameters of Elcombe and Pryor (1970). Using these data, the mean square amplitudes of a typical host atom (say, atom 1) can be computed using (Maradudin et al 1971)

$$
\left\langle u_{1}^{2}(1)\right\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{u_{1}^{2}(1, \omega)}{\omega} \operatorname{coth}\left(\frac{\hbar \omega}{2 k T}\right) \mathrm{d} \omega. \tag{20}
$$

Similarly for the interstitial

$$
\left\langle\xi^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\xi^{2}(\omega)}{\omega} \operatorname{coth}\left(\frac{\hbar \omega}{2 k T}\right) \mathrm{d} \omega. \tag{21}
$$

These integrations were performed numerically. To compare how the mean-square displacements of the host atom are affected by the interstitial, the mean-square displacements of the host atoms in the perfect crystal were also evaluated.

## 4. Results and discussion

### 4.1. Harmonic approximation (HA)

The mean-square displacements $\langle u^{2}(\mathrm{F})\rangle_{\mathrm{D}},\langle u^{2}(\mathrm{Ca})\rangle_{\mathrm{D}}\langle u^{2}(\mathrm{F})\rangle_{\mathrm{H}},\langle u^{2}(\mathrm{Ca})\rangle_{\mathrm{H}}$ and $\langle\xi^{2}\rangle$ were computed for temperatures ranging from 50 to 500 K at intervals of 50 K. The suffix D signifies that the corresponding quantity was calculated in the environment containing the interstitial. The suffix H signifies that the corresponding quantity was calculated in the perfect crystal environment in the absence of the interstitial. The calculation is performed on the harmonic approximation and the temperature depend- ence enters only through the hyperbolic term arising from the population factor. We believe that the harmonic approximation is reasonably valid up to 1000 K. Computations of the eigenvectors of the host $F^{-}$and $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ ions in the environment of the interstitial showed that they are generally different from the corresponding eigenvectors in the perfect crystal environment. In many situations involving substitutional defects, such as isotopic diffusion studies, it is customary (Achar 1970, Kalyani and Haridasan 1977) to use the eigenvectors of the perfect crystal in the defect environment. This may be adequate for simple isotopic substitutional defects, but for the impurity diffusion, the perturbed eigenvectors resulting from the presence of the impurity should be used. From our present results it is clear that for interstitial defects, these eigenvectors of the host atoms are considerably different from those of the corresponding perfect crystal.

Table 1 shows our results for the mean-square displacements of the interstitial and the host atoms at different temperatures. They are presented as $B_{\kappa}=(8 \pi^{2} / 3)\langle u^{2}(\kappa)\rangle$ in the conventional notation. Even though the eigenvectors of the host atoms were found to be different in the environment of the interstitial, the mean-square amplitudes are not noticeably different in the two situations. This shows that mutually compensating effects are present for different normal modes in the ensemble averaging process. There- fore it may be difficult to distinguish the two situations using experimental techniques which probe the crystal in only an average way.

The mean-square displacements of the interstitial $\langle \xi^2 \rangle$ are larger than those of the host atoms by about a factor of five at low temperatures and by a factor of more than three in the 1000 K range. There is no experimental information on $\langle \xi^2 \rangle$ in $\text{CaF}_2$, but the calculation of Zeller and Dederichs (1976) on FCC metals yields similar results which agree with the experimental results of Vogl *et al* (1974). Their calculation also indicated that a sizeable contribution to the enhanced value of $\langle \xi^2 \rangle$ comes from the resonant modes. Our computed values also showed the contribution from the resonant mode to be of the same order as the contribution from the remaining normal mode spectrum. So in a $\text{CaF}_2$ lattice when $\text{F}^-$ interstitials are present with a reasonably high concentration, either as a result of charge compensation due to $\text{RE}^{3+}$ substitutional defects, or because of the existence of Frenkel defects at high temperatures, the effect of large $\langle \xi^2 \rangle$ may show up in the Bragg intensities of the x-ray scattering. It is also worth considering whether such effects would show up in the modulation of the diffuse background, near the $(4n-1)$ and $(4n+1)$ neutron diffraction reflections which are observed in fluorites (Achmedzhanov *et al* 1978).

$(\text{B(F)})_{\text{H}}$ and $(\text{B(Ca)})_{\text{H}}$ computed in the present work at room temperature are 0·39 and $0\cdot 30\ \mathring{\text{A}}^2$ respectively. They can be compared with the corresponding experimental results of 0·32 and $0\cdot 39\ \mathring{\text{A}}^2$ reported by Willis (1965) obtained after least-square refinement of the neutron data. The corresponding $(\text{B(F)})_{\text{D}}$ and $(\text{B(Ca)})_{\text{D}}$ computed at room temperature are 0·34 and $0\cdot 33\ \mathring{\text{A}}^2$. It is clear that $(\text{B(F)})_{\text{D}}$ and $(\text{B(Ca)})_{\text{D}}$ are in slightly better agreement with the experimental results. Thus the results corresponding to the relaxed positions of the host atoms near an interstitial agree better with the experimental results. However, Willis (1965) obtained a larger value of $0\cdot 53\ \mathring{\text{A}}^2$ for $\langle u^2(F)\rangle_{\text{H}}$ when the $\text{F}^-$ ion was in the normal lattice site. He could get better agreement only when the $\text{F}^-$ ion was allowed to move towards the interstitial site. Our calculation refers to the presence at the interstitial site of a $\text{F}^-$ ion which because of Coulomb repulsion causes the host $\text{F}^-$ ions to move away from it. The second-neighbour $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ host ions move inward because of Coulombic attraction.

### 4.2. Quasiharmonic approximation (QHA)

Since we wish to compare our results with those of the experiment at higher temperatures, it is instructive to consider at this stage, the effect of anharmonicity at such temperatures. Due to the formidably difficult nature of the anharmonic calculations arising from phonon-phonon interactions the quasiharmonic approximation must be used in

defect systems such as this. The underlying assumption in this approximation is that the phonon frequency shifts arise solely from volume effects resulting from the thermal expansion of the lattice.

Since high-temperature thermal expansion data $\beta$ is lacking for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$, we assumed that the polynomial expansion of Srideshmukh and Deshpande (1964), which was fitted to moderately high temperature on $\beta$, holds good for 1000 and 1500 K. Looking at the temperature variation of $\beta$ in most of the materials at high temperatures, where it is reasonably constant, the present estimated values of $\beta$ are within the expected range. This is further justified by computing the phonon shift of the Raman frequency at 1200 K using this estimated $\beta$ value and the mode gamma computed by Radha Govindarajan *et al* (1979) using the shell model of lattice dynamics. The phonon shift for the Raman mode at 1200 K came out to be $38 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, in very reasonable agreement with the experimental result of $30 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ by Mead and Wilkinson (1977). The effect of temperature on the Green functions in the QHA was taken into account by a scaling procedure. It was found that to a good approximation,
$$
G(T, x)=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(T)}{\omega_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0)} G(0, x).
\tag{23}
$$

Therefore
$$
G(T, \omega)=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(T)}{\omega_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0)} G\left(0, \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{L}}(0) \omega}{\omega_{\mathrm{L}}(T)}\right).
\tag{24}
$$

The results are presented in table 2.

### 4.3. Mean-square displacements at high temperatures

We can compare our results on the high-temperature values of $\langle u^2(\text{F})\rangle$ and $\langle u^2(\text{Ca})\rangle$ with the experimental results of Thomas (1976) based on neutron scattering measurements on powder samples. He reports values of 0.034 and $0.085\ \text{\AA}^2$ for $\langle u^2(\text{F})\rangle$ at 973 and 1423 K respectively. These values are comparable with our results of 0.047 and $0.070\ \text{\AA}^2$ at 1000 K and 1500 K respectively under HA. Under QHA the corresponding results are 0.061 and $0.095\ \text{\AA}^2$ whereas the $\langle u^2(\text{F})\rangle_{\text{D}}^{\text{QHA}}$ are 0.077 and $0.107\ \text{\AA}^2$. Thus the experimental results especially near 1500 K lie between the results under HA and QHA. Note that the experimentally estimated values from neutron scattering intensities by Thomas were always the lower bound of the expected $\langle u^2\rangle$, since in his estimation, he has neglected the correction for thermal diffuse scattering (TDS). In such a context, it can be seen that the currently calculated results indeed show good agreement with experiment. The $\langle u^2(\text{Ca})\rangle$ reported at 1423 K is $0.033\ \text{\AA}^2$ whereas our computed result even under HA shows a higher value of $0.055\ \text{\AA}^2$. However, the experimental data have an inherent uncertaintity of about $\pm 12\%$ and the magnitude of the TDS correction is uncertain. Notice the dramatic increase of $\langle u^2(\text{I})\rangle$ at high temperature when the QHA is employed. This is as expected since the modes become softer at such high temperatures. In principle, the experimental values for $\langle u^2(\text{F})\rangle$ could be combined with the calculated values of the host crystal and for lattice $\text{F}^-$ ions adjacent to the interstitials $\langle u^2(\text{F})\rangle_{\text{D}}$, to calculate the concentration of interstitials at high temperatures. However, this must await results on single crystals to which TDS corrections are applied. It will also be necessary to examine the possible effect of the vacancies on the $\langle u^2\rangle$ for $\text{F}^-$ ions that are nearest neighbours to the vacancies.

### 5. Conclusions

A scattering matrix formalism was used to study the mean-square displacements of the $\text{F}^-$ ion interstitial and its neighbouring host atoms in $\text{CaF}_2$. It was found that the displacements of the host atom in the defect environment are generally different from those in the perfect crystal environment. However, the mean-square amplitudes are not very much different in the two situations. The mean-square displacement of the interstitial is considerably larger than the host atoms. These results are similar to those of Zeller and Dederichs for FCC metals which in turn are in agreement with experiment. Our calculated results for the host atoms also show good agreement with experimental results at room temperature. The results at high temperature for $\langle u^2(F)\rangle$ agree fairly well with the experimental data whereas those computed for $\langle u^2(\text{Ca})\rangle$ are rather larger than the reported experimental results. The omission of TDS corrections may be the major reason for this discrepancy.
