# ELECTRON IMPURITY SCATTERING IN INDIUM: A PSEUDOPOTENTIAL STUDY

J.C.C. DE RUITER, A. LODDER and G. QUELLE

Natuurkundig Laboratorium, Vrije Universiteit, Postbus 7161, 1007 MC Amsterdam, The Netherlands

Received 8 July 1988

Lifetimes, transport relaxation times, residual resistivities and Dingle temperatures are calculated for the vacancy and a number of impurities in indium, using the 6-$\psi$PW (pseudopotential-plane-wave) method with Heine-Abarenkov-Animahu model potentials. The results are compared with results according to more approximate models, like the phase shift model and the free electron model, and with experiment. The influence of lattice distortion is studied carefully. The impurities studied are Ag, Mg, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, Tl, Sn, Pb, Sb and Bi.

Long range lattice distortion effects are not negligible, but if the distortion is weak to moderate they do not change the anisotropy very much. It is therefore concluded that the KKR-Green's function method will lead to reliable Dingle temperatures if it is applied in this range, but it may fail for larger distortions. Further, the Ziman approximation for the relaxation time compares very well with the exact iterative solution of the Boltzmann equation. A satisfactory agreement is found with preliminary experimental results for second zone and third zone $\beta$-arm Dingle temperatures available for the impurities Sn and Pb.

## 1. Introduction

Simple metals form an interesting class of hosts. Although several of them have a rather complicated Fermi surface, like the trivalent metals Al and In, the relatively simple pseudo- potential method [1] represents its form remark- ably well. This is important in studies of elec- tron-impurity scattering properties [2,3], since the host wave function is responsible for the anisotropy of relaxation times and De Haas-Van Alphen scattering quantities like Dingle tem- peratures. Therefore Sorbello [4] and Pfändner et al. [5], using a pseudopotential method, ob- tained anisotropic transport relaxation times in Al, leading to a good agreement with measured impurity resistivities for impurities like Mg, Si, Zn, Ga and Ge. Also an ab initio KKR-Green's function method [6,7], which accounts for the scattering in the local region of an impurity exactly if one allows for a muffin-tin description of the alloy potential, leads to better results if the host Fermi surface is represented better.

At present the KKR-Green's function method has been developed to a standard method in describing the scattering of electrons by arbitrary impurities in arbitrary metallic hosts [8]. For the stronger scattering transition and rare earth met- als a pseudopotential description fails. However, in one respect the pseudopotential method is superior to the KKR-Green's function method. In the account of lattice distortion the latter method fails completely as for the long range effects, while these effects can be incorporated quite easily in the first method [1,9]. In order to investigate the contribution of these long range effects one needs the aid of the pseudopotential method, but then one has to restrict oneself to a simple metal.

Aluminum and indium are two serious candi- dates. Both have a sufficiently complicated Fermi surface so that anisotropy studies are worthwhile. However, in addition to conclusions about anisotropy and lattice distortion effects one automatically obtains results for resistivities and Dingle temperatures, being appropriate av- erages of anisotropic quantities. Measured values are available for both metals, but for indium

they are not well described yet, contrary to the situation for aluminum [4,5]. Another reason for choosing indium in this study is that, in spite of the resemblance of the Fermi surfaces of In and Al, the wave functions differ strongly in their partial-wave character, leading to marked differ- ences in the scattering anisotropy.

The calculations have been done for isolated vacancies and the impurities Ag, Mg, Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, Tl, Sn, Pb, Sb and Bi. This choice was based on the criteria of solubility in indium, existence of experimental data and availability of pseudopotential parameters, while it is interest- ing to realize, that, apart from Mg being a typical bivalent simple metal atom, these im- purities represent rather completely the elec- tronic properties of neighbouring columns and rows for indium in the periodic system.

For the bare ion potential we used the model developed by Heine, Abarenkov and Animalu [10]. For details we refer to the review by Heine and Weaire [11]. This non-local model potential can be seen as a reasonable compromise between more general but computationally awkward potentials developed later [12] and simple local potentials. The latter have been used widely but cannot be fitted to spectral data because of their unrealistic angular momentum dependence. A local potential, which can be seen as a natural approximation of the Heine-Abarenkov-- Animalu potential, is the empty core model of Ashcroft [13]. In the absence of non-local model potential data for Ag, our calculations for In(Ag) were done with this potential, which was also used in the scattering calculations for Al mentioned above [4,5].

In section 2 expressions for the lifetime, the relaxation time, the impurity resistivity and the Dingle temperature are given. Section 3 summa- rizes some data of the host metal and lattice distortion data for the different impurities. In section 4 results for scattering rates and transport relaxation times are given. Lattice distortion ef- fects are discussed in some detail. In section 5 results for the resistivity are presented and com- pared with experiment. Dingle temperatures are given and discussed in section 6. The paper ends with concluding remarks.

## 2. Expressions for scattering quantities

It is mainly for clarity that the expressions for the different scattering quantities, with emphasis on special features, are presented. In various forms they have been introduced and used in studies of Al [4,5,9,14], to which we refer for details.

A central quantity is the electron-impurity scattering rate
$$
\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})=\int_{\mathrm{FS}} \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{k}^{\prime} P\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}, \boldsymbol{k}\right) \quad(1)
$$
for an electron in the Bloch state $\psi_{k}$ labeled by $\boldsymbol{k}$, which in practice denotes a point on the Fermi surface (FS). The integral sign stands for sum- mation over all final states, and the transition probability is given by
$$
P\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}, \boldsymbol{k}\right)=c N \frac{2 \pi}{\hbar}\left|V_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{k}}\right|^{2} \delta\left(E_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-E_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right). \quad(2)
$$

This quantity is proportional to the concen- tration of impurities $c$ and the number of host atoms $N$ in the crystal volume $\Omega$, and it contains the transition matrix element in Born approxi- mation, which for a superposition of (pseudo-) plane waves (" $\psi$ PW's"),
$$
\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{\Omega^{1 / 2}} \sum_{j=1}^{6} a_{j}(\boldsymbol{k}) \exp \left\{\mathrm{i}\left(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{G}_{j}\right) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}\right\}, \quad(3)
$$
obtains the form
$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{k}}= & \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j j^{\prime}} a_{j^{\prime}}\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\right) a_{j}(\boldsymbol{k}) \\
& \times\left\{v^{\operatorname{imp}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{j^{\prime} j}\right)+\left(S\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{j^{\prime} j}\right)-1\right) v^{\operatorname{host}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{j^{\prime} j}\right)\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$\boldsymbol{G}_{j}$ is a reciprocal lattice vector, $\boldsymbol{q}_{j^{\prime} j}=\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{k}-$ $\boldsymbol{G}_{j^{\prime}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{j}$ and the host wave function coefficients $a_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})$ are found, after calculating an appropriate point $\boldsymbol{k}$ on the FS, by solving the pseudo-Hamil- tonian $6 \times 6$ eigenvalue equation at the Fermi energy. Instead of four $\psi$ PW's as in the case of Al, having the FCC structure, for In six recipro- cal lattice vectors mix due to the lower FCT

symmetry (see section 3). The quantities $v^{\mathrm{imp}}(q)$ and $v^{\mathrm{host}}(q)$ are the HAA model potential form factors for an impurity and host ion respectively, localized and screened [10,11] on the basis of the "on the Fermi sphere approximation" (|$\boldsymbol{k} - \boldsymbol{G}_j$| = |$\boldsymbol{k}' - \boldsymbol{G}_{j'}$| = $k_{\mathrm{F}}$) using the host Fermi wave vector length $k_{\mathrm{F}}$, and normalized on the host atomic volume $\Omega_0 = \Omega/N$. In the structure factor

$$
\begin{aligned}
S(\boldsymbol{q}) \equiv & \sum_{\boldsymbol{l} \neq 0}\left\{\exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{q} \cdot\left(\boldsymbol{l}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{l}}\right))-\exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{q} \cdot \boldsymbol{l})\right\} \\
= & \sum_{\boldsymbol{l} \neq 0} \mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{q} \cdot \boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{l}} \exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{q} \cdot \boldsymbol{l}) \\
& +S_{2}(\boldsymbol{q})+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
$$

lattice distortion is accounted for. Here $\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{l}}$ is the shift of a host atom from its perfect lattice position $\boldsymbol{l}$ (see also eq. 14).

The Dingle temperature is simply proportional to an average of the scattering rate over an extremal orbit on the Fermi suface

$$
T_{\mathrm{D}}^{*}=\frac{\hbar m_{\mathrm{c}}}{2 \pi k_{\mathrm{B}} m_{\mathrm{c}}^{*}}\left\langle\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\right\rangle_{\text {orbit }}.
$$

Renormalization by the experimentally observed cyclotron mass takes care of the electron-phonon enhancement [15], which is neglected in a band structure calculation.

Transport properties require solution of the Boltzmann equation, which for electron impurity scattering can be written in terms of the vector mean free path $\Lambda(\boldsymbol{k})$, the electron velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ at the point $\boldsymbol{k}$ and $\tau_{0}(\boldsymbol{k})$ [14]

$$
\Lambda(\boldsymbol{k})=\tau_{0}(\boldsymbol{k})\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{k})+\int_{\mathrm{FS}} \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{k}^{\prime} \Lambda\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\right) P\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}, \boldsymbol{k}\right)\right]. \quad(7)
$$

Within the 6-$\psi$PW scheme, the velocity corresponding to the state $\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}$ is given by

$$
\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{k})=\frac{\hbar}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{6}\left|a_{j}(\boldsymbol{k})\right|^{2}\left(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{G}_{j}\right).
$$

In the FCT symmetry of indium the conductivity $\sigma$ is still diagonal, but $\sigma_{x x}=\sigma_{y y} \neq \sigma_{z z}$. The impurity resistivity $\rho$ is anisotropic and given by

$$
\rho_{a}^{-1}=\sigma_{x x}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi^{3} \hbar} \int_{\mathrm{FS}} v_{x}(\boldsymbol{k}) \Lambda_{x}(\boldsymbol{k}) \mathrm{d} s_{\boldsymbol{k}} / v(\boldsymbol{k})
$$

along a crystal $a$-axis, and by

$$
\rho_{c}^{-1}=\sigma_{z z}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi^{3} \hbar} \int_{\mathrm{FS}} v_{z}(\boldsymbol{k}) \Lambda_{z}(\boldsymbol{k}) \mathrm{d} s_{\boldsymbol{k}} / v(\boldsymbol{k})
$$

along the $c$-axis. The FS integration elements are denoted by $\mathrm{d} s_{\boldsymbol{k}}$, and $v(\boldsymbol{k})$ is the magnitude of the velocity. Similarly, the integrations in (1) and (7), and in (11) below, can be replaced by $\Omega / 8 \pi^{3} \hbar \int_{\mathrm{FS}} \mathrm{d} s_{\boldsymbol{k}} / v(\boldsymbol{k})$, due to the delta function in the transition probability (2).

The expressions described so far will form the basis of our impurity scattering calculations in indium. In discussing subsequent approximate expressions, we first mention that in all literature until now the structure factor (5) in the transition matrix element (4) has been approximated by its angular average, leading to a function of the absolute value $q$. We will compare results obtained by evaluting the full $\boldsymbol{q}$ dependent $S$ with results based on the angularly averaged structure factor.

Sometimes an approximate solution of the Boltzmann equation is used, in which the vector mean free path is replaced by the product $\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{k})$ $\times \tau(\boldsymbol{k})$. In a step which is correct for a spherically Fermi surface and a spherically symmetric scatterer one arrives at the following expression for the inverse transport relaxation time:

$$
\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})=\int_{\mathrm{FS}} \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\left(1-\hat{v}(\boldsymbol{k}) \cdot \hat{v}\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\right)\right) P\left(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}, \boldsymbol{k}\right), \quad(11)
$$

in which $\hat{v}$ is the unit vector $\boldsymbol{v} / v$. Expression (11) is called the Ziman approximation [16]. This expression has been criticized [17], but, if it is applied as it stands, it contains valuable host and impurity information through the velocities on the Fermi surface and the full transition probability (2) with (4). In solving the Boltzmann equation numerically by iteration the Ziman approximation forms a good starting point. Only a few iterations lead towards convergence [14].

In the free electron model the wave function

(3) reduces to one plane wave, the matrix ele- ment (4) becomes simply

$$
V(q)=\frac{1}{N}\left\{v^{\mathrm{imp}}(q)+(S(q)-1) v^{\mathrm{host}}(q)\right\}, \quad(12)
$$

in which $\boldsymbol{q}=\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{k}$, the Ziman expression holds exactly, both $\tau$ and $\tau_{0}$ are isotropic and the resistivity reduces to the classical form

$$
\rho=\frac{m}{n e^{2} \tau}=\frac{3 \pi^{2} m}{e^{2} k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3} \tau}. \quad(13)
$$

In our calculations we will use the potential (12), which contains a structure factor (the angularly averaged one) and therefore implies the possibi- lity to distinguish results with and without lattice distortion, even for the free electron model.

Finally we mention the phase shift model in- troduced by Sorbello [4]. The aim of this model is to include in the description strongly scattering impurities also. The full $6-\psi$ PW host wave func- tion is used and the matrix element in (4) of the form (12) is replaced by a $t$-matrix element expressed in terms of the Born phase shifts corresponding to (12). Since by this trick the potential matrix element (4) becomes a $t$-matrix element $T_{k^{\prime} k}$, the optical theorem can be applied for the scattering rate. Although this model is only partially based on a derivation and mainly on a reasoning by analogy, it is interesting in its attempt to introduce elements of scattering theory in pseudopotential calculations. There- fore, we will show also results obtained with this phase shift model, both for $\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ and $\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$, using for this latter quantity a tentative expres- sion analogous to Sorbello's phase shift formula for the scattering rate.

### 3. Data for the host and lattice distortion data

Indium has a face-centred tetragonal (FCT) structure with a $c / a$ ratio of 1.0831 . The lattice constant $a=8.6090$ atomic units. The Fermi sur- face of this trivalent metal is similar to that of aluminum and does not deviate much in form from the free electron Fermi surface obtained according to the Harrison construction. The de- tails are investigated thoroughly $[18,19]$ and the main features are represented well by the pseudopotential 6-$\psi$PW scheme of Ashcroft and Lawrence [20].

In the numerical calculations the $\boldsymbol{k}$-dependent quantities were evaluated in the irreducible wedge, which for the FCT structure is $1 / 16$ th of the Brillouin zone. The points $\boldsymbol{k}$ on the Fermi surface were solved by varying $\boldsymbol{k}$ along suitably chosen lines and testing where the 6-$\psi$PW Hamiltonian eigenvalue equation satisfies $E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=$ $E_{\mathrm{F}}$. The integrals over the Fermi surface were of course replaced by summations over a grid of small surface elements. We calculated $239 \boldsymbol{k}$ points in the irreducible wedge and defined 239 small FS elements so that each $\boldsymbol{k}$ was the centre of a surface element. For the integrations over the whole FS, e.g. in (1) and (9), the $\boldsymbol{k}$ points, surface elements $\mathrm{d} s_{\boldsymbol{k}}$ and other quantities needed like the velocity (8) were extended to other wedges by application of the 16 FCT symmetry transformations. To minimize the numerical error the grid had a varying mesh over the Fermi surface, such that the area of a surface element was smaller in regions with a higher curvature. Further details are not interesting for the present purpose. We just mention that the Fermi surface consists of a large second zone central hole surface and small prolate electron arms in the third zone, traditionally referred to as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ arms. The $\alpha$ arms are very small, and we focus further discussion mainly on the second zone and $\beta$ arms.

Information about lattice distortion due to the presence of impurities is available from X-ray data [21]. A simple elastical model [22] provides the connection with the radial displacements $\boldsymbol{l}_{l}$ of host atoms

$$
\boldsymbol{u}_{l}=A \frac{\boldsymbol{l}}{l^{3}}, \quad(14)
$$

which are required in the evaluation of the struc- ture factor (5). This connection leads to the lattice distortion parameter $D$ given by

$$
D \equiv \frac{4 \pi A}{\Omega_{0}}=\frac{\Delta \Omega_{0}}{\gamma \Omega_{0}},
\tag{15}
$$

where $\Omega_{0}$ is the perfect host unit cell volume, $\Delta \Omega_{0}$ the measured volume change per defect and $\gamma$ the Poisson ratio, which for indium is equal to 1.13. The used values of $D$ for the different defects studied are given in the first column of table I. They are typical values, based on X-ray data where available [21], or simple atomic volume estimates as in [23]. The $D$ value for a vacancy is based on the FCT analogon of the approximation $D \simeq\left(c_{44}-c_{12}\right) / c_{11}$ for a vacancy in a cubic system [24]. It is seen that the impurities form a representative set for a lattice distortion study, since some of them induce expansion and the others induce contraction.

The first term in the expansion of the structure factor (5) is a slowly convergent series, which can be evaluated by recasting it in two quickly convergent series using an Ewald technique [9]. It forms the main contribution and in fact only for the vacancy, having the large $D$ value of 0.6, the fastly converging second term $S_{2}(\boldsymbol{q})$ was accounted for. The higher order terms are negligible for all cases.

## 4. Scattering rates and relaxation times

### 4.1. Fermi surface averages

Scattering rates or inverse life times $\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ and inverse transport relaxation times $\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ were calculated for 239 states $\boldsymbol{k}$ on a 1/16th symmetry part of the indium Fermi surface. In addition to these exact 6-$\psi$PW results, approximate values were obtained from the phase shift (PS) model. Calculations were performed with and without lattice distortion. The 6-$\psi$PW calculations with lattice distortion were done twice, using the angularly averaged structure factor and the exact form (5). In the PS model the scattering potential is assumed to be spherically symmetric, so that only the angularly averaged one could be used. The latter also applies to the free electron (FE) model.

The main results are collected in table I. The values in the second, third and fourth column are the averages for the third-zone arms and the second-zone surface, respectively. The values in column 5 and the PS results are averages over the total set of 239 points. Lattice distortion is included in all cases, the exact 6-$\psi$PW results being based on the use of $S(\boldsymbol{q})$.

It is seen that the total FS averaged scattering rates are not much different from the FE values. The $\psi$PW results for Hg and Zn are larger, those for Mg and Bi are somewhat smaller, but the differences are relatively small. For all models the inverse relaxation times are smaller than the inverse lifetimes, as expected. The few exceptions (Zn, Tl) can be explained by the rather smooth shape of the form factor (12) for these impurities in indium. As a consequence, the contributions of large angle (i.e. large $q$) scattering, being the most effective ones in (11), are relatively large for Zn and Tl. One also observes for most impurties a clear enhancement of the results for $\langle\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle$ over the FE values, both for the $\psi$PW and PS model. This was also found in studies of Al [2,14]. It was intuitively attributed to plane wave mixing. We will return to this in discussing resistivity values.

The anisotropy over the FS is largest for the impurities Ga, Tl, Sn, Pb and Bi, for which the second zone averages of $\tau_{0}^{-1}$ and $\tau^{-1}$ are about two or three times larger than the $\beta$-arm averages. The variation of the local values within each zone is similar or even stronger, with maximum values along the second zone edges near the Brillouin zone boundaries and in the $\beta$-arm bellies. For the impurities Zn, Cd and Hg on the other hand, the ratio between local maximum and minimum values is less than 1.5.

The PS results for $\langle\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle$ tend to be slightly larger than the exact 6-$\psi$PW results. This may be due to the fact that the Born phase shifts used in the PS model are based on the generally large form factors $V(q)$ in the $q$-interval $(0,2k_{\text{F}})$, whereas the exact results also incorporate the small form factors for $q>2k_{\text{F}}$. This also explains why the tendency of somewhat larger PS values is less clear for $\langle\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle$, being a quantity which depends strongly on the detailed shape of $V(q)$ near $q=2k_{\text{F}}$. The partial averages over second and third zone vary in an approximately similar way as those in the exact model and have therefore not been included in table I (some are given in table II). This similarity suggests that also in the exact model the anisotropy, or at least most of it, can be described in terms of the partial-wave character of the host wave function, elucidating again the importance of a correct host wave function for scattering quantities. This would explain the large differences between second and third-zone results for typical s-scatterers such as Ga, Tl, Sn, Pb and Bi, since the anisotropy of the host partial wave coefficients is most pronounced for $l=0$. For Zn, p-scattering is dominant, resulting in rather isotropic life and relaxation times.

Although the PS model indicates the origin of the anisotropy, it is not always a good approximation. The second-zone averages of $\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ for Ag, Mg and Cd, being equal to 9.30, 2.22 and $0.95\times10^{13}\ \text{s}^{-1}/\text{at}\%$ respectively, are clearly larger than the exact values in the fourth column. The most deviating results for $\langle\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle$ are 0.16 ($\beta$-arm) and 0.06 (second zone) for Hg and 0.04 and 0.23 for Ga, showing that also the relative anisotropy is not always correct.

### 4.2. Effect of lattice distortion

It was found that, if the distortion is weak, say $|D|<0.15$, it does not lead to drastic changes in the averaged results or the anisotropy. Therefore we do not show results for all impurities. The main effect of lattice distortion is a decrease of the scattering-potential form factor for small $q$, resulting in somewhat reduced scattering rates (except for the in indium "neutral" impurities Ga and Tl, for which lattice distortion gives the main contribution to the defect scattering potential). The effect on $\tau^{-1}$ is mostly smaller than that on $\tau_{0}^{-1}$ and not always in the same direction. Increased averages $\langle\tau^{-1}(k)\rangle$ have been found for the impurities Ga (third zone only), Tl, Pb (second zone) and Sb (second zone). Generally, the results for the $\beta$-arm are affected most.

For large values of $|D|$ the changes can be considerable as shown in table II for the impurities Zn, Ga and Pb. These impurities are typical examples of weak (Zn, Ga) and strong (Pb) scatterers, with small (Zn), moderate (Ga) or large (Pb) anisotropy. The given values are the averages $\langle\tau_{0}^{-1}(k)\rangle$ and $\langle\tau^{-1}(k)\rangle$ for the $\beta$-arm, second zone and total Fermi surface according to the 6-$\psi$PW and the PS model, and the results of the FE model. The rows indicated by a and a' contain the values calculated without lattice distortion. For the rows b and b', the distortion has been taken into account via the angularly averaged structure factor $S(q)$, whereas c and c' pertain to the exact structure factor $S(\boldsymbol{q})$ in the 6-$\psi$PW model. It is noticed that the anisotropy can be increased (Pb) as well as decreased (Ga) by the distortion.

With the only exception of $\langle\tau^{-1}(k)\rangle$ for a vacancy, the 6-$\psi$PW results based on the use of $S(\boldsymbol{q})$ are larger than those based on $S(q)$. These differences can be important if the distortion is large, as is clear from table II.

The various effects of lattice distortion depend on the type of the impurity potential and on the distortion parameter $D$. The latter dependence

can be analysed explicitly. We note that $S(\boldsymbol{q})$, neglecting the very small sum $S_{2}(\boldsymbol{q})$ mentioned in section 3, is proportional to $D$. Then it is clear from (4) that $\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ and $\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ can be written as $A+B D+C D^{2}$, where $A$ is the corresponding result for $D=0$ and $B$ and $C$ depend on $\boldsymbol{k}$ but not on $D$. Moreover, $C$ is the same for all impurities. After averaging over the FS, $C$ has the value 5.0 for $\left\langle\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\right\rangle$ and 4.1 for $\left\langle\tau^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})\right\rangle$, in $10^{13} \mathrm{~s}^{-1} /$ at $\%$. The calculations for $D=0$ and $D \neq 0$ can then be used to determine $B$ for the different impurities, and thus the sensitivity of the results to $D$. In most cases, a small change of $D$ results in relatively smaller changes of $\tau_{0}^{-1}$ and $\tau^{-1}$, which is satisfactory in view of the rough estimates used for $D$.

At this point the question rises how these results can be interpreted such, that they may bear relevance to KKR-Green's function method results. As mentioned in the introduction the latter cannot account for long range lattice dis- tortion effects. On the other hand they are local- ly superior to pseudopotential results. We con- clude that for lattice distortion parameters $|D| \leqslant$ 0.15 KKR-Green's function results can be consi- dered as reliable. The main effects, induced by the local alloy potental and the distortion of the first few shells around the impurity are accoun- ted for very well. For larger $D$ values table II shows rather large effects, both in absolute value and in anisotropy. A remedy could be, that a pseudopotential method would be used to calcu- late relative changes in order to correct KKR- Green's function results for defects with large lattice distortion.

### 4.3. Generalized Linde rule

Is there any relation between the calculated results and the type of impurity, e.g. its valence or ionic-core structure? The averaged results in table I are roughly proportional to $X^{2}$, where $X=Z^{i}-Z$ is the valence difference between impurity and host, but there are large deviations (in particular for the $X= \pm 1$ impurities).

Good parabolic fits can only be obtained for impurities belonging to the same row of the Periodic Table. A least-squares procedure using the values in column 5 of table I results in the approximations $(10^{13} \mathrm{~s}^{-1} /$ at $\%)$

for 5th row impurities:
$$\langle \tau _ { 0 } ^ { - 1 } ( \boldsymbol { k } ) \rangle = - 0. 5 9 - 0. 1 4 X + 1. 7 2 X ^ { 2 } \quad \mathrm { a n d }$$

$$\langle \tau ^ { - 1 } ( \boldsymbol { k } ) \rangle = - 0. 2 5 - 0. 1 6 X + 0. 9 2 X ^ { 2 }$$

for 6th row impurities:
$$\langle \tau _ { 0 } ^ { - 1 } ( \boldsymbol { k } ) \rangle = 0. 2 3 + 0. 7 8 X + 0. 9 6 X ^ { 2 } \quad \mathrm { a n d }$$

$$\langle \tau ^ { - 1 } ( \boldsymbol { k } ) \rangle = 0. 3 4 + 0. 5 8 X + 0. 4 3 X ^ { 2 }.$$

These relations can be considered as generaliza- tions of the well-known Linde rule [25]. In its original form, found empirically for noble met- als, this rule states that residual resistivities for a given solvent can be written as $\rho=a+b X^{2}$, where $a$ and $b$ are constants for impurities of the same period. Considering the effects of lattice distortion, Dexter [26] proposed an additional linear term in $X$, which has proved to be correct (experimentally) for many polyvalent metal al- loys [23]. Our results suggest that the rule in this form can be extended to the scattering rates and inverse relaxation times.

From a theoretical point of view, Linde's rule is not trivial. The Friedel sum rule as used by Blatt [27] rather suggests a proportionality to $X_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}$, where $X_{\mathrm{d}}=Z^{i}-Z-D Z$ is the effective charge determining the (screened) Coulombic tail of the scattering potential. This would ex- plain the above results, since $D$ depends in a roughly linear way on $X$ for impurities of the same period. However, least-squares parabolic fits to our results based on $X_{\mathrm{d}}$ instead of $X$ resulted in comparable deviations for the $\mathrm{Hg}$ row, but in much larger deviations for the $\mathrm{Ag}$ row. Apart from this, the obtained fits did not substantiate the simple argument above. They all required a large linear term in $X_{\mathrm{d}}$ and quite different coefficients for the 5th and 6th row (which are not distinguished by Friedel's sum rule).

Concluding, an explanation of Linde's rule in terms of valences and distortion parameters alone is at least incomplete. Further analysis should include the details of the ion potentials.

The iterated mean free paths deviate in direction from the Ziman-approximated mean free paths, especially for strongly curved regions of the FS. Whereas $\Lambda_{k}^{Zi}$ is locally normal to the FS, there seems to be a tendency for $\Lambda_{k}$ to have a direction normal to the most nearby relatively flat region of the FS. The angle between $\Lambda_{k}$ and $\Lambda_{k}^{Zi}$ varies from $0^{\circ}$ to $12^{\circ}$. Larger values, up to $60^{\circ}$, have been found in the $\alpha$-arms, the $\beta$-arm junctions and second-zone points near the Brillouin zone corner W [20]. However, the corresponding areas are small and for most parts of the FS the deviation is smaller than $10^{\circ}$. In combination with the mostly small difference in length, this explains the success of the Ziman approximation in calculating resistivities. The

rare occurrence of large angles between $\Lambda_{k}$ and $\Lambda_{k}^{Zi}$ is not specific for In or the 6-$\psi$PW model. Similar results have been found for Al [5] and Cu [28].

Because the anisotropy of the resistivity is small, in table IV only the average value for the resistivity is given for all defects. For the 6-$\psi$PW results still the iterative solution of the Boltzmann equation and the anisotropic structure factor $S(\boldsymbol{q})$ are used. The experimental values in table IV are those measured or quoted from other authors by Aleksandrov and Dukin [23]. The given upper and lower estimates are based on the accuracy claimed by them or on values of other authors, whichever gives the largest range.

The 6-$\psi$PW results for Ga, Sn and Pb impurities in In are in good agreement with experiment, and the deviations for Ag, Hg and Bi are relatively small. The rather large deviations for the weak scatterers Zn, Cd and Tl may be due to errors in the scattering potentials. For Zn, the resisitivity is probably smaller than the absolute accuracy that can be obtained within a pseudopotential calculation (the rather good FE result must therefore be considered as accidental). Moreover, the dilute In alloys with Ag, Zn and Sb may be less well-defined systems from a metallurgical point of view, in which case the experimental results are less reliable and more difficult to interpret. No obvious explanation can be given for the bad agreement in case of In(Mg). The low PS values for Hg and Ga indicate that this model does not work well for all types of scattering potentials.

A point of interest is the considerable enhancement of the resistivities in the $\psi$PW model over the FE estimates, resulting in mostly much better agreement with experiment. This effect was found earlier in Al [2,14] and it was attributed intuitively to plane wave mixing, which arises in the $\psi$PW model in a natural way because of the multiple plane wave character of the wave function (3) at the Brillouin zone boundaries. Small-angle matrix elements between plane waves, i.e. the large $V(q)$ values for small $q$, get a larger weight in the 6-$\psi$PW model than in the FE model. However, in a close examination of the expressions we found that this plane wave mixing is only one of the contributions. The expressions (9) and (10) for the conductivity are proportional to the FS area and the FS velocity. We calculated the ratios $4\pi k_{\text{FE}}^{2}/\int_{\text{FS}} \text{d}s_{k}$ and $v_{\text{FE}}(\int_{\text{FS}} \text{d}s_{k}/v(\boldsymbol{k}))/\int_{\text{FS}} \text{d}s_{k}$ and found the values 1.29 and 1.24 respectively. So these simple factors, resulting from a correct description of the host Fermi surface, can take care of an enhancement of the resistivity by a factor of 1.60. One can show that the anisotropy of the $k$ dependent quantities in (9) and (10) reduces this factor somewhat, which, apart from the $\psi$PW mixing effect, explains that the enhancement over FE values in table IV is largest for isotropic scatterers such as a vacancy, Zn, Cd and Hg.

No experimental result is known for the vacancy resistivity in In. Measurement of this quantity is difficult because it is coupled to the problem of determining the single vacancy concentration. It is however interesting to note that a FE calculation of Shyu et al. [29], based on a carefully screened non-local Shaw potential, resulted in values between 0.88 and $1.17\ \mu\Omega\ \text{cm/at\%}$ (depending on the way many-electron effects were taken into account). As these values are in agreement with our FE result, this supports in a global sense the vacancy (or host) potential used in our calculations.

## 6. Dingle temperatures

The amplitude of a dHvA signal is damped by a factor depending on the average of the scattering rate $\tau_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{k})$ over a dHvA orbit. In $\boldsymbol{k}$-space, a dHvA orbit corresponds to an extremal FS cross section perpendicular to the applied magnetic field.

## 7. Concluding remarks

Using Heine-Abarenkov-Animalu model potential parameters, impurity scattering in indium is studied for a number of impurities and a vacancy. The 6-$\psi$PW description of the electronic properties of indium is satisfactory. It is more reliable than the phase shift model proposed by Sorbello [4], although this model reveals the origin of anisotropy in life times and relaxation times in $k$ space. The enhancement of calculated resistivities compared with free electron results is clarified. It cannot be attributed merely to plane wave mixing, as was supposed up to now. Simple geometrical differences between the real Fermi surface and a free electron sphere already take care of an enhancement factor of 1.60.

From an analysis of the effects of lattice distortion it is concluded that the more fundamental and more generally applicable Korringa-Kohn-Rostoker Green's function method leads only to reliable Dingle temperature [7] and resistivity values [32] for weak to moderate lattice distortion.
