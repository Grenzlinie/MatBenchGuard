# Improvement of the Pt/graphene interface adhesion by metallic adatoms for fuel cell applications

Fatih G. Sen¹, Yue Qi² and Ahmet T. Alpas¹

¹Department of Mechanical, Automotive and Materials Engineering, University of Windsor, 401 Sunset Ave, Windsor, Ontario N9B 3P4 Canada
²Materials and Processes Laboratory, General Motors R&D Center 30500 Mound Rd, Warren, Michigan 48090-9055 U.S.A.

## ABSTRACT

The degradation of carbon supported Pt catalyst limits the lifetime of polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cells. The bond between Pt and carbon must be significantly strengthened in order to prevent detachment of Pt particles from carbon support. In this work, first principles calculations were carried out in an attempt to understand the role that metallic adatoms play in the enhancement of the Pt/carbon interface adhesion. Metallic adatoms including all first row transition metals as well as Li, Al, Zr, Nb, and Au were inserted into a Pt(111)/graphene interface. The work of separation required to break the interface between Pt-adatom or carbon-adatom bond was then calculated for each configuration, revealing that the carbon-adatom bond was weaker than the Pt-adatom bond, making it easier to break the interface from the carbon-adatom bond side. Among all the investigated metal adatoms, Co, Ni and V were the most promising elements for bridging Pt onto graphene surface. These metals donated charges that were distributed more evenly between carbon and Pt and formed covalent bonds with carbon and metallic bonds with Pt. The strength of the Pt-adatom bond was proportional to the amount of charge transferred from the adatom to the Pt particle. The strength of graphene-adatom bond correlated with the ratio of the charge transfer to graphite and Pt, depending on the bridging nature of the adatoms.

## INTRODUCTION

The operation lifetime of proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) still requires an improvement, while the degradation of the carbon supported Pt catalyst system causes a substantial performance decrease due to a decrease in the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) [1]. This loss of ECSA is caused by both the dissolution of Pt particles from carbon support [2], and the agglomeration of Pt particles during electrochemical cycling. The agglomeration of Pt particles occurs via two distinct mechanisms: The Ostwald ripening process and migration of Pt particles on the carbon support [3]. In order to mitigate the Pt particle agglomeration on the carbon surface, either this surface or the Pt catalyst surface--or both--should be modified [4-6]. The agglomeration of Pt particles on the carbon support was found to be mitigated either by the alloying of Pt with non-noble metals or modifying the carbon found in PEMFCs, which was believed to be due to the enhancement in the anchoring of Pt to carbon [6]. Functionalizing the carbon support with sulphur, nitrogen and phosphorus yielded a decrease in Pt particle size and increased its bond strength to carbon, while nitrogen doping additionally improved the catalytic activity of Pt particles and enhanced surface basicity [7]. Boron doped carbon was predicted [8] to have a stronger interaction with Pt, and exhibited longer term stability than pure carbon [9]. The alloying of the Pt catalyst with non-noble metals was reported to reduce the agglomeration rate of Pt catalysts on the carbon support, [10-13].

The present study aims to investigate which metallic modifications of the carbon surface, will enhance the binding strength of Pt with carbon. We used first principles methods with the calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) to study the effect of metallic modifications on Pt/graphene interface adhesion. The Pt/graphene interface was constructed by introducing a metal adatom to this location and the changes in the interface strength due to the presence of this adatom was determined by computing the work of separation.

## METHODOLOGY

High resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR TEM) investigations observed the formation of Pt catalyst and carbon support interfaces between Pt (111) and graphite (0001) surfaces at the PEMFC electrodes [14]. Our calculations, however in accordance with previous DFT calculations, showed a very weak interaction between Pt (111) and graphite (0001) [15,16]. In order to determine the possible atomic sites for adatoms, prior to interface calculations, we first studied the adsorption of metals on graphite (0001) surface via the consideration of first row transition metals (TMs): Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, and second row TMs; Zr and Nb. Au as a noble metal and simple metallic atoms such as Al and Li were also considered. A single sheet of graphite (i.e. a graphene sheet) was selected to represent the graphite (0001) surface. This approximation was shown to be appropriate for modeling the carbon surface [17], because DFT cannot capture dispersion relations between separate graphite plates. The metals studied were adsorbed on top, hollow and bridge sites on the 2x2 graphene surface. The adsorption energies were in good agreement with the previous DFT calculations [18,19]while, all of the metals preferred the hollow sites on graphene surface – except Cu, which preferred the bridge site.

The interfaces between the Pt and the metal adsorbed graphene were constructed by matching 6 layers of a Pt (111) surface (each layer containing 3 atoms) with a 2x2 graphene surface containing 8 carbon atoms. The interface model and interface registry used are shown in **Figure 1-a**. The Pt [$\bar{1}2\bar{1}$] direction was aligned in the direction of the graphene [0$\bar{1}$0], so that an interface geometry consisting of $(111)[\bar{1}2\bar{1}]_{Pt} \parallel (0001)[0\bar{1}0]_{C}$ was created. Attempts were made to match different orientations of Pt and graphene but this particular interface geometry had the lowest energy, consistent with previous the DFT studies [16]. The calculated edge dimension of the Pt (111) slab was 4.87 Å while the graphene 2x2 surface had an edge dimension of 4.92 Å. The lattice mismatch was about 1% suggesting that the misfit dislocations could be ignored. We used a 15 Å vacuum in the z-direction in order to prevent the effects of periodic images. The metal adatom was then placed in this interface geometry so that it was located between the hollow site on the graphene surface and the top site on Pt (111) surface, for which the initial separation between Pt surface and adatom was 2.0 Å. This configuration was deemed appropriate, since most metals preferred to be situated at the hollow site on the graphene.

The strength of the Pt/graphene interfaces with an adatom was determined by calculating the work of separation, $W_{sep}$, which is the energy required to reversibly separate an interface into two free surfaces. In the presence of an adatom, the interface can be separated into two distinct surfaces by breaking the bond between Pt and adatom (Pt-X), or by breaking the bond between the carbon and the adatom (C-X). Therefore, the strength of interface was computed for both cases by considering the atomic arrangements shown in Figure 2. The work of separation for breaking the Pt-X bond ($W_{sep}^{Pt-X}$) and work of separation for breaking of C-X bond ($W_{sep}^{C-X}$) are defined as

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{sep}{ }^{{ }_{P t-X}} & =\frac{1}{A}\left[E_{P t}+E_{\text {graphene}+x}-E_{P t / \text { graphene}+x}\right] \\
W_{sep}{ }^{{ }^{C-X}} & =\frac{1}{A}\left[E_{P t+x}+E_{\text {graphene}}-E_{P t / \text { graphene}+x}\right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

where $E_{Pt}, E_{Pt+x}$ and $E_{\text{graphene}}, E_{\text{graphene}+x}$ are the total energies of the relaxed 6 layer Pt(111) slab with and without adatom and the relaxed graphene (0001) surface with and without adatom respectively, with $E_{Pt / \text{graphene}+x}$ as the total energy of the relaxed interface.

All computations were performed using a projector-augmented wave (PAW) method with exchange correlation energy approximated in the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [20] to DFT as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [21,22]. Calculations were carried out by PAW-PBE [23,24] potentials, supplied by VASP. The total energies were obtained by relaxing the system according to atomic positions and minimizing the Hellman-Feynman forces using a conjugate gradient method considering the spin polarization. In all calculations, energy convergence to 1-2 meV was obtained using a 10x10x1 grid of $\Gamma$-centered k-points and a plane wave cutoff energy of 500 eV. The electronic degrees of freedom for structures were converged to $10^{-5} \mathrm{eV} /$ cell and Hellman-Feynman forces were relaxed to less than $0.05 \mathrm{eV} / \AA$. Atomic relaxations were carried out using Methfessel-Paxton smearing with a width of $\sigma=0.05 \mathrm{eV}$ and final total energy was calculated using a tetrahedron method with Blöchl corrections.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Pt/graphene Interface Strength

The calculated work of separation values for different metals according to the above methodology, are plotted in Figure 2. The work of separation for a pure Pt/graphene interface

was calculated as $0.009\ \text{J/m}^2$, and this was used as a reference for comparison purposes. The relative change in the $W_{sep}^{Pt-X}$ among the metals was quite similar to the trend for the adsorption energies of the metals on the Pt surface. Early TM elements, including Sc, Ti, Zr and Nb formed the strongest bond with Pt--$W_{sep}^{Pt-X} \geq 3.0\ \text{J/m}^2$. However, this figure reveals that a higher energy is required to break the Pt-X bond, compared to the C-X bond. Consequently, the interface would be expected to break at the C-X bond, inferring that this C-X interface should be regarded as more important than the Pt-X for controlling the overall interface strength. Figure 2 shows that among all the elements Co, Ni and V had the highest $W_{sep}^{C-X}$ with values exceeding $0.6\ \text{J/m}^2$-- identifying them as the most promising elements for strengthening the Pt/graphene interface. On the other hand, Al, Zn and Au did not bind to carbon in the presence of a Pt surface, so they are not considered suitable for enhancing the interfacial strength. Early TM elements were found to form very strong bonds with Pt, but for the configuration shown in Figure 1-b (i.e. present between the carbon and Pt), their bond with carbon weakened, with $W_{sep}^{C-X}$ values around 0.3-$0.4\ \text{J/m}^2$. These elements are not beneficial as middle TM elements for bridging Pt to carbon.

### Electronic Structure of Interfaces

To better understand the bonding strengths of the interfaces in the presence of different metal adatoms, the electronic structure of the interfaces were analyzed by considering the charge density transfer, electron localization function (ELF), and partial density of the states (PDOS). In order to determine the total amount of charge transfer at the Pt/graphene interface, Bader charge analysis [26,27] was carried out. The total charge transfer was calculated by summing up all the charges on the Pt atoms in Pt (111) slab, and on the carbon atoms in the graphene. When this was compared to the charge present in pure Pt (111) slab and graphene surfaces, it was found that charge donation from adatom to both Pt and graphene in all interfaces occurred.

ELF analysis revealed that Pt and metal adatoms would form metallic bonds as expected. Since the cohesion of the metallic materials is related to the electron density [25], larger amount of charge transfer from to adatom to Pt is expected to increase the electron density and thus would increase $W_{sep}^{Pt-X}$. Figure 3-a shows the $W_{sep}^{Pt-X}$ is proportional to the total amount of charge transfer from the adatom to Pt.

Zr, Nb, Ti and Sc donated the highest amount of charge to Pt resulting in the highest $W_{sep}^{Pt-X}$. In our previous adatom adsorption studies, we found these early transition metals also formed strong bonds with either Pt or graphene surfaces when adsorbed on either side.

These early TM elements, (Zr, Nb, Ti and Sc) also donated the highest amount of charge to carbon, compared to other elements considered. However, for the C-X interface, the large amount of charge transfer from the adatom to carbon did not always give rise to a large $W_{sep}^{C-X}$. For instance, Co donated a charge of 0.37 to carbon and 0.36 to Pt. On the other hand, Ti donated a charge of 0.84 to carbon and 1.12 to Pt. While Ti donated more charge to carbon than Co, Co exhibited a larger $W_{sep}^{C-X}$. The investigation of the PDOS of adatom and carbon atoms on graphene revealed that early TM elements donated more charge to carbon and formed bonds with higher ionic character when bonding with carbon. On the other hand, Co, Ni and V formed strong covalent bonds with carbon due to hybridization between metal $d$ and carbon $p_z$ states.

Figure 3 The relation between work of separation for a) C-X bond and b)Pt-X with the associated charge transfer from metals at Pt/graphene interface.

Since C-X interface did not have a metallic character, $W_{sep}^{C-X}$ is not expected to correlate with charge transfer to carbon. In fact, $W_{sep}^{C-X}$ vs. charge transfer to carbon plot did not show any consistent trend. The strength of the C-X bond can be assumed to depend on the adatom's capability of acting as a bridge between the Pt and the carbon. We defined the bridging ability of an adatom as the percentage of the charge donated from the adatom to the graphene, over the charge donated to the Pt as shown in Figure 3-b. This figure shows that the elements that provided larger $W_{sep}^{C-X}$ values donated more electronic charge to graphene than to Pt such that for Co this ratio was 1.03 but for Ti it was 0.75. Figure 3-b illustrates that for values of "charge transfer to carbon/charge transfer to Pt" ≥1, the element would prefer to exchange its electrons with carbon rather than with Pt.

The effects of the various alloying elements incorporated in the Pt/carbon interface have been studied in the literature [4-6], and were reported to mitigate the agglomeration of Pt on the carbon support under accelerated durability tests (ADT) or fuel cell operating conditions. The present study revealed that Co, Ni and V would form strong covalent bonds with carbon, and the modification of carbon surface with these metals will lead to the strengthening of the Pt/carbon bond, alleviating the agglomeration of Pt atoms on carbon support materials and increasing the durability of PEMFC.

## CONCLUSIONS

The effects that adding metallic adatoms had on the strength of the Pt/graphene interface were investigated using first principles calculations. The main conclusions are as follows:

1.  The carbon-metal bond is weaker than the Pt-metal bond, so that a Pt/graphene interface with adatom is expected to break at the carbon-metal interface.
2.  Early transition metals formed strong bonds with both Pt and graphene surfaces when adsorbed, but at the Pt/graphene interface, these metals chose to make strong bonds with Pt. Their bonds with carbon were weaker.
3.  Among the elements considered; Co, Ni and V were found to act as the most promising adatoms for strengthening the Pt/graphene interface, due to the formation

of strong covalent bonds with carbon and donating its charge more equally to both side as a bridge. Hence, they may be used in PEMFC electrodes where enhancement of the Pt to carbon support is needed for better catalyst retention.
