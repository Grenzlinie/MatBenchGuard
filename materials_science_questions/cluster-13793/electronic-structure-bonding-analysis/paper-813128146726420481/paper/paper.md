# Alloying effects on the electronic structure of $ZrMn_2$ intermetallic hydride

T. Matsumura, H. Yukawa, M. Morinaga*

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, School of Engineering, Nagoya University, Furo-cho, Chikusa-ku, Nagoya, Aichi, 464-8603, Japan

Received 1 November 1997; received in revised form 24 November 1997

## Abstract

The electronic structures of $ZrMn_2$ intermetallic hydride containing a variety of alloying elements are investigated by the DV-X$\alpha$ cluster method. It is found that hydrogen atom makes a strong chemical bond with Mn atoms rather than Zr atoms in pure $ZrMn_2$ hydride. The substitution of alloying elements for Mn atoms scarcely modifies the total bond strength between hydrogen and metal atoms, despite the fact that hydrogen absorption and desorption characteristics change significantly with alloying elements. On the contrary, both the Mn-Mn and the Zr-Mn interactions are greatly affected by alloying. It is shown that the ratio of the Zr-Mn bond order to the Mn-Mn bond order in a small tetrahedron containing a hydrogen atom, correlates well with experimental data of the equilibrium hydrogen pressure of alloyed $ZrMn_2$.

Keywords: Intermetallic compounds; Hydrides; $ZrMn_2$; Electronic structures

## 1. Introduction

$ZrMn_2$ is the Laves phase with the C14-type structure, and it is one of the most important hydrogen storage compounds [1]. It may be utilized as the material for clean second batteries and heat pumps, because this compound absorbs hydrogen at moderate temperatures and pressures, and forms a hydride, $ZrMn_2H_{3.46}$, in which the hydrogen content is as high as 1.7 mass% [2]. It also possesses a high potential for absorbing the hydrogen without any large hysteresis, while showing a faster hydriding rate, compared to other compounds. However, the desorption pressure of this hydride is inconveniently low at room temperature. In order to make use of this compound for practical applications at ambient temperatures, it is firstly necessary to improve its absorption and desorption characteristics. Aiming at this end, for example, the addition of ternary elements into $ZrMn_2$ has been investigated experimentally, and it has been shown that the stability of the hydride is modified largely by alloying [3,4]. However, despite such great efforts, as yet alloying effects have not been elucidated on the hydriding properties of ternary compounds.

There are some empirical rules to account for the phase stability of hydrides formed in various compounds. For example, there is a good correlation between the unit cell volume of compounds (or the interstitial hole size) and the equilibrium plateau pressure of hydrogen [5,6]. Namely, it is known that the larger the unit cell volume (or the interstitial size) is, the more stable the hydride is. However, there are still some exceptional cases which violate this rule. For example, $LaPt_5$ hydride with the $CaCu_5$-type crystal structure is very unstable, despite its large unit cell volume. Also, there is even an opposite correlation in the ternary $ZrMn_{2-x}Ni_x$ system [7]. Thus, an empirical approach is sometimes invalid, and hence a more fundamental approach is needed for the further understanding of the phase stability of hydrides.

On the other hand, theoretical calculations on the electronic structure of $ZrMn_2$ have been performed by several investigators [8,9]. However, there have been few investigations on the $ZrMn_2$ hydrides. To the best of our knowledge, any clear correlation has not been obtained between the electronic structures and the hydriding properties of pure and alloyed $ZrMn_2$. The lack of such investigations is probably due to the complexity of the crystal structure of the hydrides and also to the very poor understanding of the positions of hydrogen atoms in them. Recently, the crystal structure of $ZrMn_2H_3$ has been studied using a neutron diffraction technique [10]. $ZrMn_2H_3$ has a hexagonal $MgZn_2$-type (C14) structure with the $P6_3/mmc$ space group and there is a preferential occupancy of hydrogen atoms on the 12 k site in the

## 2. DV-Xα cluster method and cluster model

### 2.1. DV-Xα cluster method

The DV-Xα cluster method [15,16] is a molecular orbital calculation method, assuming the Hartree-Fock- Slater (HFS) approximation. In this method, the exchangecorrelation between electrons, $V_{xc}$,is given by the following Slater's Xα potential,

$$
V_{\mathrm{xc}}=-3 \alpha\left[\frac{3}{8 \pi} \rho(r)\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}, \tag{1}
$$

where $\rho(r)$ is the electron density at the position $r$, the parameter $\alpha$ is fixed at 0.7, an empirically appropriate value [17], and the self-consistent charge approximation is used in the calculation. The matrix elements of the Hamiltonian and the overlap integrals are calculated by a random sampling method. The molecular orbitals are constructed by a linear combination of numerically generated atomic orbitals (LCAO). The atomic orbitals used in the present calculation are 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s and 4p for Mn and other 3d transition metals (V, Fe, Co, Ni), 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f, 5s and 5p for Zr, and 1s for hydrogen atom.

### 2.2. Cluster model

The cluster model used is shown in Fig. 1. This is a hydrogenated cluster model constructed on the basis of the crystal structure of ZrMn₂H₃ [10]. As explained earlier, hydrogen atoms occupy preferably the 12 k sites in the crystal lattice with the P6₃/mmc space group. This site corresponds to the central site of the tetrahedron, the corners of which are occupied by two Zr atoms (indicated by A and B in the figure) and two Mn atoms (indicated by C and D (or C' and D') in the figure). As shown in Fig. 1, two hydrogen atoms are placed at each center of two tetrahedra (A B C D, A B C' D') in this cluster model. The interatomic distances between atoms in the cluster are listed in Table 1. They are obtained from the lattice parameters of ZrMn₂H₃ ($a$=0.5391 nm, $c$=0.8748 nm) [10]. Both the Mn and the Mn(4) atoms in a tetrahedron are the first-nearest-neighbor atoms from the hydrogen atom at the center, and two Zr(1) atoms are the second-nearest-neighbor atoms. The H-H distance is about 0.245 nm in this cluster model.

In order to examine alloying effects on the electronic structures, various alloying elements, M, (M=V, Fe, Co, Ni) are substituted for two Mn atoms as indicated by D and D' in the cluster model. Therefore, the cluster model is expressed as Zr₄Mn₈M₂H₂, and this has a $C_{i}$ symmetry. For comparison, another calculation is performed by using the lattice parameters of pure ZrMn₂ compound ($a$= 0.5035 nm, $c$=0.8276 nm) instead of those of ZrMn₂H₃. In this case the interatomic distances are listed in Table 2. The hydrogenation induces lattice expansion, for example, the Mn-Mn(4) interatomic distance increases from 0.256 nm to 0.274 nm, and the Mn-Zr(1) interatomic distance also increases from 0.294 nm to 0.314 nm. This is about 7% expansion in the length, and about 21% expansion in the volume.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Energy level structure, electron density of states, and energy distribution of overlapping populations for pure ZrMn₂ hydride

For pure ZrMn₂ hydride, the calculated results are shown in Fig. 2a for the energy level structure, Fig. 2b for the electron density of states, and Fig. 2c for the energy distribution of the overlapping populations. In these fig- ures, the Fermi energy level, $E_{f}$, is set at zero, and used as a reference of the energy. As is evident from Fig. 2b, the $E_{f}$ lies near the peak of the Mn 3d band, whereas the Mn 4s and 4p components are spread over a wide energy range. The dominant states of the Zr 4d component appear mainly above the $E_{f}$. Also, as shown in Fig. 2a, two hydrogen-bonding levels, $82a_{u}$ and $82a_{g}$, appear at the energy of about $-7.2$ eV and $-7.8$ eV below the $E_{f}$. The molecular orbital of the $82a_{u}$ level consists of 57.3% H 1s, 13.0% Mn 4s, 5.2% Mn 4p, 5.0% Mn 3d and 12.7% Zr 4d components, whereas that of the $82a_{g}$ level consists of 63.7% H 1s, 13.7% Mn 4s, 5.0% Mn 4p, 6.7% Mn 3d and 3.5% Zr 4d components.

In Fig. 2c, if the overlap population is positive $(+)$ a bonding-type interaction is operating between atoms, whereas if it is negative $(-)$ an anti-bonding-type inter- action is dominant between atoms. It is apparent that in both the $82a_{g}$ and $82a_{u}$ levels bonding-type interactions are operating between the Mn 3d and the H 1s electrons, and between the Mn 4s,4p and the H 1s electrons. The former Mn 3d–H 1s interaction is the bonding-type in all the energy ranges up to the Ef. However, the latter Mn 4s,4p–H 1s interaction is the anti-bonding-type in the energy range of $-4$ eV to the $E_{f}$. This means that the Mn 3d–H 1s interaction is dominant in this hydride.

### 3.3. Bond order between atoms in pure and alloyed ZrMn₂ hydrides

As explained earlier, the bond order is the overlap population of electrons between atoms, and hence this is a measure of the strength of the covalent bond between atoms. The change in the bond orders between atoms with alloying elements are shown in Fig. 4a for the metal-hydrogen bond and in Fig. 4b for the metal-metal bond. Every bond order shown in these figures is the value per atomic bond in the cluster. In the abscissa, M=Mn means pure ZrMn₂ hydride.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the Mn(1)-H, the Mn(2)-H, the Mn(3)-H and the Zr(2)-H bond orders are always negative or nearly nil, because of the long interatomic distances between them as shown in Table 1. On the other hand, both the Mn(4)-H and the M-H bond orders are large and positive, indicating that there is an attractive and strong interaction operating between them. Also, the Zr(1)-H bond order is positive, but the value is smaller than the Mn(4)-H or the M-H bond orders. From these results, it is evident that the hydrogen atom makes a stronger chemical bond with Mn atoms (or M atoms on the Mn atom sites) rather than Zr atoms, regardless of alloying elements. This is consistent with the result of the electron density distribution shown in Fig. 3. Also, the 'total' shown in Fig. 4a is the sum of these metal-hydrogen bond orders. This total bond order is nearly constant without showing any large dependence on the alloying elements, M.

On the contrary, the bond orders between the metal-metal pairs depend strongly on the alloying elements. In particular, as shown in Fig. 4b there are large changes in the bond orders of Mn(1)-Mn(4), Mn(1)-M, M-Mn(4) and Zr(1)-M. However, the average value of the Mn(1)-Mn(4) and Mn(1)-M bond orders,which is shown by the dotted line in the figure, scarcely changes with alloying elements. On the other hand, it is not the case for the M-Mn(4) and the Zr(1)-M bond orders, since both of them decrease with alloying elements. These bond orders are concerned with the chemical bond strengths between metal atoms on a tetrahedron (see Fig. 1). Thus, the alloying induces large changes in these metal-metal interactions on the tetrahedron containing a hydrogen atom.

### 3.4. Ionicities

The ionicity of each atom in the cluster is estimated according to the Mulliken population analysis [18]. The results are shown in Fig. 5. The ionicities of Zr atoms are always positive, whereas the ionicities of the hydrogen atom and the Mn atoms are always negative or nearly nil. This is reasonable since Zr is the more electropositive element than H and Mn. Also, the ionicities of alloying elements change considerably with M, following the electronegativity of M. These results indicate that charge transfer takes place mainly from the Zr atom towards the hydrogen and alloying elements except for V. Here, V is the most electropositive element next to Zr among these elements. The ionicity of the hydrogen atom is about -0.27, regardless of alloying elements, M.

### 3.5. Bond order changes with hydrogenation and attendant lattice expansion

As explained earlier, the crystal lattice of ZrMn₂ expands by the hydrogenation. Therefore, it is interesting to know how the chemical bonding between atoms is modified by the hydrogenation. So, further simulations are carried out in the following two cases by using the cluster model shown in Fig. 1, but excluding hydrogen atoms or charging interatomic distances in it.

The first case is the simulation for pure ZrMn₂ compound without containing any hydrogen atoms. The interatomic distances of this compound are listed in Table 2. The second case is the simulation for pure ZrMn₂ hydride with containing hydrogen atoms, but the interatomic distances used are still the same as in pure ZrMn₂ compound. No lattice expansion is taken into account in this simulation. In Fig. 6, these additional simulations are compared with the preceding simulation for pure ZrMn₂ hydride of having the same interatomic distances as in ZrMn₂H₃ (see Table 1). Needless to say, lattice expansion induced by the hydrogenation is counted explicitly in this simulation. A part of the results has already been presented in Fig. 4 (M=Mn). Here, for simplicity, this simulation is called the third case.

In Fig. 6, the first, the second and the third cases are denoted as ZrMn₂, ZrMn₂+H (no expansion) and ZrMn₂+H (expansion), respectively. Also, the Mn atom locating in the M atom site is denoted simply as Mn in the figure, and the other Mn atoms in the cluster are named as Mn(1)~Mn(4), following the atom notation shown in Fig. 1.

When the result of ZrMn₂ is compared with that of ZrMn₂+H(no expansion), it is evident that the Mn-Mn(4) bond order is reduced remarkably by the hydrogenation. As shown in Fig. 1, the hydrogen atom is located at the position neighboring to these Mn and Mn(4) atoms. As a result of hydrogenation the strong Mn-H chemical bond is formed, but instead the Mn-Mn(4) chemical bond is weakened. This weakening of the Mn-Mn bond strength probably makes the hydride unstable. However, this Mn-Mn(4) bond strength is possibly recovered to some extent by allowing the lattice expansion during hydrogenation. In fact, as shown in Fig. 6, the Mn-Mn(4) bond order increases by the lattice expansion of about 7% in the edge length of the tetrahedron shown in Fig. 1. It is noted here that the other bond orders between metal-metal pairs more or less decrease except for the Zr(1)-Mn bond order. A small increase in the Zr(1)-Mn bond order is, however, compensated for the small decrease in the Zr(1)-Mn(4) bond order, and hence the average Zr(1)-Mn bond order in the tetrahedron seems unchanged with the lattice expansion. In this sense, it may be said that only the Mn-Mn(4) bond order in the tetrahedron is enhanced by the lattice expansion in the course of hydrogenation. Such enhancement in the Mn-Mn(4) chemical bond strength probably makes the hydride more stable, and the hydrogen atoms tend to be absorbed readily in the compound. In other words, the easiness of the lattice expansion in the tetrahedral metal framework in which the hydrogen atom is absorbed, may concern the phase stability of the hydride in some ways. Similar results have also been obtained in other systems, LaNi₅ [13] and FeTi [29], as discussed later.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Hydrogen-metal interactions in ZrMn₂ hydride

Most of hydrogen storage compounds such as LaNi₅, ZrMn₂, Mg₂Ni and TiFe consist of both endothermic and exothermic elements in view of the heat of formation of their hydrides. An exothermic element, for example, Zr, has a strong attractive interaction with hydrogen and hence

![](./images/813128146726420481_5.jpg)

easily forms a hydride, $ZrH_2$. The endothermic element (e.g., Mn) may work to reduce such an attractive interaction, so that the hydrogen desorption process could be activated by the presence of it in the compound.

However, this naive understanding is not true in the present case. Concerning pure $ZrMn_2$ hydride, a strong bonding-type interaction is operating between the hydrogen and the Mn atoms, whereas the interaction between the Zr and the hydrogen atoms is relatively weak, as shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. 4a (M=Mn). These results clearly indicate that the hydrogen atoms makes a strong chemical bond with the Mn atom rather than the Zr atom in pure $ZrMn_2$ hydride, despite the larger affinity of Zr atoms for hydrogen than Mn atoms in the binary metal-hydrogen system. This trend has also been observed in other hydrogen storage compounds as $LaNi_5$, $Mg_2Ni$ and TiFe, where the endothermic element, Ni or Fe interacts strongly with hydrogen than the exothermic element, La or Mg or Ti in each compound [13,14,25-29].

### 4.2. Alloying effect on the hydrogen absorption and desorption characteristics of $ZrMn_2$

The alloying effect on the hydriding properties of $ZrMn_2$ has been investigated extensively, because of the fundamental interest as well as the practical importance for the hydrogen storage application [3,4,7]. The measured equilibrium hydrogen pressures at 473 K change with alloying elements, M, as shown in Fig. 7. These experimental results will be interpreted as due to the bond order changes with M. As shown in Fig. 4a, the total bond strength between hydrogen and metal atoms scarcely changes with alloying elements, despite a large variation in the hydrogen pressures as shown in Fig. 7. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5, the ionicity of hydrogen is almost constant, irrespective of alloying elements. Thus, it seems difficult to describe the measured hydrogen pressures in terms of the direct interaction between hydrogen and metal atoms.

On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 4b, the M-Mn(4) bond order changes considerably with the alloying elements, M. This bond order is concerned with the metal-metal pairs existing on a tetrahedron shown in Fig. 8a. As described before, the lattice expansion will occur so as to recover this Mn (or M on the Mn sites)-Mn interaction during hydrogenation.

In other words, Mn and M atoms tend to be displaced in the direction being away from the hydrogen at the center of the tetrahedron. However, as is evident from the atomic arrangements shown in Fig. 8a, such displacements may

measured equilibrium plateau pressure of hydrogen except for Ni.

According to this calculation, the Ni addition into ZrMn₂ makes the hydride unstable. Experimentally, two-step plateaus of hydrogen pressure are observed in the PCT (Pressure-Composition-Temperature) diagram of such a Ni-containing ZrMn₂ [7]. This implies that the hydride which is commonly formed in the other alloyed ZrMn₂, becomes unstable and another hydride appears in it as the hydrogenation proceeds. It is also reported that an empirical rule between the unit cell volume and the hydrogen pressure is valid in the other alloyed ZrMn₂, but invalid only in the Ni-containing ZrMn₂. In addition, for the (Zr₀.₈Ti₀.₂)(Mn₀.₄₋ₓNi₀.₅₊ₓV₀.₅₅)₂.₀ system, it is shown that the substitution of Ni atoms for Mn atoms makes the hydride unstable [30]. These experimental results appear to support the present calculation, but further experiments are still needed to draw a conclusive result on this point.

### 4.3. Comparison in the hydride stability between ZrMn₂ and other compound systems

#### 4.3.1. Comparison with LaNi₅ system

The result on the ZrMn₂ system is similar to the result on the LaNi₅ system [13]. According to the previous calculations [13,26], the most important crystal unit in the LaNi₅ system is an octahedron consisting of four Ni atoms and two La atoms, as shown in Fig. 8b. One hydrogen atom is located at the center of the octahedron. Both the La-Ni and Ni-Ni interactions which are strong enough to hold the framework of this small octahedron, are modified considerably by alloying. On the other hand, there is a relatively small change in the Ni-H bond order with alloying elements [13]. These results may correspond to the present results of the ZrMn₂ system shown in Fig. 4a Fig. 4b.

As might be expected from the discussion on the ZrMn₂ system, the measured hydrogen pressure for alloying LaNi₅₋ₓMₓ system [31-33] increases with increasing La-Ni (or M) bond order and with decreasing Ni (or M)-Ni bond order, when M is substituted for the Ni atom as shown in Fig. 8b. Namely, the Ni (or M)-Ni bond order and the La-Ni (or M) bond order in the LaNi₅ system correspond to the Mn (or M)-Mn bond order and the Zr-Mn (or M) bond order in the ZrMn₂ system, respectively. The lattice expansion occurs so as to recover the Ni-Ni interaction in the LaNi₅ system, as is similar to the Mn-Mn interaction in the ZrMn₂ system. Besides these systems, the TiFe system is treatable in a similar manner [29].

It is stressed here that in all these systems, any substantial changes of the crystal structure do not occur during the hydrogenation except for the onset of lattice expansion and distortion. In other words, the hydride structure is the derivative one of the original compound, and hydrogen atoms occupy a part of the interstitial sites in the crystal lattice of the compound. In such a case the easiness of the lattice expansion during the hydrogenation will be controlled strongly by the nature of chemical bonds between metal atoms in the original compound.

#### 4.3.2. Comparison with Mg₂Ni system

However, such an approach is no longer valid in the Mg₂Ni system, in which a hydride, Mg₂NiH₄, of having a completely different crystal structure from that of Mg₂Ni appears in the course of hydrogenation above 518 K. In this sense, this system is in contrast to the above systems. According to our previous calculations [14,29], the most important crystal unit in the Mg₂Ni system is a small cube containing Ni (or M) and Mg atoms, as shown in Fig. 8c. The hydrogen atoms occupy randomly a two third of the octahedral positions around an Ni atom [34]. When an alloying element, M, is substituted for the Ni atom at the body center site, the calculated M-H bond order is significantly large, and there is an attractive and strong interaction operating between the alloying element and the hydrogen atom. Furthermore, the magnitude of this M-H bond order changes largely with M. In response to this change, the M-Mg bond order varies in a approximately reverse way. Also, because of weak Ni-Ni and Mg-Mg interactions, the structural stability of alloyed Mg₂Ni hydride will be attributable to the existence of strong M-Mg and M-H interactions in it. In fact, the measured enthalpy of hydride formation in Mg₂Ni₀.₇₅M₀.₂₅, changes in a approximately similar way as does the M-Mg bond order or the M-H bond order [14]. It is noted here that the M-H chemical bond plays a more important role in the hydride stability in this Mg₂Ni system, compared to the LaNi₅ and the ZrMn₂ systems. The importance further increases in the bcc V system, in which the M-H interaction is responsible mainly for the stability of VH₂ hydride [28,35]. In this case, the VH₂ has a CaF₂-type structure, completely different from the bcc structure of V.

Thus, the hydrogen absorption or desorption characteristics are very different among the compounds. But they are well understood in terms of the nature of the chemical bond between atoms in a small polyhedron in which the absorbed hydrogen atom is located.

## 5. Conclusion

The alloying effects on the electronic structures of the ZrMn₂ hydride are investigated by the DV-Xα cluster method in order to understand the hydrogen absorption and desorption characteristics of alloyed ZrMn₂.

The hydrogen atom is found to make a strong chemical bond with the Mn atoms rather than the Zr atoms in pure ZrMn₂ hydride. Alloying elements such as V, Fe, Co and Ni scarcely influence the bond strengths between hydrogen and metal atoms, but instead greatly modify the bond strengths between Mn atoms or between Mn and Zr atoms,

all of which neighbor on the hydrogen atom. The Zr-Mn and the Mn-Mn interactions are considered to be mainly responsible for the hydriding properties of this alloyed compound. In fact, the ratio of the Zr-Mn bond orders to the Mn-Mn bond orders correlates well with the measured equilibrium plateau pressure of hydrogen. This result quite resembles the result of $LaNi_5$ and TiFe compounds.
