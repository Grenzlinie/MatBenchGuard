# Effect of hydrogen-doping on bonding properties of $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$

## ABSTRACT
Using the first principles method based on the density functional theory, we investigated the effect of hydrogen-doping on bonding properties of $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$. The formation energies of hydrogen interstitials in three possible positions were calculated. The results show that hydrogen favors residing near the (0 0 1) Si plane. In these positions, hydrogen is hybridized most with 1s states of lattice atoms (Si and C), instead of Ti. The presence of hydrogen does not substantially influence the bonding nature of $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$; chemical bonding is characterized by the hybridizations of Ti d–Si p and Ti d–C p states, and yields high strength. This is contrary to hydrogen-doping in transition metals, where the electron of hydrogen fills in the $d$ bands of the metals and, as a consequence, decreases the cohesive strength of the lattice.

### 1. Introduction
$\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ belongs to MAX phases ($\text{M}_{n+1}\text{AX}_n$, where $n=1,2$, or 3, M is an early transition metal, A is an A-group element, and X is either C or N). The phase was first synthesized in powder form in the 1960s by Jeitschko and Nowotny [1]. However, it did not attract people's attention until Barsoum and El-Raghy reported that high purity polycrystalline bulk samples of $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ can be fabricated by a hot-pressing route [2]. Recently, a lot of works [3–6] being done is due to the factor that $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ and other materials of MAX phases [7,8] have a number of exceptional properties that combine metallic and ceramic characteristics: it is elastically rigid, oxidation resistant, and maintains its strength to high temperatures, just like ceramics; on the other hand, it is a good electric and thermal conductor, readily machinable, relatively soft, and highly thermal-shock resistant, just like metals [9,10]. At higher temperature, they can undergo a brittle-to-plastic transition [11]. Given these unique properties, it can be considered for many applications, one of which is to be a promising candidate of high temperature structural materials [12–14]. Materials that can act as engine parts for structural applications should possess properties not only with excellent mechanics, but also with resistance to the chemical erosion of environments. A lot of studies reported that $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ is exceptionally oxidation resistant [13,15]. On the other hand, during manufacture and/or service, $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ will inevitably be subjected to invasion of other chemical elements, e.g. in H-rich environments. This is well known that the behavior of hydrogen plays a very important role in mechanical properties of metals. It is because dissolved hydrogen atoms cause the degradation of mechanical properties by decreasing the ductility of metals, which thus limits the applications of metals in some especial conditions. At present, two popular theories can explain hydrogen embrittlement better, which are the lattice decohesion model [16,17] and the hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity model [18,19]. The former model mainly illustrates that the dissolved hydrogen reduces the cohesive strength of the lattice, i.e., interatomic bonds, and thereby promotes decohesion of materials, especially for transition metals. Despite many years of research, the problem of hydrogen-induced embrittlement is not solved yet. We then pose a question: can $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ resist hydrogen-induced embrittlement, or say, does hydrogen degrade the mechanical properties of $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$, just like metals since it also has metallic properties? To our knowledge, little works have been done on the behavior of hydrogen atoms. Most of the research works concerned with $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ were focused on its synthesis and physical properties [20–23]. *Ab initio* calculations [21,24–26] showed that the bonding character of these kinds of MAX phases exhibits a mixture of covalent–ionic and metallic, which may be responsible for the unusual mechanical properties of the MAX phases. Therefore, there is a strong need to evaluate the effect of hydrogen on $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ in order to extend its applications.

In this work, we studied the electronic structure and bonding properties of $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$ in the presence of hydrogen, using the first principles calculations. We analyzed the effect of hydrogen on chemical bonding. The purpose of this paper is to probe whether hydrogen-doping also induces the lattice decohesion in $\text{Ti}_3\text{SiC}_2$, as it does in transition metals, from the chemical bonding perspective.

## 2. Calculation method

The first principles calculations used here were based on the density functional theory (DFT). We used the CASTEP code [27] to carry out electronic state calculations and structure optimization. The total energies were calculated using the plane-wave pseudopotential method [27]. Interactions of electrons with ion cores were represented by the norm-conserving pseudopotential for Ti, Si, C, and H atoms [28]. The electronic exchange-correlation energy was treated according to the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). The cutoff energy of plane-wave basis for wave function was 670 eV. Supercell calculation (2 × 1 × 1) consisting of 24 atoms was performed. The Brillouin zone (BZ) was sampled with 5 × 9 × 2 k-points by the Monkhorst Pack scheme [29]. The total energy converged to within 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mRy/unit cell during the self-consistency cycle.

## 3. Results and discussion

Ti₃SiC₂ has a hexagonal structure with space group P63/mmc (Fig. 1). For this structure, Ti occupies 2a and 4f, Si 2b, and C 4f Wyckoff positions. The structure can be regarded as two edgeshared Ti₆C octahedron layers, which are linked together by a two-dimensional closed packed Si layer. Noting that Ti atoms have different coordination environments, we denote Ti atoms sitting at 2a sites as Ti(1), and Ti in 4f as Ti(2) for convenient discussion. The former is bonded only to heterogeneous C atoms, and the latter to both C and Si on its two sides. After optimizing the system, we obtained lattice constants of a ≈ 0.3082 nm and c ≈ 1.7605 nm, which are in reasonable agreement with the results [30].

Referring to the structure of Ti₃SiC₂, there are three possible interstitial positions with large free volumes (Fig. 1): a tetrahedral interstitial (I-Ti) surrounded by three Ti(1) and one Ti(2) atoms, a hexahedral interstitial (I-SiTi) surrounded by three Si and two Ti(2) atoms, and a tetrahedral interstitial (I-SiC) surrounded by three Si and one C atoms. The formation energies of H-interstitial were obtained by optimizing both the lattice parameters and the coordinates of atoms in the supercell after putting a hydrogen atom in the above interstitial positions. The formation energy is calculated as follows:

$$
E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{f}}=E\left(\mathrm{Ti}_{3} \mathrm{SiC}_{2}+\mathrm{H}\right)-E\left(\mathrm{Ti}_{3} \mathrm{SiC}_{2}\right)-E(\mathrm{H})
\tag{1}
$$

where $E(\mathrm{Ti}_{3} \mathrm{SiC}_{2}+\mathrm{H})$ refers to the energy of bulk Ti₃SiC₂ with a hydrogen interstitial, $E(\mathrm{Ti}_{3} \mathrm{SiC}_{2})$ is the energy of perfect Ti₃SiC₂ crystal, and $E(\mathrm{H})$ is the energy of an isolated hydrogen atom. Table 1 shows the results of the formation energy for hydrogen. It can be clearly seen that hydrogen is most stable in I-SiC, followed by I-SiTi, and then I-Ti, where energy discrepancy between I-SiTi and I-SiC is very small. The volume change of the system caused by hydrogen is in the order of I-SiC (0.55%), I-SiTi (0.90%), and I-Ti (1.45%). Generally, based on classical concepts of elastic strain, large deformation corresponding to large lattice relaxation increases the formation energy of interstitial atoms. Therefore, it can be understood in our present results listed in Table 1. It indicates that elastic interaction may predominate the ordering of the formation energy of H interstitials. Fig. 2 schematically shows the variations of the interstitial configurations, where hydrogen forces its neighboring atoms to move outward. For I-SiC and I-SiTi, hydrogen still remains at the center of the free spacing. Different from the former two cases, I-Ti is not a stable position. Fig. 2c shows that hydrogen of I-Ti passes through the basal plane composed of Ti(1) atoms, and forms a covalence bond with a C atom just below the Ti(1) plane. The bond length of C–H is 1.15 Å, which almost equals that of C–H in methane.

In order to understand the underlying mechanism of hydrogen stability, electronic analysis was performed for the different interstitial positions. Fig. 3 depicts the partial density of states (PDOS) for Ti₃SiC₂ with and without hydrogendoping. In the defect-free Ti₃SiC₂, PDOS in Fig. 3a shows that the valence bonds of the lowest energy are formed mainly by carbon 2s states with a small mixture of Ti 4s and 3d states and the hybridization of Si sp states. The higher valence bonds come from Si 3s bonds. From –6 to –1 eV, the valence bonds are contributed by the hybridization of Ti(1, 2) 3d–C 2p states, as well as the hydridization of Ti(2) 3d–Si 3p states. When hydrogen is doped, Fig. 3b indicates that hydrogen atoms of both I-SiC and I-SiTi form strong covalent bonds of H–Si, because of hybridization of H 1s and Si 3s states. For I-SiC,

Besides PDOS analysis, there are other methods, with all of which we can comprehensively understand the bonding behaviors [31]. Here, we calculated the charge density distribution with H-doping for each situation, as seen in Fig. 4 which also illustrates the case having no impurities of H for comparison. Fig. 4a clearly shows a character of chain structure (Ti(2)-C-Ti(1)-C-Ti(2)) in Ti₃SiC₂. Every two chains are linked by a Si atom. After doping, the charge density distribution of the chain still preserves its original configuration for the cases of H in I-SiTi and in I-SiC, as shown in Fig. 4b and d although H forms a covalent bond with Si for I-SiTi, and with Si and C for I-SiC. For H in I-Ti, Fig. 4c exhibits the strong covalent bond between C and H. These are consistent with the results of PDOS.

It is worth noting that hydrogen is hybridized primarily with s states of lattice atoms in Ti₃SiC₂. Extant studies [16,17] have showed that when H is doped in transition metals, the electrons of hydrogen atoms fill in the d bands of the transition metals, which decreases the cohesive strength of the cubic cleavage plane. This means that the effect of hydrogen on the electronic structure of Ti₃SiC₂ is different from that on transition metals. Hydrogen is almost not hybridized with d states of transition element Ti in Ti₃SiC₂. According to [32], the d states of Ti play an important role in chemical bonding of Ti₃SiC₂. That is to say, hydrogen cannot substantially change the bonding characteristics of Ti₃SiC₂. Although hydrogen of I-Ti causes the hybridization of C s-p states and forms a strong bond with carbon, I-Ti is not the energetically favorable interstitial position for hydrogen. Therefore, it seems that the feature of bonding of Ti₃SiC₂ may not be destroyed by hydrogen-doping. In other words, Ti(2)-C-Ti(1)-C-Ti(2)-Si chains [32], which contribute the high strength and modulus, still retain their strong covalent bonding due to the hybridization of Ti(1,2) 3d-C 2p. Also, the Si-Ti bonding derived from hybridizing Si 2p and Ti (2) 3d states has little influences from hydrogen. Generally, bond hybridizations of Ti d-C p and Ti d-Si p stabilize the structure of MAX phase [23,33-34].

Comparing the formation energies of hydrogen interstitials in different positions, hydrogen energetically favors going to the sites I-SiC and I-SiTi. As a consequence, hydrogen is easy to be segregated near the basal (0 0 1) Si plane. If more H atoms accumulate near the (0 0 1) Si plane, they would form bubbles. Considering the relatively weak Si-Ti bonding on the Si plane [21], H bubble may induce the system cleavage or delamination

## 4. Conclusion

We investigated the bonding properties of Ti₃SiC₂ in the presence of hydrogen using the first principle method based on DFT. It was found that hydrogen generally hybridizes just with s states of Si and sp hybridized states of C. The feature of chemical bonding in Ti₃SiC₂ is not changed substantially by hydrogen-doping. This is different from the behavior of hydrogen-doping in transition metals, where hybridization occurs between H s and metallic atom d states, which consequently reduces the cohesive strength of the lattice, i.e. interatomic bonds in metals. The results imply that Ti₃SiC₂ may possess the properties to some extent of resisting the hydrogen embrittlement originating from the lattice decohesion model.
