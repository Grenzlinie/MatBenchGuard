# Voxel-based Lattice Model for Elastic Homogenization and Fracture of Bi-continuous and Particulate Composites

## Problem background
Classical homogenization methods often overlook the complex three-dimensional arrangement of phases in composite materials. Understanding how the 3D microstructure—whether bi-continuous or particulate—determines the macroscopic elastic stiffness and the propensity for interfacial debonding is critical for predictive materials design. This task implements a non‑local 3D voxel‑based lattice model to compute the effective elastic constants (bulk and shear moduli) of two‑phase composites and to analyze their fracture behavior under tensile loading. Your objective is to quantify how the inclusion volume fraction, interface compliance, and spatial arrangement of the reinforcing phase influence the elastic response and the crack‑surface statistics.

## Approach
Build a computational framework around a voxel‑based lattice model with simple‑cubic packing. Each voxel interacts with its first and second nearest neighbors through axial springs. The spring stiffnesses are derived from the local phase’s Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio using an energy‑equivalency condition: the potential energy of a voxel cluster under homogeneous deformation is equated to that of the corresponding continuum element. Two interface assignment rules are considered—one where the interface springs take the properties of the inclusion, and one where they take the properties of the matrix.

Elastic homogenization is performed by applying prescribed strain states (uniform tri‑axial extension and three rotated bi‑axial‑extension/uni‑axial‑compression load cases) to a representative volume element, computing the total strain energy with an implicit linear‑elastic solver, and then extracting the effective bulk modulus K and shear modulus G from the energy relations. For fracture, quasi‑static uniaxial tension is simulated with an explicit dynamic solver and a critical‑elongation failure criterion for the springs; lateral periodic boundary conditions are used. The digitised microstructures—bi‑continuous composites generated by stochastic reconstruction and particulate composites generated by hard‑particle Monte Carlo—are created directly from the geometric rules described in the method, so no external data files are required.

## Reproduction target
1. Generate cubic 128×128×128 voxel representative volume elements for bi‑continuous composites and particulate composites at inclusion volume fractions 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8. Additionally, create three distinct particulate microstructures at 50 % volume fraction that differ in their spatial distribution (e.g., random, clustered, dispersed).

2. For all volume‑fraction/microstructure/interface combinations, run the elastic homogenization procedure and report the effective bulk modulus K (GPa) and shear modulus G (GPa).

3. For the three 50 % particulate microstructures, run quasi‑static tensile fracture simulations and compute: (a) the percentage of crack‑surface voxels lying in the inclusion, in the matrix, and in the whole domain; (b) the reaction force on the top face as a function of the applied strain.

The reproduced trends should reflect the influence of the inclusion content, interface compliance, and spatial arrangement, without assuming a specific absolute value.

## Workflow steps

### Step 1: Generate 3D voxel microstructures (RVE)
- Role: process
- Action: Construct cubic 128x128x128 voxel representative volume elements for bi-continuous composites (stochastic reconstruction) and particulate composites (hard-particle Monte Carlo) at inclusion volume fractions 0.2, 0.5, 0.8. Also generate three distinct 50% volume fraction particulate microstructures with different spatial distributions (e.g., random, clustered, dispersed).
- Evidence: `/app/outputs/voxel_phase_arrays.npy`

### Step 2: Compute microstructural descriptors
- Role: process
- Action: For the three 50% volume fraction particulate microstructures, compute the two-point correlation function S2(r) and the lineal-path function L(r) for the inclusion phase. Save the curves as evidence.
- Evidence: `/app/outputs/descriptors_s2_l.csv`

### Step 3: Assign voxel-level spring parameters
- Role: process
- Action: For every voxel in each RVE, use the phase-specific material constants (Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν) with the energy-equivalency formulas to compute pairwise stiffnesses α1, α2 and the nonlocal parameter t. Set interface spring properties according to the two interface assignment rules (interface type 1: inclusion-like; type 2: matrix-like).
- Evidence: none

### Step 4: Run elastic homogenization simulations
- Role: process
- Action: For each combination of composite type, volume fraction, and interface type: (i) apply uniform tri-axial extension and compute total strain energy U(1); (ii) apply three rotated bi-axial extension with uni-axial compression states and compute the average total strain energy U(2). Use an implicit linear-elastic solver suitable for the non-local lattice model.
- Evidence: `/app/outputs/strain_energies.csv`

### Step 5: Compute effective elastic constants
- Role: scored (load-bearing)
- Action: From the stored strain energies U(1) and U(2), for each case compute the effective bulk modulus K and shear modulus G using the energy-based homogenization formulas. Record the results in a CSV file.
- Output file: `/app/outputs/effective_moduli.csv`
- Format: csv
- Contract: CSV with columns: system (string, one of 'bi-continuous' or 'particulate'), volume_fraction (float), interface_type (integer, 1 or 2), K (float, GPa), G (float, GPa).
- Scoring: scored by hidden verifier

### Step 6: Simulate quasi-static fracture under uniaxial tension
- Role: process
- Action: For each of the three 50% volume fraction particulate microstructures, apply uniaxial tension with lateral periodic boundary conditions using an explicit dynamic solver. Apply a critical-elongation failure criterion (0.1% strain for first-neighbor springs, 0.1%/√2 for second-neighbor springs). Record the final crack surface voxel map and the reaction force on the top face vs. applied strain.
- Evidence: none

### Step 7: Analyze fracture statistics – crack surface ratios
- Role: scored (load-bearing)
- Action: From the fracture simulation outputs, compute the ratios (percentage) of voxels on the crack surface that belong to the inclusion phase, the matrix phase, and the total domain. Save the results to a CSV file.
- Output file: `/app/outputs/fracture_crack_surface.csv`
- Format: csv
- Contract: CSV with columns: microstructure_id (integer 1,2,3), inclusion_ratio (float, %), matrix_ratio (float, %), total_ratio (float, %).
- Scoring: scored by hidden verifier

### Step 8: Analyze fracture statistics – force-strain curves
- Role: scored
- Action: From the fracture simulation outputs, compute the reaction force (N) on the top face as a function of applied strain for each of the three microstructures. Save the force-strain data to a CSV file.
- Output file: `/app/outputs/fracture_force_strain.csv`
- Format: csv
- Contract: CSV with columns: microstructure_id (integer 1,2,3), strain (float, dimensionless), force (float, N).
- Scoring: scored by hidden verifier

## How you are scored
A hidden verifier examines each of your output files independently and combines their scores into a final reward. For the elastic moduli, the verifier compares your reported K and G against reference values (accounting for numerical scatter that arises from different implementations) and checks that the interface‑type assignment influences the moduli in a physically plausible way. For the fracture artifacts, the verifier inspects the crack‑surface ratios and the force–strain curves to confirm that the three microstructures are clearly differentiated and that certain quantitative trends (e.g., monotonic ordering of the total crack‑surface ratio and the peak reaction force) are satisfied. The weight of each scored file reflects its importance in the overall reproduction.
