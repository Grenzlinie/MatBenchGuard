A theoretical model is developed to predict the Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE) and thermal mismatch stress of composites with staggered hard platelets in parallel alignment in a soft matrix. The theoretical predictions agree well with the Finite Element Method (FEM) simulations. The results show that different from sandwich composites, the effective CTEs of staggered composite can be tailored by adjusting the aspect ratio of reinforced platelets even with the same volume fraction, which makes the staggered composites more thermal-elastically compatible with the neighboring structures or materials. Moreover, the thermal mismatch stress in the two phases of staggered composite can be reduced through designing microstructure geometry parameters. The staggered composites therefore may have potential application in thermal protections.

Keywords: Staggered composite; coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE); thermal mismatch stress.

## 1. Introduction

Ultra-High-Temperature Ceramics (UHTC) mainly include some borides and carbide which have high melting-points. These ceramics possess excellent high-temperature properties such as strength, stiffness, hardness, which makes them the most potential materials in high-temperature applications [Bronson *et al.*, 1991; Monterverde *et al.*, 2008; Yang *et al.*, 2008].

Current researches mainly focus on enhancing the properties of UHTC such as oxidation resistance, high-temperature strength, high-temperature toughness, and thermal shock resistance. Some works indicate that by designing the microstructure of materials, the mechanic performance of UHTC can be improved [Monteverde

## 2. Theoretical Model

### 2.1. Sandwich composites

In order to investigate the thermal elastics properties of the staggered composites, we firstly investigate sandwich composite, which is a simpler case. Some results of sandwich composites are the extreme cases of the staggered composites.

We follow the approach of continuum mechanics to study the thermal elastic properties of sandwich composite shown in Fig. 1(b). The stress and strain in each phase are averaged in our model to simplify derivation, and the following thermal-elastic constitutive equations are used.

Constitutive equations:
$$
\varepsilon_{x}^{H}=\frac{1}{E_{H}}\left(\sigma_{x}^{H}-\nu_{H} \sigma_{y}^{H}-\nu_{H} \sigma_{z}^{H}\right)+\alpha_{H} \Delta T \tag{1}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{y}^{H}=\frac{1}{E_{H}}\left(\sigma_{y}^{H}-\nu_{H} \sigma_{x}^{H}-\nu_{H} \sigma_{z}^{H}\right)+\alpha_{H} \Delta T \tag{2}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{z}^{H}=\frac{1}{E_{H}}\left(\sigma_{z}^{H}-\nu_{H} \sigma_{x}^{H}-\nu_{H} \sigma_{y}^{H}\right)+\alpha_{H} \Delta T \tag{3}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{x}^{S}=\frac{1}{E_{S}}\left(\sigma_{x}^{S}-\nu_{S} \sigma_{y}^{S}-\nu_{S} \sigma_{z}^{S}\right)+\alpha_{S} \Delta T \tag{4}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{y}^{S}=\frac{1}{E_{S}}\left(\sigma_{y}^{S}-\nu_{S} \sigma_{x}^{S}-\nu_{S} \sigma_{z}^{S}\right)+\alpha_{S} \Delta T \tag{5}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{z}^{S}=\frac{1}{E_{S}}\left(\sigma_{z}^{S}-\nu_{S} \sigma_{x}^{S}-\nu_{S} \sigma_{y}^{S}\right)+\alpha_{S} \Delta T \tag{6}
$$

where $\sigma$ and $\varepsilon$ are the stress and strain, $E$ and $\nu$ are the Young's modulus and Poisson ratio, $\alpha$ is the CTE, $\Delta T$ is the temperature change. The superscripts or subscripts "H" and "S" represent hard material and soft material, respectively.

In this paper, only the thermal-induced strain and stress of composites are studied, and no external load is applied. Different from the Reuss model, we do not assume that the stresses in each phase along $x$-direction or $y$-direction are zero; and we just require the average stresses of the composite in these directions to be zero. The equilibrium equations are listed as follow.

Equilibrium equations:
$$
h_{H} \sigma_{x}^{H}+h_{S} \sigma_{x}^{S}=0 \tag{7}
$$

$$
h_{H} \sigma_{y}^{H}+h_{S} \sigma_{y}^{S}=0 \tag{8}
$$

$$
\sigma_{z}^{H}=0 \tag{9}
$$

$$
\sigma_{z}^{S}=0 \tag{10}
$$

where $h_{H}$, $h_{S}$ are the thickness of the hard material and soft material, respectively.

It is assumed that the hard material and the soft material are glued firmly. Therefore, the strains of the two phases are the same in the $x$- and $y$-directions.

The kinetics equations are listed as follow.

Kinetics equation:

$$
\varepsilon_{y}^{H}=\varepsilon_{y}^{S} \tag{11}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{x}^{H}=\varepsilon_{x}^{S} \tag{12}
$$

There are totally twelve equations listed above, and the number of the unknown quantities such as stresses, strains are also twelve. By solving equations (1)-(12), we can obtain the stress and strain in the two phases.

$$
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{x}=\varepsilon_{x}^{H}=\varepsilon_{x}^{S}=\frac{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right) \alpha_{H}+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right) \alpha_{S}}{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)} \Delta T \tag{13}
$$

$$
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{y}=\varepsilon_{y}^{H}=\varepsilon_{y}^{S}=\frac{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right) \alpha_{H}+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right) \alpha_{S}}{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)} \Delta T \tag{14}
$$

$$
\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{S}=\tilde{\sigma}_{y}^{S}=-\frac{E_{H} E_{S} h_{H}}{E_{H} h_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+E_{S} h_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)}\left(\alpha_{S}-\alpha_{H}\right) \Delta T \tag{15}
$$

$$
\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{H}=\tilde{\sigma}_{y}^{H}=\frac{E_{H} E_{S} h_{S}}{E_{H} h_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+E_{S} h_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)}\left(\alpha_{S}-\alpha_{H}\right) \Delta T \tag{16}
$$

The symbol "~" here represents the quantities of sandwich composites, which will be used in the formulae for the staggered composite. The CTE of the sandwich composites can then be obtained according to its definition.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\alpha}_{x}= & \frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{x}}{\Delta T}=\frac{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right) \alpha_{H}+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right) \alpha_{S}}{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)} \\
\tilde{\alpha}_{y}= & \frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{y}}{\Delta T}=\frac{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right) \alpha_{H}+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right) \alpha_{S}}{h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)} \\
\tilde{\alpha}_{z}= & \frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{z}}{\Delta T}=\frac{h_{H} \alpha_{H}\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1+\nu_{H}\right)-2 h_{S} E_{H} \nu_{S}\right]}{\left(h_{H}+h_{S}\right)\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)\right]} \\
& +\frac{h_{S} \alpha_{S}\left[h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)+h_{H} E_{H}\left(1+\nu_{S}\right)-2 h_{H} E_{S} \nu_{H}\right)}{\left(h_{H}+h_{S}\right)\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)\right]}
\end{aligned} \tag{17}
$$

### 2.2. Staggered composites

The derivation of the staggered composites is similar to that for the sandwich composites, though there are some differences making the formulae of the staggered structure more complicated. A representative unit is chosen as shown in Fig. 2(b) to carry out the analysis.

The constitutive equations and the equilibrium equations (Eqs. (1)-(10)) still hold for staggered composites. The kinetics equation in the $y$-direction is also the same as equation (11). But the relationship for strain in the $x$-direction needs to be replaced by the following equation.

Kinetics equation:

$$
\varepsilon_{x}^{S}=\varepsilon_{x}^{H}+\frac{\left(h_{H}+h_{S}\right) \Delta d}{h_{S} l} \tag{18}
$$

where $l$ is the length of the hard platelets; $d$ is the length of longitudinal gap between hard platelets, and $\Delta d$ is the elongation of $d$. Equation (18) can be obtained by considering the geometric relation in staggered composites. The details can be accessed in Liu et al.'s paper [Liu et al., 2006].

Because an additional unknown variable $\Delta d$ is introduced, the above equations are not enough to determine all the variables, so one more governing equation is required. The energy minimization is then performed here. The elastic energy stored in the representative unit can be decomposed into two parts.

$$
U_{t o t a l}=U_{H}+U_{S} \tag{19}
$$

where $U_{H}$, $U_{S}$ are the elastic energy stored in hard platelets and soft matrix, respectively. Assuming the shear strain in the hard platelets is negligible, the energy in the soft matrix can be decomposed into two parts related to normal strains and shear strains [Ji and Gao 2004]. The total energy has the following form.

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_{t o t a l} & =U_{H}+U_{S}^{\varepsilon}+U_{S}^{\gamma} \\
& =\frac{1}{2}\left[\sigma_{x}^{H}\left(\varepsilon_{x}^{H}-\alpha_{H} \Delta T\right)+\sigma_{y}^{H}\left(\varepsilon_{y}^{H}-\alpha_{H} \Delta T\right)\right] h_{H} \frac{l}{2} \\
& \quad+\frac{1}{2}\left[\sigma_{x}^{S}\left(\varepsilon_{x}^{S}-\alpha_{S} \Delta T\right)+\sigma_{y}^{S}\left(\varepsilon_{y}^{S}-\alpha_{S} \Delta T\right)\right] h_{S} \frac{l}{2}+\frac{\gamma G_{s}(\Delta d)^{2} l}{16 h_{s}}
\end{aligned} \tag{20}
$$

where $U_{S}^{\varepsilon}$, $U_{S}^{\gamma}$ are the stored energies related to the normal strain and the shear strain in the soft matrix respectively. $G_{S}$ is the shear modulus of soft matrix. $\gamma$ is the energy modification factor which has the following form [Liu et al., 2006].

$$
\gamma=\left[1+\frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{\left(h_{H}+h_{S}\right) l}{h_{S}(l+d)}\right)^{2}\right]\left(\frac{l-d}{l+d}\right) \tag{21}
$$

By solving equations (1)-(11) and equation (18), all the variables can be expressed as the functions of $\Delta d$, then substituting them into Eq. (20), the total energy can be written as:

$$
U_{t o t a l}=U_{t o t a l}(\Delta d) \tag{22}
$$

From the energy minimization, one more governing equation can be obtained as follow.

$$
\frac{d U_{t o t a l}}{d(\Delta d)}=0 \tag{23}
$$

Thus we can determine $\Delta d$ as,

$$
\Delta d=\frac{\frac{\tilde{\sigma}^{S}}{E_{S}}\left[2 \beta_{1}-\nu_{S}\left(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\right)\right]-\frac{\tilde{\sigma}^{H}}{E_{H}}\left[2 \beta_{1}-\nu_{H}\left(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\right)\right]}{\frac{2}{E_{H} h_{H}}\left(\beta_{1}^{2}-\nu_{H} \beta_{1} \beta_{2}\right)+\frac{2}{E_{S} h_{S}}\left(\beta_{1}^{2}-\nu_{S} \beta_{1} \beta_{2}\right)+\frac{\gamma G_{S}}{2 h_{S}}}
\tag{24}
$$

where

$$
\beta_{1}=\frac{h_{H}^{2} E_{H} E_{S}\left(h_{H} E_{H}+h_{S} E_{S}\right)}{c_{H} l\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1+\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1+\nu_{H}\right)\right]\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)\right]}
\tag{25}
$$

$$
\beta_{2}=\frac{h_{H}^{2} E_{H} E_{S}\left(\nu_{S} h_{H} E_{H}+\nu_{H} h_{S} E_{S}\right)}{c_{H} l\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1+\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1+\nu_{H}\right)\right]\left[h_{H} E_{H}\left(1-\nu_{S}\right)+h_{S} E_{S}\left(1-\nu_{H}\right)\right]}
\tag{26}
$$

and $c_{H}$ is the volume fraction of the hard phase $c_{H}=h_{H} /\left(h_{H}+h_{S}\right)$.

Furthermore, all other variables can be determined. The CTEs of staggered composite in $x$-direction and $y$-direction are

$$
\alpha_{x}=\frac{\varepsilon_{x}}{\Delta T}=\tilde{\alpha}_{x}+\frac{l\left[\frac{1}{E_{H} h_{H}}\left(\beta_{1}-\nu_{H} \beta_{2}\right)\right]+d\left[-\frac{1}{E_{S} h_{S}}\left(\beta_{1}-\nu_{S} \beta_{2}\right)\right]}{l+d} \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta T}
\tag{27}
$$

$$
\alpha_{y}=\frac{\varepsilon_{y}}{\Delta T}=\tilde{\alpha}_{y}+\frac{1}{E_{H} h_{H}}\left(\beta_{2}-\nu_{H} \beta_{1}\right) \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta T}
\tag{28}
$$

$$
\alpha_{z}=\frac{\varepsilon_{z}}{\Delta T}=\tilde{\alpha}_{z}+\frac{-\frac{\nu_{h}}{E_{h}}+\frac{\nu_{s}}{E_{s}}}{h_{h}+h_{s}}\left(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\right) \frac{\Delta d}{\Delta T}
\tag{29}
$$

And the average thermal mismatch stresses in the hard and soft phases are

$$
\sigma_{x}^{H}=\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{H}+\frac{1}{h_{H}} \beta_{1} \Delta d
\tag{30}
$$

$$
\sigma_{x}^{S}=\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{S}-\frac{1}{h_{S}} \beta_{1} \Delta d
\tag{31}
$$

$$
\sigma_{y}^{H}=\tilde{\sigma}_{y}^{H}+\frac{1}{h_{H}} \beta_{2} \Delta d
\tag{32}
$$

$$
\sigma_{y}^{S}=\tilde{\sigma}_{y}^{S}-\frac{1}{h_{S}} \beta_{2} \Delta d
\tag{33}
$$

It is noted that the CTE and thermal mismatch stress of the staggered composites are obviously different from their counterparts for sandwich composite, i.e. $\tilde{\alpha}, \tilde{\sigma}$. In the next section, the influence of the staggered microstructure will be further discussed.

### 3. Results and Discussions

We also carry out the FEM calculations to check the validity of our theoretical model. By considering the periodicity and symmetry, a representative unit as shown in Fig. 2(b) is chosen for simulation. All boundary surfaces are kept flat and have

no rotation. The nondimensional material constants are taken as follows:

The Young's modulus: $\frac{E_H}{E_S}=100$

The Poisson ratio: $\nu_H=0.2$, $\nu_S=0.3$

The coefficient of thermal expansion: $\frac{\alpha_S}{\alpha_H}=10$,

The geometry parameters: $\frac{h_S}{h_H}=1$, $\frac{d}{h_H}=1$, $\frac{l}{h_H}=3,10,20,35$

### 3.1. CTE of the staggered composite

Figure 3 shows the longitudinal CTE for the staggered composite as a function of the aspect ratio of hard phase platelets, and the volume-averaged estimation of CTEs, $c_H\alpha_H+(1-c_H)\alpha_S$, is used in normalization. In this example case for theoretical and FEM analyses, the CTE of the soft phase is assumed larger than that of hard phase, and $\alpha_S/\alpha_H=10$. The volume fraction of the hard phase is kept constant when the aspect ratio changes to isolate its influence. It can be seen that the prediction from our theoretical model agrees well with the FEM simulation. When the aspect ratio is large enough, the longitudinal CTE of staggered composites approaches to the CTE of the sandwich composites. When the aspect ratio is below 20, the CTE in the $x$-direction increases significantly as the aspect ratio decreases. It is interesting to note that the CTE of a composite depends not only on the CTEs and moduli of constituent phases, but also its microstructure such as the

### 3.2. The thermal mismatch stress

When the staggered composite experiences a temperature change $\Delta T$, the thermal mismatch stresses in the two phases are studied by both theoretical model and FEM analysis. Figure 5 shows the relation between the aspect ratio and the average thermal mismatch stress in the hard platelets. The reference stress $(\alpha_{S}-\alpha_{H})\Delta T E_{S}$ is used for nondimensionalization. Considering that in reality hard materials are usually brittle and soft materials are usually tough, the hard platelet is therefore more dangerous due to the thermal mismatch, and the following research will mainly focus on the thermal stress in the hard phase. It is noted from Fig. 5 that the average thermal mismatch stresses of hard platelet would decrease significantly as its aspect

It can also be observed that the maximum thermal mismatch stresses appears at the center of the hard platelet, which is attributed to the shear-lag effect — although the stress is low at the ends of hard platelets, it increases along the longitudinal direction by accumulating or balancing the shear stress in the soft phase. Therefore, the shorter hard platelets have lower thermal stress. This result has an important implication that the thermal mismatch stress can be efficiently reduced through microstructure design. Thus the staggered composites have better thermal-shock resistance. Actually, the similar strategy has been widely used in the railway, in which a gap between two rail pieces is deliberately kept to reduce the thermal stress. The effect of the Poisson ratio of the soft phase on the thermal stress is also investigated.
