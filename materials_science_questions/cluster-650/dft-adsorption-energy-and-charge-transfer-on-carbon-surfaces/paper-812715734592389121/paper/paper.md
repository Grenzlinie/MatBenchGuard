## ABSTRACT

Hydrogen storage properties of Li-decorated graphene oxides containing epoxy and hydroxyl groups are studied by using density functional theory. The Li atoms form $Li_4O/Li_3OH$ clusters and are anchored strongly on the graphene surface with binding energies of $-3.20$ and $-2.84$ eV. The clusters transfer electrons to the graphene substrate, and the Li atoms exist as $Li^+$ cations with strong adsorption ability for $H_2$ molecules. Each Li atom can adsorb at least $2H_2$ molecules with adsorption energies greater than $-0.20$ eV/$H_2$. The hydrogen storage properties of Li-decorated graphene at different oxidation degrees are studied. The computations show that the adsorption energy of $H_2$ is $-0.22$ eV/$H_2$ and the hydrogen storage capacity is 6.04 wt% at the oxidation ratio $O/C=1/16$. When the O/C ratio is 1:8, the storage capacity reaches 10.26 wt% and the adsorption energy is $-0.15$ eV/$H_2$. These results

## Introduction

Hydrogen is an ideal energy carrier since it has the highest energy density and the combustion produces only water [1–3]. One of the key obstacles to the wide use of hydrogen energy, especially for the hydrogen fuel-cell powered vehicles, is the high density and reversible storage of hydrogen at ambient temperature [4–6]. The United States Department of Energy (USDE) sets three technical targets for 2020: (i) a gravimetric density of at least 5.5%, (ii) reversible operation at temperatures –40–60 °C, and (iii) a charging/discharging rate of 1.5 kg H₂/min [7]. Due to the large surface areas and light weight, carbon-based nanomaterials were considered as good candidates for hydrogen storage and explored extensively [1–3,8–10]. However, the storage capacity of pure carbon materials is restricted due to the weak adsorption of H₂ molecules. The adsorption energy for an efficient reversible adsorption/desorption process at ambient temperature and moderate pressures should be around –0.2 eV per H₂ molecule [11,12], which is between typical physisorption (less than –0.1 eV/H₂) and chemisorption (more than –0.6 eV/H₂). Therefore, the pure carbon nanomaterials cannot satisfied the requirements for practical application for hydrogen storage.

Recent studies suggested two approaches to enhance the adsorption of H₂ molecules on host materials. One approach is to induce polarization of H₂ molecules by the electric field generated by alkali or alkaline earth metals supported on the nanomaterials [13–16]. The other approach is through the Kubas interaction: the interaction of H₂ σ and σ* orbitals with transition metal d orbitals [17–19]. However, the participation of the transition metal d orbitals makes H₂ molecules dissociate easily. The transition metal atoms tend to aggregate on the substrate because of the large cohesive energy of the bulk (~4.0 eV) and the heavy mass is also unfavourable for high H₂ storage capacities. For example, Psofogiannakis et al. studied the mechanism of hydrogen storage by hydrogen spillover on a Pt-doped graphite surface, and they found H₂ dissociate spontaneously on a Pt cluster and measured a storage capacity of 3.0 wt% at room temperature and 2 MPa [20,21].

Due to its light weight, lithium is a preferred candidate for surface decoration of carbon-based nanomaterials used for hydrogen storage. Computations showed that Li decorated graphene can reach a hydrogen storage capacity 12.8 wt% [15]. Li-functionalized 2D g-C₆N₈ and g-C₃N₄ were also regarded as efficient hydrogen storage materials, and the storage capacities reach 7.55 wt % and 9.20 wt%, respectively [22,23]. However, these results were obtained under ideal conditions that the Li atoms are highly dispersed. The cohesive energies of alkali metals are much smaller than the transition metals, but the interactions of pure carbon materials with alkali metals are usually weak and cannot make the metal atoms disperse over the substrate [24,25]. The clustering of the metal atoms reduces the adsorption sites for H₂ molecules and leads to much lower hydrogen storage capacities. To surmount the issue of poor dispersion of metal atoms, the carbon materials can be chemically modified by introducing structural defects or doping with impurities [26–30]. Introducing some of the oxygen-containing functional groups into graphene also be an effective way to enhance the binding of Li atoms. Theoretical calculations suggested that metal atoms can be steadily anchored by the epoxy and hydroxyl on the graphene oxides (GO) surface, and stay active for adsorbing H₂ with moderate strength [31,32]. In most cases, GO contains both epoxy (-O) and hydroxyl (-OH) groups. The types of oxygen-containing groups and the oxidation degrees can be controlled by adjusting the reduction time, temperature, and reducing agents [33–36].

In this paper we investigate the Li decoration on reduced graphene oxides (RGO) containing epoxy and hydroxyl groups and the hydrogen storage properties of the modified complexes by using density functional theory. It is found that Li₄O/Li₃OH clusters can be formed and bound strongly on the graphene substrate. Each Li atom of Li₄O/Li₃OH can adsorb at least 2H₂ molecules with adsorption energies around –0.20 eV/H₂. The calculations for Li-decorated graphene with different oxidation degrees show that the hydrogen storage capacity (HSC) and the adsorption energy Eₐd reaches 6.04 wt% and –0.22 eV/H₂ at O/C = 1/16, and 10.26 wt% and –0.15 eV/H₂ at O/C = 1/8.

## Calculation method

The structure optimization, energetics, and electronic properties are calculated using density functional theory by the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [37,38]. The projector augmented wave method (PAW) is used [39], and the valence electron wave functions are expanded in plane-wave basis set with the energy cutoff 500 eV. The exchange-correlation interactions are expressed with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the form of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [40]. van der Waals (vdW) interaction is important for the adsorption of H₂ molecules, we include it by using the empirical correction scheme of Grimme (DFT+D2) [41]. The Brillouin zone is sampled with 5 × 5 × 1 for a (4 × 4) supercell by Monkhorst-Pack k-point scheme. 20 Å is set along the (001) direction (perpendicular to the graphene surface) to avoid the interaction between layers. The Gauss function with $\sigma = 0.2$ eV is adopted for the smearing method. The convergence criterion of $10^{-5}$ eV for energies has been used, and geometry optimization is performed until the forces acting on each ion become less than $10^{-2}$ eV/Å. Our test calculations show that the above parameters offer reliable convergence.

The degree of oxidation and the chemical compositions of GO depend on the experimental conditions, but the epoxy (-O-) and hydroxyl (-OH) are usually the main O-containing groups, and these groups are distributed randomly on the graphene [42,43]. Lerf and Klinowski proposed a graphene model which consists of both sp² and sp³ hybridized carbon atoms with epoxy and hydroxyl inside the GO surface and carboxyl suspending at the edge [44,45]. In this work, we use the periodic conditions and built RGO cells containing epoxy/hydroxyl groups on the 4 × 4 supercell of graphene. Different configurations consisting of one to four O/OH groups are optimized and the most stable structures are shown in Fig. 1. Fig. 1a is the 4 × 4 supercell of graphene, frames Fig. 1b and c are the configurations of O/OH groups at O/C = 1/32, and Fig. 1d-f are O/OH groups at O/C = 1/16 and 1/8 respectively. These results are consistent with the configurations reported previously [46].

The binding energy of Li atoms on pure graphene (G) or RGO is defined as

$$
E_{\text{b-Li}} = \left(E_{\text{G/R-GO-}x\text{Li}} - E_{\text{G/R-GO}} - xE_{\text{Li}}\right)/x \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{\text{G/R-GO-}x\text{Li}}$ is the total energy of decorating x Li atoms on the 4 × 4 supercell of G or RGO, $E_{\text{G/R-GO}}$ is the energy of the 4 × 4 supercell of G/RGO, and $E_{\text{Li}}$ is the energy of isolated Li atom. The binding energy of $\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}$ ($n=1$–$4$, $m=1$–$3$) clusters on the 4 × 4 supercell of graphene is defined as

$$
E_{\text{b-cluster}} = \left(E_{\text{G} - y\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}} - E_{\text{G}} - yE_{\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}}\right)/y \tag{2}
$$

where $E_{\text{G-cluster}}$ denotes the total energy of a 4 × 4 supercell binding y $\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}$ clusters, $E_{\text{G}}$ is the energy of 4 × 4 graphene supercell, and $E_{\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}}$ is the energy of an isolated $\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}$ cluster. The average adsorption energy of $\text{H}_2$ molecules on $\text{Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}$-decorated graphene 4 × 4 supercell is defined as

$$
E_{\text{ad}} = \left(E_{\text{G-Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}-z\text{H}_2} - E_{\text{G-Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}} - zE_{\text{H}_2}\right)/z \tag{3}
$$

here $E_{\text{G-Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}-z\text{H}_2}$ is the total energy of the system adsorbing z $\text{H}_2$ molecules, $E_{\text{G-Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}}$ is the energy of $\text{Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}$ decorated 4 × 4 supercell of graphene, and $E_{\text{H}_2}$ is the energy of free $\text{H}_2$ molecule.

## Results and discussion

### The decoration of Li atoms on RGO

Firstly, we consider the decoration of single Li atom on a 4 × 4 supercell of pure graphene. We optimized the structures for a Li atom at three nonequivalent sites: above the carbon ring, the C–C bond, and the C atom, separately. The results show that the Li atom moves to the top of the carbon ring in all these cases (Fig. 2a). The binding energy of a Li atom on a 4 × 4 supercell of the pure graphene is $-1.73$ eV.

For the decoration of Li atom on the 4 × 4 supercell containing one O atom, the optimization for different initial structures leads to two types of stable configurations as shown in Fig. 2b. When the Li atom is initially placed in the white region, it will bind with the O atom. When the Li atom is placed in the gray region, the Li atom will relax to the top of the carbon rings. For the first Li atom, it forms a O-Li group with a O-Li bond length of $1.75$ Å; the O atom is bond on the top of a carbon atom, and the vertical distances from the O and Li atoms to the graphene plane are $1.98$ Å and $2.13$ Å, respectively. When two Li atoms are added, the O atom moves further away from graphene, and the Li atoms are situated above two carbon rings opposite the O atom. When there are three Li atoms in the white region, they combine with the O atom to form a $Li_3O$ cluster with the O-Li distance of $1.70$ Å. For the $4\times4$ supercell containing one O atom, the bottom Li atoms get closer to the graphene and the Li-O distances increase with the number of Li atoms, but the O atom is closest to the graphene in the $\text{Li}_3\text{O}$ cluster (the OLi group has a different motif). In the case of containing an OH group, both the $d_{\text{O-graphene}}$ and $d_{\text{Li-graphene}}$ decrease but the $d_{\text{O-Li}}$ increases with increasing the Li atoms. On the $4\times4$ supercell having one O atom, the binding energy of a single Li atom is $-3.16$ eV, and the binding energy of the OLi group on the graphene is $-1.89$ eV. Since the $\text{Li}_2\text{O}$ is a very stable species, the average binding energy of the Li atoms is $-3.29$ eV, but the interaction between the $\text{Li}_2\text{O}$ and the graphene is only $-0.77$ eV. The binding energy of the Li atoms is largest for the $\text{Li}_3\text{O}$ cluster, it reaches $-3.56$ eV. The average binding energy of the Li atoms is $-3.27$ eV for the $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ cluster formed on the graphene, and the binding of the $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ cluster with the graphene is strongest ($-2.84$ eV). It suggests the Li atoms on the epoxy oxidized graphene will form the tetrahedral $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ cluster. For the graphene containing the hydroxyl group, $\text{LiOH}$ is a very stable species and it combines only weakly to the graphene. The average binding energy of the Li atoms decrease with increasing the Li atoms but it still reaches $-3.48$ eV in the $\text{Li}_3\text{OH}$ cluster. The interaction of $\text{Li}_3\text{OH}$ with the graphene is strongest, with a binding energy of $-3.20$ eV. This means the tetrahedral $\text{Li}_3\text{OH}$ cluster is the final product for the Li-decorated RGO with hydroxyl groups.

The above results show that the Li atoms can interact strongly with the O/OH groups on the RGO. The binding energy of the Li atoms is much larger than the cohesive energy of the bulk lithium, so they will not aggregate on the RGO substrate. The Li atoms will combine with the O/OH groups and form tetrahedral $\text{Li}_4\text{O/Li}_3\text{OH}$ clusters, and these clusters can be strongly anchored on the graphene surface.

To investigate the interaction mechanism between the $\text{Li}n\text{O/Li}m\text{OH}$ clusters and graphene, we analyzed the charge distribution using the Bader model [47,48]. For the isolated $\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}$ clusters, the charge values on the Li atoms are $0.85-0.88$ e in $\text{LiO}$, $\text{Li}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{LiOH}$, and the charges are about $0.65$ e in $\text{Li}_n\text{O}$ ($n=3,4$) and $0.59$ e in $\text{Li}_m\text{OH}$ ($m=2,3$). The charge values on the O atoms increase with the number of Li atoms. For the $\text{Li}_n\text{O/Li}_m\text{OH}$ clusters formed on the graphene, the charge values on the Li atoms are about $0.86$ e in all the cases, except the top Li atom in $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ is $0.67$ e. The negative charges on the O atoms increase with the number of Li atoms in the $\text{Li}_n\text{O}$ clusters but decrease with the Li atoms in $\text{Li}_m\text{OH}$. The charges on the H atoms falls in the range $0.58-0.64$ e. It is clear that the charge values on the Li atoms keeps close and the charges on the O atoms change moderately. The LiO group is electron deficient and it gets electrons from graphene; when the Li atoms increase, the clusters transfer their electrons to the graphene.

The decoration of Li atoms on the graphene oxides at O/C ratios of 1:16 and 1:8 are studied. We optimized the 4 × 4 graphene supercell decorated with two, three, and four Li₄O or Li₃OH groups with different configurations; Fig. 6 shows the most stable structures. When we place one Li₄O and one Li₃OH on the 4 × 4 supercell (O/C = 1:16, denoted as C1), the Li₄O and Li₃OH clusters are situated on opposite sides of the graphene in the most stable structure. The average binding energy of Li atoms is –3.20 eV, and the average binding energy of the clusters is –2.85 eV. In the case of O/C = 1:8, we consider two different decoration modes. ## Conclusion

The hydrogen storage properties of Li-decorated RGO containing epoxy and hydroxyl groups are studied by using density functional theory. When Li atom decorates on the O-neighbour positions, the epoxy and hydroxyl groups become the aggregation core of Li atoms. A single O atom can bind 4 Li atoms and form a tetrahedral $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ cluster on the graphene, while a single OH can combine three Li atoms to form a $\text{Li}_3\text{OH}$ cluster. The binding energies of the $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ and $\text{Li}_3\text{OH}$ clusters on graphene reaches $-3.20$ and $-2.84$ eV respectively, and the average binding energy of the Li atoms is greater than $-3.0$ eV. Since the clusters transfer electrons to the graphene, Li atoms exist as $\text{Li}^+$ ions and exhibit strong $\text{H}_2$ adsorption ability. The $\text{Li}_4\text{O}$ cluster can adsorb $9\text{H}_2$ molecules with an adsorption energy of $-0.20$ eV/$\text{H}_2$. The $\text{Li}_3\text{OH}$ cluster can adsorb $6\text{H}_2$ molecules and the adsorption energy reaches $-0.23$ eV/$\text{H}_2$. This strong adsorption is due to the electrostatic interaction between the polarized $\text{H}_2$ molecules and the $\text{Li}^+$ ions. We investigated the hydrogen storage properties of Li-decorated graphene at greater oxidation ratios. When the oxidation ratio increases, the HSC increases but the adsorption energy decreases gradually. At a medium O/C ratio of 1:16, the calculations show that the average adsorption energy of $\text{H}_2$ molecules is $-0.22$ eV/$\text{H}_2$ and the HSC is 6.04 wt%, which meet the goals set by the US Department of Energy. When the O/C ratio is 1:8, a high HSC 10.26 wt% is obtained, and the adsorption energy is $-0.15$ eV/$\text{H}_2$. We hope these theoretical results can guide the experimental preparation for hydrogen storage materials with high storage capacity and good reversible kinetics at ambient temperatures.
