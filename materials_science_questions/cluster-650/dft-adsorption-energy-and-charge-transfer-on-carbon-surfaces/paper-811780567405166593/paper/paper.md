# First-principles study of the IVA group atoms adsorption on graphene
Haigen Gao, $^{1}$ Jian Zhou, $^{1,2,a)}$ Minghui Lu, $^{1}$ Wei Fa, $^{2}$ and Yanfeng Chen $^{1}$
$^{1}$ Department of Material Science and Engineering and National Laboratory of Solid State Microstructures, Nanjing University, Nanjing 210093, People's Republic of China
$^{2}$ Department of Physics, Group of Computational Condensed Matter Physics, National Laboratory of Solid State Microstructures, Nanjing University, Nanjing 210093, People's Republic of China

(Received 26 February 2010; accepted 2 May 2010; published online 4 June 2010)

The adsorption of five different IVA group atoms on graphene is studied by using the density functional theory. The adsorption energy, geometric, and electronic structure are calculated. We find that the adsorption energy decreases when the atomic number of adsorbate increases. The interaction between the adsorbate and graphene also changes from the strong covalent bond to the weak van der Waals interaction from C to Pb. For C and Si atoms, the stablest adsorption site is the bridge site, while for Ge, Sn, and Pb atoms, both the top and bridge sites are same stable. The spin-polarized calculations show that graphene could be magnetic when the IVA group atom adsorbed on it. Detailed analysis shows that the magnetic moment comes from the localized $p$ orbital of IVA group atom. © 2010 American Institute of Physics. [doi:10.1063/1.3437640]

## I. INTRODUCTION
Graphene, a single-layered of graphite, has attracted much attention due to its unique two-dimensional (2D) geometric structure and Dirac-fermion electronic properties. $^{1,2}$ Many studies focus on its good transporting properties since the charge carrier mobility of graphene can reach more than $10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}.^{3,4}$

However, in the practical devices, semiconductor is sometimes more useful than a simple metal wire. $^{5}$ In order to tailor the electronic properties of graphene for future applications in devices, one of the important method is to adsorb atoms on graphene. And then it is quite important to study the interaction between the graphene and atoms, clusters, or even molecules. Many works have been done in this area in the recent years.

Hydrogen atom is a good choice since C and H are the basic elements of organic matter. The H adsorption on graphene can induce magnetic moment about $1 \ \mu_{B}$, and possibly to be ferromagnetic when two H atoms are located on the same sublattice of graphene. $^{6-12}$ And some other studies focus on the stability, adsorption, and magnetism of the $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ and other small molecules. $^{13-17}$ Recently a new kind of 2D crystal, called graphane, has been predicted theoretically $^{18,19}$ and then synthesized in experiment. $^{20}$ Graphane is nothing but a fully hydrogenated graphene, in which the hydrogen atoms remove the conducting $\pi$ bands near the Fermi energy $(E_{\mathrm{F}})$ of graphene. As a result, graphane is a wide gap semiconductor.

Besides the H adsorption, the electronic properties of graphene can be modified by different kinds of adsorbates. Some of works are about the nonmetal atoms adsorption on graphene. $^{21-24}$ For example, Zhou et al. $^{22}$ calculated the $\mathrm{Cl}$, $\mathrm{S}$, and $\mathrm{P}$ adsorption on graphene and find that the S-graphene is semiconductor, while Cl- and P-graphene are both metallic. And more intereting is that P-graphene exhibit a magnetic moment of $0.86 \ \mu_{B}$. Another work shows that N-graphene is magnetic, while F- and O-graphene are both nonmagnetic. $^{23}$ These examples demonstrate that the electronic properties of graphene can be completely changed by adsorption of the nonmetal atoms.

However, more works are focused on the metal atoms adsorption on graphene, especially the alkali metals and transition metals. $^{25-41}$ Chan et al. $^{41}$ have calculated 12 different metal adatoms on graphene systematically. They find that for the I-III groups adatoms, the interaction between the adatoms and graphene are ionic bonds and the electronic properties of graphene change little. While for the transition, noble metals, and the Sn atom, the covalent bonding are formed and the graphene's electronic states are strong hybridization with the adsorbate.

In this paper, we will study all the IVA group atoms adsorption on graphene, since IVA group atoms include both nonmetallic (C and Si) and metallic atoms (Ge, Sn, and Pb). Our work will focus on its geometric and electronic structure after adsorption. The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, the computational details are described. The main numerical results and discussions are given in Sec. III. Finally, in Sec. IV, a conclusion is presented.

## II. COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS
Graphene is a 2D crystal containing two carbon atoms in the unit cell. Each carbon atom has three neighboring carbon atoms connected by three $\sigma$ bonds and the $p_{z}$ electrons form the delocalized $\pi$ bond. In order to study the single atom adsorbed on graphene, we build a $4×4×1$ supercell from the graphene primitive cell, and using a large vacuum between the two adjacent layers.

The geometric and electronic structure of the IVA group atoms adsorption on the graphene is calculated by the density functional theory (DFT) in the generalized gradient approximation implemented in the Vienna $Ab$ Initio Simulation Package (VASP), $^{42,43}$ in which the projected augmented wave

## III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS
### A. Adsorption energy and geometric structure
The possible adsorption sites of atoms on graphene are top, bridge, and hollow sites, as shown in Fig. 1. In the top site adsorption, the adsorbed atom is located directly above a carbon atom in graphene. In the bridge site, the adsorbed atom is above the middle of the C-C bond, while in the hollow site case, it is located above the center of hexagon of graphene.

We have calculated the total energies of all the adsorption sites for all the adsorbed atoms. In order to compare the stability of different sites and adsorbates, we have calculated the adsorption energy for all cases, which is defined as follows:

$$
E_{\mathrm{ad}}=E_{\mathrm{ag}}-\left(E_{\mathrm{a}}+E_{\mathrm{g}}\right), \quad(1)
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{ad}}$ is the adsorption energy, $E_{\mathrm{ag}}$ is the total energy of the whole system (including the adsorbates and graphene), $E_{\mathrm{a}}$ is the total energy of adsorbed atom, and $E_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the total energy of isolated graphene. We also define a diffusion barrier, which is just the energy difference between two configurations. $^{41}$ More accurate calculation of energy barrier needs other special method, for example, the nudged elastic band method. $^{47,48}$ In order to describe the structure distortion after the adsorption, we define the height difference $\Delta h$ $=z_{\max }-z_{\min }$ in the graphene, where $z_{\max }$ and $z_{\min }$ is the maxima and minima value of the $z$ coordinate of the carbon atoms in graphene. And the bond length $d_{\mathrm{ac}}$ is the distance between the adsorbate and its nearest carbon atom in graphene.

In our calculations, the original distance between the adsorbed atom and graphene is set to be 2 Å and the final distance, i.e., the bond length between them, is obtained by structural optimization. The adsorption energy, defined in the Eq. (1), is listed in Table I. From the Table I, we can see that in general the adsorption energy decreases (in its absolute value) with increasing atomic number for the same adsorption site. That is to say, when the adsorbed atom becomes larger, the adsorption on graphene becomes less stable. This trend is also found in the adsorption of the IIIA group atoms. $^{41}$

unstablest one. The adsorption energies of C and Si at the B site are $-1.545$ eV and $-0.554$ eV respectively, which show the C and Si atoms can strongly bind to the B site of the graphene. While at the H site, the adsorption energy is very small, which implies that the adsorbate and graphene are not chemically bonded and the interaction between them is most likely van der Waals interaction. The adsorption at H site is most likely the physical adsorption. For the T site, the adsorption energies of C and Si are smaller than those at the B site but much larger than those at the H site. Therefore we think that at the T site, the C and Si atoms still have relative strong bonds with graphene.

And for the Ge, Sn, and Pb atoms, both the T and B site have almost the same adsorption energies, which are about 0.2–0.4 eV, much smaller than those of the C and Si atoms. This implies that the interaction between the Ge (Sn and Pb) and the graphene could be relatively weak. The adsorption energies of the Ge, Sn, and Pb at the H site are almost 0, which show an obviously physical adsorption between them. We also note that the adsorption energy of Sn is well consistent with the value in the Ref. 41.

From the above discussion, we can find that for the IVA group atoms adsorbed on graphene, the B site is always the stablest site. However, when the radius of atom increases, the diffusion barrier between the B and T site disappears, that is to say, both the B and T site are same stable for large IVA group atoms. The H site is always the unstablest site, which is in fact physical adsorption. This can also be seen from the distortion of the graphene after adsorption. For example, when the C adsorbed on the B site of graphene, its adsorption energy is $-1.535$ eV, largest in all our calculated systems. Therefore, this strong bond between them induces a largest distortion in graphene, whose height difference $\Delta h$ is $0.46$ Å. As for the H site, the $\Delta h$ is almost 0, which in fact reflects the weak van der Waals interaction between the adsorbate and graphene. The bond length between the adsorbate and carbon atoms in graphene also shows the same fact. For example, at the H site, the bond length between the adsorbate and carbon atom in graphene is more than $3$ Å, which is really beyond the range of bonding.

Since the adsorption energies of all IVA atoms at H site are considerably smaller than that at B and T sites, we think the H site is possibly an energy maximum on graphene. We have done some calculations that the adsorbates are initially displaced away from the exact H site about 0.02 or $0.05$ Å in $x$ or $y$ direction. After optimization, the adsorbates shift to the B site easily. These results show that in fact the H site is an energy maximum and is very unstable for adsorption for all IVA atoms. In an experimental circumstance, it is then not possible to adsorb IVA atoms at H site on graphene, since any thermal fluctuation will drive adsorbates from H site to B site.

We have to note that DFT calculation usually cannot give an accurate adsorption energy compared to other $ab$ $initio$ method and to the experimental results.⁴⁹ Especially, the DFT code, used in our calculation, cannot deal with the van der Waals interactions, some of the adsorption energy in our calculation should have large errors. However, DFT calculation can provide the qualitatively prediction and trend of the adsorption energy in a cheap and effective way. So, we think that our results still have good reference value to the experiments.

## B. Electronic structure and magnetic property

After discussing the geometric structure and adsorption energy, we now study the electronic properties of different adsorbed graphene. In fact, the electronic properties are determined by the type of adsorption. For physical adsorption, the interaction between the adsorbate and graphene is very week and the electronic properties of graphene should remain nearly unchanged. As for the chemical adsorption, the new formed bond should alter the graphene's electronic structure more evidently.

Figure 2 shows the total DOS of C-graphene with different adsorption site. The first obvious phenomenon is that there is a DOS peak at the $E_{\text{F}}$ in all three cases, which should be zero in the pure graphene [the dotted line in Fig. 2(c)]. And the DOS of spin up and down are not symmetrical near the $E_{\text{F}}$, which implies the system should be magnetic. We will discuss the origin of the DOS peak and the magnetic moment later. The second thing we find is that the DOS of C-graphene at H site is almost same as the one of pure graphene, except that an additional peak is found in C-graphene at the $E_{\text{F}}$ [Fig. 2(c)]. However, the DOS of C-graphene at B site is a little different: there are more DOS peaks below the $E_{\text{F}}$ and the peak at the $E_{\text{F}}$ is also suppressed significantly [Fig. 2(b)]. The DOS of C-graphene at T site seems at a intermediate state between the B and H site [Fig.

2(a)]. We think this reflects the strength of interaction between the C and graphene. For the C-graphene, the B site configuration has the largest adsorption energy $(-1.535\ \text{eV})$ and, therefore, has the strongest covalent bond between the C and graphene. As a result, the DOS of graphene is changed significantly by the adsorbate. However, for the H site configuration, the adsorption energy is only $-0.158\ \text{eV}$, which implies that the interaction between the C and graphene is weak. Therefore, the DOS of C-graphene looks just like the pure graphene. So, the characteristic of DOS is well consistent with their geometry structure. When the atomic number increases, the adsorption energy decreases and the interaction between the adsorbate and graphene become weaker and weaker for all the adsorption site. So the DOS of them will become more like the DOS of pure graphene with an additional peak at the $E_{\text{F}}$, which is not shown here.

Now, we will analyze the DOS peak at the $E_{\text{F}}$ and the origin of the magnetic moment in the adsorbed graphene. We take the C-graphene at T site configuration as an example. Figure 3 shows the $s$ and $p$ DOS of the adsorbed C atom and two other C atoms in graphene (one is just below the adsorbate and the other is far away from it). The $s$ orbital is always far below the $E_{\text{F}}$, which is fully occupied and we can ignore it now. We can clearly see that the DOS peak mainly comes from the $p$ orbital of the adsorbed C atom [Fig. 3(a)]. In Fig. 3(b), the C atom in graphene, which is below the adsorbate, also has a little peak at $E_{\text{F}}$, however it is much lower than the one of adsorbed C. And the C atom in graphene, which is far away from the adsorbate, shows no DOS at the $E_{\text{F}}$, as shown in Fig. 3(c). And furthermore, we can see that in Fig. 3(a) the $p$ DOS of adsorbed C atom is quite localized and highly spin-polarized: the spin up $p$ orbital is occupied a lot, while the spin down $p$ orbital is almost empty. This is the origin of the magnetic moment in the adsorbed graphene.

In Fig. 3(a), we can also find some small peaks of $p$ DOS below the $E_{\text{F}}$, which in fact reflects the interaction between the adsorbate and graphene. So, it is necessary to have some more detailed analysis about the $p$ DOS of the adsorbate. We have calculated the orbital projected DOS of C-graphene for all the three configuration, shown in Fig. 4. For the T site configuration in Fig. 4(a), we can see that the DOS peak at $E_{\text{F}}$ in fact comes from the $p_{x}$ and $p_{y}$ orbitals of adsorbed C atom and its $p_{z}$ orbital is located at the energy of around $-4$ to $-2\ \text{eV}$. This detailed phenomenon is linked with the geometry structure of the T site configuration. Since in the T site configuration the adsorbate is directly above the C atom in graphene, therefore the $p_{z}$ orbital of adsorbate forms bond with the C atom below and then their energy is lowered. However, the $p_{x}$ and $p_{y}$ orbitals of adsorbed C atom cannot form bonds with any other atoms and become two dangling bonds. As a result, these two orbitals form the very localized bands in the system and the localized bands of adsorbate are the origin of the magnetic moment. Since C atom has two $p$ electrons and the $p$ orbital is almost totally spin-polarized, it will show a net magnetic moment about $2\ \mu_{B}$. This is consistent with our calculated results in Table I.

Of course, it is a little different for other configurations. In the C-graphene at the B site configuration, because of the structure distortion, one of the $p_{x}$ and $p_{y}$ orbitals of adsorbed C atom can possibly form bonds with the two nearest C atoms graphene. Only the other nonbonded $p_{x}$ or $p_{y}$ orbital dangles [$p_{y}$ in our case, as shown in Fig. 4(b)]. Therefore, the net magnetic moment of C-graphene at B site configuration is quite small.

In H site configuration, the interaction between the adsorbate and the graphene is weak, and therefore, the adsorbed C atom looks like an isolated C atom and the graphene is just like a pure graphene. Since the isolated C atom has $2\ \mu_B$ net magnetic moment, therefore the C-graphene in the H site configuration also has $2\ \mu_B$ net magnetic moment. However, we can also see from Fig. 4(c) that the three $p$ orbitals are not exact equal and there is a little splitting because of the existence of graphene, which can be thought as a crystal splitting. We will see that this kind of splitting is much larger for the large adsorbate atoms.

When the atomic number increases, the bond length between the adsorbate and the C atom in graphene increases, and the adsorption energy decreases. As a result, the adsorbed atom looks more like a isolated atom, i.e., all the three $p$ orbitals cannot form bonds with the graphene. However, on the other hand, when the $p$ orbitals changed from the $2p$ of C to $6p$ of Pb, the splitting of $p_z$ and the other two $p$ orbitals increases. We take the Pb-graphene as an example, which is shown in Fig. 5. From the three DOS, we can find that there is no any peak below the $E_F$, which reflect the weak interaction between Pb and graphene. The $p_z$ orbital has a relative high energy because of the existence of graphene and therefore it is almost empty. The two $p$ electrons of Pb occupy the $p_x$ and $p_y$ and contribute $2\ \mu_B$ magnetic moment in the Pb-graphene.

We also find that in fact all the three DOS in Fig. 5 are quite similar, which implies that for the large adsorbate, e.g., Pb, the adsorption site is no longer important. Both the adsorption energy and the electronic properties of such large atom adsorbed graphene are changed little by different adsorption site. However, for small atom adsorbed graphene, the adsorption site is important. In order to be more vivid, we present the spin-polarized charge density of C-graphene and Pb-graphene at different configuration in Fig. 6. For example, for the C-graphene at B site configuration (top-center panel in Fig. 6), the spin-polarized charge density looks like a $p_x$ or $p_y$ orbital, which is quite different with other two configurations. However, for the Pb-graphene, the spin-polarized charge density of all the three configuration (bottom three panels in Fig. 6) seem same.

## IV. CONCLUSION

We have calculated the adsorption energy, geometric, and electronic structure of the IVA group atoms adsorbed on graphene. The bridge site is always the stablest site for all the IVA group atoms. However, for Ge, Sn, and Pb, the diffusion barrier between the bridge and top site disappears, and the bridge and top sites are both stable.

The interaction between the adsorbate and graphene decreases as the atomic number increases. For the C adsorbed graphene, a strong covalent bonding between C and graphene is found (except for the hollow configuration), while for the Pb adsorbed graphene, no bond is found between them and the van der Waals forces dominate their interaction.

It is interesting to find that all the IVA group atoms adsorbed graphene shows an obvious net magnetic moment. Detailed analysis shows that the magnetic moment comes from the $p$ orbital of the adsorbate. More precisely, most of the magnetic moment comes from the localized in-plane $p$ orbitals (i.e., $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals) of the adsorbate.

We think the C and Si are perhaps useful in order to change the electronic properties of graphene by adsorption with a proper concentration, due to their relative large adsorption energy. And Ge, Sn, and Pb adsorption are maybe not stable and not useful due to their weak interaction with the graphene.
