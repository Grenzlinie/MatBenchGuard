# TELLURIUM THERMODYNAMICS IN AUSTENITIC IRON

G. GUPTA, D.G.C. ROBERTSON and M.E. SCHLESINGER

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Missouri-Rolla,
Rolla, Missouri, U.S.A. 65409-0340

(Received in revised form April, 2005)

Abstract — A two-zone furnace arrangement was used to determine the solubility of tellurium vapour in $\gamma$-iron foils at 1273 and 1548 K. Liquid tellurium held at a lower temperature generated Te (g) and $\mathrm{Te}_{2}$ (g) at a known partial pressure which was equilibrated with foils held at a higher temperature. Knowledge of the two temperatures allowed the calculation of the activity of tellurium with respect to the solid reference state. Analysis of the equilibrated foils allowed the calculation of the infinite dilution activity coefficient of tellurium which is expressed as a function of temperature.

Résumé — On a utilisé un arrangement à deux zones d'un four pour déterminer la solubilité de la vapeur de tellure dans des feuilles de fer $\gamma$ à 1273 et à 1548 K. Le tellure liquide, maintenu à une température plus basse, a engendré du Te (g) et du $\mathrm{Te}_{2}$ (g) à une pression partielle connue qui était mise en équilibre avec les feuilles maintenues à une température plus élevée. Le fait de connaître les deux températures nous a permis de calculer l'activité du tellure par rapport à l'état solide de référence. L'analyse des feuilles en équilibre a permis le calcul du coefficient d'activité de dilution infinie du tellure, lequel est exprimé en fonction de la température.

## INTRODUCTION

For some years, tellurium has been added to steel to promote machinability [1-6]. The use of tellurium reduces work place hygiene and environmental concerns associated with the use of lead for this purpose [3] and is actually more effective when added along with sulfur [2,5]. Tellurium is particularly attractive due to its specific gravity (6.24) which is closer to that of iron than that of other machinability additives. This reduces macrosegregation in tellurium treated steels.

Most of the previous experimental measurements of phase equilibria in the iron tellurium system have focussed on high tellurium compositions [7-10]. Attempted measurements of tellurium thermodynamics in pure iron or iron base alloys have been rare and contradictory. Figure 1 shows the currently assessed phase diagram [9]. The solubility limit of Te in $\alpha$-Fe, 1.5 at% (0.66 wt%) is derived from a single measurement at 1106 K by Ipser *et al.* [7]. Abrikosov *et al.* [10] reported much higher solid solubilities of Te in both $\alpha$-Fe and $\gamma$-Fe, but the reliability of their results is questionable. More recently, Hoffmann *et al.* have investigated tellurium solubility in $\mathrm{FeTe}_{0.9}$ saturated $\alpha$-Fe and $\gamma$-Fe [4]. Their measured solubilities in $\alpha$-Fe were 50 wppm at 973 K and 120 wppm at 1123 K; in $\gamma$-Fe, solubilities were 5 wppm at 1203 K and 22 wppm at 1273 K. Given the limited number of experimental results, none can be recommended with any confidence. The solubility of tellurium in molten iron or in iron base alloys has not been examined.

A three-year program is currently underway to better determine the thermodynamics of iron rich Fe-Te and Fe-C-Te solutions in both the liquid and the solid state. The initial stages of this research included the measurement of tellurium

## EXPERIMENTAL

Figure 2 illustrates the experimental apparatus used for equili- bration of molten tellurium and solid or liquid iron base alloys. The apparatus is similar to that previously used for the measurement of zinc solubility in iron and iron carbon alloys [11]. The key part is an alumina “reactor bomb” around which two furnaces are located. The lower furnace operates at a predetermined temperature below the boiling point of tellurium (1263 K), while the upper furnace operates at temperatures up to 1873 K. A ceramic plug in the bottom of the bomb serves as the bottom for a molten tellurium pool whose temperature is maintained by the lower furnace. The tellurium pool is created by charging the bomb with solid tellurium and repeatedly purging with argon to remove the air and then heating it under a vacuum. As the tellurium melts, it flows into the annulus between the ceramic plug and the reactor bomb. When the molten tellurium flows out of the lower furnace hot zone, it freezes (aided by a water cooling coil wrapped around the outside of the bomb). The freezing tellurium seals the tube, keeping the air out and the tellurium in. A sufficient charge of tellurium metal was added to maintain a pool with about a 2 cm depth after the freezing metal in the lower part of the annulus sealed the system.

Upon reaching the set temperature, the molten tellurium pool generated tellurium vapour:

$$\mathrm{Te}\ (l) = \mathrm{Te}\ (g) \tag{1}$$

$$\mathrm{Te}\ (l) = ^1/_2\ \mathrm{Te}_2\ (g) \tag{2}$$

![](./images/813168819378847746_8.jpg)

Fig. 2. Reactor bomb used for equilibrating molten tellurium with ferrous alloys.

The evacuation of the bomb prior to melting meant that Te (g) and $\mathrm{Te}_2$ (g) were the only vapour species present at significant levels inside the bomb, so the total pressure was equal to $p_{\mathrm{Te}(g)}$ + $p_{\mathrm{Te2}(g)}$. As the vapour rises in the bomb, the increasing temperature causes some of the $\mathrm{Te}_2$ vapour to decompose.

$$\mathrm{Te}_2\ (g) = 2\ \mathrm{Te}\ (g) \tag{3}$$

The set temperature in the top of the bomb sets the equilibrium constant $K_3$; the total pressure is the same as that set in the bottom of the bomb. As a result, knowing the temperatures in the bottom and top zones allows the calculation of $p_{\mathrm{Te}(g)}$ and $p_{\mathrm{Te2}(g)}$ in the top zone of the bomb. Knowing these values and the equilibrium constant for either Reaction 1 or 2 allows the calculation of the activity of tellurium in the top zone with respect to the liquid reference state.

$$K_1 = p_{\mathrm{Te}(g)}/a_{\mathrm{Te}(l)} \tag{4}$$

Analysis of the equilibrated foil or molten alloy for tellurium determines the mole fraction of tellurium. Dividing this into the activity determines the infinite dilution activity coefficient $\gamma^\circ_{\mathrm{Te}}$:

$$a_{\mathrm{Te}(l)}/X_{\mathrm{Te}} = \gamma^\circ_{\mathrm{Te}} \tag{5}$$

For equilibrations with solid iron, thin foils (1.25 $\mu$m; 99.95% Fe) were cut into sections of roughly 2.5×5.0 cm size and cleaned with kerosene, acetone and alcohol. The mass of each foil piece was about 1.0 g. The foils were then loosely rolled into a coil and placed in an alumina crucible. The tellurium charge consisted of about 125 g of technical grade material (99.5% Te) cast into small rods with a mass of roughly 35 g. These were placed on the support shield shown in Figure 2 and the assembly was inserted into the alumina tube that served as the reactor bomb. The thermocouple protection tube contained two thermocouples that controlled the temperature of the two zones in the bomb and also supported the crucible at the top containing the iron foil. Once the bomb assembly was completed, it was inserted through the two furnaces. Although an assembly similar to this had been used previously to measure zinc solubility in ferrous alloys [11], molten tellurium proved more difficult to work with. In particular, the tellurium solidified only reluctantly in the annulus between the ceramic plug and the bomb wall. As a result, the heating procedure involved holding at a temperature just above the melting point of tellurium (723 K) for a substantial time in order to encourage the formation of a solidified tellurium “seal” in the annulus. Adding a thin sheet of refractory paper to the side of the plug which partially filled the annulus, also proved helpful.

Once the seal was established, the tube was repeatedly flushed with argon and evacuated and the two zones were heated to predetermined temperatures. Figure 3 shows the tellurium analysis of foils held at 1373 K and equilibrated with molten tellurium held at 798 K for varying lengths of time. Although the scatter is appreciable, it was decided from

these results that a holding time of 24 hours would be sufficient to ensure equilibration. As a result, subsequent experiments were all conducted using this holding time. Upon completion of the experiments, the bomb apparatus was withdrawn from the furnaces and the top quenched with compressed air to stabilize the foil as quickly as possible.

As the results in Figure 3 show, equilibrium tellurium concentrations in the iron foils were low at this temperature which created an analytical problem. The analytical approach adopted was that used by Hoffmann *et al*. [4] using an electron microprobe analysis (EMPA) with wavelength dispersive spectrometry (WDS). This analysis was performed by the Electron Microprobe Laboratory at the University of Oklahoma using a Cameca SX50 instrument. The recovered foils were sectioned and mounted; a transept of readings (10-12) was taken across the cross section of each sample at three separate locations. The use of a 20 kV accelerating potential, 100 nA beam current and 20 $\mu$m diameter spot resulted in estimated beam excitation volumes of 1.5 to 2.0 $\mu$m diameter and depth. Even with proper calibration and maximized excitation volumes, the minimum 3-sigma detection limit was 134 ppm Te. As a result, the uncertainty level in the analytical results is larger than desired.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 4 shows the cross section (350$\times$) of iron foil at 1273 K equilibrated with tellurium vapour generated by molten tellurium held at 798 K. Inspection at higher magnification showed occasional small white precipitates with a composition similar to that of the intermetallic compound FeTe$_{0.9}$. However, no evidence of FeTe$_{0.9}$ was found at the surface and for the most part, the precipitates were small and far between. As a result, it is apparent that the precipitates were the result of decomposition of the $\gamma$-Fe solid solution during cooling of the sample and that a single phase alloy existed during equilibration.

Table I lists the analyzed Te contents (in wppm) of the equilibrated foil along one of the transepts. The spots used for analysis were roughly equidistant. The second to last analysis exhibits the impact of the small precipitates in the microstructure which explains the need for a large number of analyses to improve the overall accuracy of the results. The lack of a higher Te concentration in the spots at either edge of the sample reinforces the conclusions that the 24 hour equilibration time was sufficient and that no precipitate formed during equilibration.
