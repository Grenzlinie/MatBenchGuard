# BIOMASS GASIFICATION AS A MEANS FOR AVOIDING FOULING AND CORROSION DURING COMBUSTION

Milton Blander
Quest Research,1004 East 167th Place, South Holland, IL 60473-3114

## ABSTRACT

Gasification of biomass at $600$-$800^oC$ can produce an effluent gas for use in a combustor for power production after removal of the solid products of gasification by hot gas cleanup (e.g., aspen wood). This will avoid the fouling and/or corrosion often found in biomass combustion. Biomasses which form liquids below $800^oC$ (e.g., wheat straw) require the use of additives which raise the lowest temperatures for the presence of inorganic liquids to over $800^oC$. This alternative path for avoiding the fouling and corrosion found during combustion of many biomasses probably can be applied to a broad range of analogous materials.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The substitution of biomass for fossil fuels will help to minimize the release of carbon dioxide to the atmosphere and decrease the potential for global warming. Because problems of fouling and corrosion often arise during biomass combustion, it is important to determine the chemistries of combustion in order to solve these problems. We will illustrate a solution to a problem of fouling found in the combustion of aspen wood [1], where we have determined from a sophisticated thermodynamic computer calculation that a small amount of a fouling and potentially corrosive molten salt (carbonate-sulfate mixtures) is present at temperatures down to about $803^oC$. It was shown that the addition of sulfur (as $SO_2$ for example) raised the temperature below which there should be no liquid to $912^oC$ [2]. In this paper, we will describe another simple and practical solution and show that with the gasification of biomass before combustion (which is generally done at $600$-$800^oC$) one can avoid the formation of fouling and corrosive liquids and, in principle, the process should be capable of providing gaseous combustor fuels which present no significant problems. We will also discuss this alternative method for a very different type of biomass, wheat straw.

## 2. CALCULATIONS

Our calculations of the total equilibrium chemistry of the gasification of aspen wood was made using the FACT[3] computer system developed at the Ecole Polytechnique in Montreal. It uses the CHEMSAGE algorithm [4] for free energy minimization, has a database of close to 7000 solid, liquid and gaseous species and incorporates theoretical equations [5-8] which have

been shown to accurately predict the thermodynamic properties of possible solid and liquid multicomponent salt solutions from generally known properties of the binary subsystems. In the range of temperatures for gasification of biomass (600-800°C), the gaseous products are often not at total equilibrium. However, if a molten salt is calculated to form at equilibrium, it is likely to be close to equilibrium with the simple molecular and/or organic species which are small enough to be close to equilibrium. The disequilibrium in gasification probably involves the breakdown of the very complex polymeric organic structures in biomass which have only a very small effect on the inorganic chemistry governing the fouling and corrosive liquids which can form. Major deviations from equilibrium of the organic species have been observed. One observation is that the small fractions of compounds having C/H ratios higher than alkanes are overabundant and a second observation is that upon cooling, a composition of gasification products which should deposit soot, does not do so. This observation implies that there is a large nucleation barrier to the formation of solid carbon products (e.g., graphite, buckminsterfullerenes, diamonds etc.) so that carbon becomes supersaturated in the gas. The first observation may be a result of the kinetically hindered breakdown of the complex polymeric compounds in biomass and the second is related to the theoretically predicted difficulty of nucleating complex solid structures which tend to have large surface free energies. In addition, the supersaturation of carbon can increase the production and help to preserve the disequilibrium abundances of organic compounds with high C/H ratios. The supersaturation of C tends to drive reactions such as, e.g., $C + CH_4 = C_2H_4$ to the right or slows an analogous back reaction, which both enhance the abundance of molecules with high C/H ratios. In any case, the lack of sooting implies that the resultant gases will have a higher caloric value than one would have at full equilibrium since the carbon is metastably maintained as gaseous species with hypothetical chemical potentials of carbon higher than pure graphite. Our calculations provide a measure of the possible formation of fouling and corrosive molten salts and should help to guide the choice of gasification conditions.

These calculations focus on indirect gasification of aspen wood with added water at 3 atmospheres. We simplify this calculation by assuming an external heating of the products. One could also get similar heating by adding water and enough oxygen (or air) to the gasification input to reach the desired temperature. The addition of oxygen or air is desirable but does lead to some dilution of the energy content of the gaseous product. Aside from this dilution, our calculations obtained similar results at different pressures and different amounts of added gases (oxygen, air) so that our conclusions concerning the inorganic chemistry are valid for a wide range of compositions of the added materials. ## 3. RESULTS

Equilibrium gasification products were calculated for 100 g of wood at three different ratios of the weight of water to that of the wood, 0.5/1, 0.75/1 and 1/1 at temperatures 600, 700 and 800°C. Below 797°C there was no molten carbonate rich salt (or other liquid phase) present. Consequently, gasification below this temperature should not produce fouling or corrosive liquids. (It should be noted that at the 0.5/1 and 1/1 ratios the molten salt is present only at temperatures somewhat above 800°C and for the 0.75/1 ratios of water/wood the molten carbonate was present down to 797°C.) It is probably important to be well below 800°C to insure that temperature fluctuations do not lead to the production of molten salts. The most abundant gases are given below in Table 2 for 800, 700 and 600°C for the three water/wood weight ratios.

To understand the implications of these calculations we need to know the temperature at which carbon tends to form at equilibrium and the amount of carbon deposited. For the water/wood ratios of 0.5/1, 0.75/1 and 1/1 solid carbon precipitates form at 799, 709 and 629°C. The amounts (in moles) of carbon deposited at 800, 700 and 600°C are given in the last row of Table 2. The observation that solid carbon does not nucleate and condense means that the carbon remains in the gas and is supersaturated to a thermodynamic activity greater than 1. Most of the carbon is likely to be present largely as CO and CH₄ as well as in lesser amounts of other molecules (e.g., CO and CH₄). This kinetic blocking of sooting raises the caloric content of the product gas above equilibrium values. This rise is highest at the low water/wood ratios and lowest at the high water wood ratios.

It is remarkable that the different gas compositions at equilibrium and at any one temperature are somewhat similar, especially at 600°C. The mole fractions of all the molecules (except for the relatively inert nitrogen) are very close to each other so that the

caloric values of the gases at $600^oC$ are very much alike at equilibrium. This similarity stems from the presence of carbon at an activity of unity and water at the same partial pressures. These two species control the total compositions of the major gaseous constituents. The larger water/wood ratios do lead to many more moles of gas and a higher total caloric value. However, in the case of disequilibrium with carbon remaining in the gas, the caloric content per mole of gas will be higher at lower water/wood ratios because the supersaturated carbon has theoretical activities greater than unity and the caloric content will be above the equilibrium value.

The most energy will be obtained if the products of gasification are used as a fuel at the temperature at which it exits the gasifier. Because there are products of gasification which are present as solid powders which are likely to be carried through in the effluent gas, it is advisable to separate the solids from the gas, especially the carbonate solids which might form a liquid when the gas is combusted. This separation might be accomplished by cyclones and or filters which operate at fairly high temperatures $(600-800^oC)$. The resultant gas is likely to be adequate for combustion in a normal combustor. For use in turbines at about $1000^oC$, which are usually more efficient producers of electricity than methods using combustors, the removal of particulates should be more complete than is necessary for combustors. Of the total 100g (13 elemental moles) of aspen wood used, the amounts of solids are relatively small and, except for the carbonates, they are relatively benign. The solids vary little with variations in the water/wood weight ratios. The differences reflect small differences in the total chemistry. For example, at $800^oC$ at the 0.5/1 and 0.75/1 water/wood weight ratio, the $K_2Ca(CO_3)_2$ solid appears whereas at the ratio of 1/1 solid $K_2Ca_2(CO_3)_3$ appears because of the higher partial pressure of $CO_2$ (see Table 2) which reacts with the CaO and drives the reaction

$$\mathrm{CaO}+\mathrm{CO}_{2}+\mathrm{K}_{2} \mathrm{Ca}\left(\mathrm{CO}_{3}\right)_{2}=\mathrm{K}_{2} \mathrm{Ca}_{2}\left(\mathrm{CO}_{3}\right)_{3}$$

to the right. At $800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ when the weight ratio of water/wood is 0.75 / 1, the carbonate chemistry differs because some of the liquid potassium-calcium carbonate salt is present which crystallizes at about $797^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The amounts of solids are relatively small for biomass and it is likely that these compounds are relatively benign in a combustor but can lead to problems in a turbine which can undergo some erosion upon impact of the dust particles. Careful separation of the solids is probably necessary for use in a turbine.

We thus see that gasification of aspen wood can lead to a useful gaseous product below $797^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ with no fouling or corrosive liquids being present. Addition of a fixed amount of oxygen can be used to bring the final gas up to a chosen temperature where the kinetics of gasification are rapid enough so as to leave little char behind and the desirable products are volatile. The addition of oxygen to the gasification process is not likely to alter the inorganic chemistry but will lead to more $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ in the gas and to a smaller caloric value. There are several variables that might be used to optimize any particular system in the use of oxygen and water additions and our conclusions are likely to be valid for a wide range of possible variations.

The amounts, the 'stickiness' and the high viscosity of this liquid is a threat to combustors. The addition of alumina in some form has been shown to raise the temperature below which there is no liquid condensate. For example, a mixture of 80 elemental moles of mixed waste paper to 100 elemental moles of wheat straw (Table 4) leads to combustion products which do not form an inorganic liquid phase below $837^o$C. The product of the gasification of a well mixed combination of these two biomasses and subsequent hot gas cleanup could be used for combustion. Other materials such as powdered clay, or coal contain a lot of alumina and might be used similarly. Because wheat straw produces much more solid materials than aspen wood, it may be unlikely that the effluents of gasification could be adequately cleaned up for use in a turbine.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

Gasification of biomass at $600$-$800^o$C and the subsequent combustion of the effluent gaseous products is an alternate method for power production which helps to eliminate problems with fouling and corrosion. Because the temperatures for gasification are relatively low, the condensed products for aspen wood gasification are all solids; with well mixed additions of alumina rich materials, even wheat straw, one of the worst fouling and corrosive biomass fuels, might be combusted with no significant problems of fouling and corrosion after hot gas cleanup. It is probable that many other biomass materials could be utilized similarly, some with and some without additives to enhance their performance. The two biomasses discussed (aspen wood and wheat straw) represent two types which can be used to classify many biomass materials (no silicon with potassium and siliceous with potassium) which might behave well in gasification-combustion processes.
