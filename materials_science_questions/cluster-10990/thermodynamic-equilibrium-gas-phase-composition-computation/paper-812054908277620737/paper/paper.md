# Thermal Decomposition in the System Si-Y-Al-O-N

**DONALD R. MESSIER*** and EILEEN J. DEGUIRE*

Army Materials and Mechanics Research Center, Watertown, Massachusetts 02172

The limited transparency of high N (13.3 at.%) glasses in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N was found to be due to Si precipitates that resulted from thermal decomposition during melting. Thermodynamic analysis supported experimental findings that decomposition in N₂ at 200 kPa resulted from oxide-nitride reactions and not from simple dissociation of Si₃N₄. The Si content of the glass was reduced much more by substitution of AlN for Si₃N₄ in the glass batch and melting at low N₂ pressure (200 kPa) than it was by merely melting in N₂ at high pressure (1.6 MPa). The thermodynamic analysis suggested that decomposition could be minimized effectively by melting under a modest partial pressure of SiO(g), but this could not be verified experimentally.

## I. Introduction

n the first reported attempt to densify Si₃N₄ by pressureless sintering,¹ it was recognized that thermal decomposition retarded sintering. Subsequent workers²·³ showed that sintering under high N₂ pressure produced material that was near theoretical density. None of these papers, however, proposed specific thermal decomposition reactions or mechanisms.

Messier and Gazza,⁴ in an investigation of the thermal decomposition of Al₂O₃-Si₃N₄ mixtures, observed significant weight losses in material heated in N₂ at only 1450°C. In that paper, they listed oxide-nitride reactions that could account for decomposition in that system with or without the presence of Al. Mitomo et al.⁵ further considered thermal decomposition and its effect on sintering of materials in the system Si-Al-O-N and suggested the use of an overpressure of SiO(g) to suppress decomposition. They accomplished the latter by use of oxide-nitride mixtures as buffer powders. In a subsequent investigation, Ditchek et al.⁶ reported that the combination of a buffer powder and N₂ overpressure reduced thermal decomposition in the system Si-Al-O-N more effectively than did the use of either medium alone. Neither of these papers, however, listed specific high-temperature reactions that would account for the observed thermal decomposition behavior.

Several recent papers⁷⁻⁹ have considered decomposition reactions in Si₃N₄ systems in more detail. While Greskovich and Prochazka⁷ recognized the probable significance of oxynitride decomposition reactions, their paper focused on the importance of thermal decomposition of Si₃N₄ into its constituent elements as did a latter paper by Baik and Raj.⁹ Lange⁸ investigated thermal decomposition in the system Si-Y-O-N and concluded that weight loss during sintering resulted from the reaction between Si₃N₄ and SiO₂ to form SiO(g) and N₂.

The present study, while relevant to the behavior of oxynitride systems in general, was initiated specifically to investigate the preparation and properties of glasses in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N. Glass formation in that system and related ones, first reported by Jack and co-workers,¹⁰·¹¹ has been further examined by several investigators.¹²⁻¹⁶ Although it is known that a certain amount of thermal decomposition always occurs during glass melting,¹²⁻¹⁴,¹⁶ only cursory attempts have been made to consider decomposition in any detail.¹⁴·¹⁷ Furthermore, the commonly observed dark grey to black color of high N glasses¹¹·¹³·¹⁴·¹⁶ has not been explained.

While the literature cited thus far on various Si-M-O-N systems was invaluable in characterization of the thermal decomposition behavior of such systems, further work was clearly needed. Objectives of the present investigation were to provide additional decomposition data and to formulate a reasonably complete and consistent description of thermal decomposition behavior in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N.

## II. Experimental Procedures

### (1) Glass Preparation

The glass used in this investigation was composed of 13.3% Al, 13.3% Si, 13.3% Y, 46.7% O, and 13.3% N (in at.%). Batches for glass preparation consisted of either mixtures of Al₂O₃,⁎ Si₃N₄,† SiO₂,‡ and Y₂O₃§ or mixtures of AlN,¶ SiO₂,‡ and Y₂O₃.§ The batches were formulated by weighing the powders in the desired amounts and mixing them in polyethylene containers with Al₂O₃ balls. The resulting mixtures were then isostatically pressed into compacts for consolidation and for ease of handling. The compacts were subsequently broken into chunks and loaded into screw-top BN crucibles for melting. Batch weights were in the range from 10 to 50 g.

### (2) Glass Characterization
In addition to recording weight-loss data for each run, charac- terization data were obtained on specimens cut from the glass ingots with a diamond cutoff wheel. The principal characterization techniques that were used include chemical analysis, X-ray dif- fraction, reflected light optical microscopy, and scanning electron microscopy.

## III. Results and Discussion
### (1) Glass Preparation and Characterization
As reported previously, $^{15,16}$ the glasses that were prepared gave amorphous X-ray diffraction patterns. Examination by optical microscopy, however, revealed that they sometimes contained minor amounts of crystalline material (probably from incom- plete melting or devitrification during cooling) and that they always contained spherical, metallic-looking particles. Further- more, the number of metallic spheres increased with the $N$ content of the glass.
Table I lists the nominal starting composition and final analyzed compositions of glasses made with three different $N$ sources, i.e., standard-purity $Si_{3} ~N_{4},^{\dagger}$ high-purity $Si_{3} ~N_{4},^{\dagger}$ and AlN. $^{\top}$ Noteworthy in Table I is that the $O$ contents of the glasses generally decrease significantly during melting, while the $N$ contents increase slightly. Also apparent are tendencies for $Al$ and $Y$ to increase and $Si$ to decrease slightly during melting.
In Fig. 1 the macroscopic appearances of two glass specimens fabricated previously $^{16}$ (Figs. $1(a)$ and $1(b)$ ) are compared with that of a specimen from this study (Fig. $1(b^{\prime})$ ). The glass shown in Fig. 1(a) contained 6.3 at.\% $N$ , while the compositions of those shown in Figs. $1(b)$ and $1(b^{\prime})$ were the same (both contained13.3 at.\% $N$ ) but were prepared with $Si_{3} ~N_{4}$ and AlN, respectively.
From examination of a large number of Si-Y-Al-O-N glass specimens, it was concluded that the typical grey to black color(Fig. 1) of the glasses owed to the presence of the aforementioned metallic particles. Figure 2 shows a particularly large particle which was the strength-limiting defect in a bend specimen. As noted in Fig. 2, EDAX showed that the sphere consisted of $Si$ and Fe; as seen in Table I, Fe is a significant impurity in this glass. From the foregoing observations, it was concluded that the metallic particles were undesirable because (1) they reduce glass trans- parency, (2) they can be strength-limiting defects, and (3) they may act as unwanted nucleation sites in crystallization of the glasses to form glass-ceramics.
After recognition of the above problems, attempts were initiated to eliminate the $Si$ particles by changing processing conditions. Although it was suspected that the particles could have come from reactions between the melts and the BN crucibles, similar results were obtained melting in Mo crucibles. The latter finding as well as the fact that the glasses contained little, if any, B indicates that reaction between the melts and BN crucibles was negligible. At- tempts to minimize decomposition by changing heat treatment conditions, i.e., increasing heating and/or quenching rates, or melting for shorter times at higher temperatures, were similarly unsuccessful. These experiments did show, however, that weight loss (around $1 \%$ ) during melting was about the same whatever the heating conditions were. Since weight loss kinetics were not exam- ined in detail, uncertainties remain as to how much, if any, of the weight loss occurred prior to melting and as to how the rate of weight loss changed with time. It should be added, however, that thermal decomposition continued for as long as the glass was held at the melting temperature. In a further attempt to define the prob- lem, a N-free glass was melted under the same conditions used for the oxynitride glasses with a surprising result: a few metallic glob- ules appeared in the resulting glass.

The results of experiments done to modify processing by chang- ing batch components and by melting under high $N_{2}$ pressure are illustrated in Fig. 3. The white "dots" seen in the photomicro- graphs are Si-rich particles. The glasses in Figs. 3(A) through $3(C)$ were made with four components, $Al_{2} O_{3}, Si_{3} ~N_{4}, SiO_{2}$ , and $Y_{2} O_{3}$ , while the one in Fig. $3(D)$ was made from AlN, $SiO_{2}$ , and $Y_{2} O_{3}$ . Comparison of Figs. $3(A)$ and $3(B)$ shows that the use of high-purity $Si_{3} ~N_{4}$ in the latter had little effect in reducing the content of metallic particles. It is therefore unlikely that Fe impurities contribute significantly to their presence. It is evident from Fig. $3(C)$ that while reduced, the amount of metallic phase was by no means eliminated by melting in high-pressure $N_{2}$ . That figure also indicates another problem, i.e., crystalline material that probably resulted from devitrification under the slow cooling rate in the high-pressure furnace. As discussed below, the glass made from three components, Fig. $3(D)$ and Fig. $1(b^{\prime})$ , on the other hand, is lighter colored, more transparent, and contains much less metallic phase than any of the others.
The foregoing experiments and observations enabled a clear understanding to be formed of the thermal decomposition of Si-Y- Al-O-N glasses heated at high temperatures. That understanding formed the basis for the following thermodynamic analysis of thermal decomposition in the system.

### (2) Thermodynamic Analysis of Thermal Decomposition
This section summarizes the results of an analysis of high- temperature reactions in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N that is consistent with all observed behavior in the system. Simplifying assumptions that were made in the thermodynamic calculations and experi- mental observations that led to the list of chemical reactions de- scribing the behavior of the system are given in the following.
Thermodynamic data used in the calculations were obtained from the JANAF tables. $^{18}$ Because solution data are completely lacking for the system, the present simplified analysis assumes that the oxide and nitride phases are in their standard states at unit activity for the temperatures of interest. Because $Y_{2} O_{3}$ is stable compared to other oxides in the system and because none of the evidence suggests that YN is involved in the decomposition process, the analysis deals only with reactions in the system Al-Si- N-O. As indicated below, such reactions are sufficient to explain all of the observed decomposition phenomena.
Although all glass melting was done in a closed system, i.e., a vacuum furnace with a static $N_{2}$ atmosphere, some weight loss

present, $Si_{3}N_{4}$ is thermodynamically stable at the $N_{2}$ pressure

decomposition of $Si_{3}N_{4}$ is enhanced by Fe as follows:

$$\mathrm{Fe}(s)+\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{~N}_{4}(s)=\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{Fe}(l)+2 \mathrm{~N}_{2}(g)$$

whereby decomposition pressure is increased because the activity
of Si is reduced by solution with Fe. While such may have been the
case in experiments in which impure $Si_{3}N_{4}$ was used, decom-
position was not noticeably diminished by use of high-purity $Si_{3}N_{4}$
as a batch component.

In view of the foregoing considerations, the following list of
high-temperature reactions is offered as being sufficient to account
for all observed thermal decomposition behavior in the system
Si-Y-Al-O-N:

$$\mathrm{SiO}_{2}(s)=\mathrm{Si}(l)+\mathrm{O}_{2}(g) \tag{1}$$

$$\mathrm{SiO}_{2}(s)=\mathrm{SiO}(g)+1 / 2 \mathrm{O}_{2}(g) \tag{2}$$

$$\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{~N}_{4}(s)+\mathrm{SiO}_{2}(s)=2 \mathrm{SiO}(g)+2 \mathrm{Si}(l)+2 \mathrm{~N}_{2}(g) \tag{3}$$

$$\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{~N}_{4}(s)+\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}(s)=3 \mathrm{SiO}(g)+2 \mathrm{AlN}(s)+\mathrm{N}_{2}(g) \tag{4}$$

$$\mathrm{Si}_{3} \mathrm{~N}_{4}(s)+3 \mathrm{SiO}_{2}(s)=6 \mathrm{SiO}(g)+2 \mathrm{~N}_{2}(g) \tag{5}$$

$$2 \mathrm{AlN}(s)+\mathrm{SiO}_{2}(s)=\mathrm{SiO}(g)+\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}(g)+\mathrm{N}_{2}(g) \tag{6}$$

The temperature dependencies of the partial pressures of vapor
species from these reactions in $N_{2}$ at 101.3 kPa are shown in

Fig. 4. The partial pressures (around 10 kPa) of $SiO(g)$ from
reactions (3) to (5) at a typical glass-melting temperature (2000 K)
are clearly high enough to account for significant thermal decom-
position at that temperature.

It is apparent from Fig. 4 that the Si observed in the quenched
glasses comes from reaction (3) and not from the simple dis-
sociation of $Si_{3}N_{4}$ into the elements, a point overlooked in previ-
ous investigations.⁴⁷⁸ Note also that decomposition pressure at
2000 K is 2 orders of magnitude less when AlN rather than $Si_{3}N_{4}$
is used as the N source in the glass batch. Figure 4 further indicates
why some metallic phase was seen even in a N-free glass; the
oxygen partial pressure in the furnace must have been low enough
to allow thermal decomposition of $SiO_{2}$ via reaction (1).

Although the decomposition process in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N
is obviously complex, the decomposition reactions listed above are
qualitatively consistent with the chemical analyses in Table I.
Since all of the reactions produce $SiO(g)$, one would expect losses
of Si and O during melting, and the data in Table I show that such
is generally the case. Because of the O loss, and because melting
is done in $N_{2}$, the relative N contents of the glasses increase slightly
as compared to the initial composition. Similarly, the prefer-
ential loss of Si increases the relative amounts of Al and Y during
melting.

Since the use of high $N_{2}$ overpressures has been reported to
suppress thermal decomposition in the sintering of $Si_{3}N_{4},^{2,3,6,7}$ it
is of interest to estimate how effective increasing $N_{2}$ pressure is
in retarding decomposition in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N. Figure 5
shows decomposition partial pressures vs $N_{2}$ pressure (up to
10 MPa) for relevant chemical reactions considered in this paper.
It is apparent from that figure that, while increasing $N_{2}$ pressure
is effective in reducing decomposition from reactions (5) and (6),
it clearly has little effect on reaction (4). Reaction (3), which
accounts for the presence of Si in the glasses, is only moderately
suppressed by increasing $N_{2}$ pressure; it is therefore unsurprising

that, as illustrated by Fig. 3(C), increasing $N_2$ pressure to 1.6 MPa only moderately reduced the Si content of the glass.

It may be further concluded from the present analysis that use of an overpressure of $N_2$ alone is a relatively ineffective means of suppressing thermal decomposition in this system or in systems using $Y_2O_3$ and $Al_2O_3$ together as sintering aids. Preferred, al- though possibly impracticable, approaches would be to maintain an overpressure of $SiO(g)$ in the system⁵ or to use overpressures of both $N_2$ and $SiO(g).^6$

Cursory attempts made in this study to minimize the Si content of the glasses by using a $Si_3N_4/SiO_2$ buffer powder mixture to generate $SiO(g)$ were unsuccessful, possibly because of an unsuit- able experimental configuration, or because of unfavorable kinet- ics. On the other hand, Figs. 1 and 3 demonstrate that the use of AlN (rather than $Si_3N_4$) as a batch component was effective in reducing the Si content of the glass. A disadvantage of that ap- proach, however, is that high N compositions cannot be formulated with AlN alone as the N-containing batch component.

That thermal decomposition to form Si was reduced by using AlN as a batch component has another implication regarding glass- forming kinetics: if thermal decomposition occurred from a homo-geneous melt, the choice of starting materials ($Si_3N_4$ vs AlN) would be irrelevant; the final result would be the same. It is therefore possible that some of the observed effects may involve decomposition before melting. Resolution of that question, how- ever, would require a more detailed investigation of glass-melting kinetics than was done in this study.

### IV. Summary
In summary, the following may be concluded:
A. The limited transparency and grey to black color of Si-Y- Al-O-N glasses result from the presence of Si precipitates in the glasses.
B. Those precipitates cannot be eliminated in $Si_3N_4$-derived glasses by increasing $Si_3N_4$ purity, changing crucible materials, changing heat treatment, or melting under high $N_2$ pressure (1.6 MPa).
C. The Si precipitates and weight losses during melting result from oxide-nitride decomposition reactions and not from dis- sociation of $Si_3N_4$ into the elements.
D. As predicted by thermodynamic analysis, replacement of $Si_3N_4$ with AlN as the N source in the starting batch greatly reduces the Si content of the resulting glass.
E. Increasing $N_2$ pressure is a relatively ineffective means of suppressing thermal decomposition in the system Si-Y-Al-O-N at high temperatures.
F. In principle, thermal decomposition can be suppressed by maintaining a modest partial pressure of $SiO(g)$ in the melting atmosphere. Doing that, however, may be difficult experimentally.
