# High-temperature oxidation of sintered silicon carbide under pure $\mathbf{CO_2}$ at low pressure: active-passive transition

M. Balat *, R. Berjoan, G. Pichelin, D. Rochman

IMP-CNRS, Institut de science et de génie des Matériaux et Procédés, UPR 8521, BP 5, 66125 Font-Romeu Odeillo Cédex, France

Received 25 November 1997; accepted 4 February 1998

## Abstract
The study of the oxidation of SiC under $\text{CO}_2$ at high temperature and low pressure is achieved by means of theoretical and experimental determinations of the transition zone between the *passive* oxidation with formation of a protective silica layer and the *active* oxidation with major vaporization of SiO and etching of the SiC material. The theoretical determination is done using both models: a computer model ‘‘Solgasmix’’, applied for a closed system at equilibrium and the other, our analytical model, which takes into account the mass transfer at the solid/gas interface. The comparison is done for these two models and then with the experimental results. XPS analyses are performed on ‘‘passive’’ and ‘‘active’’ samples to confirm the position of the transition line. Moreover, SEM micrographs complete the diagnostics. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Oxidation; Active-to-passive transition; Carbon dioxide; High temperature; Silicon carbide; XPS

## 1. Introduction
The study of the oxidation of SiC under $\text{CO}_2$ at high temperature and low pressure is the first part of a work that includes the evaluation of oxidation resistance under molecular ($\text{CO}_2$) and partially disso- ciated (by microwave generation, $\text{CO/CO}_2$) atmo- spheres, and the determination of the recombination of carbon monoxide and atomic oxygen at the sur- face of ceramics for aerospace applications (Martian atmospheric entry of space vehicle). In this paper, we present the theoretical and experimental determi- nations of the transition zone between the passive oxidation with formation of a protective silica layer and the active oxidation with the major vaporization of SiO under pure carbon dioxide environment. The theoretical determination is done using both models: a computer model called Solgasmix, applied for a closed system at equilibrium, and our analytical model that takes into account the mass transfer at the solid/gas interface. A comparison is made between these two models and with the experimental results.

Works on the oxidation of silicon-based ceramics under pure $\text{CO}_2$ or $\text{CO/CO}_2$ mixtures, at high tem- perature, are scarce, especially for the determination of the transition between active and passive oxida- tion regimes.

Antill and Warburton [1] have determined only two points for the active-to-passive transition in the oxidation of $\beta$-SiC, at 1273 and 1473 K, the $\text{CO}_2$ partial pressure for the transition being: (i) $\leq 0.40$ Pa and between 1 and 20 Pa, for the experimental

Ervin [2] concludes that similar behaviours are obtained for oxidation of SiC in air, $CO_{2}$ or $O_{2}$ atmospheres, but that under carbon monoxide the result is different. There is no reaction between SiC and CO until 1523 K, for a total pressure of $10^{5} Pa$ CO, but at 1573 K and higher, the silicon carbide surface is blackened and etched. Moreover, Ervin shows that a preoxidized SiC is more resistant to the reaction with CO than a bare sample. In this case, the blackening of the surface at 1573 K occurs only in the pores of the silica layer.

Using a thermodynamical analysis in the Si–C–O system, at high temperature, Gulbransen and Jansson [3] explain Ervin’s results, and confirm that, under a CO atmosphere, no reaction is expected between SiC and CO at 1400 K. Below 1600 K, SiC reacts with CO to form solid carbon and $CO_{2}$. But at 1800 K, SiC reacts with CO to form solid carbon and volatile SiO and a little $CO_{2}$.

Butt et al. [4] give some results about the reaction between SiC (three types of SiC: B-doped, Al-doped and siliconized) and CO, from 1373 to 1573 K, at low pressure (133 Pa). The reactive gas is composed of $37.8\% N_{2}$, $41.1\% H_{2}$ and $21.1\%$ CO in volume. After reaction, the SiC material is covered with amorphous carbon soot, quickly removed in ultra- sonic water bath. The reaction is, in this case, more severe than in a nitrogen-based gas composed of $98.2\% N_{2}$, $1.2\% H_{2}$ and $0.6\%$ CO. At higher pres- sure (98.6 kPa), the reaction between SiC and the endothermic gas leads to the formation of carbon and silica needles or particles.

Only Narushima et al. have worked on the active- to-passive transition [5] after studies on active oxida- tion [6]. The active-to-passive transition is deter- mined on CVD $\beta$-SiC in a $CO-CO_{2}$ atmosphere at a total pressure of $10^{5}$ Pa and from 1823 to 1923 K. A theoretical approach is done, using the model of Wagner [7] and the transition is given by the relation $\ln P_{CO_{2}}/P_{CO}=f(1/T)$. The discrepancy between the theoretical calculation and the experimental results reach two orders of magnitude and is attributed to the decomposition of $SiO_{2}$ due to the high partial pressure of SiO.

Narushima et al. [5] have also observed a domain where the surface of the samples present some bub- bles, due to the diffusion of CO through the silica layer, which is slower than that of $CO_{2}$. In a previ- ous paper [6], Narushima et al. describe only active oxidation of CVD $\beta$-SiC under $CO/CO_{2}$ mixture. In this case, experiments are conducted from gas pres- sure ratio $P_{CO_{2}}/P_{CO}$ from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$, at a total pressure of $10^{5}$ Pa. They distinguish three cases: (i) when $P_{CO_{2}}/P_{CO}$, ratio of the partial pressure of $CO_{2}$ and CO far from the solid/gas interface (i.e., in the bulk), is less than a critical value corresponding to the maximum mass loss rate, then amorphous carbon is present on the SiC surface; this carbon is formed by the active oxidation of SiC (verification on alu- mina samples); (ii) when $P_{CO_{2}}/P_{CO}$ is closed to the critical value, active oxidation is observed leading to a SiC bare surface; finally, (iii) when $P_{CO_{2}}/P_{CO}$ is higher than the critical value, silica is formed on the surface with a mass loss also; the silica is present as discrete small particles on the SiC surface.

So, after this literature review, one can conclude that no study was already done on the active-to-pas- sive transition in the oxidation of SiC under pure $CO_{2}$ at high temperature and total low pressure.

## 2. Theoretical determination of the transition

### 2.1. Eriksson’s model: closed system

Eriksson’s model ‘‘Solgasmix’’ [8] determines, for given temperature and total pressure, the equilib- rium composition of complex chemical closed sys- tems. This code can take into account 10 different elements, 99 compounds and 20 different phases. The calculation is based on the free-enthalpy mini- mization method for constant temperature and pres- sure values with the following constraints: mass conservation and Gibbs phase rule.

Our system is composed of a gaseous phase: SiO, $CO_{2}$, CO, $O_{2}$, O and a solid phase: SiC and in some times the searched compound $SiO_{2}$. These are the main compounds we have to take into account. We have verified that there is no influence of other gaseous compounds such as $O_{3}$, $Si_{2}$, $Si_{3}$, C, $C_{2}$, SiC, $Si_{2}C$, $C_{2}Si$, …on the transition temperature and pressure. So, the chemical system has been simplified.

### 2.2. Dynamic model: open system

The proposed model takes into account the mass transfer at the solid/gas interface by coupling with the equilibrium thermodynamical equations. This model is one-dimensional, independent of the system geometry [10]. The main hypothesis is that the mass transfer is controlled by diffusion in the gaseous phase, so the thermodynamical equilibrium is established at the interface.

Before passivation by the silica layer, the solid-gas interface is composed of the solid silicon carbide layer, and the following gaseous species: SiO, CO₂, CO, O₂ and O (like in the preceding model). For each phase, the mass balance for each atomic compound is established, and the interface mass conservation is applied. The flux density $J_i$, of the compound $i$ at the interface, is expressed by:

$$
J_{i}=\left(-D_{i} / \delta_{i} R T\right)\left(P_{i}^{\infty}-P_{i}^{w}\right)+V\left(P_{i}^{w} / R T\right) \quad(1)
$$

with $D_i$ the mass transfer coefficient of the species $i$ in the carrying gas CO₂, $\delta_i$ the thickness of the boundary layer of concentration, $R$ the ideal gas constant, $T$ the temperature, $P_i^\infty$ and $P_i^w$ the partial pressures of the species $i$ in the bulk (outside the boundary concentration layer) and at the interface, respectively. The first term expresses a diffusive flux density as a linear function of the difference of concentrations of gas "$i$" in the boundary layer, the second is the convective flux density of the gas phase moving in the normal direction from the interface. The term $V$ is the mean molar gas velocity called barycentric gas velocity and is defined by:

$$
V=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} J_{i}}{\frac{1}{R T} \sum_{i=1}^{n} P_{i}^{w}} \quad(2)
$$

So, finally, Eq. (1) becomes:

$$
J_{i}=\left(-D_{i} / \delta_{i} R T\right)\left(P_{i}^{\infty}-P_{i}^{w}\right)+\left(J P_{i}^{w} / P\right) \quad(3)
$$

with $J=\sum_{i=1}^{n} J_{i}$ and $P$ the total pressure.

In the gaseous phase:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \left(J_{\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{G}}\right)=2 J_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}}^{\mathrm{G}}+J_{\mathrm{SiO}}^{\mathrm{G}}+J_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{G}}+2 J_{\mathrm{O}_{2}}^{\mathrm{G}}+J_{\mathrm{O}}^{G} \\
& \left(J_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\mathrm{G}}\right)=J_{\mathrm{SiO}}^{\mathrm{G}} \\
& \left(J_{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{G}}\right)=J_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}}^{\mathrm{G}}+J_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{G}}
\end{aligned} \quad(4)
$$

In the condensed phase:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \left(J_{\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{S}}\right)=0 \\
& \left(J_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\mathrm{S}}\right)=J_{\mathrm{SiC}}^{\mathrm{S}} \\
& \left(J_{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{S}}\right)=J_{\mathrm{SiC}}^{\mathrm{S}}
\end{aligned} \quad(5)
$$

Accounting for the principle of mass conservation at the interface $J_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{G}}=J_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{S}}$, the following system is obtained:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& J_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}}+2 J_{\mathrm{SiO}}+2 J_{\mathrm{O}_{2}}+J_{\mathrm{O}}=0 \\
& J_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}}+J_{\mathrm{CO}}=J_{\mathrm{SiO}}
\end{aligned} \quad(6)
$$

So, we obtain two linearly independent flux equations according to the Gibbs variance (open system) calculation (equal to two).

Now, the thermodynamic equilibria are used:– during active oxidation, silicon monoxide is vaporized with the reaction:

$$
\mathrm{SiC}_{(\mathrm{S})}+2 \mathrm{CO}_{2(\mathrm{~g})}=\mathrm{SiO}_{(\mathrm{g})}+3 \mathrm{CO}_{(\mathrm{g})} \quad K_{1}
$$

and during passive oxidation, a protective silica layer is formed with the reaction:

$$
\mathrm{SiC}_{(\mathrm{S})}+3 \mathrm{CO}_{2(\mathrm{~g})}=\mathrm{SiO}_{2(\mathrm{~S})}+4 \mathrm{CO}_{(\mathrm{g})} \quad K_{2}
$$

and also:

$$
\mathrm{CO}_{2}=\mathrm{CO}+\frac{1}{2} \mathrm{O}_{2} \quad K_{3}
$$

$$
\mathrm{O}_{2}=2 \mathrm{O} \quad K_{4}
$$

The solution of the system is obtained by the verification of equilibrium $K_{2}$.

Finally, the following expressions are obtained for the calculation of the $\text{CO}_{2}$ partial pressure in the bulk gas, far from the interface $P_{\text{CO}_{2}}^{\infty}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(1+K)\left[\frac{A}{1-B}+\frac{1}{1-B}\left(2 \frac{\alpha+\beta \gamma}{1-\beta \delta}+\frac{\gamma+\alpha \delta}{1-\beta \delta}\right)\right] \\
&+I+\frac{\gamma+\alpha \delta}{1-\beta \delta}=0
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&I+G+(H+K)\left[\frac{A}{1-B}+\frac{1}{1-B}\left(2 \frac{\alpha+\beta \gamma}{1-\beta \delta}\right.\right. \\
&\left.\left.+\frac{\gamma+\alpha \delta}{1-\beta \delta}\right)\right]-\frac{\alpha+\beta \gamma}{1-\beta \delta}=0
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

with the following variable change:
$$
\begin{gathered}
\alpha=\frac{C-C B+A D}{1-B-2 D}, \quad \beta=\frac{D}{1-B-2 D} \\
\gamma=\frac{E-B E+F A}{1-F-B}, \quad \delta=\frac{2 F}{1-F-B}
\end{gathered}
$$

$$
A=-\frac{D_{\mathrm{O}}}{\delta_{\mathrm{O}} R T}\left[P_{\mathrm{O}}^{\infty}-K_{4}^{1 / 2} K_{3} K_{2}^{-1 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{1 / 3}\right]
$$

$$
B=K_{4}^{1 / 2} K_{3} K_{2}^{-1 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{1 / 3} \frac{1}{P}
$$

$$
C=\frac{D_{\mathrm{SiO}}}{\delta_{\mathrm{SiO}} R T} K_{1}^{1 / 2} K_{2}^{-2 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{w}\right)^{-1 / 3}
$$

$$
D=K_{1}^{1 / 2} K_{2}^{-2 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{-1 / 3} \frac{1}{P}
$$

$$
E=-\frac{D_{\mathrm{O}}}{\delta_{O} R T}\left[P_{\mathrm{O}_{2}}^{\infty}-K_{3}^{2} K_{2}^{-2 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{2 / 3}\right]
$$

$$
F=K_{3}^{2} K_{2}^{-2 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{2 / 3} \frac{1}{P}
$$

$$
G=-\frac{D_{\mathrm{CO}}}{\delta_{\mathrm{CO}} R T}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\infty}-P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right), \quad H=\frac{P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}}{P}
$$

$$
I=-\frac{D_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}}}{\delta_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}} R T}\left[P_{\mathrm{CO}_{2}}^{\infty}-K_{2}^{-1 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{4 / 3}\right]
$$

$$
K=K_{2}^{-1 / 3}\left(P_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{w}}\right)^{4 / 3} \frac{1}{P}
$$

## 3. Experimental study

### 3.1. Experimental procedure

The samples used for our oxidation study under
$\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ are sintered $\alpha$-SiC (6H) containing about 1
wt% boron as major impurity, manufactured by
'Céramiques et Composites' (France). The sample
dimensions are 25 mm diameter and 3 mm high. The
$\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ gas is from Air Liquide (purity N45).

The experimental solar set-up called MESOX
(Moyen d'Essai Solaire d'OXydation), used for the
oxidation of the SiC samples has been described
elsewhere [11]. The total $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ pressure and the $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$
flow $(1.11 × 10^{-6} \mathrm{~m}^{3} \mathrm{~s}^{-1})$ are fixed before each
experiment.

Temperature changes are controlled by the grad-
ual opening of a shutter placed between the sample
and the concentrated solar radiation: it is near 8 K/s.
Oxidation experiments are conducted at constant
sample surface temperature during 600 s. After oxi-
dation, at constant temperature, samples cool at 20
K/s. The duration of the temperature plateau is
sufficient to indicate the oxidation regime (active or
passive). In fact, during the rise in temperature and
the cooling step at every $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ pressure, silica forma-
tion always occurs. However, for active oxidation
conditions, the surfaces of the samples are very
damaged, as will be described, and the silica forma-
tion that occurs during the cooling period does not
change the surface morphology resulting from the
etching of the sample.

After each oxidation experiment, three analytical
methods are used to detect the oxidation regime.
First, the samples are weighed, to detect possible
weight loss resulting from the oxidation treatment,
which can indicate an active oxidation regime. Sec-
ondly, the surfaces of the oxidized samples are ana-
lyzed by using an SIA 200 Cameca Riber XPS
spectrometer equipped with a Mac 2 double stage
electron analyser; the surfaces of the samples being
excited by an Al-K $\alpha$ X-ray beam $(h \nu=1486.6 \mathrm{eV})$.
The Si 2p, O 1s and C 1s photoelectron lines are
examined. Due to large electrostatic charging effects
occurring on the surfaces, particularly for the sam-
ples coated with thick silica layers, the C 1s line due
to contaminant atmospheric carbon generally re-
ported at 285 eV [12], or to the silicon carbide
reported elsewhere at 282.7 eV [13], were used to
normalize the binding energies of the photoelectron
peaks.

The third method used is scanning electron mi-
croscopy (SEM) for the observation of the surface
morphology.

### 3.2. Experimental results

More than 30 samples have been tested under
different $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ pressures and temperatures to estimate
the transition limit. Our results are reported in Fig. 1
and Table 3.

For the samples represented with $(\bigcirc)$, the surface
is damaged and they exhibit significant weight loss.
The oxidation result is the major vaporization of SiO
(active regime). However, some of these last samples
may present silica layers on the surfaces, but not
passive ones, the silica being present as small spheres
or a very porous layer.

The samples represented with $(\bigcirc)$ are covered
with a thin protective silica layer which was detected
by X-ray diffraction $(\alpha-\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$, cristobalite) for some
of them, or by XPS measurements for all. These
samples $(\bigcirc)$ are under passive oxidation.

For temperatures higher than 1920 K, and at
pressures upper than 6000 Pa, the surface of the
samples presents some bubbles, and so the silica
layer that may exist in some cases, is not protective.
So, we can define, such as Nickel [14], another
active zone, called by Nickel "active II", corre-
sponding to the fact that the oxidation product is a
gas that cannot be removed fast enough through the
formed layer by diffusion or small bubbles migra-
tion. So, large bubbles are formed leading to possible
spallation of the silica layer. This corrosion mode

The different SEM and XPS results obtained for the various pressure and temperature conditions investigated in this work can be summarized, for some illustrative examples reported on Figs. 2–4.

Fig. 2 shows the SEM micrographs of the surfaces of an SiC untreated reference sample, one under passive oxidation conditions resulting in the formation of a smooth surface constituted by a silica layer (sample H), one under active oxidation conditions (sample R) leading to an etched surface, and two active oxidations resulting in the formation of a silica layer, which present many holes or small spheres (samples O and Z). This silica cannot protect the SiC sample against oxidation.

Fig. 3 shows the Si 2p photoelectron peaks collected for the SiC reference sample, sample R (active oxidation) and sample H (passive oxidation). For the active conditions (sample R), the Si 2p line has been fitted into two Gaussian components at 100.5 and 102.5 eV, which are attributed respectively to SiC and SiO₂ [12]. The SiC component at 100.5 eV is very predominant, showing the active oxidation process. For sample H, the three components located at 100.4, 103.2 and 104.7 eV are attributed respectively to SiC, present under the SiO₂ layer and/or in small holes, a thin layer of SiO₂, and a thicker SiO₂ passive layer. The two different positions at 103.2 and 104.7 eV are attributed to different charging effects on the surface of the SiO₂ layer and on the surface of SiC.

For samples Z and O (Fig. 4), three or four Si 2p components are extracted by fitting the Si 2p lines. This result is due to the presence of various kinds of silica, as shown on the micrographs (Fig. 2): massive silica, small spheres and silica present on the SiC crystallites surface. The presence of these two or three types of silica is confirmed by the O 1s peak fittings, also shown on Fig. 4. In fact, the number of O 1s components is similar to the number of Si 2p components attributed to SiO₂ (102.6 and 105 eV for sample Z, and 103.5, 105.2, 107 eV for sample O). In the same way, the binding energy shifts between the O 1s components are similar to those between the Si 2p components. The XPS results obtained for samples O and Z are in good agreement with the SEM observations showing very complicated surface morphologies resulting in different charging effects on various silica layers.

We think that this result can be explained by considering the reduction of $SiO_{2}$ by the carbon monoxide resulting from the reaction $K_{2}$. This reaction can increase the CO partial pressure especially near the solid surface. As a consequence, the silica formed at the surface can be reduced by the carbon monoxide. In order to illustrate this behaviour, we have calculated, by using the Solgasmix model, the SiO partial pressures at the surface of $SiO_{2}$: $P_{SiO}^{w}$, for $SiO_{2}$ in pure $CO_{2}$ or in pure CO, and for two total pressures $P_{T}$, at two temperatures: 1600 and 2000 K. So, we can conclude that the CO resulting from the SiC oxidation can reduce the silica layer. On the contrary, the CO produced during the oxidation of SiC in air does not reduce the silica layer because oxygen is present. Finally, we think that the oxidation process of SiC under low $CO_{2}$ pressure is the result of two reactions: the first one is the oxidation of SiC by $CO_{2}$, and the second one is the reduction of the silica layer by the carbon monoxide produced by the first reaction.

For the lowest temperatures, the rate of the CO partial pressure at the solid/gas interface $(P_{CO}^{w})$ increase because the oxidation of SiC is too low to induce the destruction of the passivating silica layer. For the highest temperatures, the silica layer is not formed. However, there is an intermediate temperature range, depending on the partial pressure of $CO_{2}$, for which a silica layer can be formed and then reduced by a local increase of the CO pressure. This can explain the presence of highly damaged silica layers.

## 5. Conclusion
The active-to-passive transition pressure for the oxidation of SiC under $CO_{2}$, at high temperature, is calculated by using two theoretical models. The difference between the results provided by Solgasmix and our dynamical model is due to the mass transfer parameters that are included in the last model. The deviation between the pressure transition calculated, for given temperature, from the two models is about 20%.

Our experimental results show a typical behaviour for the samples oxidized under $CO_{2}$. In contrast to the oxidation in air [11], for which the oxidation results in the formation of an homogeneous silica layer for passive conditions, or in an etching of the surface for active conditions, the presence of heterogeneous surfaces with silicon carbide and silica is observed in many cases. In this case, the formed silica layer is reduced by the carbon monoxide present at the interface.
