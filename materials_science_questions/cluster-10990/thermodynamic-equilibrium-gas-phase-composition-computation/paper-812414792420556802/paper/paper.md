# VAPOR PRESSURES AND VAPOR COMPOSITIONS IN EQUILIBRIUM WITH HYPOSTOICHIOMETRIC URANIUM DIOXIDE AT HIGH TEMPERATURES

David W. GREEN and Leonard LEIBOWITZ

Chemical Engineering Division, Argonne National Laboratory, 9700 South Cass Avenue, Argonne, Illinois 60439, USA

Received 31 July 1981; accepted 16 November 1981

Thermodynamic functions of the gaseous species, thermodynamic functions of the condensed phase, and an oxygen-potential model have been combined to calculate the vapor pressures and vapor compositions in equilibrium with condensed-phase $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}$ for $1500 \leqslant T \leqslant 6000 \mathrm{~K}$ and $0 \leqslant x \leqslant 0.5$. A method for extending the oxygen-potential model of Blackburn to the liquid region has been derived and evaluated. Results of these calculations show that the oxygen-to-uranium ratio of the vapor is larger than that of the condensed phase with which it is in equilibrium for most of the ranges of $T$ and $x$ of interest. Near $6000 \mathrm{~K}$ the vapor is very oxygen-rich so that the composition of the condensed phase would be changed considerably by even a few percent vaporization. In general, the vapor in equilibrium with $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}$ is poorly approximated at $\mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})$, the species $\mathrm{U}$, $\mathrm{UO}, \mathrm{UO}_{3}, \mathrm{O}_{2}$, and $\mathrm{O}$ each have higher partial pressures than $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ for some conditions. The calculated total pressures at high temperatures are in good agreement with those recommended by the International Working Group on Fast Reactors (IWGFR).

## 1. Introduction

Reactor-safety analyses require thermophysical property data on reactor materials from low temperatures up to about $6000 \mathrm{~K}$. One of the most important properties, the vapor pressure of reactor fuel, has been the subject of considerable investigation for many years. A review by Ackermann, Rauh and Rand [1] of the data on solid $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ shows there is reasonably good agreement among many of the reported studies. Some experimental and theoretical investigations on liquid $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ have been reported; however, considerable uncertainty about its properties remains. Pressures in equilibrium with liquid $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ have been measured by transpiration [2] as well as by newly devised pulse techniques [3-5]. Calculations of properties using corresponding-states theory [6,7], significant-structures theory [8], and oxygen-potential theories [9] have also been performed. Most of these studies have failed to allow for the fact that the vapor in equilibrium with $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ does not consist simply of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ molecules, but is rather a complex mixture of various species, including the $\mathrm{O}, \mathrm{O}_{2}, \mathrm{U}, \mathrm{UO}, \mathrm{UO}_{2}$, and $\mathrm{UO}_{3}$ molecules.

## 2. Methods

The total pressure, $p$(total), in equilibrium with $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}$ condensed phase is the sum of the partial pressures of all vapor species. If we consider only the neutral species we may write:
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\text { total })= & p(\mathrm{U})+p(\mathrm{UO})+p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right) \\
& +p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{3}\right)+p(\mathrm{O})+p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

The oxygen-to-uranium molar ration in the gas phase, $R(\text{gas})$, is given by:
$$
\begin{aligned}
R(\text { gas })= & {\left[p(\mathrm{O})+2 p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)+p(\mathrm{UO})\right.} \\
& \left.+2 p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)+3 p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{3}\right)\right] \\
& \times\left[p(\mathrm{U})+p(\mathrm{UO})+p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)+p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{3}\right)\right]^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
$$

Thus, to determine both $p$(total) and $R$(gas) we need to determine the individual partial pressures as functions of both temperature and condensed-phase composition.

The set of partial pressures given by eq. (1) must satisfy equilibria among themselves and with the condensed phase. Of the several possible choices of independent equilibria, the following set is a convenient one:
$$\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightleftharpoons 2 \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{g}) \tag{3}$$

$$\frac{x}{2} \mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})+\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{c}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \tag{4}$$

$$\mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{UO}(\mathrm{g})+\frac{1}{2} \mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \tag{5}$$

$$\frac{1}{2} \mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})+\mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{UO}_{3}(\mathrm{~g}) \tag{6}$$

$$\mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{U}(\mathrm{g})+\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \tag{7}$$
where c represents the condensed phase.

If we know the oxygen pressure, $p(\mathrm{O}_{2})$, from the oxygen potential the required partial pressures may be obtained from eqs. (3)-(7), in sequence, using the following relationships:
$$\ln p(\mathrm{O})=\frac{1}{2} \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)-\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}(\mathrm{O}) / R T \tag{8}$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)= & \frac{x}{2} \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)+\left[\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{c}\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~g}\right)\right] / R T
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln p(\mathrm{UO})= & \ln p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2} \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)+\left[\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~g}\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}(\mathrm{UO}, \mathrm{g})\right] / R T
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{3}\right)= & \ln p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2} \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)+\left[\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~g}\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{3}, \mathrm{~g}\right)\right] / R T
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln p(\mathrm{U})= & \ln p\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)-\ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)+\left[\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~g}\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}(\mathrm{U}, \mathrm{g})\right] / R T.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
$$

These equations define not only the method that is used, but the data requirements as well.

\begin{align*}
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}(\text{UO}_{2},c)&=\Delta H_{f}^{\circ}(\text{UO}_{2},\alpha,T_{c})-T\left[\text{fef}'(\text{UO}_{2},c)\right.\\
&\quad\left.-\text{fef}'(\text{U, ref})-\text{fef}'(\text{O}_{2},\text{g})\right],\tag{13}
\end{align*}
$$
where $\text{fef}'=-\left[G^{\circ}-H^{\circ}(T_{c})\right]/T$. The $\Delta H_{f}^{\circ}(\text{UO}_{2},\alpha,T_{c})$ $=-1084.9\ \text{kJ/mol}$ is from Rand et al. [16] and has been confirmed by Johnson and Steele [17]. Values for $\text{fef}'(\text{U, ref})$ [14] and $\text{fef}'(\text{O}_{2},\text{g})$ [13] were taken from standard tabulations.

The free energy of formation of $\text{UO}_{2-x}$ is related to the partial molal free energies of $\text{U(ref)}$ and $\text{O}_{2}(\text{g})$, the activity of $\text{U}$, and the pressure of $\text{O}_{2}$ by:
$$
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}(\text{UO}_{2-x},c)=\Delta\overline{G}^{\circ}(\text{U, ref})+\left(1-\frac{x}{2}\right)\Delta\overline{G}^{\circ}(\text{O}_{2},\text{g}),\tag{14}
$$
where:
$$
\Delta\overline{G}^{\circ}(\text{U, ref})=RT\ln a(\text{U})\tag{15}
$$
$$
\Delta\overline{G}^{\circ}(\text{O}_{2},\text{g})=RT\ln p(\text{O}_{2}).\tag{16}
$$

The Gibbs-Duhem equation gives (for $n$ moles):
$$
n(\text{O}_{2})\ \text{d}\Delta\overline{G}^{\circ}(\text{O}_{2},\text{g})+n(\text{U})\ \text{d}\Delta\overline{G}^{\circ}(\text{U, ref})=0,\quad(17)
$$
so that the variation of $\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}(\text{UO}_{2-x},c)$ with $x$ from eq.

(14) becomes:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x} \Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{c}\right)=\frac{-\Delta \bar{G}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}, \mathrm{~g}\right)}{2}. \tag{18}
$$

Eq. (18) may be integrated from $x=0$ (i.e., $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$) to $x=x^{\prime}$ (hypostoichiometric $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$) to give:
$$
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{c}\right)-\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{c}\right)=\frac{R T}{2} \int_{0}^{x^{\prime}} \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right) \mathrm{d} x. \tag{19}
$$

If phase boundaries must be crossed to integrate eq. (18) or if two phases exist in equilibrium, we must modify eq. (19). The $\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{c})$ values at each temperature, $T$, in each of the regions defined by fig. 2, are given by:
$$
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{~s}\right)=\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~s}\right)-\Delta(0, x) \tag{20}
$$
for Regions I and III,
$$
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-z}, \mathrm{~s}\right)=\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~s}\right)-\Delta(0, z) \tag{21}
$$
for Region II with $z<x$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-z}, \mathrm{~s}\right) &=\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-y}, \mathrm{l}\right)+\Phi \\
&=\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~s}\right)-\Delta(0, z)
\end{aligned} \tag{22}
$$
for Region IV with $z<x<y$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{l}\right) &=\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~s}\right)-\Delta(0, z) \\
&-\Delta(y, x)-\Phi
\end{aligned} \tag{23}
$$
for Region V with $z<y<x$, and
$$
\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}, \mathrm{l}\right)=\Delta G_{f}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{l}\right)-\Delta(0, x) \tag{24}
$$
for Region VI, where
$$
\Delta(a, b)-\frac{R T}{2} \int_{a}^{b} \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right) \mathrm{d} x \tag{25}
$$
and
$$
\Phi=\left(\frac{y-z}{2}\right) R T \ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right) \tag{26}
$$
and the overall composition of the system is $\mathrm{O} / \mathrm{U}=2$ $-x$ in all cases.

Thus, in all cases we must know $p(\mathrm{O}_{2})$ as a function of $x$ and $T$ for all regions of interest. The oxygen-potential models required to do this are considered after the phase-diagram boundaries shown in fig. 2 are defined

### 3.3. The phase boundaries

Our interest in the phase diagram stems almost entirely from its use in the evaluation of the adjustable parameters in the oxygen-potential model for the liquid, which is discussed later in this paper. The use of a phase diagram that is based on experimental data results in model parameters that are consistent with these data. A secondary interest in the phase diagram comes from the need to define the condensed phase that is in equilibrium with the vapor because the calculations are done in slightly different ways for different regions of the phase diagram.

We are particularly interested in the vapor pressures and vapor compositions at high temperatures for $0.00 < x < 0.10$ We seek an oxygen potential for the solid that is consistent with available experimental data. We seek an oxygen-potential model for the liquid that has few adjustable parameters and a sound physical basis so that it may be used up to 6000 K. Our calculations have shown that the detailed nature of the solid-liquid equilibrium portion of the phase diagram is of relatively minor importance to the characteristics of the equilibrium vapor (the partial pressures).

The phase diagram that we have used is shown in fig. 2. The regions of this figure are:
Region I: $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{s})$ for $T \leqslant 2700 \mathrm{~K}$
Region II: $\mathrm{UO}_{2-z}(\mathrm{s})+\mathrm{U}(\mathrm{l})$
Region III: $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{s})$ for $2700 \leqslant T \leqslant 3120 \mathrm{~K}$
Region IV: $\mathrm{UO}_{2-y}(\mathrm{l})+\mathrm{UO}_{2-z}(\mathrm{s})$

Region V: $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{l})$ for $T<3120 \mathrm{~K}$
Region VI: $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{l})$ for $T>3120 \mathrm{~K}$.

In every case, the overall composition of the system is defined by $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}$ [i.e., the oxygen-to-uranium ratio is $(2-x)$ ]; the subscripts $y$ and $z$ are not equal to $x$.

The solidus, the liquidus, and the oxygen-potential functions for both the solid and liquid must be consistent among themselves, irrespective of their origin (experimental or theoretical). It must be true for all values of $T$ within the range for which a solid-liquid equilibrium is possible that a solid of composition given by $x_{\mathrm{s}}$ (the solidus) is in equilibrium with both a liquid of composition given by $x_{1}$ (the liquidus) and $\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})$ at a pressure given by the oxygen potential. If we use an oxygen-potential model with a particular functional form and if we accept a particular functional form for the solidus, the functional form of the liquidus is no longer independent.

The phase diagram for the U/O system has been evaluated recently by Rand et al. [16] and we have accepted its major features in constructing fig. 2. There is experimental support for much of this diagram, however there are uncertainties, particularly for the high temperature boundaries. It is not possible, however, to accept both the solidus and liquidus of Rand et al. [16] unless an oxygen-potential model is available with the $x$ and $T$ dependence that is required by the functional forms of the solidus and liquidus

In fig. 2, we have chosen linear approximations to the solidus of Rand et al. [16] and to the boundary between regions I and II for $T>2500 \mathrm{~K}$ in order to emphasize the uncertainties of these lines. Any other approximation, such as finding an analytical functions for these lines, does not affect the conclusions we have reached. The other characteristics of this diagram are:

1. The lower phase boundary for $1500 \leqslant T \leqslant 2500 \mathrm{~K}$ is that of Winslow [18];

2. The monotectic temperature is $2700 \mathrm{~K}$; the values of the $\mathrm{O} / \mathrm{U}$ ratio are $1.50(x=0.50)$ and $1.67(x=033)$, to the nearest 0.01 , at the phase-boundary intersections shown in fig. 2;

3. The normal melting temperature of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}(x=0)$ is $3120 \mathrm{~K}$;

4. The liquidus is defined by the solidus and by the requirement that for each temperature the oxygen pressures obtained from the oxygen-potential models for the solid and liquid at the solidus and liquidus, respectively, must be equal.

We have chosen an oxygen-potential model, which is discussed in the next section, for the solid (regions I and III in fig. 2) because it does satisfactorily account for the $x$ and $T$ dependence of the known data. We have extended this model to the liquid, with parameters to be evaluated, because there is a physical basis for the model and because our trial calculations have shown it to yield values for $p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)$ that are relatively insensitive to the values of the parameters and to the details of the phase diagram. We have made several alternative choices for the solidus and liquidus for the purposes of testing sensitivity. The results of these trial calculations have led us to some conclusions that will be discussed in the next section

### 3.4. Oxygen potentials for solid $\mathrm{UO}_{2-}$
An oxygen-potential model for $\mathrm{UO}_{2 \pm}(\mathrm{s})$ has been developed by Blackburn [19], and the parameters in this model have been evaluated and updated to include the most recent data [20,21]. The most recent evaluation is consistent with the lower-phase boundary of Winslow [18] and the $\Delta G_{\mathrm{f}}^{\circ}$ values represented by the equation in table 1. This model can be used to calculate $p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)$ values as functions of $T$ and $x$ in regions I and III of fig. 2. These values of $p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)$ obtained from the model are similar to those obtained from previous evaluations of the model parameters [19,20], and the comparisons with experimental data are similar to those discussed previously [18]. In general, the $p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)$ values are consistent with the experimental data.

We have used the model developed by Blackburn with the most recent values of the model parameters [21] for regions I and III of the phase diagram. We shall discuss briefly some of the assumptions of this model and compare the parameter values to expected values so that we can provide some physical basis for extension of the model to the other regions of the phase diagram shown in fig. 2.

For $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{~s})$, i.e., regions I and III of fig 2, the total uranium balance requires:
$$
\left(\mathrm{U}^{6+}\right)+\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)+\left(\mathrm{U}^{2+}\right)=1 \tag{27}
$$
and the charge balance requires:
$$
3\left(\mathrm{U}^{6+}\right)+2\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)+\left(\mathrm{U}^{2+}\right)=2-x. \tag{28}
$$

Blackburn considered the following two equilibria:
$$
2 \mathrm{U}^{4+} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{U}^{2+}+\mathrm{U}^{6+} \tag{29}
$$
$$
2 \mathrm{U}^{2+}+\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightleftharpoons 2 \mathrm{U}^{4+}+2 \mathrm{O}^{2-}, \tag{30}
$$
with respective equlibrium constants:
$$
K_{1}=\frac{\left(\mathrm{U}^{6+}\right)\left(\mathrm{U}^{2+}\right)}{\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)^{2}} \tag{31}
$$
$$
K_{2}=\frac{\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)^{2}\left(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\right)^{2}}{\left(\mathrm{U}^{2+}\right)^{2} p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)}, \tag{32}
$$

that have temperature dependences given by:
$$\ln K_{1}=A_{1}+B_{1} / T\qquad(33)$$

$$\ln K_{2}=A_{2}+B_{2} / T.\qquad(34)$$

Blackburn has derived [21] values for the four coefficients in eqs. (33) and (34) based on the most recent data.

Some understanding of the physical basis of these parameter values can be obtained by recognizing that the form of eqs. (33) and (34) follows from $\Delta G^{\circ}=\Delta H^{\circ}$ $-T \Delta S^{\circ}=-R T \ln K$. Thus, we may associate $A_{1}$ with $\Delta S^{\circ} / R$ for reaction (29) and $A_{2}$ with $\Delta S^{\circ} / R$ for reaction (30). Furthermore, we may reasonably expect that $A_{1}$ is small because of the nature of reaction (29) and we may also expect $A_{2}$ to be relatively large and negative at 3000 K because reaction (30) involves the loss of one mole of gas. These expectations are in agreement with numerical estimates [10] of $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$ based on known data for other systems.

To solve for $p(O_{2})$ at a particular $T$ and $x$, using Blackburn's constants in table 2, we combine eqs. (27) and (28) to get:
$$\left(\mathrm{U}^{2+}\right)=\left[1+x-\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)\right] / 2\qquad(35)$$

$$\left(\mathrm{U}^{6+}\right)=\left[1-x-\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)\right] / 2.\qquad(36)$$

We may substitute eqs. (35) and (36) into eq. (31) and solve for $(U^{4+})$ in terms of $x$ and $K_{1}$
$$\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)=\frac{-1+\left[1-\left(x^{2}-1\right)\left(4 K_{1}-1\right)\right]^{1 / 2}}{4 K_{1}-1}\qquad(37)$$

Because $K_{1}$ is known from eq. (33) and $x$ is known, we may determine both $(U^{4+})$ from eq. (37) and $(U^{2+})$ from eq. (35). These values can be substituted into eqs.
(32) and (34) to solve for $p(O_{2})$:
$$\ln p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)=2 \ln \frac{\left(\mathrm{U}^{4+}\right)(2-x)}{\left(\mathrm{U}^{2+}\right)}-A_{2}-B_{2} / T,\qquad(38)$$
where we have used $(O^{2-})=2-x$.

### 3.5. Oxygen potentials for liquid $UO_{2-x}$

We shall assume that the basic ideas of the oxygen-potential model of Blackburn, as discussed in the previous section for the solid, are valid for a $UO_{2-x}(l)$ condensed phase. Thus, we assume there are equilibria among the $U^{2+},U^{4+},U^{6+}$, and $O^{2-}$ ions in the liquid and $O_{2}(g)$, so that there are analogs to eqs. (27)-(34) that are valid for a liquid condensed phase. In particular, for the equilibria with $UO_{2-x}(l)$ the analogs to eqs. (33) and (34) are:
$$\ln K_{11}=A_{11}+B_{11} / T\qquad(39)$$

$$\ln K_{21}=A_{21}+B_{21} / T.\qquad(40)$$

Although it might be reasonable to evaluate the coefficients in eqs. (39) and (40) by assuming the entropy changes for eqs. (29) and (30) are equal to their analogs for a liquid $UO_{2-x}$ (and, thus, $A_{1}=A_{11}$ and $A_{2}=A_{21}$), we can obtain a better approximation for these parameter values by examining the entropy changes for other oxide systems.

We define $\Delta_{2}$ as the entropy difference $\Delta S_{1}^{\circ}-\Delta S_{s}^{\circ}$ where the subscripts l and s refer to the liquid and solid $UO_{2-x}$ phases and the entropy changes are for reaction (30), which may be written as
$$2 \mathrm{UO}(\mathrm{s})+\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightleftharpoons 2 \mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~s}),$$
and its liquid analog. Then
$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta_{2}= & \Delta S_{1}^{\circ}-\Delta S_{\mathrm{s}}^{\circ}=R\left[A_{21}-A_{2}\right] \\
= & 2\left[S^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, 1\right)-S^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}, \mathrm{~s}\right)\right]-2\left[S^{\circ}(\mathrm{UO}, 1)\right. \\
& \left.-S^{\circ}(\mathrm{UO}, \mathrm{s})\right] \\
= & 2\left[\Delta S_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{UO}_{2}\right)-\Delta S_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(\mathrm{UO})\right],
\end{aligned}\qquad(41)$$
where $\Delta S_{m}^{\circ}$ is the entropy of melting. An estimate of $\Delta_{2}$ can be obtained from the data for similar systems [10] for which the average value of $R_{m}[R_{m} \equiv \Delta S_{m}(T_{m})/(R$ $\cdot n)$ , where $n$ is the number of atoms per molecule, $R$ is the gas constant, and $T_{m}$ is the melting temperature in K], is $R_{m}=1.4$. The data for $UO_{2}$ [10] give $R_{m}=1.0$. Thus, a reasonable approximation for $A_{21}$ is:
$$\begin{aligned}
A_{21} & =A_{2}+\Delta_{2} / R=A_{2}+2 R_{\mathrm{m}} \\
& =A_{2}+2.8.
\end{aligned}\qquad(42)$$

By a completely analogous procedure, we may use

our average $R_{\mathrm{m}}$ to better estimate $A_{11}$. If we let $\Delta S_{\mathrm{s}}^{\circ}$ be for reaction (29), which may be written as
$$2 \mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{~s}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{UO}(\mathrm{s})+\mathrm{UO}_{3}(\mathrm{~s}),$$
and $\Delta S_{1}^{\circ}$ be for its liquid analog, then.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_{1} & =\Delta S_{1}^{\circ}-\Delta S_{\mathrm{s}}^{\circ} \\
& =2 R_{\mathrm{m}}+4 R_{\mathrm{m}}-2\left(3 R_{\mathrm{m}}\right) \\
& =0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{43}
$$

Now $B_{11}$ and $B_{21}$, can be derived from the known equality of $p\left(\mathrm{O}_{2}\right)$, as calculated by eq. (38) and the analogous equation for the liquid: (1) at 2700 K for $\mathrm{UO}_{150}(\mathrm{l})$ and $\mathrm{UO}_{167}(\mathrm{~s})$; and (2) at 3120 K for$\mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{l})$ and $\mathrm{UO}_{2}(\mathrm{s})$. The complete set of coefficients for this approximation is given in table 2.

This method of evaluating the model coefficients for $\mathrm{UO}_{2-x}(\mathrm{l})$ obtains the four unknown coefficients $(A_{11}$, $B_{11}, A_{21}$ and $B_{21}$) from data at two temperatures (2700 and 3120 K) and from a fixed relationship between the solid and liquid entropy changes (i.e, by fixing the relationship between $A_{1}$ and $A_{11}$ and between $A_{2}$ and $A_{21}$). Some alternatives were tried to test the sensitivity of the results (vapor pressures and vapor compositions) to both the selection of the phase boundary lines and to the methods of the model parameters.

One alternative, which was tested extensively, was to use points from both the solids and liquidus of Rand et al. [16] to evaluate all four parameters in the oxygen- potential model for the liquid. This alternative failed because the functional form for the liquidus that is required by the functional form of the oxygen-potential model and that of the solidus is inconsistent with the liquidus that is given by Rand et al. [16], irrespective of the values of the model parameters. There is no reason to believe that this liquidus, which is based largely on the data of Latta and Fryxell [22], is sufficiently reliable near 3120 K to cause revision of the oxygen-potential model. The linear solidus we have used in fig. 2 is not experimentally distinguishable from the solidus chosen by Rand et al. [16], but the liquidus in fig. 2 is different in both its functional form and in the values except at $T=2700$ and $T=3120$ K. Thus, the parameters that we have evaluated for the oxygen-potential model from the phase diagram in fig. 2 are certainly consistent with the solidus proposed by Rand et al. [16]. within the uncer- tainties of the experimental data and may also be con- sistent with the liquidus, depending on one's judgment of the reliability of the data.

Another alternative we tested was to use the parame- ters of the solid model for the full range of $T$ and $x$. This alternative in effect ignores the liquid portions of the phase diagram. For $\mathrm{UO}_{200}$ and $\mathrm{UO}_{196}$ the total pressures calculated from this alternative are $74 \%$ and $60 \%$ higher, respectively at 5000 K than those calculated from the parameter values in table 2. The vapor is also calculated to be more oxygen-rich with these alternative parameter values. The differences in total pressure of less than a factor of two are not large compared to some of those discussed later in this manuscript. There is good reason to expect that the parameters we have evaluated by taking account of the solid-liquid equilibrium should be superior to this alternative, which ignores the phase diagram. We have concluded from these test calcula- tions that the calculational method used in this work is not overly sensitive to either the details of the phase diagram or the values of the oxygen-potential parame- ters.

Several other alternatives, including the oxygen- potential models discussed in the next section, have been considered by determining the effects at high temperatures on both the total pressure and the vapor composition. We have concluded our calculational ap- proach is not overly sensitive to uncertain parameters and does have a physical basis that is reasonable. Im- provements in experimental data, such as the phase boundaries, will alter the numerical results somewhat, but not to the extent that major changes in the conclu- sions would be expected

## 4. Results

The vapor composition in equilibrium with a $UO_{1.96}$ condensed phase, which is a typical composition for a reactor fuel, is shown in fig. 3. Because the principal vapor species for $5000 \leqslant T \leqslant 6000$ K are oxygen-rich ($O$, $O_2$, $UO_3$), the oxygen-to-uranium ratio of the vapor will be higher than 1.96.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The results obtained from application of the approach outlined in fig. 1 contain information about the total pressure of the equilibrium vapor (usually available from other methods) and the composition of the vapor (not always available from alternative methods).

The calculations show that the total pressure in equilibrium with a condensed-phase $UO_{2-x}$ is relatively insensitive to the value of $x$ in the range $0 \leqslant x \leqslant$ 0.1. This conclusion is important because the process of vaporization itself changes the oxygen-to-uranium ratio of the condensed phase. Thus, the extent of vaporization of a sample (or its reactions with other substances present in the system) may lead to changes in the oxygen-to-uranium ratio of the condensed phase. We have found that there is not a correspondingly large effect on the total pressure.

The thermophysical properties of the vapor that depend on the vapor composition are sensitive to the value of $x$. Partial pressures of the vapor species, particularly at high temperatures, do depend on the extent of vaporization as well as on the value of $x$. The results shown in fig. 4 for a $UO_{1.96}$ condensed phase may be generalized to other values of $x$. There will, of course, be quantitative differences, but qualitatively for all $x$, the vapor will consist of several species whose relative importance changes with both $x$ and $T$. Thermophysical properties of the vapor in equilibrium with $UO_{2-x}$ are, in general, poorly approximated as those of $UO_{2}(g)$. Furthermore, no single mixture of gases is an adequate approximation because the composition of the mixture is sensitive to both $x$ and $T$. Account should be taken of the complex nature of the vapor in most calculations of vapor properties.

The total pressure in equilibrium with a $UO_{2}$ condensed phase has been the subject of numerous investigations and calculations. The IWGFR equation [27] has rather large error limits that reflect, in part, the difficulty of obtaining reliable experimental information at temperatures above 3000 K. The agreement of our calculated total pressure with the IWGFR equation, as shown in fig. 6, is excellent.

The vapor in equilibrium with a $UO_{2-x}$ condensed phase consists of many species of which $O, O_{2}, U, UO$, $UO_{2}, UO_{3}$, and their singly charged positive ions have been considered. It has been implicitly assumed that all other conceivable vapor species (e.g., $U_{2}O, U_{2}, U-O-O$, $(UO)_{2}$) have sufficiently low partial pressures that their presence does not significantly affect the total pressure or the $O/U$ ratio of the vapor. It is expected that this assumption is valid or at least that the uncertainty introduced by this assumption is smaller than the uncertainties from other sources. This expectation is based, in part, on the absence of data suggesting that there are other major vapor species below 3000 K

Our calculations are based on the need to extrapo- late reliably from 3000 K, where much of the available data begin to become seriously deficient, to 6000 K, where available data do not exist. Although a 3000-K extrapolation is hardly small, in many ways this factor of two in $T$ is no more difficult or dangerous than extrapolating from room temperature to 600 K. Many of the thermodynamic properties of interest are depen- dent primarily on the ratio of a characteristic energy to the value of $kT$ or the exponential of this ratio. Many of the experimental data available for the U-O vapor system are for $2000<T<3000$ K and some data are available for $T>3000$ K. Thus, we have some guidance for this extrapolation of a factor of two in $T$

There is no good way to discuss the uncertainties involved in these calculations without making qualita- tive judgments. As fig.1 shows, there are many pieces that must fit together in order to obtain reliable vapor pressures and compositions. Each component has an uncertainty. The gas-phase thermodynamic functions are relatively well established, but uncertainties remain (and probably will remain for some time) in the elec- tronic contributions to the functions. The condensed- phase thermodynamic functions must be extrapolated, and the assumption of a constant heat capacity could be improved. The oxygen-potential model has a sound thermodynamic basis, but there are insufficient data upon which a reliable evaluation of the parameters can be made. The scatter is large in the data points used to determine the oxygen-uranium phase diagram, particu- larly at tempratures near the melting point Measure- ments of properties of the $UO_{2}$ system at temperatures above 3120 K (the melting point) would by useful. It would also be useful to measure properties of systems other than the uranium-oxygen system in order to provide an experimental basis for the discussion of the errors expected in the pulse techniques [3-5].

The above discussion suggests the need for continu- ing experimental and supporting theoretical work to help reduce the uncertainty in the thermophysical prop- erties of the uranium-oxygen system at high tempera- tures. The results we have obtained should provide a reasonable standard for comparing experimental mea- surements and theoretical results. One clear implication of our results for other studies is that the vapor com- position is variable, and, to avoid potential problems, this variation must be considered in whatever method is used.

One area of potentially fruitful work is the calcula- tion (or measurement) of the properties of the uranium- oxygen system near the critical point. In many previous discussions of critical phenomena, the complex nature of the vapor phase in equilibrium with $UO_{2-x}$ was not recognized. It would seem reasonable that the oxygen- rich nature of the vapor cannot continue to increasewith temperature in the manner suggested in fig.5 Perhaps a careful consideration of the expected behav- ior above 6000 K can help provide some limits on the oxygen-potential model.

Another promising area of work is the determination of the expected deviations from ideal gas behavior for the complex vapor system.

Although nonideal equations for $UO_{2}$ may be de rived, their application to the real system that consists of multiple vapor species is limited.

In summary, our results provide strong support for the IWGFR total-pressure equation and, in our judg- ment, reduce the uncertainty in that equation at high temperatures. The vapor in equilibrium with a $UO_{2-x}$ condensed phase is poorly approximated as $UO_{2}( g)$ for most conditions of interest.
