# The deposition of diamond films via the oxyacetylene torch: experimental results and thermodynamic predictions *

Ruo-Bao Wang $^{1}$, M. Sommer $^{2}$ and F.W. Smith

Department of Physics, The City College of the City University of New York, New York, New York 10031, USA

Received 16 May 1991; manuscript received in final form 6 November 1991

The deposition of diamond films has been carried out via the oxyacetylene torch onto Mo and Si substrates. The phase diagram for deposition of diamond in the substrate temperature $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ versus reactant ratio O/C plane has been experimentally determined. The growth of diamond has been observed for $650 \leq T_{\mathrm{s}} \leq 1320^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $0.89 \leq \mathrm{O}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2} \leq 0.99$. The thermodynamic quasi-equilibrium model for the CVD of diamond has been extended to the C-H-O system and applied to $\mathrm{O}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ mixtures. Predictions for the rates of formation of gaseous species and of solid carbon have been obtained as functions of $T_{\mathrm{s}}$, the O/C ratio, and pressure. The CVD phase diagram for the oxyacetylene torch has been predicted and compared with our own experimental results and those of other workers. The distributions of film morphologies on the substrate surfaces are described and explained in terms of the changing O/C ratio in the flame and the variation of the substrate temperature within the growth region.

## 1. Introduction

The deposition of diamond films at atmospheric pressure with an oxyacetylene torch was first reported in 1988 by Hirose and Kondo [1] and was confirmed by Hanssen et al. [2] in the same year. In 1989, Carrington et al. [3] also deposited diamond films using an oxygen-ethylene flame. A great deal of effort [4-14] has been concentrated on this method in order: (1) to determine the range of deposition parameters for which diamond films can be grown, (2) to observe the film morphology, and (3) to determine the main precursors and their concentrations in the mixed gases just above the substrate. The advantages of this method of diamond film growth include deposition in the ambient atmosphere with a high growth rate (of the order of 100 $\mu \mathrm{m} / \mathrm{h}$ ), the simplicity of the deposition method, and the low cost of the equipment.

Various workers have obtained somewhat different results for the growth conditions leading to the deposition of diamond films. Hirose and coworkers [1,5,10] found diamond deposition under acetylene-rich conditions, i.e., $R=\mathrm{O}_{2} /$ $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}=0.7-1.0$. Hanssen et al. [12], however, have apparently found diamond film growth also under oxygen-rich conditions, with $R$ lying between 0.9 and 1.08.

In this paper, the diamond film growth region has been determined and the distribution of film morphologies has been observed by optical and scanning electron microscopy. It has been found that the diamond film growth region is located in the range $R=0.89-0.99$ with substrate temperature $T_{\mathrm{s}}=650-1320^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and that the observed film structures gradually changed from the center of growth region to its boundary. These observations are proposed to be due to the variation of both the $\mathrm{O} / \mathrm{C}$ ratio within the flame and $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ across the growth region.

## 2. Experimental method and results

The experimental arrangement used here for the deposition of diamond films is shown in fig. 1. The substrates (Mo, Si) were placed on a water- cooled copper holder. In order to adjust $T_{s}$ , stain less steel plates of different thicknesses were inserted between the substrates and the copper holder. Small holes were drilled into the sides of the Mo substrates for the insertion of a thermo- couple. Another thermocouple was located be- tween the copper holder and the stainless steel plate in order to measure the temperature of the top surface of the copper holder. The surface temperature of the substrate $T_{s}$ could then be calculated using the two measured temperatures and the known thermal conductivities of Mo and stainless steel. At the same time, the surface temperature of the substrate was measured using a two-color pyrometer operating at wavelengths of 0.97 and $1.08 \mu m$ . An optical pyrometer was not found to be suitable for accurate temperature measurement in this case, due to interference from the visible light emitted by the acetylene feather. It was used, however, to observe the growth of the films. It should also be noted that when the two-color pyrometer was used, care had to be taken to eliminate any incandescent parti- cles on the substrate surface from the field of view. The accuracy of the temperature measure- ment is estimated to be $\pm 30^{\circ} C$ . The values of $T_{s}$ quoted in this paper refer to the maximum tem- perature in the center of the region in contact with the feather.

## 3. Thermodynamic model and predictions

The thermodynamic quasi-equilibrium (QE) model which has previously been successfully applied to the CVD of diamond from $\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} / \mathrm{H}_{2}$ mixtures [15,16] has now been extended to the growth of diamond via the oxyacetylene torch by including oxygen as an additional reactant [17]. The goal of this thermodynamic approach is to identify the important stable species in the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}-$ O system and to determine the temperature-dependent etching and deposition rates of solid carbon (graphite and diamond). Since the principal ideas of the model remain unchanged and have been explained in detail elsewhere [15,16] for the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ system, it will suffice here to point out which equations need to be modified for the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{O}$ system.

The basis of the QE model remains the assumption that thermochemical equilibrium exists between the solid carbon C(s) surface and the vapor species *desorbed* or evaporated from it. In addition to the hydrocarbon species $\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y}$ present in the $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ system, the reactions between

C(s), hydrogen, and oxygen yield products such as $\mathrm{CO}, \mathrm{CO}_{2}, \mathrm{CHO}, \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$, etc. A total of 23 different gaseous species $(\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{H}_{2}, \mathrm{CH}, \mathrm{CH}_{2}, \mathrm{CH}_{3}, \mathrm{CH}_{4}$, $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}, \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}, \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}, \mathrm{O}, \mathrm{O}_{2}, \mathrm{CO}, \mathrm{CO}_{2}, \mathrm{C}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{2}, \mathrm{OH}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}, \mathrm{HO}_{2}, \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{HO}, \mathrm{HCO}, \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4} \mathrm{O}, \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{C}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{3}$) have been considered in our calculations. The rate of evaporation $R_{\mathrm{e}}(\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z})$ of specie $\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z}$, produced via the schematic reaction

$$
x \mathrm{C}(\mathrm{s})+\frac{1}{2} y \mathrm{H}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})+\frac{1}{2} z \mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \leftrightarrow \mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z}(\mathrm{~g}),
\tag{1}
$$

can be expressed [18] in terms of the equilibrium constant $K(\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z})$ and the equilibrium partial pressures of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{O}_{2}$, $P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathrm{H}_{2})$ and $P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathrm{O}_{2})$ respectively, by

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mathrm{e}}(\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z})= & \eta^{\prime} K(\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z}) \\
& \times \frac{P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathrm{H}_{2})^{y / 2} P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathrm{O}_{2})^{z / 2}}{\left(2 \pi m k_{\mathrm{B}} T\right)^{1 / 2}}. \quad (2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, $m$ is the molecular mass of the species $\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z}$, $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the absolute temperature of the carbon surface, and $\eta^{\prime}$ is the desorption coefficient of species $\mathrm{C}_{x} \mathrm{H}_{y} \mathrm{O}_{z}$ from the carbon surface.

Upon addition of oxygen to the C-H system two constraints must be satisfied, namely the conservation of hydrogen *and* oxygen atoms arriving at and leaving the carbon surface. These constraints constitute a pair of nonlinear equations in two unknowns whose solutions correspond to $P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathrm{H}_{2})$ and $P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathrm{O}_{2})$. After finding the solutions, the evaporation rates of the various species are determined from eq. (2) and the net deposition rate $R_{\mathrm{d}}$ of solid carbon (graphite or diamond) is given by the difference between the incoming and outgoing fluxes of carbon. We note that diffusion of air into the flame has been excluded from the model.

In fig. 6 we present the predicted rates of evaporation of $\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{H}_{2}, \mathrm{CH}_{4}, \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}, \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}, \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$, $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{CO}$, as well as the rate of deposition of graphite, the only form of solid carbon considered here, as functions of temperature $T$ from 500 to 3500 K. These values correspond to an incoming gas mixture consisting of $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ with an $\mathrm{O} / \mathrm{C}$ ratio $=0.95$ and a total pressure of 760 Torr $(1.013 \times 10^{5} \mathrm{~Pa})$. For temperatures below $\sim 900 \mathrm{~K}$, hydrogen preferentially reacts with oxygen to produce $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ at the expense of forming $\mathrm{CH}_{4}$. The remaining oxygen atoms react to form $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$. When the evaporation of $\mathrm{CO}$ starts to dominate above $\sim 900 \mathrm{~K}$, the rates of evaporation of $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ are seen to drop rapidly. Between 600 and $\sim 3100 \mathrm{~K}, \mathrm{H}_{2}$ is the most important hydrogen-containing product while atomic hydrogen becomes significant for $T>2600$ K. The rates of evaporation of $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}$ are not important until $\sim 2900 \mathrm{~K}$. Qualitatively similar predictions for the equilibrium gas concentrations of carbon-containing species have been obtained by Matsui et al. [5] for $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2} / \mathrm{O}_{2}$ $=1.1$.

Fig. 6. Predictions of the thermodynamic quasi-equilibrium model for the rates of evaporation of vapor species for the C-H-O system as functions of temperature. These rates correspond to a mixture of $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ with $R=\mathrm{O}_{2}$ / $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}=0.95$ at a pressure of 760 Torr. The deposition rate for solid carbon (graphite), C(s), is also shown.

Deposition of graphite is predicted to occur for all temperatures up to $\sim 3200 \mathrm{~K}$, but it is apparent from fig. 6 that its rate of deposition drops rapidly when evaporation of $\mathrm{CO}$ starts to prevail above around 900 K. Since more carbon is supplied by the incoming $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ than is consumed by $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ in the formation of $\mathrm{CO}$, the deposition rate remains finite and is seen to level off between 1200 and 2800 K. Only when additional etching

by $H_2$ becomes important via the formation of $C_2H_2$ and $C_2H$ above 2900 K does the deposition rate of graphite finally drop to zero for $T > 3150$ K.

In fig. 7 the predicted rates of evaporation of gaseous species and of deposition of graphite are illustrated for a stoichiometric gas mixture consisting of equal amounts of $O_2$ and $C_2H_2$, i.e., $R = 1.0$. Upon comparison of figs. 6 and 7 we note that, while the evaporation rates of gaseous species are almost identical, the deposition rate of graphite has decreased significantly. When $R = 1.0$, graphite can be deposited only for temperatures up to ~ 1600 K. In fact, our model predicts that for $O/C > 1.0$ no solid carbon, in the form of either diamond or graphite, can be deposited at elevated temperatures ($T > 1100$ K) due to the formation of CO.

Although not shown in figs. 6 or 7, the QE model predicts that the rates of evaporation of C-containing species such as $CO$, $CH_4$, $C_2H_2$, and $C_2H$ are higher from diamond than from graphite, as was also found for $CH_4$, $C_2H_2$, and $C_2H$ in the C-H system [15,16]. This difference results solely from the higher free energy of diamond relative to graphite. The rate of evaporation of $CO_2$, however, is actually lower for diamond for $T > 900$ K than for graphite. This is consistent with the dominance of the reaction $C(s) + CO_2 \leftrightarrow 2CO$ in the forward direction for $T > 900$ K for either form of solid carbon. It should be noted that due to the existence of the solid carbon surface in the model, which is the equivalent of an unlimited C source, our model does not predict the suppression of hydrocarbons in the presence of oxygen, in contrast to experimental observations [17].

The predicted stability regions for both graphite and diamond are shown in the CVD phase diagram for the C-H-O system in fig. 8 where substrate temperature $T_s$ corresponds to the vertical and O/C ratio to the horizontal axis. Also included in this figure is the experimentally determined region for the growth of diamond from fig. 2. Note that solid carbon is predicted to be stable only to the left of and below the boundary which corresponds to $R_d = 0$. When the O/C ratio is less than unity, deposition of C(s) via the oxyacetylene torch is in principle possible over a wide range of temperatures while our model predicts that the stability region of C(s) shrinks considerably when $O/C > 1.0$, as has been pointed out above. We note that the growth of either form of solid carbon is predicted to be completely suppressed for $O/C > 2.5$, for all temperatures. This corresponds to the stoichiometry expressed

by the following overall combustion reaction
which dominates for low $T$:

$$2 \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}+5 \mathrm{O}_{2} \leftrightarrow 4 \mathrm{CO}_{2}+2 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}. \tag{3}$$

Kosky and McAtee [6] have also presented a phase diagram for the C-H-O system for $0.8 \leq$ $\mathrm{O}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2} \leq 1.25$ and $700 \leq T \leq 2300^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, which shows a similar phase boundary between regions of the growth and etching of solid carbon.

For the calculations in this paper, all the adsorption and desorption coefficients of gaseous species on the solid carbon surface, $\eta$ and $\eta^{\prime}$, respectively, have been set equal to unity. Therefore, the predicted stability region of diamond is found in fig. 8 to lie inside the stability region of graphite, as expected. No effort has been made yet to identify the kinetic factors which lead to the observed growth of diamond under the present experimental conditions. We have shown previously [15,16] that when the QE model for the C-H system was modified in order to account for the experimentally observed enhanced etching of graphite by hydrogen, the observed stability of diamond could be understood. We expect that enhanced etching of graphite by oxygen-containing species also occurs.

Also included in fig. 8 is the phase boundary for diamond at a lower pressure of 76 Torr. It is interesting to note that the stability region of diamond (and also of graphite) decreases with decreasing pressure, in contrast to the C-H system [15,16]. Since chemical reactions, which consume at least as many moles of gas as are produced, are favored under high pressure conditions, the formation of $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ via the reaction $\mathrm{C}(\mathrm{s})$ $+\mathrm{O}_{2} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{CO}_{2}$ is favored over the formation of $\mathrm{CO}$ via the reaction $\mathrm{C}(\mathrm{s})+\frac{1}{2} \mathrm{O}_{2} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{CO}$. Etching of C(s) via production of CO is thus suppressed at higher pressures, resulting in an expanded growth region.

### 4. Discussion

As mentioned above, the morphologies of the carbon films grown via the oxyacetylene torch typically change as one moves radially outward from the center of the growth region. Some typical distributions of structures are shown schematically in figs. 4a-4c. In fig. 4a, the film structure over the entire growth region consisted of diamond particles with either (100) and/or (111) faces. The sizes of the (100) and (111) faces gradually decreased from the center of the growth region to its boundary. As examples: a film deposited on Mo with $R=0.93$ and $T_{\mathrm{s}}=1060^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ consisted of diamond particles with only (111) faces; another film deposited on Mo with $R=0.98$ and $T_{\mathrm{s}}=1180^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ displayed only (100) faces, while a third film deposited on Mo with $R=0.94$ and $T_{\mathrm{s}}=1050^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ showed both (111) and (100) faces. Fig. 3a illustrates a cubo-octahedral diamond particle with (100) and modified (111) faces, similar to that observed by Kim et al. [19].

Many films on Si substrates showed more complicated structures, as indicated schematically in figs. 4b and 4c. Fig. 4b corresponds to a film deposited on Si with $R=0.86$ and $T_{\mathrm{s}}=920^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ where ball-like particles were found in the center while multi-octahedral particles (fig. 3b) were found in an annular region near the boundary of the growth region. Similar multi-octahedral particles have also been observed by Ravi et al. [11] and Snail et al. [14] for growth via the oxyacetylene torch. Fig. 4c corresponds, on the other hand, to a film with $R=0.88$ and $T_{\mathrm{s}}=900^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. Here ball-like particles again appeared in the center, while two annular regions were also present, the inner one containing multi-octahedral diamond particles and the outer one containing diamond particles with (111) faces. These results are consistent with those of Cappelli and Paul [9] and Lee et al. [20]. The film morphologies shown in figs. 4b and 4c were never observed on Mo substrates.

The distribution of film structures on Mo and Si substrates described above can be explained on the basis of the experimental phase diagram shown in fig. 2 and the schematic phase diagram shown in fig. 9. As soon as the pre-mixed $O_{2}$ and $C_{2} H_{2}$ leave the torch nozzle, they immediately burn to form the flame. Oxygen from the sur- rounding air will diffuse into the outer flame, causing the actual O/C ratio to change over the cross-section of the flame. We assume that the O/C ratio in the center of the flame corresponds to the $O_{2} / C_{2} H_{2}$ ratio of the pre-mixed gases and that the O/C ratio will increase outwards, presumably reaching the value of unity at the edge of the feather. Oxygen diffusing into the outer flame from the surrounding air and reach- ing the feather will be rapidly consumed by excess carbon. Therefore, the O/C ratio within the feather may actually deviate from its value at the center of the flame only in a narrow region near the boundary of the feather. Another factor influ- encing the observed film morphologies is the non-uniform substrate temperature throughout the growth region. The temperature is highest at the center of the growth region, and gradually decreases outwards.

Based on these two factors, the distribution of film morphologies within the growth region can be qualitatively understood on the basis of fig. 9, as follows. Suppose point A in fig. 9 corresponds to the center and point B to the boundary of the growth region. Then, both the O/C ratio $R$ and $T_{s}$ will change across the substrate from point A to point B, i.e., $R_{A} \to R_{B}$ and $T_{sA} \to T_{sB}$ . In this case, well-defined diamond particles will be ob- served throughout the growth region, as shown in fig. 4a. Likewise, the film morphologies shown in both fig. 4b and fig. 4c can be understood on the basis of point C in fig. 9 corresponding to the center and point D to the boundary of the growth region. Finally, points E and F can correspond to the deposition conditions for some of the films deposited on Mo substrates. Thus, most of the observed film morphologies can be explained on the basis of the experimentally observed phase diagram shown in fig. 2 with the help of fig. 9.

The growth of diamond was found here to be possible for $0.89 \leq R \leq 0.99$ and $650 \leq T_{s} \leq$ 1320°C, as mentioned earlier. The deposition of diamond only at temperatures below $\sim 1350^{\circ} C$ is likely to be due to the desorption of hydrogen (and oxygen) from the growing diamond surfaces and their subsequent graphitization. It should be noted that the homoepitaxial growth of diamond via the oxyacetylene torch has recently been ob- served up to $1500^{\circ} C$ by Snail and Hanssen [21]. No solid carbon (either diamond or graphite) could be deposited for $R \geq 1.0$ due to the disap pearance of the feather. In order to compare our own as well as the experimental results of other workers with the predictions of our thermody-namic model, one could define a "critical" $O_{2} /$  $C_{2} H_{2}$ ratio, $R_{crit }$ , which is solely determined by the disappearance of the feather and which ap- parently depends on the individual experimental arrangement. For example, $R_{crit }=1.0,1.08$ , and1.0 for Hirose et al. [1,5], Hanssen et al. [12], and this experiment, respectively.

Recently, Oakes et al. [13] have explored the growth of diamond when $H_{2}$ is either added to or substituted for $C_{2} H_{2}$ in the oxyacetylene torch. Without hydrogen addition/substitution, dia- mond could be sucessfully grown [12] for $0.9 \leq R$ $\leq 1.08$ . When $H_{2}$ was added to the $O_{2} / C_{2} H_{2}$ 

gas mixture, with the O/C ratio kept constant at 1.03, the diamond growth rate at $T_{\mathrm{s}}=950^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ was observed to decrease significantly when the ratio $\mathrm{H}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ exceeded 0.25. In the hydrogen substitution experiments, the ratio $\mathrm{O}_{2} /\left(\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}+\mathrm{H}_{2}\right)$ was maintained at 1.03 while hydrogen was substituted for $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ such that the $\mathrm{H}_{2} /\left(\mathrm{H}_{2}+\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}\right)$ ratio varied from 0.0 to 0.05. Diamond growth at $T_{\mathrm{s}}=950^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ was observed to be completely suppressed at $\mathrm{H}_{2} /\left(\mathrm{H}_{2}+\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}\right)=0.05$. It should be noted that the above experiments were performed for an O/C ratio = 1.03 which is very close to the observed critical ratio [13] $R_{\text {crit }}=1.08$.

In order to determine whether these experimental results can be understood on the basis of our thermodynamic model, the ratio O/C = 0.95 was chosen which is just to the left of the etch-growth boundary, i.e. within the deposition region. The model predictions then indicate that the addition of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ does not significantly reduce the diamond growth rate until the $\mathrm{H}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ ratio exceeds 9. This is a much higher ratio than observed experimentally [13]. However, while in the model $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ addition essentially leads only to more $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ product, experimentally $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ can influence the appearance and temperature of the flame.

The model can, however, successfully predict that substitution of $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ by $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ with $\mathrm{H}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ $\geq 0.06$ does indeed quickly stop diamond growth. By keeping the ratio $\mathrm{O}_{2} /\left(\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}+\mathrm{H}_{2}\right)$ constant, substitution of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ for $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ actually increases the ratio O/C so that only a small amount of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ is necessary to cross the etch-growth boundary, as was previously pointed out by Oakes et al. [13].

## 5. Conclusions
Diamond films have been deposited via the oxyacetylene torch for $R=\mathrm{O}_{2} / \mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{2}$ between 0.89 and 0.99 and $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ between 650 and $1320^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The experimentally determined phase diagram for the growth of diamond has been found to be quite consistent with the predictions of the thermodynamic quasi-equilibrium model. The observed variations in film morphologies on the substrates have been explained in terms of the variations of the O/C ratio and $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ within the growth region.
