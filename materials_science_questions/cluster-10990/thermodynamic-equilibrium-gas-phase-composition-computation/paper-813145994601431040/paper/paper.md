## ABSTRACT

Co-combustion of coal and biomass is a low-cost, large-scale, and efficient way to utilize biomass energy, which has a wide range of potential applications. However, biomass, especially herbaceous fuels, contains high levels of volatile K, Na, and Cl, the use of which may result in ash-related operational problems, such as corrosion, fouling, and slagging during thermal utilization. The aim of this study is to investigate the effects of wheat straw and temperature on the release and transformation of alkali metal species during co-combustion of coal and S-rich wheat straw. Results indicate that the amounts of K and Na released during co-combustion could be reduced by the effects of Fe, Ti, S, Si, and Al from blended fuels. At lower wheat straw shares, the release of K decreased due to reactions of KCl with Fe species, and Ti species, forming $K_2Fe_2O_4$ and $K_2TiO_3$. At high wheat straw shares, the release of K could be mainly captured in the form of $K_2SO_4$; small amounts of $KAlSiO_4$ were also observed in the bottom ash. When the wheat straw share was 80 wt.%, increasing temperatures enhanced the release of KCl(g) and NaCl(g) at 600–800 °C. By contrast, in the range of 800–1000 °C, the amounts of these gases released exhibited no apparent association with temperature. Compared with the release of K, fuel K was mainly retained in the bottom ash. The $K_2SO_4$ content in the bottom ash decreased with increasing temperature in the range of 600–1000 °C, whereas the fraction of K in the form of $KAlSiO_4$ and $KAlSiO_6$ increased with increasing temperature, especially at temperatures between 800 and 1000 °C.

## 1. Introduction

Co-combustion of coal and biomass for energy generation has attracted considerable attention because the technology offers a number of environmental, technical, and economical benefits [1,2]. In addition to reduction of $CO_2$ emissions, NOx and $SO_2$ emissions can also be controlled during co-combustion of biomass with coal [3–5], and capital investments can be saved by using existing infrastructures. Existing coal-fired power systems may be utilized for co-combustion [6,7].

Unfortunately, biomass, especially herbaceous fuels, contains higher levels of alkali metals and Cl than coal, and its use can cause serious ash-related problems, such as agglomeration, corrosion, fouling, and slagging; these problems can result in unscheduled shut-downs and increases in operational costs [8–10]. As co-firing biomass and coal is a promising alternative that may counteract ash-related problems through the effects of S and minerals in coal, continued developments in co-firing technology remain an urgent necessity [11]. In most existing co-firing power plants, biomass as a substitute energy source can only be co-combusted with coal at relatively low ratios to avoid severe ash-related problems. In the UK, the maximum share of biomass in pulverized fuel power plants is 10% (on an energy basis) [12]. Aho et al. [13] found that Cynara could be co-combusted with Spanish coal on an energy basis of 30% without operational problems.

The behavior of alkali metals during combustion is well known to have a direct correlation with ash-related problems and strongly depends on both fuel composition and temperature [14–16]. A large amount of information can be obtained from the release of alkali metals during biomass combustion, especially of wooden fuels. However, the physical and chemical characteristics of biomass and coal are significantly different. Despite the publication of some reports, investigations related to co-combustion of biomass and coal are usually carried out at limited biomass fractions and fixed temperatures. Wheat straw is one of the most important agricultural residues in China. However, most of this material is directly burned in open fields, which pollutes the environment and wastes biomass resources [17,18]. Much attention has been paid to investigating co-combustion characteristics and pollutant emissions. While the interactions of alkali metals and Cl during co-combustion of straw and coal have been reported by [19], reports regarding the release and transformation characteristics of alkali metals during co-combustion of indigenous wheat straw and coal are limited.

The aim of this study is to investigate the effects of indigenous wheat straw and temperature on the release and transformation of alkali metals during co-combustion of coal and S-rich wheat straw. The results of this work may help determine a suitable fuel mixture and temperature combination that can effectively control the formation of alkali metal species to alleviate ash-related problems. The crystalline compounds of bottom ashes were subjected to X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), and thermodynamic calculations were performed using the software HSC 6.0 to support the interpretation of the experimental data.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Fuel preparation

Sub-bituminous coal (Tiefa Coal Ltd., Liaoning province, China) and S-rich wheat straw (Tangshan city, Hebei province, China) were chosen as co-firing fuels in this study. Samples were pulverized and sieved into particle sizes less than 0.15 mm in diameter. Prior to the experiments, samples were stored under dry conditions at room temperature. Proximate, ultimate, and ash composition analysis results of the fuels are given in Table 1. Wheat straw has observably high concentrations of S, Cl, and K compared with coal. Wheat straw-coal blends were prepared with wheat straw shares of 0 wt.%, 20 wt.%, 50 wt.%, 80 wt.%, and 100 wt.% (w/w).

### 2.2. Experimental setup

Coal, wheat straw, and their blends were combusted in a laboratory-scale fixed-bed reactor (Fig. 1). The detailed set-up diagram and experimental procedures are described elsewhere [20]. The quartz tube was 460 mm in height and had an inner diameter of 56 mm. The temperature in the reaction zone was monitored by a thermocouple.

Prior to each experiment, the reactor was purged with an air flow of 0.2 m³/h and heated to the desired temperature. In each run, 2 g of the sample was inserted into the bottom of the quartz tube and then combusted. The residence time was 30 min. The K, Na, and Cl in the flue gas were absorbed by four bottles containing 150 mL of de-ionized water. The K, Na, Cl, and $SO_4^{2-}$ condensed inside the quartz tube were determined by rinsing with de-ionized water. The contents of these species in the bottom ash were determined by immersing ash in de-ionized water for 24 h. Water-soluble ions were analyzed by ion chromatography (IC). The bottom ash was observed using XRD to determine crystalline compounds. Three repeated experiments were performed under each condition and the results were averaged to ensure reproducibility.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Effect of S-rich wheat straw on the release and transformation of alkali metals

During wheat straw and coal co-combustion, changes in the release of K and Na may be caused not only by physical dilution because of blending effects but also by chemical interactions of species in the wheat straw and/or coal. Thus, to differentiate between these effects, the measured versus predicted amounts of K and Na released from wheat straw/coal blends at 850 °C were plotted (Fig. 2). The predicted values were based on a linear combination of the measured values for the pure wheat straw and pure coal. The release of K and Na from the wheat straw and coal blends was lower than that from pure wheat straw. Regardless of the wheat straw share in the fuel, the predicted amounts of released K and Na were higher than the amounts measured during co-combustion, which indicates that the release of K and Na species can be inhibited by interactions between wheat straw and coal. Dayton and Belle-Oudry [6] reported that the release of KCl could be counteracted by clay minerals in coal. Robinson [21] found that the stickiness and Cl content of deposits could be reduced by reactions between alkali species from straw and S from coal during co-firing of straw and coal blends. Compared with the predicted amounts of K, the measured amounts of K decreased by 56%, 30%, and 81% in blends with 80 wt.%, 50 wt.%, and 20 wt.% wheat straw, respectively. Similar data for the amounts of Na released were also acquired. The reduction percentage of Na released from the fuels caused by synergistic reactions between two pure fuels was 32%, 63%, and 50% in fuels with 80 wt.%, 50 wt.%, and 20 wt.% wheat straw, respectively. To gain information on the fate of alkali metal species and support the experimental data, chemical equilibrium calculations were carried out using HSC 6.0 software based on a method of Gibbs energy minimization [22]. Molar compositions for the multi-element and multi-phase materials were calculated on the basis of 1 kg of dry sample, and data from Table 1; the elements C, H, N, O, S, Cl, Si, Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al, Fe, and Ti were considered. During calculations, the pressure was 0.1 MPa and the excess air ratio was 1.2.

Fig. 3 shows the calculation equilibrium distribution of K and Na species as a function of wheat straw share in the fuel at 850 °C. The main gaseous potassium species was KCl(g), which increased with increasing wheat straw percentage in the fuel; this finding suggests that the K released in experiments appears as KCl(g) and depends upon the composition content of K in the blend. On the other hand, NaCl(g) was the dominant sodium species in the gaseous phase, and its concentration slightly increased with increasing wheat straw content.

The amount of KCl(g) was very low compared with that of other K species retained in the solid phase. Most of the K species took the form of $K_2O\cdot Fe_2O_3$, $K_2TiO_3$, and $K_2SO_4$. $KAlSiO_4$(K) and $KAlSiO_4$ were likewise observed. The molar fraction of $K_2O\cdot Fe_2O_3$ and $K_2TiO_3$ sharply decreased with increasing levels of wheat straw in the blend; $K_2SO_4$ displayed the opposite trend. This phenomenon may be explained by the fact that wheat straw is of lower Fe and Ti content than coal. However, wheat straw has higher S contents than coal. These results are supported by the crystalline phases in the bottom ash identified by powder XRD (Table 2). The major crystalline compounds present in the bottom ash during wheat straw combustion were $SiO_2$ and $K_2SO_4$. Results for conditions with 50 wt.% wheat straw show that the crystalline phases in the bottom ash included $SiO_2$ and $KAlSiO_4$. From Fig. 3, the main sodium species included $Na_2SO_4$, $Na_2SiO_3$, $NaAlSiO_4$, and $Na_2CO_3$. With less than 80 wt.% wheat straw, the molar fractions of $Na_2SiO_3$, $NaAlSiO_4$, and $Na_2CO_3$ showed no apparent association with the concentration of wheat straw in the blend. However, the molar fraction of these chemical species sharply increased with over 80 wt.% wheat straw, whereas the molar fraction of $Na_2SO_4$ quickly decreased.

### 3.2. Effect of temperature on the release and transformation of alkali metals

The distribution of K, Na, Cl, and S as a function of temperature during co-combustion of coal and sulfur-rich wheat straw is shown in Fig. 5. The share of wheat straw in the fuels was 80 wt.% (w/w). In the temperature range of 600-1000 °C, the release of K was relatively low (<25%). The release of K gradually increased with increasing temperature at 600-800 °C (from 2% to 25%). Increasing temperature had little effect on K release. For example, when the temperature was between 800 and 1000 °C, only 1% increment in K release was observed. Most of the fuel K was in the form of $K_2SO_4$ or aluminosilicates retained in the bottom ash. Water-soluble K in the bottom ash sharply decreased from 60% at 600 °C to 1% at 900 °C. Almost no water-soluble K was detected in the bottom ash at 1000 °C.

Na release increased with the temperature, peaking at 800 °C and then slightly decreasing with further increases in temperature. Between 600 and 800 °C, water-soluble Na retained in the bottom ash showed no association with temperature. However, when the temperature was above 800 °C, water-soluble Na was found to decrease from 9% at 800 °C to 2% at 1000 °C, which may be due to the capture of Si or Al. The release of Cl was also strongly dependent on temperature, especially in the range of 600-800 °C. At 600 °C, approximately 44% of the fuel Cl was released, and this release increased with temperature until 94% Cl was released at 800 °C. The release of Cl was nearly complete and independent of temperature in the range of 800-1000 °C. The trend of water-soluble Cl in the bottom ash contrasted that of Cl release, decreasing from around 37% at 600 °C to around 0% at 1000 °C.

Molar ratios of (K + Na)/Cl at different temperatures were calculated to obtain information on the formation of Cl species in the gas phase and the transformation of alkali chlorides to other alkali compounds (see Table 3). The molar ratio of (K + Na)/Cl released from the fuel was much lower than 1, which indicates that the form of chlorine was mainly HCl(g) except for KCl(g) and NaCl(g); these findings are supported by the results of thermodynamic equilibrium calculations (Fig. 6). K and Na were probably released as KCl(g) and NaCl(g).

The amount of $SO_4^{2-}$ condensed inside the quartz tube was also investigated (Fig. 5). Only 0.5% S in the form of $SO_4^{2-}$ condensed inside the quartz tube at 600 °C. In the range of 600-700 °C, the release of KCl(g), NaCl(g), and $SO_2(g)$ increased with increasing temperature. KCl(g) and NaCl(g) may convert to KCl(s) and NaCl(s) in the cooling process of flue gas. Then, KCl(s) and NaCl(s) reacted with $SO_2(g)$ to form $K_2SO_4(s)$ and $Na_2SO_4(s)$. The molar ratio of (K + Na)/2S condensed inside the quartz tube was nearly equal to 1 at 700 °C (Table 3), which indicates that condensed K and Na inside the quartz tube present mainly in the form of $K_2SO_4(s)$ and $Na_2SO_4(s)$. In the interval of 700-800 °C, the fraction of S in the form of $SO_4^{2-}$ remained constant, which illustrates that the amounts of $K_2SO_4(s)$ and $Na_2SO_4(s)$ are independent of temperature. By contrast, the fraction of S in the form of $SO_4^{2-}$ sharply increased at 800-900 °C, and the molar ratio of (K + Na)/2S condensed inside the quartz tube decreased from 2.15 at 800 °C to 1.20 at 900 °C. Thus, it may be concluded that the reaction of alkali sulfation became stronger in this temperature interval. At 900-1000 °C, increasing temperature had little influence on the fraction of S condensed in the form of $SO_4^{2-}$ because the sulfation of alkali chlorides is in equilibrium. Most of the K and Na condensed inside the quartz tube at 1000 °C were found in the form of sulfates according to the molar ratio of (K + Na)/2S.

The molar ratio of water-soluble (K + Na)/2S in the bottom ash is shown in Table 3. The molar ratio was above 1 at 600 °C, which illustrates that $K_2SO_4(s)$ and $Na_2SO_4(s)$ were the main water-soluble species for K and Na, consistent with the results of thermodynamic equilibrium calculations (Fig. 6). KCl(s), which was detected by XRD, may be another water-soluble K species (see Table 4). The molar ratio of water-soluble (K + Na)/2S in the bottom ash was close to or below 1 in the temperature interval of 700-1000 °C, and equilibrium calculations showed that the main alkali metal species are alkali sulfates. ### 4. Conclusions

The effects of indigenous wheat straw in fuel blends and temperature on the release and transformation of alkali metal species during co-combustion of coal and wheat straw were investigated in a laboratory-scale vertical tube furnace using powder XRD and thermodynamic equilibrium calculations. The amounts of K and Na released in gaseous form during co-combustion were less than the predicted amounts based on the results of pure fuel combustion because of the presence of Fe, Ti, S, Si, and Al in the mixtures. Compared with the predicted amounts of K, the measured amounts decreased by 56%, 30%, and 81% at wheat straw shares of 80 wt.%, 50 wt.%, and 20 wt.%, respectively. The reduction percentage of Na released from fuels caused by synergistic reactions between wheat straw and coal was 32%, 63%, and 50% at wheat straw shares of 80 wt.%, 50 wt.%, and 20 wt.%, respectively.

At lower wheat straw shares, the release of K decreased due to reactions of straw KCl with coal Fe species, and Ti species, forming K₂Fe₂O₄ and K₂TiO₃, which sharply decreased with increasing wheat straw share in the fuel. At high wheat straw shares, the release of K could be mainly captured in the form of K₂SO₄; KAlSiO₄ was also observed in the bottom ash. The main water-soluble K and Na species in the bottom ash from 80 wt.% wheat straw share included K₂SO₄ and Na₂SO₄.

The amounts of KCl(g) and NaCl(g) released from the blend of 80 wt.% wheat straw share increased with increasing temperature in the range of 600–800 °C but were independent of temperature at 800–1000 °C. Temperature further impacted the sulfation of KCl(g, s) and NaCl(g, s), forming K₂SO₄(g, s) and Na₂SO₄(g, s), which condensed or adhered to the inner walls of the quartz tube. These effects, however, differed at different temperature regions. Compared with the amount of KCl(g) released, most of the fuel K existed in the bottom ash as K₂SO₄ or aluminosilicates. While the amount of K₂SO₄ in the bottom ash decreased with increasing temperature in the interval of 600–1000 °C, the fraction of K in the form of KAlSiO₄ and KAlSiO₆ increased with increasing temperature, especially at temperatures between 800 and 1000 °C.
