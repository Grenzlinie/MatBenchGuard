# First-principles prediction of a new Dirac-fermion material: silicon germanide monolayer

Hongcai Zhou, Mingwen Zhao, Xiaoming Zhang, Wenzheng Dong,
Xiaopeng Wang, Hongxia Bu and Aizhu Wang

## Abstract
From first-principles calculations, we proposed a silicon germanide (SiGe) analog of silicene.
This SiGe monolayer is stable and free from imaginary frequency in the phonon spectrum.
The electronic band structure near the Fermi level can be characterized by Dirac cones with
the Fermi velocity comparable to that of silicene. The Ge and Si atoms in SiGe monolayer
exhibit different tendencies in binding with hydrogen atoms, making sublattice-selective
hydrogenation and consequently electron spin-polarization possible.

## 1. Introduction
Graphene, a two-dimensional (2D) honeycomb network of
carbon atoms, is currently a material of increasing interest,
due to the great promise for future electronics technology
as well as fundamental physics applications [1–4]. The
fascinating properties of graphene are closely related to the
linear energy–momentum dispersion relations at the Dirac
point. The carriers in these bands characterized by Dirac cone
will behave as massless Dirac fermions with ultrafast velocity
(~1/300 of velocity of light). Other group-IV elements, such
as silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge), have also been predicted
to have stable honeycomb structures, which are named as
silicene and germanene [5–9]. Although the honeycomb
lattices of silicene and germanene differ significantly from
graphene, i.e., the atoms of the two sublattices are no longer
on the same plane, their electronic structures have much in
common with those of graphene [10]. Dirac cones are well
preserved in these two honeycomb lattices. Very recently,
silicene has been experimentally demonstrated in the form
of epitaxial sheets [11, 12] and nanoribbons [13] on silver
surfaces. The progress in the synthesis of silicene is exciting,
due to its compatibility with current silicon technology.

Ge has much superiority over Si, but it is less studied.
The electron effective mass and specific inductive capacity
of Ge are smaller than Si, which holds great promise for
microelectronics devices and infrared detectors. The exciton
Bohr radius in bulk Ge crystal (23.4 nm) is much longer
than that in silicon crystal (4.9 nm) [14], suggesting that
the size-confinement effects in Ge nanomaterials will be
much more significant than those in Si nanomaterials. Ge
quantum dots, if incorporated into the traditional silicon
technology, can improve the performance of circuit [15–17].
Two-dimensional germanene, a Ge analogy of silicene, has
been proposed as a Dirac-fermion material with linear
energy–momentum dispersion relations in the region near the
Fermi level being characterized by a Dirac cone [5, 7, 18, 19].
Unfortunately, this novel 2D honeycomb network of Ge atoms
has not been synthesized up to now. This may be related to the
energetic disadvantage of germanene against silicene [5].

An interesting question naturally arises: can the stability
of germanene be improved by incorporating another species
into the framework while keeping the electronic structures
unchanged? Generally, dopant will modify the electronic
structures of host material via introducing local states near
the Fermi level. The tradition of combining properties of
two elements near in the periodic table, like Si and Ge, to

obtain new and fascinating properties, has been demonstrated to be successful in the case of alloys, superlattices and nanostructures [20–23]. In this work, we proposed a novel 2D honeycomb framework formed by replacing half of Ge atoms in germanene alternatively with Si atoms. The atomic arrangement of this hypothetical material resembles the silicon carbide monolayer [24–26], and we refer to it as silicon germanide (SiGe) monolayer hereafter. Using first-principles calculations, we show that SiGe monolayer is energetically more favorable than germanene and free from imaginary frequency in the phonon spectrum. The energetic favorability and kinetic stability imply the plausibility of this novel material. Interestingly, the linear energy–momentum dispersion relations characterized by Dirac cone are well preserved in the band structures of SiGe monolayer. The Fermi velocity is also comparable to that of silicene. The greatest advantage of this SiGe monolayer over silicene and graphene is that the Ge and Si atoms in the SiGe monolayer exhibit different tendencies to bind with hydrogen atoms. This feature makes half-hydrogenation possible which is crucial for tuning the electronic structures and spin-polarization of this SiGe monolayer. The Dirac-fermion characteristics and tunable electronic structures of this novel 2D material hold great promise for applications in nanoscaled electronic devices.

## 2. Methods and computational details

Our first-principles calculations were performed in the framework of density-functional theory (DFT) which is implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package known as VASP [27]. The electron–electron interactions were treated within a generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the form of PW91 for the exchange–correlation functional [28]. The energy cutoff employed for plane-wave expansion of electron wavefunctions of both Si and Ge atoms were set to 400 eV. The Brillouin zone (BZ) integration was sampled on a grid of $8 \times 8 \times 1$ $k$-points for structural optimizations and $16 \times 16 \times 1$ $k$-points for the electronic structures calculations. Structural optimizations were carried out using a conjugate gradient method until the remanent force on each atom is less than $0.001$ eV $\text{\AA}^{-1}$. The phonon spectra were calculated using a Propho package on the basis of the dynamics matrices obtained from VASP calculations.

## 3. Results and discussion

In contrast to the plane structure of graphene, both silicene and germanene have low-buckled honeycomb structures [5], partly due to the $\text{sp}^{3}$-hybridization tendency of Si and Ge atoms whereas carbon atom prefers $\text{sp}^{2}$-hybridization. In these buckled structures, the atoms belonging to different sublattices are no longer on the same plane. We kept this feature in the framework of SiGe monolayer, i.e. Ge and Si atoms are placed alternately in the two sublattices, as shown in the inset of figure 1(a). To search for the energetically most stable configuration, we calculated the cohesive energies of the SiGe monolayers with different lattice constants while the symmetry of the honeycomb lattice was preserved. The cohesive energy was defined as the difference between the total energy of SiGe monolayer and sum of the total energies of isolated Si and Ge atoms. The variation of cohesive energy as a function of lattice constant is plotted in figure 1(a). The corresponding data of silicene and germanene are also proposed for the purpose of comparison. The distinct minima in this figure correspond to low-buckled (LB) and high-buckled (HB) honeycomb structures of these monolayers. The LB and HB configurations are energetically comparable, with the former slightly lower in energy than the latter for silicene and SiGe monolayer and a converse order for germanene, as shown in table 1. However, this does not mean that both LB and HB structures are stable (or metastable) forms of these two-dimensional materials. Previous work has showed that HB configurations are unstable

In order to test the kinetic stabilities, we calculated the phonon spectra of the LB and HB configurations of SiGe monolayer. It was found that the HB configuration has imaginary phonon frequencies whereas the LB configuration is free from imaginary frequencies, as shown in figure 1(b). This implies that similar to the cases of silicene and germanene, the HB configuration of SiGe monolayer is unstable, even though it is only slightly higher in energy than the LB configuration. The instability of the HB SiGe monolayer was further confirmed by an optimization process without any constraints of symmetry and lattice constants, which indicated that HB configuration exhibits a clear tendency to convert to LB configuration. Therefore, in the following sections, we focus on the most table LB configuration of SiGe monolayer.

The structural parameters of the LB SiGe monolayer are listed in table 1. The Ge–Si bond length was calculated to be 2.36 Å, slightly shorter than the value of the Ge–Ge bond in germanene, 2.40 Å, and longer than that of the Si–Si bond in silicene 2.28 Å. The lattice constant (3.94 Å) and buckling amplitude (0.58 Å) of SiGe monolayer also lie between the corresponding values of silicene and germanene. The cohesive energy of SiGe monolayer is lower than that of germanene by about 9.1%, implying that the incorporation of silicon atoms improves the energetic stability of germanene.

The phonon dispersion relations along high-symmetric directions in BZ of SiGe monolayer are plotted in figure 1(b). These phonon spectra were calculated by the force-constant theory combined with the VASP code. Both silicene and SiGe monolayer are free from imaginary phonon frequencies, implying the kinetic stability of these configurations. The optical and acoustical branches are well separated in these spectra. Two acoustical branches have linear dispersion relations as the wavevector ($k$) closes to the center of the BZ (Γ point), in common with the features of acoustical branches of bulk materials, whereas the transverse branch displays a quadratic dispersion relation near the Γ point, since the force constants related with the transverse motion of atoms decay rapidly [29]. It is noteworthy that the transverse acoustical phonon branch has imaginary frequencies near the Γ point, indicating that germanene may be unstable for the particular mode with long wavelength [5]. The maximal frequency of optical branches of SiGe monolayer is higher than that of germanene by about 42.14%. Based on the phonon spectra, we also evaluated the contribution of lattice vibration to the free energies ($\Delta F$) of these three monolayers at room temperature ($T=300$ K) using the following expression:

$$
\Delta F=\sum_{i}\left[\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{i}+k_{\text{B}}T\ln\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\hbar\omega_{i}}{k_{\text{B}}T}}\right)\right]
$$

where $\omega_{i}$ is the frequency, and $k_{\text{B}}$ is the Boltzmann constant. The calculated data of $\Delta F$ are listed in table 1. It is clear that the $\Delta F$ of SiGe is lower than that of silicene. Taking the effects of lattice vibration into account, SiGe monolayer is more favorable than germanene by about 0.73 eV/cell. This implies the high plausibility of SiGe monolayer over germanene.

The calculated electronic band structures of SiGe monolayer are presented in figure 2(a). The $\pi$ and $\pi^{*}$ bands which originate from the $p_{z}$ orbitals of Ge and Si atoms perpendicular to the basal plane cross at $K$ and $K'$ points at the Fermi level. Around the crossing point, the two bands are linear as shown by the inset of this figure, which can be characterized by Dirac cones. The charge carrier (electrons and holes) in these bands will behave as massless Dirac fermions, similar to the cases of the well-known Dirac-fermion materials such as graphene [2, 18], silicene [5, 8, 18, 30], and germanene [18, 30]. We also evaluated the Fermi velocity ($v_{\text{f}}$) of SiGe monolayer by fitting the $\pi$ and $\pi^{*}$ bands at $\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{K}+\boldsymbol{q}$ to the expression of $v_{\text{f}}=E(q)/\hbar|\boldsymbol{q}|$.

The Fermi velocities of electrons and holes are identical in our calculations, which are $0.56 \times 10^6$ m s$^{-1}$. This value is very close to that of silicene. The Dirac-fermion feature of this SiGe monolayer is quite interesting, because the honeycomb lattices of two species, such as silicon carbide (SiC) and boron nitride (BN) monolayers always have a bandgap. The unexpected characteristics of SiGe monolayer are related to the similarity between Ge and Si atoms. The electronegativity of Si and Ge atoms is very close with a difference of only 0.1 (Pauling scale). Therefore the electron transfer between Si and Ge atoms in SiGe monolayer is neglectable. This can be further confirmed by the differential electron density distribution as shown in figure 2(b). For SiC monolayer, significant charge transfer between Si and C opens up a bandgap [31, 32].

Previous works have shown that the electronic properties of graphene and silicene can be well tuned through hydrogenation in different patterns. For example, hydrogenating one sublattice of these honeycomb frameworks (half-hydrogenation) induces electron spin-polarization and even ferromagnetism of another sublattice [33–36]. However, the equivalence of the two sublattices makes half-hydrogenation difficult to achieve. The advantage of SiGe monolayer is that hydrogen atoms may have different binding preferences for Si and Ge atoms of the two sublattices. We first calculated two half-hydrogenation patterns of SiGe monolayer: hydrogenating all the Si atoms (HSiGe) and hydrogenating all the Ge atoms (SiGeH). For graphene and silicene, these two patterns are equivalent. However, our calculations show that the HSiGe pattern is energetically more favorable than the SiGeH pattern by about 32 meV per hydrogen atom, implying that Si atoms have higher binding preference to hydrogen atoms than Ge atoms in SiGe monolayer. We also calculated the cohesive energies of $\text{SiH}_4$ and $\text{GeH}_4$ molecules to further confirm this feature. The Si–H bond in $\text{SiH}_4$ is stronger than the Ge–H bond in $\text{GeH}_4$ by about 1.528 eV. The high binding preference of Si to H is quite crucial for achieving half-hydrogenation of SiGe monolayer in experiments.

The length of the Si–H bond in HSiGe is about $1.517$ Å, which is shorter than that of the Ge–H bond in SiGeH by about $0.069$ Å in table 2. This value is also close to that of Si–H bond in $\text{SiH}_4$ molecule ($1.518$ Å), suggesting that H atoms chemically bond to the Si atoms of SiGe monolayer. The Si–Ge bond length is elongated to $2.415$ Å compared with the value of pristine SiGe monolayer, $2.357$ Å. The buckling amplitude is increased to $0.74$ Å in HSiGe, due to the hydrogenation.

We then calculated the spin-polarization and magnetic ordering of HSiGe. Starting from different initial spin configurations in a $2 \times 2$ supercell of HSiGe, self-consistent DFT calculations gave different orderings of magnetic moments which are characterized by ferromagnetic (FM), antiferromagnetic (AFM), and nonmagnetic (NM) patterns, as shown in figure 3(a). Our calculations indicate that FM is energetically more favorable than AFM by about 10.35 meV/cell. This implies that, similar to the cases of half-hydrogenated graphene and silicene, the local magnetic moments prefer a FM ordering at the ground state of HSiGe. The spin-polarized electron density $\Delta\rho = \rho\uparrow - \rho\downarrow$ plotted in figure 3(b) indicates that the local magnetic moments that align parallelly locate mainly at the Ge atoms. Each Ge atom contributes a local magnetic moment of $1\ \mu_{\text{B}}$. Based on this feature, we performed Monte Carlo (MC) simulations within a 2D Ising model to study the thermal stability of the ferromagnetism in HSiGe. The possible values of $\hat{m}$ are 1 and $-1$, because the calculated local magnetic moment in a primitive cell sheet is $1\ \mu_{\text{B}}$. We employed a $100 \times 100$ supercell containing $10^4$ local magnetic moments. The simulations lasted for $1 \times 10^9$ loops. In each loop, the spins on all sites in the supercell changed according to the spin states of Ge yielding $10^{13}$ trials. The temperature-dependent magnetic moment per unit cell obtained from MC simulations is shown in figure 3(c). It can be seen that the magnetic moment per unit cell decreases to $0.6\ \mu_{\text{B}}$ at a temperature of $\sim$105 K, and becomes $0\ \mu_{\text{B}}$ when temperature is higher than 115 K. To make the ferromagnetic–paramagnetic transition clearer, we calculated the heat capacity ($C$) of the system using the expression $C = \lim_{\Delta T \to 0} \frac{\Delta E_{\text{T}}}{\Delta T}$, where $\Delta E_{\text{T}}$ is the change of total energy of the system as the temperature is increased from $T$ to $T + \Delta T$. The calculated $C$ as a function of temperature is shown in the inset of figure 3(c). It is obvious that the Curie temperature ($T_{\text{c}}$) is about 110 K and the ferromagnetic–paramagnetic transition is a second-order phase transition.

The spin-resolved electronic band structures and density of states (DOS) of HSiGe are presented in figure 4. Half-hydrogenation opens up a bandgap of 0.649 eV in HSiGe, in contrast to the zero-band-gap feature of pristine SiGe monolayer. Flat bands appear in the region near the Fermi level with a split of 0.905 eV between the spin-up and spin-down branches, which are responsible for the local magnetic moments. The electronic density of states projected onto the orbitals of different atoms indicate that the flat bands are mainly contributed from the p orbitals of Ge atoms. Therefore, the appearance of the local magnetic moments and the FM ordering can be ascribed to the breakage of $\pi$-orbital network due to half-hydrogenation.

## 4. Conclusions
Using first-principles calculations, we proposed a novel two-dimensional Dirac-fermion material with a buckling honeycomb network of Si and Ge atoms. Compared with germanene, the incorporation of Si atoms improves not only the energetic stability but also the kinetic stability of the monolayer. Linear dispersion relations of the valence and conduction bands around the K and K' points characterized by Dirac cones are preserved in SiGe monolayer. The Fermi velocities of the carriers of SiGe monolayer are very close to those of silicene and germanene and slightly lower than those of graphene. Hydrogen atoms have higher binding preference to Si atoms than to Ge atoms in SiGe monolayer, which facilitates the achievement of half-hydrogenation of the monolayer. The half-hydrogenated SiGe monolayer (HSiGe) has local magnetic moments locating at the Ge atoms and stable ferromagnetic ordering of these magnetic moments. Monte Carlo simulations within a 2D Ising model indicate that the Curie temperature of the HSiGe is about 110 K. The Dirac-fermion feature, energetic and kinetic advantages over germanene, and easily-achieved ferromagnetism of SiGe monolayer may hold great promise for fundamental physics studies and future electronics technology.
