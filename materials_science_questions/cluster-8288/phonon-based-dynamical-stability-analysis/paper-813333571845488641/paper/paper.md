**COMMUNICATION**

# Lowest enthalpy polymorph of cold-compressed graphite phase

Da Li, Kuo Bao, Fubo Tian, Zhenwu Zeng, Zhi He, Bingbing Liu and Tian Cui*

Received 21st December 2011, Accepted 9th February 2012
DOI: 10.1039/c2cp24066a

Based on an *ab initio* evolutionary algorithm, a novel carbon polymorph with an orthorhombic *Cmcm* symmetry is predicted, named as *C* carbon, which has the lowest enthalpy among the previously proposed cold-compressed graphite phases.

Carbon is one of the most important elements in nature. It is a fundamental building block of carbonaceous organic material, humans, and other life on Earth. Due to the unique electronic arrangement, carbon can form $\text{sp}^3$, $\text{sp}^2$, and sp hybridized chemical bonds, which allows it to combine with other elements easily. In nature, elemental carbon has three best-known forms, graphite, diamond, and amorphous carbon. Some other forms of carbon have been synthesized such as fullerenes,¹ carbon nanotubes,² and graphene too.³ These forms have large impacts in relevant areas of chemistry, physics, and materials. The conversion mechanisms between various forms of carbon have long been a topic of interest since these polymorphs have obvious differences in both electronic and mechanical properties. Without catalysts, graphite can be converted to hexagonal and cubic diamonds at the pressures above 15 GPa and temperatures above $\sim1300$ K.⁴⁻⁹ On the other hand, by applying pressure to graphite at room temperature, a structural phase transition occurs above 14 GPa as characterized by several experiments.¹⁰⁻¹⁴ Remarkably, this new phase is a potential superhard material as shown by broadening ruby fluorescence lines and its ability to indent diamond anvils.¹³ However, the crystal structure of this cold-compressed graphite phase remains unknown. Some previously reported forms of carbon were thought as potential candidates such as hexagonal diamond, an intermediate phase between graphite and diamond, and an amorphous phase.¹⁰,¹⁵⁻¹⁷ However, no Raman bands are observed in the $1330\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ frequency region characteristic of hexagonal/cubic diamonds,¹⁸ and the measured hydrostatic XRD patterns have revealed that Bragg peaks persisted and remained traceable to the original graphite pattern, ruling out the possibility of amorphous carbon.¹³ A series of hybrid diamond–graphite structures composed of a mixture of $\text{sp}^2$ and $\text{sp}^3$ bonds were proposed as the structural candidates, but none of them can account for the experimental XRD.¹⁹ Recently, a series of cold-compressed graphite phases with $\text{sp}^3$ hybridized covalent bonds had been proposed, including the monoclinic *M* carbon, body-centered tetragonal bct-C₄ carbon,²⁰,²¹ and *W* carbon.²² Very recently, a new 16 atoms' $\text{sp}^3$ hybridized carbon with space group *Cnmm* has been reported by two groups, they named it as *oC16-II*,²³ and Cco-C₈ carbon.²⁴ The phase transformation pathways, anisotropy, near *K*-edge spectroscopy, and mechanical properties of those cold-compressed graphite phases have been studied. However, the lowest energetic crystal structure is still under debate, the XRD experiment cannot be explained accurately and the mechanism of superhard properties remains largely unexplored.

In this communication, by means of the *ab initio* evolutionary algorithm, we predict a new polymorph of elemental carbon with *Cmcm* symmetry that has very intriguing physical properties. This structure is more energetically favorable than previously proposed bct-C₄, *M* carbon, and *W* carbon. It has a lowest phase transition pressure at about 6.5 GPa. We name this novel orthorhombic polymorph as *C* carbon. Electronic structures reveal that it is a semiconductor with a direct band gap around 4.38 eV. The calculation of charge density difference and charge density confirm the existence of strong covalent bonds in *C* carbon. And *C* carbon had a highly incompressible property among cold-compressed graphite phases with a Vickers hardness of 56 GPa, smaller than diamond (94.4 GPa) but comparable with the cubic boron nitride (62.4 GPa). Its XRD also matches the experimental data well.

Using the *ab initio* evolutionary algorithm,²⁵⁻²⁷ we performed variable-cell structure prediction simulations for carbon containing two-twenty atoms in the simulation cell at 10, 30, 50, and 80 GPa, respectively. We explored a larger pressure range than previous work.²⁰ Our simulations reveal a novel orthorhombic structure with *Cmcm* symmetry as depicted in Fig. 1(a). A graphite-like atomic arrangement can be clearly observed in every layer of *C* carbon. And *C* carbon can be understood as distorted graphite, but with 24 atoms in its unit cell. At 15 GPa, the equilibrium lattice parameters are $a = 2.469\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, $b = 11.170\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, $c = 4.802\ \mathring{\text{A}}$. Within this structure, four inequivalent atoms occupy the crystallographic $8f$ and $4c$ sites in the unit cell, which are (0.0, 0.868, $-0.482$), (0.0, 0.442, $-0.421$), (0.0, 0.778, $-0.25$), and (0.0, 0.2, $-0.25$) positions. Similar to *M* carbon and *W* carbon, *C* carbon is made of exclusively three-dimensional $\text{sp}^3$ hybridized covalent bonds. Upon compression of graphite, sliding and buckling of the planes are accompanied by the formation of $\text{sp}^3$ bonds connecting the planes. The $\text{sp}^2$ bonds of graphite convert to $\text{sp}^3$ bonds in this process. This process is not finished instantaneously. But all the $\text{sp}^2$ bonds convert to $\text{sp}^3$ bonds in the end.

This process has been confirmed using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI NEB) method. $^{22}$ The $C$ carbon becomes stable relative to graphite above 6.5 GPa, and is more stable than bct-C$_4$, $M$ carbon, $W$ carbon, $o$C16-II, and Cco-C$_8$ carbon. The $C$ carbon has the lowest-enthalpy among the previously proposed cold-compressed graphite phases (Fig. 1(b)). Further, the $C$ carbon structure is also consistent with the experimental suggestion that cold-compressed graphite should have either orthorhombic or monoclinic symmetry. $^{13}$

We have calculated the phonon dispersion within a wide pressure range up to 50 GPa (Fig. 1(c)). No imaginary frequencies are observed throughout the whole Brillouin zone, confirming dynamical stability of the $C$ carbon. The electronic band structure of $C$ carbon at 0 GPa is shown in Fig. 1(d). The direct band gap at the G point is 4.38 eV, which is very similar to the LDA gap (4.39 eV) for $W$ carbon$^{22}$ as shown in Table 1. And it is remarkably larger than the value of 2.56 eV for bct-C$_4$ and 3.60 eV for $M$ carbon. Therefore, $C$ carbon is also expected to be as optically transparent as $W$ carbon. The lengths of bonds of $C$ carbon between two layers of six fold-rings at 0 GPa are 1.515 and 1.663 Å. The former is a little smaller than the bond length of diamond (1.531 Å). The latter is comparable to the interfullerene bond length of the pressure-polymerized fullerenes (1.64–1.68 Å).$^{28}$ As shown in Fig. 2(a), the charge density difference in the (1 0 0) plane indicates extremely strong covalent $C$–$C$ bonding between two layers of six fold-rings. On the other hand, the covalency and the network lattice can be distinctly seen through the isosurface of the charge density of $C$ carbon shown in Fig. 2(b), where the isovalue we chose is 0.20. We find that the bonds in the $C$ carbon display a high directionality, pointing out strong covalent character. The atom partial density of state (PDOS) of $C$ carbon is very similar to that of diamond as shown in Fig. 2(d). The bonding states below the Fermi level show a smoother shape for $C$ carbon as compared to that of diamond, indicating some distortion of the sp$^3$ hybridization. This phenomenon is very similar to that of $M$ carbon and $W$ carbon. The high valence electron densities, highly directional bonds, and strong sp$^3$ hybridization result in high bulk modulus and high hardness. From this point, the $C$ carbon is expected to be a potential superhard material.

By fitting the calculated total energy as a function of volume to the third-order Birch–Murnaghan equation, we gain the bulk modulus ($B_0$) of $C$ carbon as 427.8 GPa, which is larger than that of c-BN (396 GPa)$^{29}$ and a little smaller than the value of diamond (466 GPa). And the value is very close to that of $M$ carbon (431.2 GPa).$^{20}$ The calculated volume, bulk modulus at zero pressure are listed in Table 1 and compared to available experimental data for diamond$^{30}$ and calculated data for bct-C$_4$, $M$ carbon, and $W$ carbon. A large bulk modulus indicates that $C$ carbon cannot be compressed easily. To further compare the compressibility of $C$ carbon with the other cold-compressed graphite phases, diamond, c-BN, $M$ carbon, bct-C$_4$, $o$C16-II, and $W$ carbon under pressure, the $P$–$V$ relations [equation of state (EOS)] are plotted in Fig. 2(c). The descending order of compressibility is c-BN $>$ $W$ carbon (bct-C$_4$) $>$ $M$ carbon $>$ $C$ carbon $>$ $o$C16-II $>$ diamond. In order to explore the bonding properties, the Vickers hardness $H_{\mathrm{v}}$ of $C$ carbon was studied. The calculated hardness $H_{\mathrm{v}}$ of $C$ carbon is 56 GPa, which is obtained from an empirical formula. $^{32}$ Remarkably, the hardness of $C$ carbon is around $1/3$ softer than diamond (94.4 GPa), and is comparable with the c-BN (62.4 GPa). The predicted high bulk modulus and hardness explained the experimental observation that the cold-compressed graphite sample left a "ring crack" on the diamond anvils cell. To explore the bonding properties between the atoms of $C$ carbon quantitatively, we have calculated the Mulliken population to estimate the strength of covalent bonds. It is found that the Mulliken overlap population of $C-C$ bonds in $C$ carbon is in the range of 0.67-0.76, comparable to that of diamond (0.75).

To confirm the crystal structure of $C$ carbon further, we have simulated XRD patterns of graphite and $C$ carbon, shown in Fig. 3, compared to the experimental ones at various pressures. $^{13}$ Both $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonding were observed in the experimental cold-compressed graphite sample. $^{13}$ However, $C$ carbon can only contribute the $\sigma$ bonds because $C$ carbon is made of exclusively three-dimensional $\mathrm{sp}^{3}$ hybridized covalent bonds. The $\pi$ bonding character of the cold-compressed graphite sample comes from the graphite. We have thus proposed that the experimental cold-compressed graphite sample in ref. 13 is actually a mixture of graphite and $C$ carbon. This can also be confirmed by the electrical measurement that shows a small increase in electrical resistivity above $20 \mathrm{GPa}.^{33}$ The strongest peaks for $C$ carbon are located in the region between $8.5-11^{\circ}$ and $15-17.5^{\circ}$, which is similar to previously reported $M$ carbon and $W$ carbon. $^{20,22}$ The relatively weak peaks of $C$ carbon and graphite in the range of $10-14^{\circ}$ have merged into the background. The peaks at $\sim 8.5^{\circ}, \sim 16^{\circ}$ and $\sim 17^{\circ}$ contribute to the peak broadening. The peaks at $\sim 7^{\circ}$ have been covered by the peaks of graphite. The predicted XRD of $C$ carbon and graphite at 18.4 GPa together can successfully reproduce the experimental XRD at 18.4 GPa. Considering the effect of pressure, more and more graphite transforms into $C$ carbon. This can also be confirmed by the change of (3,0)/(4,0)ab phase transforms to $\mathrm{sp}^{3}-(4+6+8)$ ring structure. $^{19}$ With increasing pressure, the diffraction peaks of $C$ carbon become prominent in XRD. The marked change of the experimental XRD patterns from 13.7 to 23.9 GPa is attributed to the increase of the ratio of $C$ carbon in the graphite phase.

![](./images/813333571845488641_4.jpg)

Fig. 4 The simulated Raman spectra of C carbon, oC16-II, M carbon, W carbon, and bct-C₄ at 15 GPa.

In order to distinguish the cold-compressed phases in high pressure experiments, we calculated the Raman spectra of the cold-compressed phases at 15 GPa, shown in Fig. 4. The results show that the bct-C₄ phase has a simple spectrum with only three peaks at the range of $1050-1400 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The $M$ carbon and $W$ carbon have a similar range of peaks. The stronger peaks of $M$ carbon are located at 900 and $1220 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The corresponding peaks of $W$ carbon move to the high frequency range. The $o$ C16-II has two stronger peaks at about 1200 and $1350 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and a little peak at about $650 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The $C$ carbon has three stronger peaks at about 950, 1200, and $1300 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. However, the $C$ carbon has two new peaks in the range of $600-800 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ in the Raman spectra. This allows the $C$ carbon to be distinguished clearly in Raman spectra.

In summary, we have extensively explored the polymorph of cold-compressed graphite by an ab initio evolutionary algorithm based on DFT. $\dagger$ We have predicted a novel carbon polymorph, $C$ carbon, that has the space group Cmcm. This structure is more energetically favorable than previously proposed bct-C₄, $M$ carbon and $W$ carbon. It has a lowest phase transition pressure at about 6.5 GPa among previously proposed cold-compressed graphite phases. This is very important in the synthesis of superhard materials. The synthesis conditions can be achieved easily. The calculations on the EOS and energy of the $C$ carbon show that it is a superhard material with a much higher incompressibility and a bulk modulus (427.8 GPa), and a direct band gap about 4.4 eV. The Vickers hardness of $C$ carbon (56 GPa) is found to be smaller than diamond but comparable with that of cubic boron nitride. The simulated XRD can match the experimental data well. The calculated Raman spectra indicate that the $C$ carbon has a unique feature which is different to other cold-compressed graphite phases. Two new peaks in the range of $600-800 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ have been found in the Raman spectra of $C$ carbon. This allows it to be distinguished clearly in Raman spectra.
