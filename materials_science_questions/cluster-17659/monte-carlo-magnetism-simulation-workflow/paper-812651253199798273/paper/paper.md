# Stress-annealing in $\boldsymbol{Fe_{73.5}Cu_1Nb_3Si_{13.5}B_9}$ amorphous alloy ribbon

G. R. Aranda and J. González$^{\text{a)}}$
Department of Materials Physics, Faculty of Chemistry, Basque Country University,
P. Manuel de Lardizabal 3, 20009 San Sebastián, Spain

K. Kułakowski
Faculty of Physics and Nuclear Techniques, University of Mining and Metallurgy, al.Mickiewicza 30,
30-059 Kraków, Poland

J. Echeberría
Departamento de Materiales, CEIT, P. Manuel de Lardizabal 15, 20018 San Sebastián, Spain

(Received 4 August 1999; accepted for publication 3 February 2000)

Investigations on measurements of the coercive field and the magnetization work of nanocrystalline $Fe_{73.5}Cu_1Nb_3Si_{13.5}B_9$ alloy are reported. Nanocrystalline samples are prepared from an amorphous precursor by Joule heating in the presence of tensile stress of 420 MPa. Values of the parameters of the thermal treatment are chosen as to develop a different degree of the nanocrystalline character. Results on the magnetization work $W$ are interpreted in terms of the random anisotropy model. Fitting of the experimental curves of $W$ against applied stress allows us to obtain the annealing dependence of the magnetostriction $\lambda$ and the stress-induced anisotropy $K_{\text{ann}}$. © 2000 American Institute of Physics. [S0021-8979(00)07809-9]

## I. INTRODUCTION

Current research on nanocrystalline $Fe_{73.5}Cu_1Nb_3Si_{13.5}B_9$ alloy, which is known under the trademark name FINEMET, has been stimulated by their technological applications due to the excellent soft magnetic properties of this system.$^{1-3}$ Nanocrystalline phase of this alloy is obtained by means of a primary crystallization of amorphous ribbon during the isothermal annealing above the crystallization temperature. The shape and size of the crystallites depend on the alloy composition and, at a particular annealing temperature, they are controlled by a competition between the kinetics of growth and the minimization of the grain surface energy. The crystallites formed inside the metallic matrix are protected from chemically reactive environment and, additionally, the intergranular phase prevents their agglomeration. Thus, the nanocrystalline alloys consist structurally of fine crystalline grains with a long-range atomic order and the amorphous matrix that exhibits a short-range order. The grains are randomly oriented but their atomic structure is identical, whereas a certain degree of inhomogeneity is observed in the residual amorphous matrix.$^{4}$ This inhomogeneity can produce local magnetic anisotropy and it can vary with the thermal treatments.

According to the random anisotropy model proposed by Herzer,$^{2,3}$ the magnetic softness of the system results from the formation of large magnetic domains, where the intergrain distance is smaller than the exchange correlation length. This leads to an extremely low average anisotropy. The measurable macroscopic properties (e.g., the magnetic permeability $\mu$ and coercive field, $H_c$) should strongly depend on the grain size $D.^{3}$ Besides the grain size, other factors contribute to the soft magnetic behavior:$^{3}$ the absence of Fe–B crystallites and the very low value of magnetostriction, which can be understood, in the first approximation, an average over the Fe–Si crystallites and the remaining amorphous phase.

It is well known that a shape of the magnetization curve of amorphous ferromagnets can be affected by annealing under the influence of mechanical stress.$^{5,6}$ External as well as internal stresses are coupled to magnetic degrees of freedom via the magnetoelastic effect. This enables the magnetic properties to be tailored to specific requirements which results from technological interest.

This work is devoted to the study of the coercivity and the magnetization work of $Fe_{73.5}Cu_1Nb_3Si_{13.5}B_9$ alloy, where the crystallization process is performed by stress current annealing. The type of annealing (under mechanical tensile stress) and the annealing parameters (annealing temperature, annealing time, and strength of tensile stress) were chosen as to develop the beginning of the first stage of the nanocrystallization process, as well as to induce a transverse magnetic anisotropy. The study is similar to that performed on the $Fe_{73.5}Cu_1Ta_3Si_{13.5}B_9$ alloy in Ref. 7. It includes the phenomenology of the induction anisotropy as well as the measurements of the hysteretic parameters: coercive force, initial susceptibility, and magnetization work. Experimental results on the magnetization work are supplemented by analytical and numerical calculations, performed within the random anisotropy model.$^{3,8}$

## II. EXPERIMENT

Amorphous ribbons (2 mm wide and 25 $\mu$m thick) of nominal composition $Fe_{73.5}Cu_1Nb_3Si_{13.5}B_9$ were produced by rapid solidification by Vacuumschmelze GmbH, Hanau, Germany, and kindly provided by Dr. G. Herzer. Straight pieces (10 cm of length of these ribbons) were annealed using the

![](./images/812651253199798273_3.jpg)

Joule heating technique, $^9$ known as current annealing. During the thermal treatment carried out at two values of electric current, 1.45 and 1.50 A with different times, a tensile stress $\sigma_{\text{ann}}$=420 MPa was applied to the samples. (It should be noted that this kind of treatment produces a remarkable brittleness of the samples. As a consequence, the subsequent measurements of the magnetic parameters when applying tensile stress become difficult and should be done with care, to avoid the break of the sample.) After this treatment, the samples were studied with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to determine the nanocrystalline character and the mean crystallite size.

Longitudinal magnetization curves were measured in as-quenched and annealed samples by means of a conventional induction setup, which allows us to obtain the dc hysteresis loop of the sample (coercive field and saturation magnetization). Figure 1 shows the longitudinal hysteresis loop of the as-quenched and stress-annealed samples. The shape of these curves indicates that a transverse magnetic anisotropy has been induced during the annealing.

The device also allowed the application of longitudinal tensile stress during the measurements. From the hysteresis loops we get information on the initial magnetic susceptibility. The magnetization work was calculated according to the expression

$$
W=\mu_{0} \int H d M, \tag{1}
$$

where $H$ is the longitudinal magnetic field, and the integral is carried on from the remanent magnetization until $M=1$ T (owing to the fact that the linear region of this magnetization curve corresponding to coherent magnetization rotation, linked mainly to the transverse magnetic anisotropy, takes place below 1 T). For this purpose, we have first obtained the magnetization curve $M_{z}-H_{z}$. The magnetization work is calculated as the area between this magnetization curve, the ordinate axis ($M$), and the parallel to the horizontal axis $M=M_{s}$. More details can be found in Ref. 9.

### A. Microstructure analysis

In order to analyze the effect of the microstructure on the magnetic behavior, (especially the evolution of the magnetization work with the applied tensile stress), the samples were stress annealed (conditions have been mentioned above). Three samples have been chosen to be investigated by means of TEM treated by stress annealing, namely: treated at 1.45 A for 2 min (sample B), treated at 1.45 A for 10 min (sample D), and treated at 1.5 A for 15 min (sample E). The exemplary microstructures obtained by TEM in samples B, D, and E are shown in Figs. 2(a)–2(c), respectively. These results enabled us to evaluate the average grain size and fraction of crystalline volume of the sample. Table I shows the data obtained for the analyzed samples. The image of sample B reveals the presence of very small grains (diameter $\lesssim 2$ nm) with a fractional volume around 1–2%. The relative fraction volume of crystalline phase results to be around 50% (see Table I). None of the TEM micrographs of Figs. 1(b) and 1(c) shows any texture of the sample.

### B. Magnetic behavior

Applied stress dependence of the coercive field and the magnetization work were measured for three samples: A, C, and D. The magnetic field was increased until the magnetization $M$ was equal to 1 T. The sample A was annealed at 1.5 A for 1 min, and the sample C at 1.5 A for 5 min. In Figs. 3 and 4, experimental results are presented on the coercive field and the magnetization work, respectively, as dependent on the applied stress. For both quantities, plots are shown for various times of annealing. In the Fig. 4, additional curves are marked which come from the calculations. These curves will be described in Sec. III.

While the coercive field $H_c$ depends only weakly on the applied stress $\sigma_{\text{ann}}$ for the amorphous sample A, it decreases remarkably with stress for the sample D, which is nanocrystalline, for the applied stress less than 10 MPa. Some traces of such a decrease seem to be also present for the sample C. The oscillation of the coercive field at large applied tensile stress could be explained by considering the different contributions to the coercivity proposed for this alloy$^{10}$ (i.e., fluctuations of the magnetoelastic anisotropy and the pinning by the ribbon thickness minima). The magnetization work $W$ decreases with the applied stress and increases with the time of annealing. The latter effect is due to a magnetic anisotropy, which arises as a result of stress annealing. It is believed that the stress derivative of $W$ is proportional to the magnetostriction $\lambda$. We will give below the conditions when this statement is exact.

Initial magnetic susceptibility $\chi'$ is also measured for different time and current annealing. These results are shown in Fig. 5. As we can see, $\chi$ shows a maximum for short annealing time, then it decreases after 5 or 10 min of annealing. Although it could be deduced from the TEM micrographs that the soft nanocrystalline phase is not destroyed yet at this time of annealing, we note again that the stress-annealing-induced perpendicular anisotropy must reduce the initial susceptibility, which is measured along the ribbon axis. The reduction of the coercive field with the time of annealing coincides with the enhancement of the initial susceptibility. However, $H_c$ does not increase back as soon as $\chi$ is reduced.

### III. CALCULATIONS OF THE MAGNETIC WORK IN THE RANDOM ANISOTROPY MODEL

As it was argued by the present authors in Ref. 11, the effective temperature depends on the size of magnetic domains and it can be much smaller than the room temperature. Expressions for the magnetization work will be given at first, which are valid at $T=0$. As long as the final magnetization is $M_s$, i.e., the sample is driven to saturation, the expressions are analytical.

![](./images/812651253199798273_7.jpg)

FIG. 5. Annealing time dependence of the initial susceptibility.

In subsections III A and III B we describe the calculations performed with the assumption that the only result of the stress annealing is the creation of the magnetic anisotropy perpendicular to the ribbon axis. The results of this approach will be marked in Table II by (a). However, one could also express the effect of the stress annealing by a modification of the distribution of local easy axis. For completeness, we have also performed these kinds of calculations, and they are described in subsections III C and III D. The results of this approach will be marked in Table II by (b).

### A. The ground state
Easy axis of the magnetic domains is assumed to be distributed isotropically. Annealing will be taken into account by an introduction of an extra internal stress, which enhances the magnetization work, as it is experimentally observed (see Fig. 3). Let us consider a plane (x, z) formed by an external magnetic field, which is directed along the z axis, and an easy axis of a domain, which forms an angle $\theta$ with the field. Obviously, a local magnetic moment lies in the plane. Its angle with the field is denoted by $\theta'$. When the external field is reduced to zero, the domain is supposed to be in the remanent state, i.e., the angle $\theta'$ is positive, and its value can be found from the minimum of energy, i.e.
$$
\partial E(\theta, \theta') / \partial \theta'=0. \qquad (2)
$$

The energy density can be written as
$$
E\left(\theta, \theta^{\prime}\right)=\langle K\rangle\left[\cos ^{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{\prime}\right)-x \cos ^{2} \theta^{\prime}\right], \qquad (3)
$$
where $\langle K\rangle$ is the magnetic anisotropy within a domain, averaged over domains, and
$$
x=\left(3 \lambda \sigma / 2-K_{\text {ann }}\right) /\langle K\rangle, \qquad (4)
$$
where $\lambda$ is the magnetostriction, $\sigma$ is the applied tensile stress, and $K_{\text {ann }}$ is the above mentioned anisotropy induced while annealing. The condition (2) can be written as
$$
\left(x+\cos ^{2} \theta\right) t g 2 \theta^{\prime}=\sin ^{2} \theta. \qquad (5)
$$

When integrating over $\theta$, two identities are helpful:
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \operatorname{sign} \cos ^{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{\prime}\right)=\operatorname{sign}\left(1+x \cos ^{2} \theta\right), \\
& \operatorname{sign} \cos ^{2} \theta^{\prime}=\operatorname{sign}\left(x+\cos ^{2} \theta\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The magnetization work $W$ dependence on the applied tensile stress can be calculated as the difference of magnetic densities of energy, integrated over the upper hemisphere: $0<\theta<\pi / 2,0<\varphi<2 \pi$. The result does not depend on $\varphi$. If $x>0$, the result is
$$
\begin{aligned}
W /(V\langle K\rangle)= & 5 / 12-x / 4+(1-x)^{2} / \\
& (8 \sqrt{ } x) \ln \left[(1+\sqrt{ } x)^{2} /|1-x|\right]
\end{aligned}
$$
and if $x<0$, we get
$$
\begin{aligned}
W /(V\langle K\rangle)= & 1 / 6-x / 2+|1+x| / 4+(1-x)^{2} / \\
& (8 \sqrt{ } x) \arcsin \left[-4 x /(1-x)^{2}\right]^{1 / 2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In both these expressions, $V$ is the domain volume. Expressions (6) and (7) are easy to generalized also for the case of anisotropic distribution of the directions of easy axis.

If the magnetization work is performed up to a given value of $M$, the calculations are to be done numerically. Bisection method is used to determine an effective magnetic field, which produces a given value of the magnetization. In our case, $M / M_{s}=0.79365$.

### B. Thermal dependence
Temperature is taken into account by means of the partition function formalism. $^{11,12}$ Calculated quantities: energy and magnetization for one domain are calculated as thermal averages $\ll \cdots \gg$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ll A(\theta) \gg= & \int d \varphi^{\prime} \int d \theta^{\prime} \sin \theta^{\prime} A\left(\theta, \theta^{\prime}, \varphi^{\prime}\right) \\
& \times \exp \left[-\beta V E\left(\theta, \theta^{\prime}, \varphi^{\prime}\right)\right] / Z,
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\theta^{\prime}$ and $\varphi^{\prime}$ describe the direction of local magnetic moment, $\theta$ and $\varphi$ indicate the direction of a local easy axis within a given domain. The result does not depend on $\varphi$ because of the uniaxial symmetry of the problem: both the external field, internal and applied stress are parallel to the $z$ axis. In Eq. (8), $Z$ is the partition function
$$
Z=\int d \varphi^{\prime} \int d \theta^{\prime} \sin \theta^{\prime} \exp \left[-\beta V E\left(\theta, \theta^{\prime}, \varphi^{\prime}\right)\right] \qquad (9)
$$
and the energy $E$ contains the Zeeman term. Finally, the magnetization work is calculated as the difference of the spatial averages of energy over $\theta$. Effective magnetic field is obtained which produces the given value of $M / M_{s}$, and this field is used to calculate the magnetization work $W$. Note that $W$ itself does not contain the Zeeman term. Note that $V$ is of the order of $10^{-21} \mathrm{~m}^{3}$ for the amorphous system and

### C. Directional distribution of easy axis

As can be seen in Table II, magnetic anisotropy frozen in the ribbon during the stress annealing is not much different for both samples C and D. We can deduce that the distribution of local easy axis is close to the equilibrium distribution. That is why we use the Gibbs probability: $P(\cos\theta)$ $=a\exp(-\gamma\cos^2\theta)$, where the factor $\gamma$ is the ratio of a local magnetic energy to the thermal energy of annealing, and $a$ is the normalization constant. Note that initially, the annealing temperature is larger than the Curie temperature of the amorphous phase; for longer annealing time, the Curie temperature increases. $^{7}$ The mechanism of creating the perpendicular magnetic anisotropy in varying conditions of annealing remains unclear. Here, the constant $\gamma$ is treated as a fitting constant, instead of $K_{\text{ann}}$. The only information which we put into $P(\cos\theta)$ is that it creates an easy plane perpendicular to the ribbon axis. This is the origin of the chosen angular dependence.

A technical note should be added, that the values of $\cos\theta$ are equally distributed on the $z$ axis, and therefore $\cos\theta$ is a proper argument for the function $P$ on a three-dimensional sphere. This geometrical fact is also used in the next subsection.

Within these assumptions, the magnetic work is calculated according to Eqs. (8) and (9), with two modifications. First, the probability $P(\cos\theta)$ is introduced to the averaging over $\theta$; the distribution is no more isotropic. The second modification is that now $K_{\text{ann}}=0$. The results of fitting are also given in the Table II, where they are marked as (b). The obtained curves are included in Fig. 4 as dotted lines.

### D. Kinetics of annealing

For the sample A, the Gibbs equilibrium distribution cannot be applied, because obviously further annealing modifies the micromagnetic state of the sample. We have found the distribution $P(\cos\theta)$ by an application of the Master Equations technique $^{12}$ in its numerical version

$$
\Delta P(i,t)=\Delta t\sum_{j}\left[-P(i,t)w(i,j)+P(j,t)w(j,i)\right], \quad (10)
$$

where $i\equiv\cos\theta$, $\Delta t$ is an arbitrary but small constant, $t$ is time expressed in $\Delta t$ units, and $w(i,j)=\exp\left[\gamma(i^2-j^2)/2\right]$ is the probability that the system flows from a state $i$ to a state $j$ between time $t$ and $t+\Delta t$. The value of the constant $\gamma$ is the same as this one found for the samples C and D. Here we neglect the difference of the kinetics, which comes from the difference between the annealing currents: 1.45 and 1.5 A. The time $t$, when the simulation is stopped, is the fitting parameter for sample 1. For $\Delta t=10^{-5}$, we have $t=1250$ time steps to obtain the accordance of $W(\sigma)$ with experimental data for the sample A. The fitted curve is given in Fig. 4(a) as a dotted line.

## IV. DISCUSSION

Our experimental data show that as a consequence of the stress annealing, a transverse magnetic anisotropy (in-plane easy axis perpendicular to the ribbon axis) is induced for the samples treated at a long annealing time. For a short time of annealing, the hysteresis loop shape indicates a magnetic softening linked mainly to the structural relaxation (with displacement of domain walls for low applied magnetic field), but a clear linear region is also observed. This soft magnetic behavior found for large applied magnetic field (linear curve) can be ascribed to the presence of an homogeneous transverse magnetic anisotropy, which is developed simultaneously to relaxation processes in the amorphous matrix. As a consequence of the stress annealing, the magnetization work increases with respect to that of the as-quenched sample, owing to the transverse induced anisotropy.

The random anisotropy model applied to the magnetic work describes reversible rotation of magnetic moments, but not the domain wall shift. The stress-induced magnetic anisotropy is possibly a partial justification of the application of this model, because in the presence of the transversal anisotropy the rotation of magnetic moments begins at smaller applied field. This argument is suggested by some irregularities of the experimental data on $W(\sigma)$ at small applied stress, which are shown in Fig. 3 a for the sample A.

The Eqs. (6) and (7) allow us to check the validity of the common opinion that the stress derivative of the magnetic work is equal to $3\lambda/2$. This is exactly true at $x=0$, which means that the actual distribution of local easy axis is not perturbed by an applied and/or internal stress. This statement is relevant when we deduce the magnetostriction from the data on the magnetization work, in particular for amorphous alloys. For a nanocrystalline system, stress annealing may be necessary for such an evaluation, because the system is very soft and $W$ becomes difficult to measure. In the presence of the transversal anisotropy, the whole curve $W(\sigma)$ can be fitted, as is done in the present work.

As we can see from Fig. 3, fitting does not provide a criterion if the perpendicular anisotropy should be taken into account separately (full curves), or as an appropriate modification of the directional distribution of local easy axis (dotted curve). In both cases, the accordance with experiment is of the same quality. In principle, both approaches describe the same effect: rotation of magnetic moments from the ribbon axis. However, the stress-induced anisotropy above $T_c$ is likely to be due to anelastic rearrangements of atoms, $^{13}$ and in principle it is not of magnetic origin. Here, the criterion is provided by the values of the fitting parameters. It is not likely that $\langle K\rangle$ increases with annealing, as is seen in Table II, results (b). On the contrary, magnetic domains increase

and contain more and more crystalline grains, which makes them more and more soft. In the Herzer model, local mag- netic anisotropy $\langle K\rangle$ varies as $V^{1 / 2}$, where $V$ is a domain volume (not to be mixed with the volume of crystalline grains).

The argument is in accordance with the reduction of the coercive field, which is produced by annealing, as is shown in Fig. 2. However, it is not clear how the modification of the directional distribution of local magnetic moments influencesthe validity of the formula $H_{c} \propto\langle K\rangle.^{3}$

Still, it seems to be obvious that actually the distribution $P(\cos \theta)$ is modified by annealing. However, we have dem onstrated that the influence of annealing cannot be reduced to this modification. Doing so, one would be forced to use the value of $\langle K\rangle$ for nanocrystalline samples, larger than the one for amorphous samples. On the other hand, if we intend to take into account both effects, we have to use many model parameters. Let us add that the application of Eq. (10) is profitable to the amorphous state itself, at least to the mecha- nism of reversible rotation.

As was mentioned at the end of Sec. II, the initial sus- ceptibility decreases for short annealing time, while the co- ercive field is still small. This should not be treated as a manifestation of FeB crystallites, but rather as a direct con- sequence of the stress-induced transversal anisotropy.

Concluding, stress annealing applied during the forma- tion of the nanocrystalline state in FINEMET ribbons in- duces the transversal magnetic anisotropy. The anisotropy influences the magnetic parameters, and in particular the magnetization work. This influence can be described within the random anisotropy model. Although the softness of the nanocrystalline phase is partially reduced by annealing, the magnetostriction coefficient $\lambda$ for stress-annealed samples can be evaluated more accurately by fitting the experimental data on the magnetization work to the model predictions.
