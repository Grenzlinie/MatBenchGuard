# Temperature and hydrogen-concentration dependence of hydrogen optic vibrational frequencies in $\alpha$ - and $\alpha'$-phase niobium hydride:
Static lattice strain and anharmonicity effects

## D. L. Tonks
Computational Physics Group, Applied Theoretical Physics Division,
Los Alamos National Laboratory, Los Alamos, New Mexico 87545

(Received 3 January 1984)

We calculate the shift and broadening of hydrogen optic vibrational frequencies in the $\alpha$ (lattice gas) and $\alpha'$ (lattice-liquid) phases of $NbH_{c}$ as a function of temperature $T$ and hydrogen concentration $c$ (H-Nb ratio). We assume the H's vibrate in local modes and linearly relate their vibrational frequencies to the static local lattice dilation through an anharmonicity parameter $\gamma$ estimated from inelastic neutron scattering data. The static local lattice dilations are obtained from a grand-canonical Monte Carlo calculation. The H lattice-gas or lattice-liquid model used in the Monte Carlo calculation is an approximation of that of Horner and Wagner, which involves a lattice-mediated H-H strain interaction and H-H hard core. Our calculations are done in that part of the region described by $0.1 < c < 0.5$ and $400 < T < 600$ K which lies outside the $\alpha$-$\alpha'$ coexistence region. Our calculated $c$- and $T$-dependent frequency shifts are roughly consistent with the available data. The calculated broadenings are too small. We estimate that the addition of thermal lattice fluctuations to the model would yield realistic broadenings.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Inelastic neutron scattering measurements $^{1-3}$ of the hydrogen optic-mode frequencies of Nb and Ta hydrides are now being carried out at considerably better resolution than previously attainable. It is, therefore, now appropriate to begin to study theoretically the temperature ($T$) and hydrogen- (H-) concentration shifts and broadenings of these frequencies that have been, heretofore, largely obscured in experimental uncertainties and typically ignored.

In this paper we perform a model calculation exploring the contribution of static, local lattice dilations and local-mode anharmonicities to H optic-mode frequency shifts and line broadening in $\alpha$- and $\alpha'$-phase $NbH_{c}$. We assume that the H's vibrate in local modes$^{4}$ and that a given local-mode excitation frequency shifts linearly with its static volume dilation according to a constant of proportionality. The magnitude of this constant is estimated via an H-Nb nearest-neighbor (NN) spring model from the measured anharmonicities $^{1}$ of highly excited H local-mode vibrational levels. Our model will be applied to calculate shifts in the local-mode excitation energy for excitation from the ground state to the first excited state, for which the most data is available. Our approximations are less appropriate for higher excitations to less harmoniclike states.

Our model does not include shifts due to H-induced changes in the electronic structure. The latter is important in the lattice modes and so may well be present for the H optic modes. However, such treatment must wait for a better understanding of the electronic structure. In our model the $T$ and $c$ dependence of the vibrational frequency shift and broadening arises from that of the local static lattice dilation, whose $T$ and $c$ dependence we obtain from a model for the lattice gas and lattice liquid.

The model of the lattice gas ($\alpha$ phase) and lattice liquid ($\alpha'$ phase) that we use is an approximation of that of Horner and Wagner$^{5}$ (HW), which they used to model the $\alpha$-$\alpha'$ phase boundary of $NbH_{c}$. The model involves a lattice-mediated H-H strain interaction and an H-H hard core. Using this model we numerically simulated the lattice-gas statistical mechanical state with a grand-canonical Monte Carlo calculation in the temperature range 400-600 K and the H-concentration (c) range 0-0.5. The concentration $c$ is the number of H's divided by the number of Nb's. We stayed in this region because below these temperatures the lattice-solid phases occur and above $c=0.5$ the HW model breaks down, predicting H concentrations much too high for the high-$c$ boundary of the $\alpha$-$\alpha'$ coexistence region. We further restrict our calculations out of the $\alpha$-$\alpha'$ coexistence region and thus do not address the questions of coherency stresses, spinodal decomposition, or inhomogeneous density waves treated by HW.

Our results for both the frequency shifts and broadening were not very temperature dependent. For example, at $c=0.3$, going from 425 to 600 K, the frequency shifted down by about 10%, while broadening increased by about 5%. The fractional frequency shift was roughly linear in $c$ (see Fig. 3), being approximately given by $-0.14c$. The broadening was not linear in $c$ and was small, e.g., about 2% for $c=0.3$ for $425 < T < 600$ K.

We did not include effects of the thermal expansion of the Nb host lattice. The overall thermal lattice volume dilation of pure Nb going from, say, 400 to 500 K, is about $0.0023.^{6}$ In our model this dilation produces a fractional frequency shift of about 0.004. This is an order of magnitude smaller than the calculated temperature effects dis-

cussed above and so we can assume that the thermal lat- tice expansion effects are small compared to those already included.

The new, high-resolution measurements mentioned ear- lier are, unfortunately, mostly for the $\beta$ phase for values of $c$ too large for our model. Hopefully, new $\alpha$-phase measurements at lower $c$ will be carried out. We did com pare our results with older measurements. The insensi- tivity of the vibrational frequency shifts to temperature in the $c$ and $T$ range considered seems to be born out experi mentally. $^{7,8}$ Also, our calculated frequency shift with $H$ concentration can be roughly confirmed by data of Refs. 8 and 9 (see Sec. IV). We note, however, that the data used for comparison are not precise enough for a final con-clusion. Our calculated frequency broadenings are $\frac{1}{3}$ to $\frac{1}{7}$  the size of the experimental ones, and so our model does not explain them, i.e., static strain effects do not dominate the broadenings. Thus, in the $c$ and $T$ range considered, the present model can account for a good part of the ex- perimental frequency $c$ dependence but not the broaden ing. This could be because the model lacks local dilations due to Nb-lattice thermal motions.

We estimated the broadening due to lattice thermal di- lations via our anharmonicity mechanism and found broadenings which were comparable to those observed in Ref. 2 for $NbH_{0.82}$ . Thus the thermal fluctuation mecha nism seems to be a promising one.

An outline of the rest of the paper follows. Section II contains a description of the lattice-gas-lattice-liquid model of HW and explains how we derived local volume dilations from it. Section III contains an estimate of the constant of proportionality relating local volume dilations to $H$ local-mode vibrational frequency shifts. An $Nb-H$  nearest-neighbor spring model is employed together with experimental data $^{1}$ on the local mode excitation energies. In Sec. IV we discuss our results, compare them with what experimental data are available, and give our con- clusions. In an Appendix we describe our grand-canonical Monte Carlo calculation. Considerations of accuracy and the results of several checks are discussed.

## II. LATTICE-GAS MODEL AND LOCAL VOLUME DILATIONS
In this section we describe the lattice-gas (lattice-liquid) model of Horner and Wagner and explain how, to a near approximation, the local static volume dilations can be obtained from it. Our measure of the local dilation is roughly the $H-Nb NN$ bond length. We show that it changes, on the average, by a fraction approximately equal to minus twice the average system energy per $H$ di vided by the trace of the double force tensor.

In $\alpha$ - and $\alpha'$ -phase $NbH_{c}$ the hydrogens are known to occupy the tetrahedral sites of the bcc host lattice. The interstitial sites are occupied randomly. In their model for these phases HW assumed that the $H-H$ interaction consisted of a hard-core repulsion and a lattice-mediated lattice strain interaction. The hard-core repulsion was taken to exclude the occupation by other hydrogens of up to third-nearest neighbors in the interstitial tetrahedral lattice. This hard core is needed to explain why, in the solidlike phases $(\beta, \delta$ , etc.) typically, less than one-sixth of the available interstitial sites are occupied.

The hard core is an approximation of the direct, screened Coulomb repulsion between hydrogens and thus is only accurate for lower $H$ concentrations, where the H's are not too close together. For example, the $\alpha-\alpha'$ phase boundary calculated by HW (Ref. 5) becomes increasingly inaccurate for $c>0.5$ . The predicted concentrations of the high- $c$ portion of the boundary are much too large. Thus our Monte Carlo calculation, which uses an approxi- mation of the HW model, also becomes unreliable for c>0.5.

The lattice strain $H-H$ interaction can be visualized as one $H$ dilating the lattice in its neighborhood, which creates a lattice strain field that interacts with a second hydrogen. The dilation is caused by the $H$ lattice interac tion that was taken by HW to be constant longitudinal forces of equal magnitude, $f_{1}$ , on the $Nb NN$ of a given H, and constant longitudinal forces of a different magni- tude, $f_{2}$ , but of the same sign, on the $Nb$ next $NN$ . The f2/f, ratio was set at approximately 0.23 to make the re- sulting double force tensor isotropic, $^{10}$ in keeping with the experimental fact that $H$ in $Nb$ is a dilation center. $^{10}$ The magnitude of $f_{1}$ can then be fixed by the experimental volume dilation of hydration. $^{10} HW$ took the trace of the double force tensor, $Tr Q$ , to be $10 eV$ , the value we will use. The other ingredient of the $H-H$ interaction is the lattice strain field produced in response to the $H-Nb$  forces. For short distances, this was calculated by HW by using static-lattice harmonic Green's functions based on a Born-von Karmán phonon model for an infinite, undilat- ed $Nb$ lattice. For longer distances and for system states above the $\alpha-\alpha'$ coexistence region, a mean-field approach was used which allowed the experimental bulk modulus to be employed. Through the bulk modulus, surface expan- sion effects are included, which, as these authors explain, are the very mechanism which affects the $\alpha-\alpha'$ phase tran sition. Surface expansion effects are important in our cal- culation as well.

We now derive the lattice dilations of a given $H$ config uration by writing and minimizing the hydrogen-lattice system potential energy. We will ignore the hard cores for now. One can add them later by simply omitting $H$ con figurations where hard cores overlap. Thus, we write $P\{n_{k}\}$ , the hydrogen-lattice system potential energy for $H$  configuration $\{n_{k}\}$ , as
$$P\left\{n_{k}\right\}=\frac{1}{2} \underline{\tilde{X}} \underline{V} \underline{X}+\underline{\tilde{L}}\left\{n_{k}\right\} \underline{X}. \quad \text { (1) }$$

In Eq. (1) $\underline{X}$ is the (column) vector of Cartesian $Nb$ dis placements, referred to perfect $Nb$ -lattice equilibrium sites, and $\underline{\tilde{X}}$ is its transpose (a row vector). $\underline{V}$ is the har monic potential-energy matrix (surface effects included), and $-\underline{L}\{n_{k}\}$ is the vector of forces on $Nb$ atoms due to the presence of hydrogens. HW took $\underline{V}$ to be that of the pure $Nb$ host lattice. The vector $\underline{L}\{n_{k}\}$ is taken to equal $\sum_{k} n_{k} \underline{l}^{k}$ , where $-\underline{l}^{k}$ is the vector of forces on $Nb$ atoms due to an $H$ at interstitial site $k$ and $n_{k}$ is the $H$ occupation, i.e., 0 or 1, of the site $k$ . Thus, the forces on the $Nb$  atoms are additive, which will result in the $H-H$ interac tions being two body in nature. Henceforth, we will drop

the $\{n_k\}$ and write $P$ and $\underline{L}$.

When $\underline{X}=0$ the system is not in equilibrium. To find $\underline{X}_{0}$, the equilibrium displacements for a given $\mathrm{H}$ configuration, we minimize $P$ with respect to $\underline{X}$. This is easily done with the result that $\underline{X}_{0}$ is $-\underline{V} \underline{L}^{-1}$ and $E$, the relaxation energy, is $-\frac{1}{2} \underline{\tilde{L}} \underline{V}^{-1} \underline{L}$. Expressing $\underline{L}$ in terms of its components results in
$$
\underline{X}_{0}=-\sum_{k} \underline{V}^{-1} \underline{l}^{k} n_{k}
$$
and
$$
E=\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j, k} \epsilon_{j k}^{\prime} n_{j} n_{k},
$$
where
$$
\epsilon_{j k}^{\prime} \equiv-\underline{\tilde{l}}^{j} \underline{V}^{-1} \underline{l}^{k}. \tag{2}
$$

The $\epsilon_{j k}^{\prime}$ constitute the H-H pair interaction energies and are identical with the $W_{j k}$ of HW.

As our measure of the local lattice dilation around an $\mathrm{H}$ at site $i$, we will use the overlap between the local $\mathrm{Nb}$ displacements and the vector given by $-\underline{l}^{i} /\left(2\left|\underline{l}^{i}\right|\right)$. This vector approximately describes an $\mathrm{Nb}$ NN cage dilation about site $i$ since the second-NN components of $\underline{l}^{i}$ are only 0.23 times the size of the first. Thus, we define
$$
\delta r_{i} \equiv-\underline{\tilde{l}}^{i} \underline{X}_{0} /\left(2\left|\underline{l}^{i}\right|\right)=-\sum_{k} \epsilon_{i k}^{\prime} n_{k} /\left(2\left|\underline{l}^{i}\right|\right) \tag{3}
$$
substituting in earlier expression for $X_{0}$. The factor of 2 in the denominator makes $\delta r_{i}$ roughly the longitudinal stretch of a single $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{Nb}$ bond length. Finally, we divide $\delta r_{i}$ by
$$
r_{0} \equiv(\operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q} /\left(2\left|\underline{l}^{i}\right|\right), \tag{4}
$$
where $\underline{Q}$ is the double force tensor, to obtain $\delta r_{i} / r_{0}$, our measure of the local lattice dilation:
$$
\delta r_{i} / r_{0} \equiv-\sum_{k} \epsilon_{i k}^{\prime} n_{k} / \operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q}. \tag{5}
$$

If one sets $f_{2}$ in $\underline{Q}$ to 0 , then $r_{0}$ becomes $5^{1 / 2} a_{0} / 4$, the $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{Nb}$ NN distance where $a_{0}$ is the cubic lattice constant. Since $f_{2}$ accounts for only $27 \%$ of $\operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q}, r_{0}$ is approximately the $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{Nb}$ NN distance and $\delta r_{i} / r_{0}$ is approximately the fractional H-Nb NN length change. We use this approximation in order to use the published $\tilde{W}_{i j}$ values of HW in our calculation.

We note that $\delta r_{i} / r_{0}$ gives exactly the fractional longitudinal $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{Nb}$ NN bond change for a "true local lattice dilation," i.e., when the ratio of second-NN displacements to first NN displacements is $\left(\frac{13}{5}\right)^{1 / 2}$. We finally average $\delta r_{2} / r_{0}$ over all occupied sites $i$ to obtain $\delta \bar{r} / r_{0}$, the averaged version, and arrive at
$$
\delta \bar{r} / r_{0}=-\sum_{i, k} \epsilon_{i k}^{\prime} n_{i} n_{k} /(\operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q} N), \tag{6}
$$
where $N$ is the number of hydrogens. Recalling that $E$ is $\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i, k} \epsilon_{i k}^{\prime} n_{i} n_{k}$, we easily see that $\delta \bar{r} / r_{0}$ equals $-2 E /(\operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q} N)$, i.e., minus twice the energy per $\mathrm{H}$ divided by the double force tensor trace.

We mention immediately that in calculating $E$, we will leave out the self-energy, and so our calculated $\delta \bar{r} / r_{0}$ will contain only dilations around an $\mathrm{H}$ due to other H's and not due to the $\mathrm{H}$ itself. The part we include contains all the $c$ - and $T$-dependent effects.

We digress for a moment to briefly describe, for a system outside the $\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}$ coexistence region, how HW treated surface effects and long-range interactions in a mean-field approximation. In our calculations we use a similar approximation. First of all, HW separated $E$ into a surface-effects part and an infinite-lattice part by separating $\epsilon_{j k}^{\prime}$ into an infinite-lattice part, which we call $\epsilon_{j k}$, and the remaining surface-dependent part, which we call $\Delta \epsilon_{j k}$. They then reasoned that, in the large-system limit, the surface-effects part of $E$, i.e., $\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j, k} \Delta \epsilon_{j k} n_{j} n_{k}$, is accurately treated by the mean field, i.e., replacing $n_{k}$ by $N / N_{H}$, where $N_{H}$ is the number of interstitial sites. Finally, they divided up the $\epsilon_{j k} n_{j} n_{k}$ terms of the infinite-lattice part of $E$ into a short-range and a long-range part, according to the $j$ - $k$ distance. The mean-field approximation was then used on the long-range part. We note that resulting infinite-lattice mean-field contribution to $E$ was small compared to the surface-effects mean-field contribution.

These approximations result in the following approximate form for $E$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j, k}^{\prime \prime} \epsilon_{j k} n_{j} n_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\left(N / N_{H}\right)^{2} & \sum_{i, j}^{\prime \prime} \epsilon_{i j} \\
& -\frac{1}{2} N^{2}(\operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q} / 3)^{2} K_{T} / U, \quad(7)
\end{aligned}
$$
where $K_{T}$ is the host-lattice isothermal compressibility, $^{11}$ and $U$ is the host-lattice volume. The double prime on the sum means that only the short-range terms are to be included, i.e., for which the $j$ - $k$ separation is less than or equal to a certain defined distance. In this derivation the identity
$$
\sum_{j, k} \epsilon_{j k}^{\prime}=-N_{H}^{2}(\operatorname{Tr} \underline{Q} / 3)^{2} K_{T} / U \tag{8}
$$
is used. $^{5}$ With these approximations the surface part of $\epsilon_{j k}$ enters the formalism through $K_{T}$.

Our $\epsilon_{j k}$ are formally identical with the $\tilde{W}_{j k}$ of HW. However, in our calculation we used approximations to the HW $\tilde{W}_{j k}$, averaging those $\tilde{W}_{j k}$ with the same $j$ - $k$ separation to obtain ten $\bar{\epsilon}_{j k}$ values depending only on the $j$ - $k$ separation. Since the first seven-shell $\tilde{W}_{j k}$ values are given in Table I of Ref. 5 as depending only on the $j$ - $k$ separation to start with, the averaging is not such a drastic approximation. We averaged the HW $\tilde{W}_{j k}$ values for the first nine H-H separation distances to obtain $\bar{\epsilon}_{j k}$ values for shells $0-9$: $-4174,-2907,-1967,-1218$, $-589,-426,-212,86,190$, and $500 \mathrm{~K}$, respectively. The zeroth-shell value is for the self-energy. Only the fourth through ninth shell values were actually used due to the hard cores and the neglect of the self-energies. The ninth shell $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{Nb}$ distance is the cutoff distance for the short-range part of our $E$, as described above. HW used a longer cutoff distance. We note that HW calculated their $W_{j k}$ in the approximation of an infinite, perfect $\mathrm{Nb}$ lattice, i.e., no dilations or force-constant changes due to the H's were included.

Besides $\delta \bar{r}_{i} / r_{0}$, we also calculated the root-mean-square deviation of $\delta r_{i} / r_{0}$ for the whole lattice. The quantity was then multiplied by the anharmonicity constant of proportionality mentioned earlier to obtain an estimate of the half-width broadening of the H vibrational frequency due to local static lattice strains.

### III. ESTIMATE OF THE CONSTANT OF PROPORTIONALITY RELATING LOCAL-MODE FREQUENCY SHIFT TO THE LOCAL VOLUME DILATION

In this section we estimate the constant of proportionality relating the local-mode frequency shift to the local volume dilation of site $i$ as measured by $\delta r_{i} / r_{0}$. By the local-mode frequency we mean the energy of excitation from the ground state to the first excited state. In $\mathrm{NbH}_{c}$ this frequency is split, due to the tetragonal site symmetry, into two components, a singlet and a higher-lying doublet. We will calculate the proportionality constant for both. A perfect bcc lattice is assumed. We will use an Nb NN spring model, whose parameters will be estimated from data $^{1,12}$ giving local mode excitation frequencies.

First of all let $\psi\left(r_{i}^{\alpha}\right)$ be the interaction potential between the $\mathrm{H}$ at interstitial site $i$ and a given NN Nb atom $\alpha$ as a function of the separation distance, $r_{i}^{\alpha}$. Let $\psi^{\prime}$ be $\partial \psi\left(r_{i}^{\alpha}\right) / \partial r_{i}^{\alpha}$ evaluated at $\rho_{0}$, the H-Nb NN distance, with similar definitions for higher derivatives. It is then not difficult to show that $\omega_{z}$, the $\mathrm{H}$ singlet vibration frequency, is
$$
\omega_{z}=\left[\left(3.2 \psi^{\prime} / \rho_{0}+0.8 \psi^{\prime \prime}\right) / M_{0}\right]^{1 / 2}, \quad(9)
$$
where $M_{0}$ is the hydrogen mass. This result can be obtained by taking $\partial^{2} / \partial z^{2}$ of the total NN spring energy $\sum_{\alpha} \psi\left(r_{i}^{\alpha}\right)$, where $z$ is the $\mathrm{H}$ displacement in the direction of the singlet vibration. See Refs. 1 and 5 for further details of the site geometry. The numerical factors in the expression for $\omega_{z}$ are exact and arise from site geometry. They also apply for a local volume dilation. In a similar fashion $\omega_{x}$, the doublet vibrational frequency, is found to be
$$
\omega_{x}=\left[\left(2.4 \psi^{\prime} / \rho_{0}+1.6 \psi^{\prime \prime}\right) / M_{0}\right]^{1 / 2}. \quad(10)
$$
The $x$ subscript indicates a displacement in the direction of one of the two degenerate normal mode coordinates. Note that if $\psi^{\prime}=0, \omega_{x} / \omega_{z}=2^{1 / 2}$, which is approximately observed experimentally.

Using the above expressions, it is straightforward to assume an Nb NN cage dilation, parametrize it by using $\rho_{0, i}$, the H-Nb bond length for the Nb at site $i$, and then take the derivative of $\omega_{x}$ and $\omega_{z}$ with respect to $\rho_{0, i}$. One obtains
$$
\begin{aligned}
& d \omega_{z} / \omega_{z}=\gamma_{z} d \rho_{0, i} / \rho_{0}, \\
& d \omega_{x} / \omega_{x}=\gamma_{x} d \rho_{0, i} / \rho_{0},
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\gamma_{z}$ and $\gamma_{x}$, the proportionality constants, equal
$$
\frac{1}{2}\left(-3.2 \psi^{\prime} / \rho_{0}+3.2 \psi^{\prime \prime}+0.8 \rho_{0} \psi^{\prime \prime \prime}\right) /\left(3.2 \psi^{\prime} / \rho_{0}+0.8 \psi^{\prime \prime}\right)
$$
and
$$
\begin{array}{r}
\frac{1}{2}\left(-2.4 \psi^{\prime} / \rho_{0}+2.4 \psi^{\prime \prime}+1.6 \rho_{0} \psi^{\prime \prime \prime}\right) /\left(2.4 \psi^{\prime} / \rho_{0}+1.6 \psi^{\prime \prime}\right), \\
(12)
\end{array}
$$
respectively. We will substitute our calculated $\delta \bar{r} / r_{0}$ values in place of $d \rho_{0, i} / \rho_{0}$ above to obtain our estimate of the fractional frequency shift. The above equations also hold if one takes the H-Nb NN distance to differ from $\rho_{0}$, the bcc lattice value, as in the real system where the H's expand the NN cage. Thus, the following values for $\gamma_{z}$ and $\gamma_{x}$, which are derived from data, take into account the expansion of the Nb NN cage. The calculated values of $\gamma$ are more realistic than the calculated energy values, which assume an undilated lattice.

To approximately evaluate the above expressions for $\gamma_{z}$ and $\gamma_{x}$, we estimated $\psi^{\prime \prime \prime}$ and the ratio $\psi^{\prime} /\left(\rho_{0} \psi^{\prime \prime}\right)$. We estimated the $\psi^{\prime} /\left(\rho_{0} \psi^{\prime \prime}\right)$ ratio using the above equations for $\omega_{z}$ and $\omega_{x}$, and the values $\hbar \omega_{z}=135.2 \mathrm{meV}$ and $\hbar \omega_{x}=176.2 \mathrm{meV}$ obtained from inelastic scattering neutron data of the $\beta$-phase hydride, deuteride, and tritide. $^{12}$ These values were extracted from the data values by fitting the experimental frequencies to the form
$$
\alpha\left(M / M_{0}\right)^{-1 / 2}+\beta\left(M / M_{0}\right)^{-1}, \quad(13)
$$
where $M$ is the $\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{D}$, or tritium mass and where $\beta\left(M / M_{0}\right)^{-1}$ is due to anharmonicity. The values given correspond to the $\alpha$. See Ref. 1 for a more complete discussion. Our result was that $\psi^{\prime} /\left(\rho_{0} \psi^{\prime \prime}\right) \approx 0.08$.

The value for $\psi^{\prime \prime \prime}$ was estimated from an experimental value for the quantity
$$
12 c_{4, z}\left[\hbar /\left(2 M_{0} \omega_{z}\right)\right]^{2} \quad(14)
$$
extracted in Ref. 1 from inelastic neutron scattering data for $\mathrm{H}$ vibrational excitation energies of the $\beta$ phase. The constant $c_{4, z}$ times $z^{4}$ equals the quartic anharmonic term in the potential energy of the singlet mode. The values of this quantity extracted for various $\mathrm{Nb} \mathrm{H}_{c}$ systems were about equal to $-10 \mathrm{meV}$, the value we will use for the $\gamma$ estimates. To obtain $\psi^{\prime \prime \prime}$ from this value, we used the NN spring model formula $c_{4, z}$,
$$
c_{4, z}=\left(0.16 \psi^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}+3.8 \psi^{\prime \prime \prime} / \rho_{0}\right) / 24, \quad(15)
$$
dropping the $0.16 \psi^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}$ term which is likely to be small compared with the $3.84 \psi^{\prime \prime \prime} / \rho_{0}$ term. This formula is straightforward to derive by taking four $z$ derivatives of the total NN spring energy. Thus, we assume that
$$
c_{4, z} \approx 3.84 \psi^{\prime \prime \prime} /\left(24 \rho_{0}\right). \quad(16)
$$

The values obtained for $\gamma_{z}$ and $\gamma_{x}$ from these approximations were -5.5 and -7.5, respectively.

In our calculation we will use $\gamma=\gamma_{z}=\gamma_{x} \approx-5$ as a representative value for both singlet and doublet, keeping in mind that the $\gamma_{x}$ for the doublet is somewhat larger than that for the singlet. Thus, we will use
$$
\left(\omega-\omega_{0}\right) / \omega_{0} \equiv \gamma \delta \bar{r} / r_{0} \quad(17)
$$
for both singlet and doublet, where $\omega$ and $\omega_{0}$ are the shifted and $c=0$ frequencies of either.

This $\gamma$ value depends on the NN spring approximation, the pure bcc lattice approximation, the $\beta$-phase $\omega_{x}$ and $\omega_{z}$

values, the neglect of $\psi^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}$ in $c_{4, z}$ and the $\beta$-phase $c_{4, z}$ value. The $\mathrm{H}$ vibration is still approximately a local mode in the dense $\beta$ phase, $^{4}$ so using $\beta$-phase values, instead of $\alpha$-phase values, should not be too severe an approximation. Also, the $\beta$-phase tetragonal distortion of the bcc lattice is not large. Our neglect of $\psi^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}$ is certainly plausible, but we can say no more about its validity than that. There is evidence that the NN spring model can be improved by adding second NN springs. $^{3}$ Thus, our $\gamma=-5$ value is to be taken as approximate and qualitative, rather than precise.

Recently Sugimoto and Fukai $^{13}$ have calculated $\mathrm{H}$ vibrational frequencies and wave functions using a double Born-Meyer H-Nb interaction. They conclude that the ground state and first excited $\mathrm{H}$ vibrational state are well localized and harmoniclike, which are necessary presuppositions to this work, since it involves a perturbation on a harmonic model. They also calculated vibrational frequencies (excitation frequencies) as a function of displacements of the $\mathrm{Nb} \mathrm{NN}$ away from their equilibrium positions. Thus we can use their numbers to estimate $\gamma$ in another way. Since no figure is given for the $\mathrm{Nb}$ system, we will draw conclusions from the figure for the Ta system, whose $\mathrm{H}$ vibrations are similar to those in $\mathrm{Nb}$. From their Fig. 4(a) we estimate a $\gamma$ of roughly -8 for $\mathrm{TaH}_{c}$ for the singlet, by assuming a linear frequency shift and using $Q /(a / 2)$ values of about 0.018 and 0 . This value supports our $\gamma=-5$ value.

We note that these authors also calculated the frequency shift for a nondilation-type NN Ta displacement and found it larger than that for the NN cage dilation. If this is true for the real $\mathrm{Nb}$ system, our NN cage dilation assumption would require generalization. In that case, our results should still have qualitative validity, however.

## IV. RESULTS, COMPARISON WITH EXPERIMENT, AND CONCLUSIONS

For clarity, we will first describe how $\Delta E / N$ and $\Delta \omega / \omega_{0}$ of Fig. 1 were calculated. First, the root-mean-square (rms) deviation of the individual particle energies was calculated for the last configuration of each block of Monte Carlo (MC) configurations. (See the Appendix.) The individual particle energy of particle $i$, for example, is the energy of interaction of this particle with all the other H's, which would be written as $\sum_{j \neq i} \epsilon_{i j}^{\prime} n_{j}$, before the application of the approximations described in Sec. II. The configurational rms deviations calculated were then averaged and the result divided by two to get the $\Delta E / N$ values. The factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ is necessary to make $\Delta E / N$ comparable with $E / N$, for which a $\frac{1}{2}$ factor is included to avoid the double counting of bonds. $\Delta E / N$ was then multiplied by $2 \gamma / \operatorname{Tr} Q$ to get the value of $\Delta \omega / \omega_{0}$, the half-width vibrational frequency broadening estimate.

Figure 2 shows the MC calculated $c$ as a function of $\mu$, the chemical potential for various temperatures. The flatness of the $450-\mathrm{K}$ curve indicates that the $\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}$ phase boundary is near. The 425- and 400-K curves stop roughly at the calculated incoherent $\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}$ coexistence region boundary, i.e., where the density $c$ jumped to a larger value with a slight increase in $\mu$.

Immediately apparent from the $E/N$ and $\Delta E/N$ results of Figs. 3 and 1 is the smallness of the temperature dependence and the approximate linearity of $E/N$ with $c$. These properties can both be largely explained by the large contribution, about $57\%$, of the mean-field part of $E$ to the total. This part of $E$ is exactly linear in $c$, and temperature independent. If the mean-field part were removed, the temperature dependence of $E$ would more than double and the results would resemble more the results of other lattice-gas calculations.

To help understand our $E/N$ results, we calculated $E/N$ again, using a pair approximation (PA) for the short-range part of the infinite-lattice energy term. The short-range part becomes
$$
\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j, k}^{\prime \prime} \overline{\epsilon}_{j k}(c / 6)^{2} \exp \left(-\overline{\epsilon}_{j k} / k_{B} T\right)
\tag{18}
$$
in this approximation. The hard core is included by omitting terms for which the $i$-$j$ distance is too small. The results are shown in Fig. 4. The $c=0.1$ results for temperatures 450-600 K agree to within $5-13\%$ with corresponding values of Fig. 3 when the latter are interpolated linearly in $c$ to $c=0.1$. The $c$ dependence of the 600-K values in Fig. 4 also agrees reasonably well with that of Fig. 3. (The $c=0.4$ values agree to within $13\%$.) But for $c>0.1$ and for lower temperatures, the pair-approximation values are rather larger than those of the MC calculation. Thus the pair-approximation calculation agrees reasonably well with the MC calculation when $c$ is small $(\approx 0.1)$ or $T$ is high (600 K for $c>0.4$).

For $c>0.2$ the MC $E/N$ calculation is rather insensitive to temperature variation in comparison to the PA calculation. This insensitivity is only partly due to the influence of the hard cores. Raising the temperature from 600 to $10^{5}$ K for $c=0.3$ changes $E/N$ (MC-canonical version$^{14}$) from about $-490$ to about $-278$ K. Thus, the hard cores are not yet dominant at 600 K. The $T$ insensitivity is due to other causes.

We also calculated MC (canonical version) and PA pair-correlation functions to see how well the MC functions were reproduced by the PA versions. We calculated $\zeta_{j}^{\prime}(i)$, where $\zeta_{j}^{\prime}(i)$ is the probability of occupation of a site in shell $i$ surrounding an occupied site $j$. We divided $\zeta_{j}^{\prime}(i)$ by $c/6$ to obtain $\zeta_{j}(i)$, a normalized quantity that equals 1 for no short-range order. $\zeta_{j}(i)$ for a pair approximation is just $\exp(-\overline{\epsilon}_{j k}/k_{B}T)$ and is zero within the hard cores. For $T=600$ K and $c=0.1$, the $\zeta_{j}(i)$ values for $i=4,9$ for the MC calculation were 2.24, 1.51, 1.20, 0.94, 0.98, and 0.42; and for the PA calculation were 2.67, 2.03, 1.42, 0.87, 0.73, and 0.43. The MC values resemble the PA values. The resemblance is worse for the lower temperature, as one would expect, but is still strong enough to confirm the earlier conclusion that the PA is reasonable at $c\approx 0.1$.

Comparison of our $(\omega-\omega_{0})/\omega_{0}$ and $\Delta\omega/\omega_{0}$ results with experiment is problematical because most of the data available for the $\alpha$-$\alpha'$ phases are either of insufficient resolution$^{7-9}$ or at high densities,$^{2}$ where the HW lattice-gas model is inadequate. New high-resolution measurements$^{1-3}$ are mostly for the $\beta$ phase at low temperatures. Also, for the $\alpha,\alpha'$ measurements, at the higher temperatures where these phases exist, peak positions are obscured by large inelastic neutron scattering linewidths due to anharmonicity and other mechanisms. As a rough estimate, we obtain the experimental shift of the vibrational frequencies with $c$ by putting together vibrational frequencies of Refs. 8 and 9. When one compares the $c=0.05$, $T=(473\ \text{K})\hbar\omega_{z}=114$ meV, and $\hbar\omega_{x}=177$

meV frequencies of Ref. 9 with the c=0.46, $T=(483\ \text{K}\ \hbar\omega_z=107.52$ meV, and $\hbar\omega_x=166.87$ meV frequencies of Ref. 8, one obtains $(\omega_{0.46}-\omega_{0.05})/\omega_{0.05}$ values of -0.06 and -0.06, respectively, for the singlet (z) and the doublet (x). Both values are close to our value of -0.06 calculated using our relation $(\omega-\omega_0)/\omega_0=-0.14c$. In obtaining $\hbar\omega_x=166.87$ meV we have averaged the two values given in Ref. 8. The -0.14 slope estimated from Fig. 3 is not very temperature dependent. In view of the uncertainties in the data, we can cautiously claim that the calculation and data are of the same order of magnitude.

We now consider the shift with temperature. We will make a rough comparison with data having a c somewhat larger than our model is valid for. For the c=0.59 data of Ref. 8, $\omega_z$ shifts down by about 3% in going from 383 to 483 K, and $\omega_x$ does not shift at all. According to Fig. 3, in going from 450 to 500 K the frequency should shift down by about 2.5% for both frequencies at about c=0.55. (For this estimate the two 500-K points closest to c=0.55 were extrapolated linearly to c=0.55.) Thus, this data on temperature shifts in $\omega_z$ and $\omega_x$ are not inconsistent with the calculation, at least in the order of magnitude.

We also consider the vibrational widths. Reference 8 claims fractional widths of about 0.36 and 0.40 [full width at half maximum (FWHM)] for the c=0.46, T=483-K data, while Ref. 2 claims fractional widths of 0.17 (FWHM) to 0.21 (FWHM) for $\alpha$-phase Nb $\text{H}_{0.82}$ data at 433 K.¹⁵ These values correspond to singlet and doublet, respectively. These values are considerably larger than the $\Delta\omega/\omega_0$ value of about 0.025 (half-width) taken from Fig. 1 for c=0.45 and T=450 K. Thus, it seems clear that the static elastic strain mechanism cannot account for the experimental widths seen for $\alpha$- and $\alpha'$-phase systems of concentration c<0.5, for which our model is valid.

In Ref. 13, broadening due to thermal fluctuations was estimated to be comparable with experiment. We estimated the half-width thermal fluctuation broadening for our model by calculating the anharmonic shift for a thermally induced NN cage dilation of energy $k_BT$ assuming only the NN's move. This is straightforwardly done by finding the spring constant for the NN distortion, which can be expressed using $\epsilon_{ii}$ and $\text{Tr}\underline{Q}$. The result is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\omega_T/\omega_0&=\gamma(\delta r_i/r_0)_T\approx-\gamma(\epsilon_{ii}/\text{Tr}\underline{Q})(2k_BT/|\epsilon_{ii}|)^{1/2} \\
&=\gamma(0.036)(k_BT/2087)^{1/2},
\end{aligned}\tag{19}
$$
where the T subscript indicates the thermal fluctuation approximation. $k_BT$ was substituted for the energy of the dilation-producing $\delta r_i$. By way of approximation, we have assumed only NN forces occur in $\underline{Q}$ and $\epsilon_{ii}$. For T=600 K, $\Delta\omega_T/\omega_0$ equals 0.095, which implies a full-width value of 0.19. This value is comparable to the values 0.17 and 0.21 for $\text{NbH}_{0.82}$ of Ref. 2. It is about one-half of the values of Ref. 8. [See Ref. 15(a).] Thus, it appears that the addition of thermal fluctuations to our model could account for experimental widths. If this is so, the broadenings should be only slightly sensitive to c. In a better calculation of this effect, one would expand the NN cage dilation coordinate in terms of lattice vibrations. We note that, in our model, the thermal fluctuations do not shift the average vibrational frequency, so our model shift predictions do not need modification for T fluctuations.

Large vibrational frequency shifts (and broadenings) were reported in Ref. 2 for $\text{NbD}_{0.85}$ as the temperature was lowered through the $\alpha'$-$\beta$-phase boundary. Our model is unable to predict these, apparently in part because our modified HW lattice-gas model is inadequate for the high c involved here. We speculate that in addition a better description of the direct, electronic H-H interaction is necessary, a description by which an H near a given H will strongly and directly perturb its electronic environment.

We briefly discuss the physical reasonableness of the H-lattice interaction model used and of the lattice displacements resulting therefrom. This model reproduces the physical macroscopic displacements since its double force tensor is that extracted from experiment.⁵ Recent work¹⁶ on the static Debye-Waller factor has shown that a model very similar to the one used here predicts the Nb NN displacements of an H to "excellent agreement." Our model, then, is good for the macroscopic displacement field on the one hand and the NN displacements on the other. Hence, it should reasonably represent our H-H interactions whose separation distances lie in between. Other model choices of microscopic H-Nb forces might be possible. For example, one could choose them to affect Nb's further from the H, some possibly pointing in rather than out. However, if they were fitted to the experimental double force tensor and to the Nb NN displacements found above,¹⁶ the results should not differ much from our present results. We note that if only $f_1$ and $f_2$ are present in the model, both are fixed by the two conditions of fitting the macroscopic volume expansion and its cubic symmetry.¹⁰ Both must then be positive and $f_2$ is small, only $0.23f_1$.

In conclusion, for $\alpha,\alpha'$ systems with c<0.5, it appears that our lattice strain anharmonicity model can account for much of the observed H vibrational frequency shifts with c and possibly the shifts with temperature also. The predicted broadenings are much too small, however, and so additional broadening mechanisms are called for. According to our estimate and those of Sugimoto and Fukai,¹³ thermally induced lattice strains are a good candidate. We note that electronic effects, not included here, may well be necessary for a complete explanation of the effects, even for low c.¹⁷

## APPENDIX: DETAILS OF THE MONTE CARLO CALCULATION
In this Appendix we briefly describe our grand-canonical Monte Carlo calculation, discussing various checks and the statistical accuracy of the results. The grand-canonical ensemble for the H lattice gas or lattice liquid was simulated by moving H's, creating H's, and

destroying them according to the scheme described by Barker and Henderson. $^{18}$ Which of these three to use at a given step was chosen at random. Candidate H's to de- stroy or move and candidate creation sites were also chosen at random. We point out that the chemical poten- tial $\mu$ is fixed and the $H$ particle number fluctuates about an average in this scheme. We chose a periodic- boundary-type system of cubic shape with an edge length of $6 a_{0}$ . There are 2592 interstitial sites in this system. We included nine NN shells for each $H$ site for a total of68 neighbor sites per $H$ site. The nine-shell radius is about $a_{0}$ .

Some idea of the error incurred by using only nine NN shells in the short-range or explicitly calculated part of the elastic interaction can be obtained by comparing the sum $\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j}^{\prime \prime} \bar{\epsilon}_{i j}$ with $-\frac{1}{2}(Tr \underline{Q} / 3)^{2} N_{H} /(U c_{11})$ , its isotropic elastic limit. $^{5}$ The double prime means that only nine NN shells are included in the $j$ sum. If $\epsilon_{i j}$ were to be used and if the $j$ sum included enough NN shells, the two quantities should be identical, i.e., the maximum interac- tion distance of the sum would then be large enough for all the important interactions of field H's with a given H to have been included. For our nine-NN-shell case
$$\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j}^{\prime \prime} \bar{\epsilon}_{i j}=-14212 \mathrm{~K}, \quad \text { (A1) }$$
 while the other expression equals $-14000 ~K.^{5}$ Thus, the nine-NN-shell approximation does not seem too poor when judged in this way.

To get an idea of the sensitivity of our results to the $\bar{\epsilon}_{i j}$  values used, we changed our eight- and nine-shell values from 190 to $230 ~K$ and from 500 to $460 ~K$ , respectively, and did the $T=600 ~K$ and $c \approx 0.3,0.4$ , and 0.48 calcula tions again. The same chemical potential values as before were used. Since our eight- and nine-shell $\bar{\epsilon}_{i j}$ values were the only ones obtained from those of HW by averaging, the others not requiring averaging, this calculation also gives some idea of the error incurred by the averaging. In all three cases the average number of hydrogens fell by about $4 \%$ , the average energy per particle fell in magni tude by about $6 \%$ , and the rms deviation of the individual particle energies fell by less than $3 \%$ . Thus, the results were not too sensitive to the changes.

We estimated the statistical error of our calculated quantities by the method of Wood. $^{19}$ First, the MC steps were divided into groups or blocks with a fixed number of successive steps in each. Then, the energy per $H$ and the particle number $N$ were averaged over each block to ob tain block values. The block values, hopefully being un- correlated, were then used for the estimate of statistical error (see Ref. 19). The block values were averaged to ob- tain the final averages. We let the number of steps in each block be roughly ten times the average number of hydrogens. The number of blocks included was 700 to900, requiring roughly 150 to 200 seconds of computer time (CDC 7600) per run. The success rate per step was roughly the same for all three kinds of steps and for the $600 ~K$ runs, for example, fell from about 0.67 for $c=0.11$  to about 0.15 for $c=0.59$ . This made the number of unique configurations per run roughly constant for all the runs. Thus the degree of statistical independence of the block values did not worsen from a reduced success rate as $c$ increased. The creation and destruction success rates were quite close, as they should be. For the 600- and $500-K$ runs, for example, they differed by $0.02 \%$ to $0.4 \%$ . The estimated statistical errors for the average energy and the average particle number were with few exceptions under $0.5 \%$ of the respective averages. For the lower temperature and concentrations very close to the coex- istence region, values were typically near $1 \%$ .

We mention that unbounded critical fluctuations are not expected near the critical point of the $\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}$ transition, near $450 ~K$ and $c=0.4$ , due to coherency stresses in the system. (See Ref. 20.) To bring the model system from its initial randomly chosen configuration to equilibrium, a short prerun was performed, typically one-tenth as long as the main run.

As a check, the energy per particle was updated during the steps of a block and then calculated from scratch at the end of the block for comparison with the updated ver- sion. As an additional check, the chemical potential wascalculated from scratch for the last configuration of eachblock using the method of Widom. $^{21}$ The calculated $\mu$ (Widom) values were then averaged and compared with the input $\mu$ . The two values always agreed to better than $0.6 \%$ , with the usual figure being about $0.3 \%$ .
As an additional check, a canonical ensemble, i.e., $H$  moves only, was calculated to the same or better statistics for most of the same temperatures and $\mu$ values. The agreement with the grand-canonical results varied from good to poor. For concentrations larger than 0.1, and for T not too close to the critical temperature of about $450 ~K$ , the agreement was good. For $T=600 ~K$ and $c>0.2$ , the average particle energies agreed by $0.1 \%$ to $2 \%$ , for exam ple. The agreement at $c=0.1$ was about $10 \%$ and that at $c=0.15$ was $3.7 \%$ . For $T=500 ~K$ and $c=0.5$ , near the critical temperature, the average particle energies disagreed by $13.5 \%$ , with worse agreement for $c=0.5$ and lower $T$ . It seems likely that the disagreements at low $c$  are due to real small-system differences between the en- sembles, and that the differences near the critical tempera- ture and concentration are due to the inadequacies of the canonical ensemble with regard to "hydrodynamic slow- ing down" near phase-transition boundaries. $^{22}$ The grand-canonical ensemble does not share this inadequa- cy. $^{22}$ The good agreement between the two ensembles in the regions where the above two considerations do not ap- ply can be taken as evidence of the correctness of both programs.
As a further rough check, we approximately determined the $c$ limits of the coexistence region for the temperatures425 and $400 ~K$ . No effort (long runs) was made to avoid loss of accuracy due to critical slowing down and hys- teresis effects, so the results are approximate only. Ac- cording to Ref. 23, the grand-canonical ensemble cannot enter the region of coherency stresses, and so for fixed T our calculated $c$ should jump from the low- to the high-c boundary of the incoherent coexistence region as $\mu$ is lowered from an initial large, negative value. At 425 and400 K the particle density jumped from about 0.29 to about 0.55 and from about 0.17 to about 0.67, respective- ly. These values are inside the HW calculated incoherent

$\alpha$-$\alpha'$ phase boundary. The discrepancy with HW might be due to not using longer runs, using only nine $\bar{\epsilon}_{ij}$ values, or using a system about seven times larger than that of the HW MC calculation. Our estimated calculated critical temperature (438 K) is about 95% of that of HW. The above estimated phase boundary values seem close enough to the HW phase boundary to rule out serious programming errors.
