# Intermediate-temperature ordering in a three-state antiferromagnetic Potts model

Shafiqur Rahman and Eric Rush*
Department of Physics and Astronomy, Allegheny College, Meadville, Pennsylvania 16335

Robert H. Swendsen
Department of Physics, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15213

(Received 27 February 1998)

We investigate the ordering in a three-state antiferromagnetic Potts model on a simple cubic lattice by using an efficient cluster-flipping Monte Carlo algorithm. By considering the degeneracy due to the sublattices in two different ways, we arrive at two entirely different phases [rotationally symmetric (RS) and permutationally symmetric sublattice (PSS)] for the intermediate temperature region. Each of these phases has been claimed to be the correct phase by different authors in a recent controversy over ordering in this system. We show that the system moves from a broken sublattice symmetry phase at low temperature to the disordered phase by going through the RS phase at intermediate temperature, and that the PSS phase results from taking the sublattice degeneracy into account in an incorrect fashion. [S0163-1829(98)03738-2]

## I. INTRODUCTION

The three-state Potts model¹ on the simple-cubic lattice with antiferromagnetic nearest-neighbor interaction has been intensively studied over the past two decades. Simplicity of the Hamiltonian coupled with its unusual low- and intermediate-temperature properties have drawn the attention of many authors to this problem. Various methods such as field-theoretical approach,²⁻³ renormalization groups,⁴ Monte Carlo simulations,²,³,⁵⁻¹¹ mean-field analysis,¹² coherent anomaly,¹³ and cluster-variation methods¹⁴,¹⁵ have been employed to examine the critical behavior and the ordering of this system. While there is reasonable agreement about the critical parameters at this time, considerable controversy has recently arisen regarding its intermediate temperature ordering.

It is now generally accepted that at a temperature around $T_{c1}=1.23$, the system undergoes an order-disorder transition between the antiferromagnetic and the disordered phases and that this is a continuous transition belonging to the $XY$-model universality class. But the situation is unclear about the nature of the ordered phase(s) below this transition temperature.

Using a one-parameter rescaling argument, Berker and Kadanoff⁴ suggested in 1980 that there is no true long-range ordering in this system. Rather, the ordering decays algebraically with distance. Using Monte Carlo simulation, Banavar *et al.*²,³,⁵ predicted the existence of a long-range ordered phase known as the broken-sublattice-symmetry (BSS) phase. However, Ono⁶ concluded that the system indeed lacks spontaneous magnetization at low temperature, but has a Kosterlitz-Thouless phase below the critical point. Kikuchi and Okabe¹⁶ measured probability distribution of magnetization below the critical point and interpreted their results as evidence of the BSS phase. Ueno and Kasono¹⁷ observed a nonzero magnetization and suggested that the model belongs to a new universality class. Using a cluster-flip Monte Carlo method, Wang, Swendsen, and Kotecky⁷,⁸ found values of critical exponents quite close to those of the $XY$ model, and also found a nonvanishing magnetization. However, they also observed a divergent susceptibility indicating that the ordered phase is characterized by algebraically decaying correlations. In 1993, based on a modified cluster-variation method, Rosengren and Lapinskas¹⁴ (RL) predicted the existence of not one, but three ordered phases: the BSS phase up to $T_{c3}=0.7715$, a 12-fold degenerate phase in a very narrow temperature region between $T_{c3}$ and $T_{c2}=0.787$, and a permutationally symmetric-sublattice (PSS) phase from $T_{c2}$ to $T_{c1}$. The unusual nature of this prediction gave rise to considerable activity in this area. Subsequently, using a standard Metropolis Monte Carlo simulation, Kundrotas, Lapinskas, and Rosengren⁹ (KLR) also predicted the PSS phase, though some of the information presented by them contradicts their conclusion. On the other hand, two different Monte Carlo simulations, one by Kolesik and Suzuki¹⁰ (KS) and the other by Heilmann, Wang, and Swendsen¹¹ (HWS) indicate that the system orders in the BSS phase at low temperatures, and goes over into a so-called rotationally symmetric (RS) phase in the intermediate-temperature region before undergoing the order-disorder transition at $T_{c1}$. KS also find that the PSS phase has a higher free energy than the BSS phase up to a temperature slightly below 1.0, which is higher than the transition temperature proposed by RL. At still higher temperatures, they find that the difference in free energy between the PSS and the BSS phases is indistinguishable from zero, though they cannot rule out the possibility that it vanishes only at the critical temperature $T_{c1}$. Using the cluster-variation method in the cubic and star-cube approximations, which are more accurate than the modified cluster-variation method used by RL, Pelizzola¹⁵ recently reported that the free energy of the PSS phase is always higher (although slightly) than that of the BSS phase and therefore the former should not manifest itself. Compounding the confusion further, a recent mean-field analysis by Itakura¹² that also takes next-nearest-neighbor (NNN) interactions into account suggests that the BSS phase is realized in the zero-coupling region of the NNN interaction and the PSS phase in the strong-coupling region. In the intermediate-coupling region,

the system moves from the PSS to the BSS phase as temperature increases, exactly opposite to that reported by RL and KLR for the zero-coupling region.

The current situation with ordering then is as follows: There is agreement that at very low temperature, the system orders in the BSS phase, and that at the transition temperature $T_{c1}$, the system undergoes an order-disorder transition. In the intermediate-temperature region, however, some authors $^{9,14}$ find that the system orders in the PSS phase, while others $^{10,11}$ find that it orders in the RS phase.

Since the unusual properties of this system are a consequence of the highly degenerate ground state, we have carefully examined the notion of degeneracy for this system and how it should be taken into account during calculations. Using an efficient cluster-flip method proposed by Swendsen and Wang for Monte Carlo simulation, $^{18}$ we have examined the ordering in such a system by directly following the probability distributions of the three Potts states on the sublattices and by using an order parameter. Interestingly, by taking the degeneracy due to the sublattices into account in two different ways, we can fully reproduce the results of both groups of authors in this controversy, including the diagram of RL that shows very complicated behavior of the probability distributions of the various spin states as a function of temperature, as well as the presence of the BSS phase at low temperature and the PSS phase at intermediate temperature. Further, we show that, at least in our Monte Carlo simulations, what appears to be the PSS phase is really an average of various branches of the BSS phase over the two sublattices and arises due to taking the sublattice degeneracy into account in an incorrect manner.

## II. DESCRIPTION OF THE SYSTEM AND THE PHASES

The Hamiltonian for the three-state Potts model can be represented by
$$
H=K \sum_{\langle i, j\rangle} \delta_{s_{i}, s_{j}},\qquad(1)
$$
where $K=J / k_{B} T$ is a dimensionless coupling constant, the summation extends over nearest-neighbor pairs, $s_{i}$ is the spin on the $i$ th site and can assume the values $s_{i}=1,2,3$. $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta and $k_{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant. We set $J / k_{B}=1$ and use $T=1 / K$ as a dimensionless reduced temperature.

To distinguish between the BSS, the PSS, and the RS phases, it is best to consider the cubic lattice as a combination of two interpenetrating sublattices $A$ and $B$, such that any lattice point of one sublattice has all its nearest neighbors on the other sublattice. It is also useful to look at the expectation values or concentrations of the various Potts states on the two sublattices. These are conveniently described by
$$
c_{i j}=\left(2 / L^{3}\right) \sum_{k \in j} \delta_{s_{k}, i},\qquad(2)
$$
where $i$ represents the spin states 1,2, or $3, j$ represents the sublattices $A$ or $B$, and $L$ is the linear system size. It should be pointed out that because of degeneracy, the notations $A$ and $B$ do not represent sublattices that are "pinned down" physically (i.e., the parts of the lattice that represent sublattice $A$ at one time may constitute sublattice $B$ at another time). This point is clarified in detail later.

In the ideal BSS phase, one of the spin states is found only on one sublattice, while the other two states are distributed randomly with equal probability on the other sublattice. In reality, even at very low temperatures, there is a small probability of finding states 2 or 3 on sublattice $A$ because this causes no increase in energy when the neighbors are 3 or 2, respectively. Thus the long-range order does not saturate even at $T=0$, and the zero-temperature entropy per site is slightly larger $^{7,8}$ than the value $\frac{1}{2} \ln 2 \approx 0.346574$ that is expected for a perfect BSS phase. Wang, Swendsen, and Kotecky $^{8}$ determined this value to be 0.3673 . In terms of concentrations, the ideal BSS phase is described by $c_{1 A}=1$, $c_{2 A}=0=c_{3 A}, c_{1 B}=0, c_{2 B}=\frac{1}{2}=c_{3 B}$. Note that the two sublattices are not equivalent, even when the degeneracy due to the three-spin states are taken into account; hence the name "broken-sublattice-symmetry" phase. In the ideal PSS phase, on the other hand, two of the spin states occur with equal probability, one on each sublattice, while the third spin state is distributed equally on both sublattices. There are additional restrictions on the magnitude of the concentrations, in terms of which a typical PSS phase would be described by $c_{1 A}=c_{2 B}>\frac{1}{3}, c_{2 A}=c_{1 B}<c_{3 A}=c_{3 B}<\frac{1}{3}$. Note that the sublattices are indistinguishable when the degeneracies due to the three-spin states are taken into account; hence the name "permutationally symmetric-sublattice" phase. The rotationally symmetric phase derives its name from the fact that the distribution of a vector order parameter used to describe this phase is fully rotational symmetric. $^{10,11}$ We find that the RS phase can be described in terms of concentrations by $\frac{2}{3}>c_{1 A}>c_{2 B}>c_{3 B}>\frac{1}{3}>c_{3 A}>c_{2 A}>c_{1 B}$.

To look for transition from the BSS phase, authors who report evidence of the PSS phase $^{9,14}$ use different order parameters compared to those $^{10,11}$ finding the RS phase, making a direct comparison between their works difficult. There is also disagreement among these authors as to the validity of these order parameters for determining such transitions. As it turns out, all these parameters are actually suitable for the task. We will consider the one used by KLR. Consider the sublattice magnetizations $M_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|c_{1 j}-c_{2 j}\right|+\left|c_{2 j}-c_{3 j}\right|\right.$ $\left.+\left|c_{3 j}-c_{1 j}\right|\right)$, where $j$ again specifies the two sublattices $A$ and $B$. One can then use the absolute value of the difference between the sublattice magnetizations $M=\left|M_{A}-M_{B}\right|$ as an order parameter because its value should be one for a perfect BSS phase and zero for a perfect PSS phase. Additionally, it can be shown that the upper limit of its value is less than $\frac{2}{3}$ for the RS phase.

## III. DEGENERACY

As we will show further below, one ends up with the PSS or the RS phase, respectively, in the intermediate-temperature region depending on the way sublattice degeneracy is taken into account. Consequently, we would like to examine the notion of degeneracy carefully for this system. Consider the two sources of degeneracy: one coming from the Potts states, and the other from the sublattices. Permutation of the three Potts states and the two sublattices leads to a sixfold degeneracy. How should this be taken into account

during computations? We will clarify this point using the BSS phase as an example.

Let us start with the degeneracy due to the three Potts states. During a particular Monte Carlo step (MCS) at low temperatures where the BSS phase is present, state 1 may be the one that fills up almost the whole of one sublattice, while states 2 or 3 may be in this situation during a subsequent MCS. The signature of the BSS phase is one in which one of the sublattices is occupied mainly by a single state while the other sublattice is occupied approximately equally by the other two states; it simply does not matter which state has the highest frequency in a particular MCS. Thus the degeneracy due to the three states can be taken into account by focusing attention on the highest-, mid-, and low-frequency states at the end of each MCS, as opposed to following the behavior of particular states that may have been the highest-, mid-, and low-frequency states, say, in the first MCS.

One also must take into account the degeneracy due to the two sublattices. In a particular MCS, sublattice A may be the one to have the state that fills up almost the whole sublattice, but in a subsequent step, sublattice B may be the one having predominantly a single state. As long as the other two states are divided approximately equally on the other sublattice, we have a valid BSS phase. Thus the correct way of taking this degeneracy into account would be to collect statistics as fol- lows: again using the example of the BSS phase, if during a particular MCS, a particular sublattice has the state with the highest frequency, then statistics from that sublattice should be added to that of the sublattice that had the highest fre- quency state in the previous MCS, even if the two sublattices are different. We will call this the variable sublattice method. One could of course think of an alternate way of taking the sublattice degeneracy into account. In this second method, one would ‘‘pin down’’ the sublattices A and B. Statistics for a sublattice would always be added to that of the same sublattice for every MCS, even if the highest fre- quency states in different MCS’s appear on different sublat- tices. The sublattice degeneracy would then be taken care of by matching the highest-, mid-, and lowest-frequency states at a particular temperature to corresponding quantities at the next temperature [see Fig. 1(b), for example]. To distinguish this approach from the previous method, we will call this the fixed sublattice method. For a true PSS phase, both methods should lead to the same result because the sublattices would be permutationally symmetric. However, in absence of a true PSS phase, the second method simply represents an averag- ing process over the two sublattices and does not constitute a proper way of taking the sublattice degeneracy into account. It is interesting that by taking the sublattice degeneracy into account according to the first method, we can reproduce the results of one group,10,11 while by using the second method, we can reproduce those of the other group.9,14

## IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

Monte Carlo simulations were performed on a cubic lat- tice with nearest-neighbor interaction and periodic boundary condition. Systems with linear sizes $L=12, 16, 20, 24, 32,$ 36, and 48 were examined. The number of lattice updates or Monte Carlo steps was usually 100 000. The final spin con- figuration of one temperature was usually the starting con- figuration for the next temperature. The number of steps skipped to bring the system to equilibrium was 1000, though some calculations were performed with 10 000 steps skipped. No difference was found between the two. Both ‘‘cooling’’ and ‘‘heating’’ runs were performed. No differ- ence in the dependencies was observed.

Figure 1(a) shows the concentration of the Potts states on the two sublattices A and B as a function of temperature for $L=36$ for the variable sublattice method. From the pattern and the values of the concentrations at low temperature ($c_{1A}=0.94$, $c_{2A}=c_{3A}=0.03$, $c_{1B}=0$, $c_{2B}=c_{3B}=0.5$), one can clearly identify this as the BSS phase. These values are in agreement with those of RL (Ref. 14) and Pelizzola.¹⁵ Eventually, all the branches are expected to converge to-

wards the final value $\frac{1}{3}$ by the time the system undergoes transition to the disordered state. The low-temperature BSS behavior persists up to about $T=0.7$. A simplistic picture might suggest that the branches with concentrations larger than the final value $\frac{1}{3}$ should move downward while those with smaller values should move upward. This is not the case. The second branch (from the top) moves upward for a considerable temperature range before beginning its descent toward the final value $\frac{1}{3}$. The fifth branch also shows a small movement opposite to the expected direction, whereas all other branches move in a fashion compatible with the simple scenario given above. The curious behavior of the second branch, as well as the complementary nature of the third branch to the second, is indicative of something more than straight descent to the disordered state. The onset of this strange behavior seems to be around $T=0.5$. The temperature at which the motion of the second branch changes from upward to downward is around $T=0.8$ [about 0.9 for $L$ $=48$, see Fig. 2(a)], which is fairly close to $T=1$ that has been identified as a possible transition from the BSS to the RS phase. $^{10,11}$ We have also drawn some vertical bars with the data points to indicate standard deviations. It is clear from these bars that for the intermediate-temperature region, each branch is not as tightly defined as in the BSS phase, but can still be clearly distinguished.

Figure 1(b) shows the same information as Fig. 1(a) for the fixed sublattice method. Again the BSS phase is clearly visible for temperature up to about 0.7. However, the behavior of the different branches in the intermediate-temperature region is now quite different. Note that there appears to be a sharp transition to a phase characterized by what was termed the PSS phase. The upper two branches move sharply in opposite directions and merge to a value slightly below 0.75. The next two branches also move sharply in opposite directions and merge to a value slightly above 0.25. The lowest two branches continue together. There are now three branches in this intermediate-temperature region. The middle branch increases toward the final value $\frac{1}{3}$ in a somewhat linear fashion, while the other two show opposite curvatures that become more pronounced as the order-disorder transition temperature is approached. This entire complicated behavior reproduces exactly the behavior given in RL. However, when we look at the bars for standard deviation, we understand immediately that this interpretation is not correct. Essentially what has happened is that by pinning down the sublattices, we have ended up with values of the respective quantities averaged over the two sublattices. It is interesting that in the work of RL, the upper branch of the PSS phase starts out with a value 0.75 that lies exactly midway between the values 1.0 and 0.5 of the two branches of the BSS phase that merge to give this branch. Similarly, the second branch of the PSS phase starts with a value 0.25 that lies exactly in the middle of the corresponding values 0.5 and 0 of the branches that merge to give rise to this branch. Why is the low-temperature region immune from this averaging process? Though correlation times for energy remain reasonable, $^{8}$ for larger sample sizes and at low temperatures the sublattice with the highest frequency does not change as frequently. We have verified this point by examining the behavior of the sublattices at low temperatures. If the number of MCS is very large or the system size is small, the highest frequency state shows up in both sublattices leading to some "jumpiness" of the branches even in the BSS phase. Notice the large difference in the standard deviation bars between Figs. 1(a) and 1(b). Indeed, the standard deviations in Fig. 1(b) are sufficiently large so as to allow them to be fully mapped into Fig. 1(a). It should also be mentioned that the "transition" temperature increases somewhat with system size, going from about 0.4 for $L=16$ to 0.8 for $L=48$. Figure 2 represents the same quantities as Fig. 1 for a larger sample size $(L=48)$. We want to emphasize that the different results of the two methods do not arise from statistical error. Rather, the error is a more fundamental one, namely, the fixed sublattice method does not take the twofold degeneracy due to the sublattices into account in a proper way.

### V. CONCLUSION

By taking sublattice degeneracy into account in two different ways, we have shown that we can reproduce the re-

sults of both sides in the recent controversy on ordering in the intermediate-temperature region for the three-spin anti- ferromagnetic Potts model. The PSS phase, at least in the Monte Carlo simulation, appears to be an artifact of taking the sublattice degeneracy into account in an incorrect fash- ion. The system actually moves from the BSS to the disor- dered phase through a more complicated RS phase. This re- sult is in agreement with those of KS and HWS. Hopefully, this work will put to rest the current controversy over the intermediate-temperature ordering in this system.
