# Analysis of phonons in graphene sheets by means of HREELS measurement and *ab initio* calculation

H. Yanagisawa, $^{1,*}$ T. Tanaka, $^{1}$ Y. Ishida, $^{1}$ M. Matsue, $^{1}$ E. Rokuta, $^{1}$ S. Otani $^{2}$ and C. Oshima $^{1}$

$^{1}$ Department of Applied Physics, Waseda University, 3-4-1 Okubo, Shinjuku-ku Tokyo 169-8555, Japan
$^{2}$ National Institute for Materials Science, 1-1Namiki, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-0044, Japan

Received 19 September 2003; Revised 27 November 2003; Accepted 21 December 2003

We have grown epitaxial thin sheets of graphene on $BC_3/NbB_2(0001)$ and measured phonon dispersion curves of the graphene sheets by means of high-resolution electron energy-loss spectroscopy (HREELS). The phonon dispersion curves have been calculated on the basis of *ab initio* calculations. The observed curves were in good agreement with theoretical curves. The quality of crystallinity of the graphene layers was high compared with the bulk graphite crystals. In this paper, we demonstrate that the combination of HREELS measurement and *ab initio* calculations is extremely effective for investigation of honeycomb-structured materials. ## INTRODUCTION

Much attention has been devoted to honeycomb-layer materials, which are derivatives of graphite. They are, for instance, graphene, carbon nitride (CN), hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), metal diborides (MB₂) or binary compounds of $B_xC_y.^{1-12}$ From the viewpoint of applications to ultrahard materials⁶ or the exploration of new high-$T_c$ superconductors, $^{13-15}$ it has been recognized that knowledge of the phonon characteristics has become increasingly important. Regardless of the significance, the experimental set-up for effectively detecting phonons in honeycomb-layer materials is limited owing to the following two circumstances: the phonons in these materials generally exhibit such high vibrational energies that inelastic ion or neutron scattering spectroscopy is not always applicable; and neither infrared nor Raman spectroscopy can measure the entire region of the Brillouin zone. Among various phonon detection methods, high-resolution electron energy-loss spectroscopy (HREELS) is a powerful tool to overcome the drawbacks and to explore the phonon dispersion curves of honeycomb-layer materials. In practice, much knowledge about the phonons in honeycomb-layer materials has been obtained, and various interesting phenomena have been elucidated in HREELS experiments. $^{2,5}$

In this study we have measured the phonon dispersion curves of thin sheets of graphene on $BC_3/NbB_2(0001)$ surfaces by means of HREELS. Study on the intriguing $BC_3/NbB_2(0001)$ specimen was reported elsewhere. $^{16}$ *Ab initio* calculation has been performed to produce the phonon dispersion curves independently. By using both experimental and theoretical techniques, phonon dispersion curves are confirmed completely. In this paper, we demonstrate that simultaneous analysis by means of HREELS measurement and *ab-initio* phonon calculation is effective for investigation of honeycomb-layer materials.

## EXPERIMENTAL AND THEORETICAL

The experiments were carried out in an ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) chamber equipped with an Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) apparatus for chemical analysis, low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) optics and an HREEL spectrometer. The $NbB_2(0001)$ surface was employed as the starting substrate. In order to fabricate graphene sheets, we supplied carbon atoms via thermal migration and surface segregation from the inside of the $NbB_2$ substrate. It is worth mentioning that heating the $NbB_2(0001)$ substrate to $1500^{\circ}C$ grows monolayer honeycomb-structured sheets of $BC_3$ over a macroscopic surface area of $NbB_2(0001)$, as reported in detail elsewhere. $^{16}$ In this study, graphene sheets were grown heteroepitaxially on the $BC_3/NbB_2(0001)$ surface by annealing at $1200^{\circ}C$, and the coverage was controled by varying the heating time. In the HREELS measurement, the thicknesses of the graphene sheets are fixed at two layers and were estimated from AES analysis.

Theoretical phonon dispersion curves of a graphene sheet were obtained by performing *ab initio* calculation (DACAPO package¹⁷) within the framework of the density functional theory (DFT). The fundamental procedure is the same as that proposed by Kunc *et al*.¹⁸ In the present calculation a rectangular-shaped supercell containing 32 carbon atoms was used to constitute the model graphene sheet, and as a result all the Hellmann–Feynmann forces within the sixteenth nearest-neighbour interaction were taken into account. ## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

First, the growth mode of the graphene sheets is discussed. Figure 1 shows a change in the LEED pattern with increasing coverage of the graphene sheet. As shown in Fig. 1(a), two sets of diffraction spots were observed before the growth of the graphene. One is sharp and bright, whereas the other is sharp and less bright. They were ascribed to the BC₃ sheet and the NbB₂(0001) surface, respectively. One monolayer growth of the graphene sheets generates a third set of diffraction spots (Fig. 1(b)). As shown in the inset in Fig. 1(b), the latter spots reside in the proximity of the BC₃ spots but are clearly distinguishable. Thus, the LEED image involves three diffraction sets that are attributable to the BC₃ monolayer, the graphene sheets and the substrate surface, indicating that the monolayer of the graphene sheet grows in an epitaxial manner to the BC₃/NbB₂(0001) surface. By annealing the specimen further, not only the weak spots attributable to NbB₂(0001) but also the sharp BC₃ spots became more blurred and finally disappeared, whereas the dominant spots of graphene remained unchanged while keeping constant visibility; Fig. 1(c). The evolution means that graphene sheets continuously accumulate in an epitaxial manner, and even plausibly in a layer-by-layer growth manner. Therefore, the crystallinity of the graphene sheet is judged to be excellent from LEED observations. Later, this will be confirmed by HREEL measurement. The model of the overlayer structure is depicted schematically in Fig. 2.

Figure 3 shows HREEL spectra of the graphene/BC₃/NbB₂(0001) specimen for various wave vectors along the $\bar{\Gamma}-\bar{M}$ direction in the two-dimensional surface Brillouin zone (SBZ), which is depicted in Fig. 4. In the specular spectrum (the top curve) energy-loss peaks were not detected, although phonons in the graphene sheets are certainly located in the measured energy region. In general, the specular spectrum is dominantly excited by dynamic dipole fields generated by optically infrared active phonons. Hence, a featureless spectrum is attributable to: screening of the dipole fields due to collective motion of conduction electrons; absence or weakness of ionic bonds so that dipole fields cannot be generated; or both. Because graphite is semi-metallic and also is a covalent material, we supposed that both of these effects cause the absence of a specular loss peak. On the other hand, as denoted by the triangles, loss features appeared in the off-specular spectra where the impact scattering process prevails.²⁰ The same HREELS measurements were made also along the $\bar{\Gamma}-\bar{M}$ direction. Thus, we determined the phonon dispersion relation experimentally, as shown in Fig. 4.

In order to assess the properties of the graphene sheets
further, some previous studies were reviewed. $^{2,3}$ According
to Aizawa et al., the optical phonons with the out-of-plane
polarization vector (the ZO phonons) tend to be largely
softened in energy when one monolayer covers some
transition metal carbide (TMC) (111) surfaces. $^{1}$ In contrast,
such softening of the ZO phonons did not occur here, and the
phonon dispersion curves are almost equivalent to those in
bulk graphite or graphite deposited on TMC(001) surfaces.
Nagashima et al. verified that the energetic softening of the
ZO phonon is ascribed to interfacial orbital hybridization
between the $\pi$ electron of the monolayer and the d electronic
bands of the substrate. Therefore, because the present data
exhibit quite good consistency in the phonon dispersion
relation between the graphene and bulk data, it is plausible
that modifications of $\pi$ bands were not present on the
$BC_3/NbB2(0001)$ surface.

A significant exception to the above-mentioned agree-
ment between the graphene and bulk data was a discrepancy
observed in the transverse acoustic (TA) mode along the
$\Gamma - M$ direction: namely, no data points appeared for the
graphene sheets, whereas the TA mode was clearly detected
in the bulk graphite data. The HREELS selection rule states
that the TA mode along the $\overline{\Gamma}-\overline{M}$ direction should be
unobservable owing to the reflection symmetry. $^{20}$ Hence,
the contrasting result suggests that the crystalline quality
was higher in the graphene sheet than that of bulk crys-
tal. This tendency is consistent with the findings observed
in the LEED observation: diffraction spots of graphene
sheets on $BC_3/NbB_2(0001)$ are much sharper than those of
bulk graphite. $^{16}$ Furthermore, also in previous studies using
HREELS, no graphene sheets formed on various solid sur-
faces except Ni(111) have exhibited such good quality that
the selection rule applied. $^{2,21}$ Thus, our sheets were found to
be fairly excellent in crystallinity.

## CONCLUSION

Phonon dispersion curves of graphene sheets on $BC_3/NbB_2$
(0001) have been studied not only by using HREELS but
also by performing ab initio calculations. It was shown that
the ab initio calculation results match significantly well the
HREELS findings, giving much confidence in the accuracy of
the calculations as well as in the validity of the phonon data.

The combination method using both ab initio calculations
and HREELS measurements promotes a deeper understand-
ing of the dynamic properties of honeycomb-layer materials.
