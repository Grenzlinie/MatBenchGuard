# Time-resolved Raman spectroscopy for shock-driven intramolecular electron redistribution of cyclotrimethylene trinitramine (RDX)

## Abstract
Shock-driven intramolecular electron redistribution (IER) of cyclotrimethylene trinitramine (RDX) is proposed, which will be helpful for understanding the initiation and safety mechanisms of RDX materials under shock compressions. Laser-driven shock wave was performed to generate a dynamic pressure, and time-resolved Raman spectroscopy was used to probe the shock wave response of RDX. First-principle calculations were utilized to identify Raman modes and determine a pressure-dependent electron density distribution in RDX molecules. Raman peaks of RDX have blue shifts under shock loadings, which are caused by IER. According to calculations, it can be confirmed that electrons transfer from C-H and N-O bonds to N-N bonds under shock loadings in IER. We suggest that shock-driven IER will make some chemical bonds be broken more easily under shock conditions.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Energetic materials are used in a wide range of military and civilian applications. Through ages of application, it is found that some energetic materials such as RDX, HMX, and TATB are stable at room temperature and atmosphere pressure but could be decomposed, and even detonated by some stimulation such as shock, impact, or heating. Why do these energetic materials show such a feature? The electron density of chemical bonds could be a key factor to cause chemical reactions, which will redistribute in a molecule when energetic materials are stimulated by external stimulation. This electron density redistribution can be equivalent to a spatial movement of electrons in a molecule, which can be regarded as intramolecular electron redistribution (IER) from the whole molecular perspective. IER will make the strength of some chemical bonds weaken, which enables these chemical bonds to be broken easily and thereby results in the initiation of reactions. Thus, IER is one of the important mechanisms for predicting the initial reaction of energetic materials.

At present, some research work concerning the electron density distribution has been performed to predict the stability of energetic materials under impact$^{[1,2]}$ or identify the first reaction bond in decomposition.$^{[3]}$ These efforts are helpful for understanding the safety and the detonation performance of energetic materials. However,

the research about IER is still a challenge, especially for shock-driven IER, which is a fast nonequilibrium process. There is a lack of an effective experimental method to observe shock-driven IER directly and quantitatively. However, a qualitative analysis of shock-driven IER is rel- atively easy to realize because the vibrational frequency of a chemical bond has a direct correlation with the elec- tron density of this bond. Under the harmonic approxi- mation, the vibrational frequency is proportional to the square root of the force constant, which is a function of the electron density of the chemical bond. Thus, the vibrational frequency shift of chemical bonds under shock waves is directly caused by shock-driven IER. At present, time-resolved Raman spectroscopy is an effective method to observe the vibrational frequency shift of ener- getic materials under shock waves. $^{[4,5]}$ Thus, we suggest that shock-driven IER can be analyzed qualitatively with the observation of Raman peak shifts under shock waves.

RDX (1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazacyclohexane) is a kind of typical high explosive, which has been researched extensively over the years. At present, the possible initial steps in RDX decomposition can be deduced according to the experiment evidences $^{[4-7]}$ and the calculation results. $^{[8-10]}$ Some mechanisms such as phase transi tions, $^{[4,6]}$ hydrogen transfers, $^{[5,8-10]}$ autocatalyses, $^{[7]}$ and bimolecular reactions $^{[5,10]}$ are proposed to possibly involve in chemical reactions of RDX. In addition, the space group and the molecular structure of most poly- morphs can be confirmed in a relatively detailed phase diagram of RDX. $^{[11-15]}$ These research works provide details concerning polymorphs and decomposition chan- nels of RDX, which can be helpful for investigating shock-driven IER. Therefore, RDX has been chosen as a model molecule in our work.

In this work, laser-driven shock wave has been used to load a shock wave on RDX particles, and nanosecond time-resolved Raman spectroscopy has been performed to observe the shock wave response of vibrational fre- quencies. In order to determine donors and acceptors in IER, first-principle calculations have been applied to cal- culate the pressure-dependent bond length and atomic charge. The specific objectives of this work are (a) to qualitatively judge shock-driven IER by Raman peak shifts, (b) to determine donors and acceptors in IER by pressure-dependent effective bond orders, and (c) to pro- pose a shock-driven IER mechanism by combining exper- iment and calculation results.

## 2 | EXPERIMENT AND CALCULATION

The experimental setup has been described in detail in our previous work. $^{[16,17]}$ Here, we will mainly introduce experimental conditions and some improvements in the experiment. As shown in Figure 1, an excitation pulse (1,064 nm, 9 ns, 650 mJ) emitted from a Q-switched Nd: YAG laser (YG980, Quantel) was focused on the shock layer to generate a shock wave, and its power density was about $2.3 \times 10^{9} ~W/cm^{2}$. A dichroscope was used to reflect a probe pulse (532 nm, 7 ns, 2 mJ) emitted from another Q-switched Nd: YAG laser (Powerlite 8010, Con- tinuum), which was then focused on the sample layer to generate Raman signals. The Raman signal was collected by a confocal system that significantly enhanced the sig- nal-to-noise ratio of Raman spectra. An intensified charge-coupled device (DH740, Andor) equipped on a spectrometer (iHR 550, Jobin Yvon) was used to detect the Raman signal. A digital generator (DG535, Stanford) was introduced to control the timings among two lasers and intensified charge-coupled device, and the temporal resolution was set to 10 ns in this experiment.

The target with a sandwich structure was used in the experiment as shown in Figure 1. The aluminum film with a thickness of $20 \mu m$ was regarded as a shock layer. RDX particles (the purity was above 99%) with diameters of about 10-30 $\mu m$ were put in ethanol (analytical reagent, the purity was above 99.7%) to form turbid liquid that was injected in a $150-\mu m$-thick cavity, and this cavity was formed by two 4-mm-thick glass substrates. RDX parti- cles filled in the cavity after subsiding to form a sample layer. The ethanol that filled in gaps among RDX parti- cles served as a pressure-transmitting medium. The target was fixed on an X-Y translation stage so that each laser pulse can be applied to a fresh location.

FIGURE 1 Schematic diagram of experimental setup. MO: micro objective; D: dichroscope; NF: notch filter [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary. com]

Density functional theory calculations were performed using the plane-wave pseudopotential method as implemented in the VASP package. $^{[18]}$ The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhf function $^{[19]}$ was adopted to describe the exchange-correlation potential. The core-valence interaction was described by the projected augmented waves. $^{[20]}$ The electronic wave functions were expanded by the plane wave basis with the kinetic energy cutoff of 800 eV. Structural relaxations with and without pressures were carried out until the maximum forces on each atom less than $0.01\ \text{eV}/\mathring{\text{A}}$. The Monkhorst-Pack grid of $3\times3\times3$ k-point was used to sample the Brillouin zones. We also used Grimme's D3 scheme with the zero damping function $^{[21]}$ to describe the dispersion interaction in the RDX crystal more accurately. The atomic charges were calculated using the Bader's charge analysis. $^{[22]}$ The effective bond orders were evaluated based on the density-derived electrostatic and chemical analysis. $^{[23]}$ The force constants and the phonon frequencies were calculated using the finite displacement method as implemented in the PHONOPY package. $^{[24]}$

## 3 | RESULTS

### 3.1 | Raman spectra under shock wave

The Raman spectra of RDX at 0 and 40 ns are taken to describe the general case of Raman signals in the frequency domain. As shown in Figure 2, the wavenumber range of the Raman signal is from 830 to $1380\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, which covers nine vibrational modes. For the mode assignment, the Raman modes of $\alpha$-RDX were calculated by ab initio calculations using the B3LYP density functional theory method with the Sadlej's medium-sized polarized basis set (SadlejpVTZ). These calculations were performed with the Gaussian 03 computation package. $^{[25]}$ The more details about this calculation work were described in our previous work. $^{[26]}$ Here, we only used a part of results to analyze the Raman modes in this experiment, and the assignments of these modes are marked in Figure 2. The nine vibrational modes are corresponding to the majority of chemical bonds or groups in an RDX molecule, such as $\text{NO}_2$ groups, $\text{N-N}$ bonds, $\text{CH}_2$ groups, and $\text{C-N}$ bonds in a triazine ring. Thus, the shock wave response of RDX molecules can be deduced by the Raman peak shifts of these nine vibrational modes. Compared with the Raman peaks at 0 ns, the Raman peaks at 40 ns have blue shifts that are caused by shock loading. The decomposition does not occur under this shock condition because no new Raman peak presents in the whole process.

In the temporal domain, the Raman peak shifts of the vibrational modes have a similar behavior. As shown in Figure 3a, the Raman peaks have rapid blue shifts from 0 to 40 ns and slowly move back after 40 ns. We suggest that IER is responsible for the Raman peak shifts under shock conditions, which is reversible under shock loading and unloading in the experiment. The times of shock loading and unloading can be deduced, and the values were about 40 and 160 ns, respectively. In addition, the peak pressure of the shock wave can be evaluated because the Raman peak shift of RDX is proportional to the pressure in the same polymorphic phase. $^{[27]}$ Here, the Raman mode at $887\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ is chosen to evaluate the peak pressure because the intensity of this peak is the strongest so that it has the best signal-to-noise ratio, and the pressure dependence of this Raman mode is $2.7\ \text{cm}^{-1}/\text{GPa}$ below $\sim4\ \text{GPa}.^{[27]}$ In this experiment, the Raman peak shift maximum value of the Raman mode at $887\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ is about $2.68\ \text{cm}^{-1}$; therefore, the peak pressure of the shock wave is about 1 GPa. Thus, in the experiment, the pressure range was changed from the atmospheric pressure to 1 GPa under shock loading.

At relatively low pressures, we suggest that the temperature change of RDX materials induced by shock loading is relatively modest, so that mechanical rather than thermal effects may often be dominant in this process; therefore, the influence of pressures is only considered in the calculation. In addition, the Raman signal in the experiment can be regarded as a statistical result for RDX particles with different crystal orientations under an approximate plane compression, which is equivalent to the Raman signal of an RDX single crystal under hydrostatic pressures. Thus, the calculation results about the response of an RDX single crystal to hydrostatic pressures can be used to explain the experiment results under shock conditions. ### 3.2 | Pressure-dependent bond lengths

The bond length is a key factor affecting IER; therefore, the pressure-dependent bond lengths were calculated to deduce the influence of pressures on the RDX molecular structure. As shown in Figure 4, the behaviors of the chemical bonds can roughly be divided into three categories. The first is that the bond length of N5-O4 increases with the pressure increase, which might be caused by intermolecular interactions in RDX crystals. The second is that the bond lengths of C3-H5, C2-N2, and N6-O5 have little decreases with the pressure increase, which

indicates that the C-H and C-N bonds in the triazine ring and the N-O bond in the axial NO₂ groups show inflexibility under pressures. The third is that the bond lengths of N1-N4 and N2-N5 obviously decrease with the pressure increase, which indicates that both the N-N bonds in the equatorial and axial NO₂ groups are flexible under pressures. In general, with the pressure increase, the whole RDX molecule has a tendency to compression.

### 3.3 | Pressure-dependent atomic charges
The change of atomic charges can be equivalent to the electron transfer in RDX molecules, which is another important factor influencing IER. The pressure-dependent effective bond order was calculated to confirm the donor and the acceptor in the electron transfer. As shown in Figure 5, the behaviors of the chemical bonds can be roughly divided into three categories. The first is that the effective bond order of C2-N2 almost does not change with the pressure increase, which indicates that the C-N bond in the triazine ring does not participate in the electron transfer. The second is that the effective bond orders of C3-H5, N5-O4, and N6-O5 decrease with the pressure increase, which indicates that the C-H bond in the triazine ring and the N-O bonds both in the equatorial and axial NO₂ groups act as the electron donors in the electron transfer. The third is that the effective bond orders of N1-N4 and N2-N5 increase with the pressure increase, which indicates that the N-N bonds both in the equatorial and axial NO₂ groups act as the electron acceptors in the electron transfer. Thus, the electrons will transfer from the C-H and N-O bonds to the N-N bonds with the pressure increase.

## 4 | DISCUSSION
### 4.1 | Electron density redistribution under pressures
The pressure makes the chemical bond length and the atomic charge change resulting in electron density redistribution in RDX molecules. According to the calculation results about pressure-dependent bond lengths, the RDX molecule roughly has a tendency to compression with the pressure increase, which makes electron densities of these chemical bonds increase so that these bonds become difficult to be broken under pressures. However, this deduction is in contradiction with some experimental findings. According to the previous research work, the sensitivity of RDX will increase while the shock pressure increases, which indicates that some chemical bonds are easier to be broken with the pressure increase. $^{[28]}$ Thus, under pressures, the change of the bond length necessarily comes with the change of atomic charges in chemical bonds. According to the calculation results about pressure-dependent effective bond orders, the change of the atomic charges in some chemical bonds makes the electron densities of these bonds decrease with the pressure increase although most of bond lengths are compressed. This electron density redistribution can be equivalent to the electron transfers in RDX molecules under pressures, which can be considered as IER from the whole molecular perspective.

### 4.2 | Shock-driven IER
The Raman peak shifts in the experiment indicate that IER exists in shock compressions of RDX molecules. The pressure-dependent effective bond orders in the calculation confirm the donor and the acceptor in IER. Combining these experiment and calculation results, we propose a shock-driven IER mechanism for RDX materials under shock conditions. As shown in Figure 6, when the RDX molecule is impacted by a shock wave, the electrons will transfer from the C-H and the N-O bonds to the N-N bonds under shock loading. ## 5 | CONCLUSION

Nanosecond time-resolved Raman spectroscopy has been performed to observe Raman peak shifts of RDX mate- rials under laser-driven shock wave. Meanwhile, first- principle calculations have been used to calculate the pressure-dependent bond lengths and atomic charges. In the experiment, the Raman peaks have blue shifts under shock loading and recover under shock unloading, which indicates that IER occurred when the shock wave acted on RDX molecules. Meanwhile, the electron donors (C-H and N-O bonds) and acceptors (N-N bonds) are confirmed in IER of RDX by the quantitative calculation of the effective bond order. According to the experiment and calculation results, we propose a shock-driven IER mechanism of RDX. While the RDX molecule is shocked, the electrons will transfer from the C-H and N-O bonds to the N-N bonds under shock loading. We suggest that this mechanism is invertible under relatively low pressure conditions and will make some chemical bonds be broken under relatively high pressure conditions. Thus, this shock-driven IER mechanism is a key factor for the shock chemistry of RDX materials.
