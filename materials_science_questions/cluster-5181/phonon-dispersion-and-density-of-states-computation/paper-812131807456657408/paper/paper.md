# The Pressure Dependence of the Electrical Resistivity Thermopower and Phonon Dispersion in Liquid Mercury $\dagger$

By A. O. E. ANIMALU

Division of Applied Physics, Stanford University, Stanford, California $\ddagger$

## ABSTRACT

The dependence of the electrical resistivity, thermopower and phonon dispersion on pressure is investigated quantitatively with a view to deter- mining whether the density of state parameter, $g=N(E_{F}) / N_{free }(E_{F})$ , introduced recently by Mott does exist and how it changes with pressure. By using the nearly-free electron approximation based on the pseudopotential calculated by Animalu and Heine, and the recent theoretical interference function, $a(q)$ of Ashcroft and Lekner, our quantitative results indicate that the resistivity of liquid $Hg$ calculated according to Ziman's theory $(g=1)$ is at least a factor 2 less than the experimental value (suggesting $g^{2}<0.5$ ) and that it increases rather than decreases with pressure, whence we deduce $\partial \ln g^{2} / \partial \ln a \sim-30$ , where $a$ is proportional to the cube root of the atomic volume, in order to restore agreement with experiment.

## § 1. INTRODUCTION

WE wish here to report a calculation of the electrical resistivity, the thermopower and the phonon dispersion in liquid $Hg$ as a function of decreasing atomic volume (under pressure). This is intended to give some information about the changes in the electronic structure of $Hg$ under pressure. In particular, it has recently been suggested by Mott (1966) (referred to as $M$ hereafter) that liquid $Hg$ and other group II metals like $Ca, Sr$ and $Ba$ have a low density of states at the Fermi level and that for such metals Ziman's theory (1961) for the resistivities breaks down. A density of states parameter:

$$g=N\left(E_{\mathrm{F}}\right) / N_{\mathrm{free}}\left(E_{\mathrm{F}}\right), \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(1)$$

i.e. the ratio of the actual density of states, $N(E_{F})$ , (fig. $4(b)$ in $M$ ) to the free-electron value, $N_{free }(E_{F})$ , was introduced and a new formula for the resistivity, $\rho$ , was suggested :

$$\rho_{\text {Mott }}=g^{-2}\left(2 \pi^{3} \hbar / e^{2} S_{\text {free }} \lambda\right), \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(2)$$

where $S_{free }$ is the area of the free-electron Fermi Surface and $\lambda$ is the mean free path. In the nearly-free electron theory $(g \approx 1), \lambda$ could be calculated with Edwards' (1958) perturbation theory in the lowest order

In particular, $g^2\sim0\cdot5$ and $\partial\ln g^2/\partial\ln a\sim-24$, were calculated in M from experiment, where $a$ is proportional to the cube root of the atomic volume. It is worth while, therefore, to carry out a new calculation of the resistivity based on Ziman's theory and to investigase the dependence on volume, using the more recent theoretical values of the interference function, $a(q)$, due to Ashcroft and Lekner (1966) and the pseudopotential calculated by Animalu and Heine (1965).

Since the total resistivity also depends on the vibrations of the ions in the solid or liquid, i.e. on the phonons present, it is also useful to investigate the phonon dispersion in liquid Hg as a function of volume. Besides giving the phonon contribution to the resistivity, we also derive additional information about the melting point (according to Lindemann's formula) and the dependence of the electrostatic and band structure energies on volume. The latter is relevant to the study of phase transformation in the structure associated with change in bonding due to compression as suggested by Abowitz and Gordon (1963) from experimental study of the compressibility of Hg alloys.

## § 2. RESISTIVITY AND THERMOPOWER

In M the change in the electrical resistivity, $\rho$, with atomic volume, $\Omega$, was calculated from experiment:
$$\Delta_{\text {Mott }}=\partial \ln \rho / \partial \ln \Omega \sim+8, \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(3)$$
which states that on compression the resistivity decreases. This is consistent with an increase in the overlap between the first and second (s-p) bands in Hg as pointed out in M. Here using Ziman's theory (1961), i.e. $g=1$, we obtain an increase of the resistivity with compression :
$$\Delta_{\text {Ziman }} \sim-2. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(4)$$

If we modify Ziman's theory to make it consistent with (3), we need
$$\partial \ln g^{2} / \partial \ln a \sim-30 \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(5)$$
to be compared with $-24$ given in M, the lattice parameter $a$ being proportional to $\sqrt[3]{\Omega}$.

To see how (4) and (5) come about we calculate $\rho$ for four atomic volumes, $\Omega=164 \cdot 5(23^{\circ} C), 155,147$ and 140 (compressed volumes) from the formula (Sundström 1965):
$$\rho_{\text {Ziman }}=\frac{3 \pi}{e^{2} \hbar^{3}} \frac{\Omega}{v_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\left\langle a U^{2}\right\rangle, \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(6)$$
where
$$\left\langle a U^{2}\right\rangle=4 \int_{0}^{1} a(y)|U(y)|^{2} y^{3} d y \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(7)$$
and $y=q / 2 k_{F}$. $U(q)$ is the screened model potential (Animalu and Heine 1965) (fig. 1) calculated at the appropriate atomic volumes, and for $a(q)$, the interference function, we use the recent theoretical values given by Ashcroft and Lekner (1966) (fig. 2) from solving the Percus-Yevick equation with packing density of $46 \%$, and ignore its variation with pressure $\dagger$. The values of $\rho$ for $g=1$ ($\rho_{\text {Ziman}}$) are shown in the table, and (4) is calculated from the values at $\Omega=155$ and $\Omega=140$ (a $10 \%$ decrease in volume) using
$$\partial \ln \rho / \partial \ln \Omega=(\Delta \rho / \rho) /(\Delta \Omega / \Omega). \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(8)$$

Writing
$$\rho_{\text {Mott }} \approx \rho_{\text {Ziman }} × g^{-2} \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(9)$$
where the factor $g^{-2}$ is actually a scaling factor which we are crudely identifying with that in (2), we derive:
$$\Delta_{\text {Mott }}=\Delta_{\text {Ziman }}-\frac{\partial \ln g^{2}}{\partial \ln \Omega}, \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(10)$$
and so from (3) and (4) we obtain (5). In this way we have investigated how $g^{2}$ may change with pressure.

## § 3. Phonon Dispersion

In order to estimate the phonon contribution to the resistivity of the liquid it would be necessary to calculate the Debye temperature, $\theta_{\mathrm{D}}$, from the phonon dispersion in the liquid. As the structure of the liquid is most probably far from being isotropic, there being evidence for some order from the measurements of the compressibility (Abowitz and Gordon 1963) and the resistivity (Ocken and Wagner 1966, Wagner and Halder 1966) of Hg alloys, we shall regard the liquid structure as a limiting form of the crystalline solid structure. The two stable phases of solid Hg, the rhombohedral and the tetragonal phases $(\alpha-\mathrm{Hg}$ (Mott and Jones 1930) and $\beta-\mathrm{Hg}$ (Atoji et al. 1959), respectively), are distortions of the

face-centred cubic (f.c.c.) structure and less than $10\%$ change in the volume forces the liquid to crystallize into the solid (R. G. Gordon, private communication). It is thus instructive (and convenient) to assume the f.c.c. phase in performing the calculation of the phonon dispersion in the liquid, i.e. a ' molten solid' model.

Most of the frequencies for the f.c.c. were pure imaginary in the harmonic approximation, throughout most of the Brillouin zone, except the transverse [111] branch, which is very low. This gives low $\theta_{\mathrm{D}}$ which is associated with the low melting point of $\mathrm{Hg}$ according to Lindemann melting formula (see, for example, Ziman 1964, p. 63). It is easy to show that a necessary condition for stability of a structure with respect to propagating phonons carrying along the polarization of the electron gas is that the function

$$D(\omega^{2})=\omega^{2}-M_{0}-M_{1} \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad (12)$$

should be positive definite, where $M_{0}$ is the dynamical matrix associated with the (perfect) lattice of ions interacting via their Coulomb fields and $M_{1}$ includes all other effects like defects, anharmonicity and the polarization of the valence electron gas due to the vibration of the ions. Within the harmonic approximation, it is thus possible to get a pure imaginary frequency by using a pseudopotential which does not tend to zero smoothly in momentum space. In $\mathrm{Hg}$, of course, imaginary frequencies correspond to instability of the f.c.c. phase, but it is necessary to make sure that instabilities are not merely due to using poor pseudopotentials.

It is possible in so doing to obtain the phonon frequencies, as in calcium (Animalu 1967), two times larger in the backward direction than in the forward direction. Such a calculation in Hg showed the same qualitative trends, characterized by pure imaginary frequencies in two extremes of forward and backward scattering. Of course, the model potential parameters in table 4 of Animalu and Heine (1965) satisfy the condition $A_{2} \simeq Z / R_{\mathrm{M}}$ required for smoothness as suggested by Animalu *et al*. (1966), and so, a reasonably good convergence was obtained. Our conclusion is that the pure imaginary frequencies obtained are not entirely unphysical: in fact, they do reflect what might be expected in doing any detailed phonon spectrum calculation in liquid metals, i.e. the need to include Debye-Waller effects through multi-phonon processes (Sham and Ziman 1963) in $\mathrm{M}_{1}$ and use thermo-dynamic Green's functions (Cowley 1963) and replace all summations by integrals.

Numerical results are shown in figs. 4 to 6. In fig. 4 we give the two terms which contribute to the phonon frequencies, viz.

$$
\omega^{2}=\omega_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+\omega_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}, \quad \text {....... (13) }
$$

where $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}$ is the bare frequencies of the ions interacting through their Coulomb field and $\omega_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}$ (usually less than zero) is the electronic part,

which is written in terms of Cochran's $G$-function computed by Animalu (1966) in the form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\omega_{\mathrm{E}}{ }^{2}\right)_{\lambda \mu}=-\omega_{\mathrm{p}}{ }^{2} \sum_{\mathbf{H}} \frac{(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{H})_{\lambda}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{H})_{\mu}}{|\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{H}|^{2}} G(|\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{H}|) & \\
& +\omega_{\mathrm{p}}{ }^{2} \sum_{\mathbf{H} \neq 0} \frac{\mathbf{H}_{\lambda} \mathbf{H}_{\mu}}{|H|^{2}} G(|\mathbf{H}|), \quad(14)
\end{aligned}
$$

the summation being over the reciprocal lattice vectors $H$. Here

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}=\left(\frac{4 \pi Z^{2} e^{2}}{\Omega m}\right)^{1 / 2} \quad \text {....... (15) }
$$

is the ion plasma frequency, $Z$ being the valence and $m$ the ionic mass. The expression for the $G$-function has been given by Animalu (1966) and the form of $G$ is shown here in fig. 3. All the frequencies are given in units $\omega_{\mathrm{p}}=1$, since in this unit the Coulomb part $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}{ }^{2}$ is the same for all f.c.c. lattices and so we take them from Vosko *et al*. (1965). The results are given only for back scattering matrix elements in the $G$-function which is then identical to those given in fig. 3 for phonon wave vectors $q>2 k_{\mathrm{F}}$, and is very close for $q<2 k_{\mathrm{F}}$.

In fig. 5 we show the squared frequencies $\omega_{\lambda \mu}{ }^{2}$ and in fig. 6 the real frequencies:

$$
\omega_{\lambda \mu}=+\sqrt{ }\left[\left(\omega_{\mathrm{c}}{ }^{2}\right)_{\lambda \mu}+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{E}}{ }^{2}\right)_{\lambda \mu}\right] \quad \text {..... (16) }
$$

for the atomic volumes $\Omega=164 \cdot 5,155$ and 140. We see that as we compress it from liquid to solid state more real frequencies appear, probably because the f.c.c. phase is favoured at high pressures.

We have already pointed out that the low values of the real frequencies are associated with low $\theta_{\mathrm{D}}\left(\theta_{\mathrm{D}} \sim 100^{\circ} \mathrm{K}\right.$ in $\left.\mathrm{Hg}\right)$. In order to understand, at least qualitatively, the phonon contribution to the resistivity of the liquid, we could compare with the relation which holds for the solid:

$$
\rho \propto T / m \theta_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}, \quad\left(T>\theta_{\mathrm{D}}\right), \quad \text {...... (17) }
$$

which gives

$$
\frac{\partial \ln \rho}{\partial \ln \Omega}=-2 \frac{\partial \ln \theta_{\mathrm{D}}}{\partial \ln \Omega} \quad \text {....... (18) }
$$

In the solid, the right-hand side of (18) is known from the Gruneisen relation (see, for example, p. 64 of Ziman (1964), to be roughly 4. The application of the relation here is very crude indeed; in fact, we would expect from the form of our dispersion curves that $-\partial \ln \theta_{\mathrm{D}} / \partial \ln \Omega$ is much less than 2, and this affirms that most of the statements about the change of the resistivity comes from Mott's factor of $g^{-2}$ in (2). We could conclude therefore that a detailed calculation of $g$ in $\mathrm{Hg}$ is worth while, and this calculation is now in progress.
