## A B S T R A C T

Pyrochlore lattice (PL) is a type of special lattice, which shows unique electronic and magnetic properties, such as flat band, spin frustration and spin liquid. However, its lattice thermal transport has never been reported. Here, we study phonon spectra and lattice thermal conductivities of pyrochlore born structures. Like the case of electron band structure, there are flat bands appearing in the phonon spectra. The flat bands not only lead to the zero group velocities of phonons but also induce strong phonon scatterings. As a result, the lattice thermal conductivity of pyrochlore born is very low (5.89 W/mK at room temperature). Thermal transport in another pyrochlore-like boron structure is also investigated. Due to the existence of band gaps in the phonon spectra, the phonon scatterings are significantly suppressed in the structure. Although there are more flat bands in the pyrochlore-like boron structure, its thermal conductivity (13.94 W/mK) is higher than that of the standard pyrochlore boron. This reveals the effect of the bandgap engineering on the thermal transport. Our results provide a fundamental reference for further studies on mechanical and thermal properties of PL structures.

## Introduction

Thermal transport ability is an important property of a material, which leads to many important applications, such as phase transition, thermal management, thermoelectric conversation and thermomagnetic effect [1-7]. Lattice thermal transports in materials are strongly related to lattice types, and thus allotropes have different lattice thermal conductivities, for example, carbon honeycombs [8] and diamond have completely different thermal transport characteristics [9,10]. As a contrast, two structures with the same type of lattice have many similar band structures in their phonon spectra, such as diamond and silicon, because they share the same lattice symmetries [10,11]. Therefore, it is very necessary to explore thermal properties of different types of lattices.

Pyrochlore lattice (PL), consisting of tetrahedral units as shown in Fig. 1(a), is a special type of lattices with geometric frustrations [12,13]. The geometry frustration leads to frustrations of electron freedom degrees, such as spin, orbital and charge [14-16]. Spin frustration has been studied extensively in pyrochlore ores made of rare earth element oxides. Many interesting spin states, such spin liquids, spin ice and spin glass [17,18], have been revealed in these materials. Comparing with the studies on spin frustration, the studies on orbital frustration in real materials are rare. This is because spin-spin interaction can affect ordering of magnetic atoms several lattice distance away while the orbital-orbital interaction is predominantly between nearest-neighbor atoms. Therefore, orbital frustration is usually observed in materials made of single element. However, most single elements are hard to construct PL. The previous studies on toy models of PL indicated that there exist flat bands and many two-fold degenerated bands in the electron band structures because of special symmetries of lattice [19-21]. The flat bands lead to a tremendous density of state and then induce not only exotic electronic, optical and magnetic transport [22,23] but also a series of many-body phenomena.

It is known that phonon spectra of a structure have many similar features with electron spectra, because they are both dominated by lattice symmetries [24,25]. Hence, one can expect that flat bands and two-fold degenerated bands will also appear on the phonon spectra of pyrochlore materials. The flat bands mean that their corresponding group velocities of phonons are approximately equal to zero, moreover, these phonons can induce strong three phonon scattering processes because they have the same frequency while momentums are arbitrary [26]. In this sense, PL may produce lower thermal conductivity. To the best of our knowledge, however, thermal transports of pyrochlore structures have never been reported.

Most elements do not like to form triangular rings, but boron is an exception [24,29]. The electron-deficient nature of boron leads to triangular rings being the most common units in the two- and three-dimensional boron allotropes [30,31]. Fortunately, we find two structures possessing PL from lot of boron allotropes, which provide important platforms to explore thermal transport properties of the special lattice.

In this work, we study phonon spectra and lattice thermal conductivities of the two pyrochlore born structures, named as PL-B4 and PL-B8 in Fig. 1. The boron atoms in PL-B4 form a standard PL, while those in PL-B8 form a pyrochlore-like lattice where each lattice consists of two boron atoms [32]. Flat bands and degenerated phonon branches have been found in the phonon spectra of the two structures. Due to strong phonon scatterings induced by flat bands, the lattice thermal conductivity of PL-B4 is very small (5.89 W/mK at room temperature), comparing with thermal conductivities of some other boron allotropes, such as borophene (143.4 W/mK) and boron nanotubes (174.86 W/mK) [33]. Although flat bands and degenerated branches also exist in PL-B8, its lattice thermal conductivity is higher (13.94 W/mK at room temperature). This is because there are band gaps appearing in the phonon spectra, which significantly suppresses the phonon scatterings. Our work not only investigate thermal transport properties of PL, but also propose a scheme for phonon bandgap engineering.

## Models and methods

The atomic structures of two boron allotropes are shown in Figs. 1(a-d). The first boron structure in Fig. 1(a) is a standard PL, a three-dimensional network of corner-sharing tetrahedra. The adjacent tetrahedrons share one atom, and thus each atom form six bonds with the nearest-neighboring atoms. Using the symmetry criterion, the coordinate position can be expressed as 16d (0.625, 0.125, 0.125). The structure is named PL-B4 because its primitive cell consists of 4 boron atoms, as shown in Fig. 1(b). The second born structure in Fig. 1(c) is a pyrochlore-like lattice, in which each lattice consists of a dimer of boron atoms. Each atom in the structure form four bonds with the nearest-neighboring atoms, and the atomic coordinate position in conventional cells can be expressed as 32e (0.570, 0.070, 0.070). This structure is named PL-B8 because its primitive cell consists of 8 atoms, as shown in Fig. 1(d). Both two structures in Figs. 1(a) and 1(c) belong to space group Oh (227), which has very high symmetry. After being fully optimized, the lattice constant of conventional cell of PL-B4 is 4.99 Å, while that of primitive cell is 3.53 Å. The bond length in PL-B4 is 1.765 Å. The lattice constants of conventional and primitive cells of PL-B8 are 8.57 and 6.06 Å, respectively. In PL-B8, the bond length in the tetrahedra is 1.706 Å and that between the tetrahedra is 1.622 Å. The cohesive energies of PL-B4 and PL-B8 are -5.96 and -5.81 eV/atom, respectively. Although the bond length in PL-B4 is larger than those in PL-B8, PL-B4 is more stable. The atomic densities of PL-B4 and PL-B8 are 0.129 and 0.051 Å⁻³, respectively.

Our calculations are performed on density functional theory (DFT) in the first-principles, which is implemented in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) approximation to the exchange-correlation functional [34-37]. The core-valence interactions were described by the projector augmented wave (PAW) potentials [34] as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code [36]. Generalized gradient approximation (GGA) developed by Perdew and Wang were adopted for the exchange-correlation potential [35]. Plane waves with a kinetic energy cutoff of 520 eV were used as the basis set [37]. The Monkhorst-Pack scheme was used to sample the Brillouin zone and calculation by a mesh of 13 × 13 × 13 and 7 × 7 × 7 k-point sampling corresponding to PL-B4 and PL-B8. The structure was optimized when the surplus Hellmann-Feynman forces on the ions are less than 0.001 eV/Å. Furthermore, the energy convergence criterion between two consecutive steps was set at 10⁻⁵ eV. The heat transport properties are calculated using the ShengBTE [38] calculation software based on the Boltzmann transport equation, where the second-order force constants and third-order force constants used as input files for the ShengBTE calculation are calculated by phonopy.py [39] and thirdorder.py [40] in combination with VASP. For the second-order force constants, PL-B4 and PL-B8 respectively use supercells of 4 × 4 × 4 and 3 × 3 × 3, the third-order force constant is calculated by using supercells of 3 × 3 × 3 and 2 × 2 × 2, respectively. The third-order force constants of PL-B4 and PL-B8 are all calculated using the sixth nearest neighbor, and the cut-off distances are 5.14 and 5.12 Å, respectively. Before calculating the properties of thermal transport, a Q-points ($N \times N \times N$) convergence test calculation was performed on it, as shown in Fig. S1 in supplementary information (SI). When $N=13$, thermal conductivities of the two structures are convergent gradually. We choose $N=15$ in the following calculations.

Boltzmann transport equations were used as the basis of Sheng-BTE software package to study the thermal transport properties of PL-B4 and PL-B8 by solving the equations iteratively. The isotropic lattice thermal conductivities of the structures can be calculated as the sum of all phonon modes $\lambda$ with branch $b$ and wave vector $q$, and it can be written as [38,41]:

$$
K_{L}=K_{\ell}^{\alpha \alpha}=\frac{1}{k_{B} T^{2} \Omega M} \sum_{\lambda} f_{0}\left(f_{0}+1\right)\left(\hbar \omega_{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(v_{\lambda}^{\alpha}\right)^{2} \tau_{\lambda}^{\alpha}, \tag{1}
$$

where $k_{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$, $\Omega$, and $M$ are the temperature, the structural volume and the number of points in the Brillouin zone ($M=N \times N \times N$), and $f_{0}$ is the number of phonons, subject to Bose-Einstein statistics, $\hbar \omega_{\lambda}$ is the phonon energy, $v_{\lambda}=\partial \omega_{\lambda} / \partial q$ is the phonon group velocity, and $\tau_{\lambda}$ is the phonon relaxation time. The inverse of the relaxation time $\tau_{\lambda}$ is equal to the phonon scattering rate $p_{\lambda}$, while $F_{\lambda}=\tau_{\lambda} v_{\lambda}$ is the mean free path of the phonon. $p_{\lambda}$ can be obtained as the sum of three-phonon scattering probabilities $\Gamma_{\lambda \lambda^{\prime} \lambda^{\prime \prime}}^{\pm}$, which can be expressed as [10,40,42]:

$$
\Gamma_{\lambda \lambda^{\prime} \lambda^{\prime \prime}}^{ \pm}=\frac{\hbar \pi}{8 N}\left\{2\left(f_{\lambda^{\prime}}-f_{\lambda^{\prime \prime}}\right) \begin{array}{l}
\delta\left(\omega_{\lambda} \pm \omega_{\lambda^{\prime}}-\omega_{\lambda^{\prime \prime}}\right) \\
f_{\lambda^{\prime}}+f_{\lambda^{\prime \prime}}+1
\end{array}\right\} \frac{\delta\left(\omega_{\lambda} \pm \omega_{\lambda^{\prime}}-\omega_{\lambda^{\prime \prime}}\right)}{\omega_{\lambda} \omega_{\lambda^{\prime}} \omega_{\lambda^{\prime \prime}}}\left|V_{\lambda \lambda^{\prime} \lambda^{\prime \prime}}^{ \pm}\right|^{2}, \tag{2}
$$

## Result and discussion

We first calculate phonon spectra and density of states (DOS) of PL-B4 and PL-B8, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. Two features deserve attention: one is flat bands on the spectra, the other is degenerate bands. In PL-B4, there is a quasi-flat band around frequency 10 THz, which can be confirmed by the peak in the DOS profile. In PL-B8, there are many flat bands in the spectra, corresponding to sharp DOS peaks. Like the electron transport in PL, the formation of phonon flat bands is originated from a destructive interference among the phonon branches. The phonons become localized degenerate states, and a superposition of these localized states form flat bands. The flatness degree of the bands is related to the interactions between the next-neighboring atoms, which is somewhat equivalent to the effect of next-neighboring hopping in electron system [44]. The distance between the next-neighboring atoms in PL-B4 is shorter, and thus the interactions between atoms are stronger. As a result, the bands in the structure are dispersive. On the contrary, the longer distance between the next-neighboring atoms results in quite flat bands in the spectra of PL-B8. In addition, one can see that there are many two-fold degenerate bands on the spectra of both structures, for example, two transverse acoustic branches TA₁ and TA₂ are degenerate. These degeneracies are determined by the high symmetry of the lattice.

The maximum phonon frequencies of PL-B4 and PL-B8 are 34.5 and 41.6 THz, respectively. These values are close to the maximum phonon frequency of diamond 39.3 THz [10]. This is because, on one hand the bond lengths in the boron structures are slightly larger than that in diamond, on the other hand the mass of boron atom is smaller than that of carbon atom. Comparing Figs. 2(a) with 2(b), one can find that a big difference between the two boron structures: the spectrum of PL-B4 has no band gap, while there are two bandgaps in the spectrum of PL-B8. The bandgap around frequency 15 THz in Fig. 2(b) is 2.54 THz, and the other bandgap around frequency 20 THz is 1.81 THz.

Based on the phonon spectra, we calculate lattice thermal conductivities \(K_L\) of PL-B4 and PL-B8 and the results are shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), respectively. The thermal conductivities decrease with the increase of temperature. This is because high-frequency phonons are excited by high temperature and the phonon scattering is strengthened. One can find two abnormal phenomena from Fig. 3. The first abnormal phenomenon is that the thermal conductivity of PL-B4 is very low, only 5.89 W/mK at room temperature \(T = 300\) K (see Fig. 3(a)). In general, the lattice thermal conductivities of light-element materials are higher, such as diamond (1640 W/mK) [10], BN (225 W/mK) [45], Si (142 W/mK) [46] and SiC (370 W/mK) [47]. Even most materials made of heavy elements have higher thermal conductivity than 20 W/mK [48,49]. The bond length in PL-B4 is 1.765 Å. Although it is larger than the bond length of diamond (1.54 Å), it is smaller than that of silicon (2.37 Å). This indicates that interactions between atoms (i.e., force constants) are strong, which usually leads to a big thermal conductivity in ballistic transport regime.

The second abnormal phenomenon is that the thermal conductivity of PL-B8 is larger than that of PL-B4, and the former is 2 or 3 times the latter. The thermal conductivity of PL-B8 at room temperature is 13.94 W/mK (see Fig. 3(b)). As mentioned above, the atomic densities of PL-B4 and PL-B8 are 0.129 and 0.051 Å⁻³, respectively. For two allotropes made of single elements, the structure with higher atomic density usually has a higher thermal conductivity because an arrangement with more dense atoms means more phonon transport channels. A comparison of thermal transports in some silicon allotropes proves this idea well [50].

To reveal the reasons behind the abnormal phenomena, we calculate phonon group velocities \(v\) and scattering rates \(p\) in the two boron structures, because the two quantities are very important for thermal transport according to Eq. (1). Figs. 4(a) and 4(b) show \(v\) of three acoustic branches and optical branch in PL-B4 and PL-B8, respectively. One can find that \(v\) of most phonons in PL-B4 are greater than those in PL-B8, especially the acoustic phonons. The

The strong phonon scatterings in PL-B4 originate from the flat band in its phonon spectrum mentioned above. The flat bands drastically enhance the three-phonon scattering processes, because the phonons on the flat bands have arbitrary momenta but a certain frequency $\omega_f$. As a result, phonon scattering is easy to occur only the other condition $\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}=\omega_{3}$ is satisfied. In this case, a flat band in PL-B4 gives rise to strong phonon scatterings.

Comparing Figs. 2(a) with 2(b), there are more flat bands existing in the latter. These flat bands should induce stronger phonon scatterings in PL-B8. However, why the thermal conductivity of PL-B8 is greater than that of PL-B4? This is because there are bandgaps in the phonon spectrum of PL-B8 (see Fig. 2(b)). The phonon scatterings are suppressed hugely by the bandgaps, especially the acoustic phonons. For example, there is a 2.54 THz bandgap above a flat band approximately locating at 13 THz in Fig. 2(b). The low-frequency phonons with $\omega_{1}<2.54$THz will not be scattered by the phonons in the flat band (frequency $\omega_{2}$), because the value $\omega_{3}\ (=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})$ falls onto the bandgap. Considering the number of low-frequency phonons is the largest, three-phonon scattering processes are suppressed by the bandgap even there is a flat band. To quantitatively show the effect of bandgaps on phonon scatterings, we compare average values of the scattering rate $\bar{p}$ of each phonon branch for PL-B4 and PL-B8 (see Table 1). One can find that the $\bar{p}$ in the phonon branches for PL-B8 are very smaller than those for PL-B4, especially the phonon branches of TA₁ and TA₂. On the other hand, we also calculate average value of the phonon group velocity $\bar{v}$ of each phonon branch for PL-B4 and PL-B8 (see Table 2). The difference of $\bar{v}$ between PL-B4 and PL-B8 is relatively small. According to Eq. (1), the thermal conductivity is approximately proportional to $\bar{v}^{2}/\bar{p}$, and then one can note that the difference of thermal conductivity contributed from TA₁ branch between PL-B4 and PL-B8 is quite small while that contributed from TA₂ is large.

Figs. 5(a) and 5(b) present weighted phase spaces of PL-B4 and PL-B8, respectively. Weighted phase space is a direct measure of the number of scattering processes allowed for each phonon. One can find that, for the low-frequency phonons, the number of scattering processes in PL-B4 is nearly 100 times that in PL-B8. Moreover, one can find that the scattering processes decrease at the low-frequency region in Fig. 5(b), and the minimum value locates at 2.54 THz, which is a finger print of the bandgap in PL-B8. Therefore, the thermal conductivity of PL-B8 is greater than that of PL-B4, although the former has a small atomic density and more flat bands.

In fact, the effect of bandgaps on thermal transport can be also reflected by Fig. 6(a), where the contributions of different frequency phonons on thermal conductivity are shown. Both thermal conductivities of PL-B4 and PL-B8 increase with the phonon frequency, because more phonons join in the transport process. The numbers of high-frequency phonons are small at room temperature ($T = 300$ K), and thus contributions of these phonons are very small. In principle, the cut-off frequency of contributive phonons in PL-B4 should be lower than that in PL-B8, because the maximum frequency of the former is lower than the latter. However, one can note that the cut-off frequencies of contributive phonons in PL-B4 and PL-B8 are approximate 18 and 13 THz, respectively. The first bandgap just locates from 13 THz. This indicates that, in PL-B8, thermal transport of the phonons above the bandgap is suppressed by the bandgap.

To further compare the different transport properties between PL-B4 and PL-B8, in Fig. 6(b), the cumulative thermal conductivity with respect to mean free paths in two structures are shown. One can find that the mean free path in PL-B4 is in the range of 0.4 ~ 20 nm, while that in PL-B8 is in the range of 5 ~200 nm. These results are consistent with the calculations of mean free paths in the two structures (see Fig. S2 in SI). Because wider range of mean free path in PL-B8, it demonstrates that, on one hand PL-B8 has a better thermal transport ability, on the other hand there is a stronger size effect on thermal transport in nano-sized PL-B8.

## Conclusion
In summary, we studied phonon spectra and lattice thermal conductivities of two pyrochlore born structures, named as PL-B4 and PL-B8. The former is a standard PL, while latter is a pyrochlore-like lattice. Flat bands and degenerated phonon branches have been found in the phonon spectra of the two structures. The flat bands induce strong phonon scatterings in PL-B4, and thus the thermal conductivity is very low (5.89 W/mK at room temperature). It indicates that the boron allotrope could be a good candidate of thermoelectric material. More flat bands appear on the phonon spectra of PL-B8, and the phonon group velocities in the structure are much smaller than those in PL-B4. However, thermal conductivity (13.94 W/mK at room temperature) of PL-B8 is greater than that of PL-B4. One of possible reason is that there are band gaps in the phonon spectra of PL-B8, which significantly suppress phonon scatterings. Weighted phase spaces, the cumulative thermal conductivity with respect to phonon frequency and mean free path are further analyzed to reveal the difference between the two boron structures. Our work provides a fundamental reference for further studies on mechanical and thermal properties of PL structures. An idea of phonon bandgap engineering is proposed to enhance thermal conductivity of some lattices, by splitting a lattice site to more atoms. It is noted that the two boron allotropes PL-B4 and PL-B8 have not been fabricated yet, so we hope that experimentalists can try to synthesize the two structures and verify our results.
