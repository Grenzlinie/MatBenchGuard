# Lattice Dynamics of Cesium Fluoride

By
Stig Rolandson $^{1)}$

The phonon dispersion relations in cesium fluoride have been measured by inelastic neutron scattering in the symmetry directions $\Delta, \Sigma$, and $\Lambda$. The measurements were per formed at 80 and 300 K using a three-axis spectrometer. The experimental results are compared with theoretical calculations based on various models of the interionic inter- actions in alkali halides. A satisfactory fit was obtained by a seven-parameter model with only the cesium ion polarizable, and the fit was only slightly improved by using more complicated models with a larger number of adjustable parameters. The frequency distri- bution has been calculated using an eleven-parameter shell model with both ions polarizable, and compared with similar calculations based on data from macroscopic measurements.

## 1. Introduction

Cesium fluoride represents in many respects an extreme case among the alkali halides. It crystallizes in the NaCl structure, and differs in this way from the other cesium halides, which all have the CsCl structure. In most alkali halides the electronic polarizability chiefly arises from the negatively charged halide ion, but for CsF the main contribution comes from the positively charged Cs ion.

The phonon spectra of most of the alkali halides have been studied by in- elastic neutron scattering. The measurements reported in this paper represent a continuation of these studies in order to investigate to which extent the extreme properties of CsF are reflected in the phonon dispersion curves. Theoret- ical calculations of the dispersion relations and frequency spectrum for this material have been done by Hardy and Karo [1] and Haridasan and Krishna murthy [2]. The two calculations take the polarizability of the ions into acount in somewhat different ways, and the results are rather different: e.g. the fre- quency spectrum in [1] has a gap which separates the acoustic and optical parts of the spectrum, while in [2] no such gap in the spectrum was obtained.

In Section 2 there is a brief description of the experimental method, and the measured phonon frequencies in the symmetry directions are presented. The experimental results are analyzed in Section 3 in terms of various models for the lattice dynamics of alkali halides. The best fit to the measured frequencies was obtained by an eleven-parameter shell model, which was used in Section 4 to generate the frequency distribution. The model calculations were also used to derive the elastic constants which have not been obtained from experimental results previously.

## 2. Experimental and Results
The specimen was a single crystal of CsF of approximately cylindrical shape, length 8 cm, diameter 2 cm and the cylinder axis parallel to a (100)-direction. The mosaic spread of the crystal was $0.3^\circ$ (full width at half maximum). The measurements were performed on a three-axis spectrometer at the R2 reactor in Studsvik. The apparatus has been described elsewhere [3]. The main part of the results was obtained with the sample at liquid nitrogen temperature, but some selected phonons were also measured at room temperature. The "constant-$\varkappa$" method with fixed outgoing neutron energy was utilized and phonon creation was always observed. Whenever possible focusing was used and inelastic structure factors were calculated to get information about regions in the reciprocal space where good intensity could be expected. This calculation was done by using the simple shell model of Woods et al. [4]. The measurements were carried out for wave vectors in the (100) and (1$\overline{1}$0) planes of the crystal.

### 3. Model Calculations

A variety of lattice dynamical models have been suggested for the atomic vibrations in ionic crystals [7 to 12]. Phonon frequencies in the high symmetry directions are calculated from some of these models and the results are compared with the measurements presented in this paper. The adjustable parameters of the different models are determined from the experimental frequencies by a least-squares fitting procedure. The models used were the rigid ion model of Kellermann [7], the shell model of Cowley et al. [8] and the breathing shell model of Schröder [10], or slight modifications of these.

The equations of motion for the shell model can be written (in matrix notation)
$$
\boldsymbol{m} \omega^{2} \boldsymbol{U}=(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{Z} \boldsymbol{C} \boldsymbol{Z}) \boldsymbol{U}+(\boldsymbol{T}+\boldsymbol{Z} \boldsymbol{C} \boldsymbol{Y}) \boldsymbol{W},
$$
$$
\mathbf{0}=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{T}}+\boldsymbol{Y} \boldsymbol{C} \boldsymbol{Z}) \boldsymbol{U}+(\boldsymbol{\zeta}+\boldsymbol{Y} \boldsymbol{C} \boldsymbol{Y}) \boldsymbol{W}.
$$
$\boldsymbol{m}$, $\boldsymbol{Z}$, and $\boldsymbol{Y}$ are diagonal matrices representing the ion masses, the ion charges and the shell charges, and $\boldsymbol{U}$ and $\boldsymbol{W}$ are the displacement vectors of the ion cores and shells. $\boldsymbol{C}$ consists of the coupling coefficients due to the long range coulomb interaction, while $\boldsymbol{R}$, $\boldsymbol{T}$, and $\boldsymbol{\zeta}$ specify the short range interaction. It is convenient to set $\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\boldsymbol{S}+\boldsymbol{K}$, where $\boldsymbol{K}$ is a diagonal matrix in which the elements $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ are the core-shell coupling constants of the positive and negative ion respectively. These equations are the basis for models 2 to 4. The number of adjustable parameters is reduced by setting $\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{T}$ for all wave vectors, assuming the short range interactions to act entirely through the outer electrons [8]. The shell charges $Y_{1}$ and $Y_{2}$ and the core-shell coupling constants $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ are replaced by the new parameters $\alpha_{1}, \alpha_{2}$ and $d_{1}, d_{2}$ which are the electrical and mechanical polarizabilities of the positive and negative ion respectively, as these are more directly related to observable physical quantities.

The rigid-ion model can be considered as a special case of the shell model with the electrical and mechanical polarizabilities equal to zero, and the equation of motion is reduced to
$$
\boldsymbol{m} \omega^{2} \boldsymbol{U}=(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{Z} \boldsymbol{C} \boldsymbol{Z}) \boldsymbol{U},
$$
where we have used the same notation as earlier.

The breathing shell model [10] is an extension of the shell model. A new "breathing" coordinate is introduced for each ion which allows the electron shells to be radially deformed. The number of adjustable parameters is kept constant by assuming that the force constants related to radial and translational deformation of the ions are equal.

The parameters of the different models are listed in Table 3, which also contains the quantity $\chi^{2}$ calculated from
$$
\chi^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\left(\frac{\omega_{i \exp }-\omega_{i \text { model }}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}}{N-K},
$$
where $N$ is the number of observations and $K$ is the number of adjustable parameters. $\omega_{i}$ denotes the frequency of the $i$-th phonon, and $\sigma_{i}$ is the estimated experimental uncertainty.

Model 1 is a rigid ion model. The ionic charge and the four parameters which determine the short range interactions (Table 3) have been obtained by making a fit to the experimental frrequencies. The agreement with experiments is acceptable for the acoustic and some of the transverse optical branches, but rather poor for the longitudinal optical branches.

Model 2 is a shell model with only the positive ions polarizable, since the fluorine ions are known to be much less polarizable. Also, the second-nearest-neighbour short-range interaction is only between the Cs ions, since the fluorine ions are considerably smaller. The agreement with experiment is good.

Model 3 is similar to model 2, but second-nearest-neighbour interactions between fluorine ions are also included. The parameters from the least squares fit (see Table 3) show that these interactions are considerably weaker than those between the Cs ions, which justifies the assumption made in model 2.

The best agreement with experiment is obtained by model 4 where both ions are polarizable and totally eleven parameters are determined from the measurements. The electronic polarizability $\alpha_{1}$ is considerably larger than $\alpha_{2}$, which is consistent with the findings of Tessman et al. [13] — though the actual values are somewhat lower than theirs. This fact has also been observed in a similar calculation for CsBr [14].

No improvement of the fit was obtained by using an eleven-parameter breathing shell model (model 5) and the differences in the calculated dispersion curves between model 4 and 5 are negligible for all branches. The two sets of parameters for these models are also rather similar and the most notable difference is in the value of $\alpha_{2}$. This is $0.84 \mathring{A}^{3}$ for model 5, which is in good agreement with the value of Tessman et al. [13], $0.759 \mathring{A}^{3}$. The corresponding value for model 4 is $0.46 \mathring{A}^{3}$.

### 4. Derived Properties

The frequency spectrum of CsF was obtained in the following manner. Model 4 and the parameter set which gave the best fit to the experimental results were used to generate phonon frequencies in a fine mesh in the irreducible part of the Brillouin zone. By using these frequencies and an interpolation formula based on a Taylor expansion to second order totally $6 \times 10^{6}$ phonon frequencies were calculated and sorted into a histogram, which is shown in Fig. 3. The channel width is $0.02 \times 10^{13} \mathrm{rad} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This frequency distribution is a smooth

curve with well distinguishable van Hove singularities and of a rather charac- teristic shape. The frequencies of the transverse optical modes are almost $q$-independent, and this gives rise to the narrow and very large peak in the fre quency distribution at $\omega$ equals $2.75 \times 10^{13} rad s^{-1}$. Due to the large mass dif ference between the ions there is a distinct gap in the frequency distribution between the optical and acoustic parts.

### 5. Discussion and Conclusions

The analysis of the experimental results in terms of different models shows that a good description of the dispersion curves can be obtained by a seven- parameter shell model, with only the positive ion polarizable and the short range interactions determined by interionic forces of type Cs-F and Cs-Cs. Only small improvements of the fit were obtained by using more sophisticated models with a larger number of adjustable parameters. The breathing shell model (model 5) and the equivalent rigid shell model (model 4) give almost identical results, so that radial deformations of the ions seem to be rather un- important in the description of the lattice dynamics of CsF.

Namjoshi et al. [15] suggested that a modified rigid ion (MRI) model gives a good description of the phonon dispersion relations in ionic crystals and they demonstrated this by a comparison of experimental and calculated phonon curves for some substances. The MRI model has five parameters and differs

from the original rigid ion model of Kellermann in the following ways. The ionic charge $Z$ is not fixed to the unit charge $e$, and the range of the short range interactions between the ions are extended from first- to the third-nearest neighbours. This model is similar to model 1 in this paper (Table 3 and Fig. 1) which gave a rather poor fit to the measured frequencies (Fig. 1). Thus the reinstatement of the rigid ion model in the MRI form appears to be questionable, at least for CsF.

A comparison between lattice dynamical calculations for CsF based on macro- scopic data [1, 2] and the results presented in this paper shows large discrepan- cies. Haridasan and Krishnamurthy have only published the frequency distri- bution, which makes a direct comparison between calculation and experiment impossible. However, their frequency distribution is very different from the one shown in Fig. 2. For example, there is no gap between the acoustic and optical frequencies and the peak due to the TO phonons is broad and less distinct than the peaks in the acoustic part of the spectrum. The frequency distribution of Karo and Hardly [9] is closer to the one obtained by us, e.g. it includes the gap just mentioned. However, their calculated phonon disper- sion curves are in many places considerably different from the experimental results. The largest difference occurs for the LA phonon at the zone boundary in the $\Delta$ direction. The experimental result is $1.388 \times 10^{13} \mathrm{rad} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ at $80 \mathrm{~K}$, while Karo and Hardy obtained $0.80 \times 10^{13} \mathrm{rad} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$. The fact that Karo and Hardy [9] did their calculation for $300 \mathrm{~K}$ cannot explain such large differences. A cause of uncertainty in the calculation is the values of the input data for $C_{44}$ and the compressibility $\beta$, since these quantities had not been determined experi- mentally and they had to use values extrapolated from the corresponding values for the other alkali fluorides. However, the extrapolated values for $C_{44}$ and $\beta$, $0.775 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{dyn} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ and $4.000 \times 10^{-12} \mathrm{~cm}^{2} / \mathrm{dyn}$, respectively, agree fairly well with the corresponding values obtained from models 2 to $5-(0.77$ to 0.82$) \times$ $\times 10^{11} \mathrm{dyn} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ and $(3.71$ to 3.76$) \times 10^{-12} \mathrm{~cm}^{2} / \mathrm{dyn}-$ and we may therefore con- clude that this uncertainty does not account for the discrepancy with experi- ment either.
