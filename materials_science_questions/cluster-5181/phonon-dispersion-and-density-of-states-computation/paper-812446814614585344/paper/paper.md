# Electron-phonon coupling in the B820 subunit form of LH1 studied by temperature dependence of optical spectra

T. Pullerrits¹, F. van Mourik*, R. Monshouwer, R.W. Visschers, R. van Grondelle

Faculty of Physics and Astronomy, Free University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands

## Abstract
The temperature dependence of absorption and selectively excited fluorescence spectra of B820 subunit of the bacterial light-harvesting antenna is modeled by using the impurity center theory of optical spectra. In order to get acceptable agreement between theory and experiment, we suggest that due to the dimeric nature of B820, the electronic transition is weakly coupled to a rather broad distribution of protein phonons.

---

## 1. Introduction
Of the total pigment content of photosynthetic organisms, only a minor fraction is capable of performing photochemistry. The majority of the pigments is associated with the so-called light-harvesting antenna (LHA). Light absorbed by the pigments in the LHA is "transported" through the LHA to the photochemical reaction center (RC) [1]. In photosynthetic purple bacteria the LHA pigments are noncovalently bound to small transmembrane polypeptides. The absorption spectra of the in vivo antenna pigments are strongly redshifted relative to the spectra of the pigments in organic solvents (880 versus 780 nm). The nature of this red-shift is still unknown, although it is clear that both exciton splitting [2] and hydrogen-bonds between the protein and the pigment play a role [3]. The core antenna of the purple bacteria (LH1) can be reversibly dissociated into a small subunit form, B820 [4]. This B820 has been shown to be a protein-bound dimer of bacteriochlorophyll (BChl) [2,5]. From site-selected fluorescence spectroscopy it was deduced that the near-IR absorption band of B820 is (contrary to what was found for special pair of the RC [6]) mainly inhomogeneously broadened [7]. The ratio of inhomogeneous to homogeneous bandwidth is an important parameter for the energy transfer kinetics in the antenna [8]. Here we have made a more quantitative assessment of the inhomogeneous broadening and homogeneous line shape of B820 by applying the impurity center theory [9,10] to the temperature-dependent absorption and selectively excited emission spectra of B820 [7].

(IDF) $\varrho(v)$, which is defined as the relative concentration of molecules having their purely electronic transition frequency at a displacement $v$ from the central frequency $v_{0}$. If the homogeneous absorption and fluorescence spectra are $I(\omega)$ and $E(\omega)$, respectively, then the selectively excited fluorescence spectra of a dilute solution (B820) can be described by a convolution-type integral.

$$
F(\omega_{\mathrm{e}}, \omega_{\mathrm{f}})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} v \, E(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}-v)[I(\omega_{\mathrm{e}}-v) \varrho(v)]. \quad (1)
$$

Here $\omega_{\mathrm{e}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{f}}$ are the excitation and detection frequencies.

A temperature-dependent homogeneous spectral band consists, in general, of a sharp Lorentzian zero-phonon line (ZPL) $\Phi_{0}(\omega, T)$ accompanied by a broad phonon wing (PW) $\Phi(\omega, T)$ [9,10]. The later consists of one- and more-phonon transitions. In the harmonic Franck-Condon electron-phonon coupling case,

$$
\Phi(\omega, T)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \Phi_{m}(\omega, T), \tag{2}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{m}(\omega, T)=& \frac{1}{m!} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} v_{1} \cdots \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} v_{m} f(v_{1}, T) \cdots f(v_{m}, T) \\
& \times \Phi_{0}(\omega-\Omega+v_{1}+\cdots+v_{m}, T). \quad (3)
\end{aligned}
$$

The PW $\Phi(\omega, T)$ is completely determined by the phonon function $f(v, T)$. In linear approximation for the electron-phonon coupling, the phonon function has a simple physical meaning, and can be represented in the form of the sum of the Stokes and anti-Stokes one-phonon sidebands as

$$
f(v, T)=[n(v, T)+1] f_{0}(v)+n(-v, T) f_{0}(-v), \quad (4)
$$

where $f_{0}(v)$ is the phonon density of states weighted by the electron-phonon coupling strength and $n(v, T)$ is the occupation number of the phonon with frequency $v$ at temperature $T$.

The function $f_{0}(v)$ can be extracted numerically from experimental PW measurements at $T=0\,\text{K}$:

$$
f_{0}(\omega)=\Phi(\omega, 0) \bigg/ \alpha-\frac{1}{\alpha \omega} \int_{0}^{\omega} \Phi(\omega-v, 0) v f_{0}(v) \mathrm{d} v. \quad (5)
$$

Here $\alpha$ is the Debye-Waller factor.

## 3. Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the excitation wavelength dependence of the laser-induced fluorescence spectrum measured at $4\,\text{K}$. The maximum of the broad featureless emission is moving together with the excitation wavelength and does not show significant narrowing even upon excitation into the red edge of the absorption band. In order to calculate the phonon function, we have assumed that the PW at $T \approx 0\,\text{K}$ has a simple analytic shape

$$
\Phi(\omega, 0)=
\begin{cases}
\omega \exp(-\omega/\mu), & 0 \leqslant \omega, \\
0, & \omega < 0,
\end{cases} \tag{6}
$$

determined by a single adjustable parameter $\mu$. Comparison with experiment gives $\mu=110\,\text{cm}^{-1}$. We do not know the Debye-Waller factor because the resonant 0-0 emission is mixed with the scattered light of the laser. Fortunately, we can estimate it from the temperature dependence of the absorption spectra which are virtually independent of temperature below $100\,\text{K}$ [7]. If the electron-phonon coupling is assumed to be too strong ($\alpha$ is small), the calculated inhomogeneous absorption spectrum (convolution of IDF and homogeneous absorption) starts to broaden with temperature

From the same simulation we estimate that the IDF has a width of $250 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. Now we can apply Eq. (5) to get $f_{0}(v)$. In Fig. 1 the experimental and calculated absorption curves at 4 K, together with the IDF are given. The fits at higher temperatures are of the same quality (not shown). The PW at 4 and 300 K, together with $f_{0}(v)$ as used in calculations, is given in Fig. 2.

Figure 3 gives the experimental and model fluorescence curves at three different temperatures if excitation is into the red edge of the absorption band. The apparent deviation from the Urbach rule of anti-Stokes emission at 160 K is due to the excitation through the anti-Stokes part of the PW. For a better understanding we have also given the density of excited molecules (in brackets in Eq. (1)) at 160 K which is responsible for this effect. From the deviation between the model and experimental traces at 77 K it is apparent that the phonon function that we have constructed from the simple shape of the phonon wing (6) does not describe the low-frequency phonons which are populated at 40-80 K very accurately. However, we have been able to get at least qualitative agreement between the experimental and model fluorescence curves at all investigated temperatures and excitation wavelengths. The deviations between calculated and experimental traces could be due to the spectral hole burning which takes place at low temperature and distorts the IDF in a complicated way. We also considered the possibility of Herzberg-Teller electron-phonon coupling besides the Franck-Condon coupling, which causes the breakdown of mirror symmetry of the conjugated homogeneous absorption and fluorescence spectra, i.e., the Debye-Waller factors for fluorescence and absorption can differ. In the case of excitation into the blue wing of the absorption this would give rise to much better quantitative agreement between model and experiment.

A further complication is due to the dimeric nature of B820. The coupling strength of the dimer is smaller than the width of the IDF [2,7]. This may imply that electron-phonon coupling parameters are not constant over the spectral band. In the blue part of the absorption band, one would also expect that the upper excitonic state of the dimer starts to play a role.

Finally, we point at the remarkable width of the phonon function at 0 K, which we have to use in order to get a reasonable agreement between model and experiment. We believe that this is due to the dimeric nature of B820, and is caused by the

uncorrelated modulation of excitonic coupling by protein movements (phonons). At the same time the electron-phonon coupling is not enhanced ($S=0.5$-$0.6$). In the special pair of the bacterial RC, a specific so-called marker mode has been described [6], which is strongly coupled to the electronic transition. It has a broad profile and a frequency of about $120\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. The width of this profile has been interpreted as a fast damping of the mode, and the structure of the phonon sideband as a Franck-Con- don progression of the mode. We have also con- sidered a symmetric shape for $f_{0}(v)$, i.e. to interpret it as a specific mode with a damping rate determined by the width of the profile. However, we were unable to get an acceptable fit to the red part of the fluorescence spectra despite the fact that we also included the BChl $a$ low-frequency vibrations in that region [11]. There- fore, we consider the existence of a "marker mode" of intermolecular vibration with well-defined frequency quite unlikely in the case of the B820 dimer.
