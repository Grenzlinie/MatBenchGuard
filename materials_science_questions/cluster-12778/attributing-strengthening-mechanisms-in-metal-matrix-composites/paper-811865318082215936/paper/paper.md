# Structural Evolution of Al-Cr Alloy during Processing

D. Vojtěch $^{1,a}$, A. Michalcová $^{1,b}$ and P. Novák $^{1,c}$

$^{1}$Department of Metals and Corrosion Engineering, Institute of Chemical Technology, Prague,
Technická 5, 166 28 Prague 6, Czech Republic

$^{a}$Dalibor.Vojtech@vscht.cz, $^{b}$michalca@vscht.cz, $^{c}$panovak@vscht.cz

**Keywords:** Rapid solidification, powder metallurgy, Al-Cr alloy, aluminium, phase transformation.

Abstract. Aluminium-chromium based alloys are promising candidates for manufacture of light components exposed to elevated temperatures. The work describes properties of Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt.%Ti alloy. The rapidly solidified powder was prepared by the pressure nitrogen melt atomization. The powder was then subject to heat treatment in order to investigate solid state phase transformations. Compaction of the powder was carried out by hot extrusion after preheating at 450 °C. Microstructure, phase composition and structural transformations on heat treatment were investigated in the as-atomized powder, as well as in the as-extruded alloy. It is found that metastable state of the rapidly solidified powder is characterized by presence of quasi-crystalline phases and supersaturated solid solution. Heating before and during the hot extrusion induces decomposition of the supersaturated solid solution and quasicrystalline to crystalline phase transformations. The hot extruded alloy has a refined recrystallized structure that remains very stable aven after long-term annealing at 400 °C. Mechanical properties of the extruded alloy are discussed in terms of strengthening mechanisms.

## Introduction

Aluminium alloys have been widely used in construction of light components for aerospace and automotive industry. In order to improve performance of these materials, especially at moderately elevated temperatures, novel alloying systems, are extensively studied. Al-based alloys containing transition metals (TM) in amounts of several weight percents have attracted a great interest, since transition metals, such as chromium, molybdenum, zirconium, iron and others are generally referred to as "slowly diffusing elements" in solid aluminium, see Fig. 1.

![](./images/811865318082215936_1.jpg)

Fig. 1 Temperature dependence of diffusion coefficients $D$ of various elements in aluminium [1].

Chromium appears to be very prospective from this viewpoint due to its very slow diffusivity and also due to low cost and good availability. In commercial wrought aluminium alloys, chromium is known to have a strong effect on recrystallization even in concentrations of a few tenths of weight percent [2]. Alloys containing higher concentrations of chromium cannot be produced through conventional casting and ingot metallurgy route, since Cr-rich intermetallics have negative effect on toughness, strength and formability. These alloys are generally produced by powder metallurgy (PM) that involves:
1. powder preparation by melt atomization, mechanical alloying or other methods and 2. powder compaction mostly performed by hot extrusion [3]. PM Al-TM alloys are characterized by refined and homogeneous structure that has positive impact on strength and thermal stability of mechanical properties [4-7].

The presented paper describes properties of Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti alloy produced by: 1. rapid solidification of melt and 2. hot extrusion of the powder. The PM Al-Cr based alloys are promising materials for elevated temperature applications and they have been considered possible competitors of heavier steels or more expensive Ti alloys and aluminium matrix composites. As was pointed out earlier, chromium has a very low diffusion coefficient in solid Al and, in addition, it exhibits an extended solid solubility in the rapidly solidified state. Besides chromium, we have focused on a system alloyed with other thermally stabilizing metals – Fe and Ti, hence certain synergic effects of additives can be anticipated.

### Experimental
The Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti alloy was prepared by melting of high purity (99.99 %) aluminium with Al-10wt.%Cr, Al-17wt.%Fe and Al-5wt.%Ti master alloys in a resistance furnace. Afterwards, the alloys were melted again in an induction furnace in nitrogen atmosphere and then atomized by nitrogen at 500 kPa pressure. Since a refined microstructure and a large fraction of metastable phases can be anticipated mainly in small powder particles solidified at high rates, a granulometric fraction of $<45\ \mu\text{m}$ was separated for subsequent study and extrusion. This powder fraction represented approx. 50 % of the total powder weight. The selected powder fraction was then encapsulated in aluminium container and preheated at $450\ ^\circ\text{C}$ for 40 minutes followed by hot extrusion in a hydraulic press at a ram speed of $0.2\ \text{mm s}^{-1}$ and extrusion ratio of 11:1. A rod of 6 mm in diameter was produced by this way.

A common metallographic procedure (mounting, grinding, polishing and etching in 0.5% HF) was used to characterize the microstructures of both the rapidly solidified (RS) powder and the extruded rod. Light microscope (LM-Olympus PME 3), scanning electron microscope (SEM-Hitachi S4700 with EDS Noran) and transmission electron microscope (TEM-JEOL JEM 1200 EX operating at 120 kV) were used. Thin foils required by TEM technique were prepared by electrolytic polishing in a solution of 75 % methanol and 25 % nitric acid at $-20^\circ\text{C}$ and 10 V. X-ray diffraction (XRD) (X'Pert Philips) was employed to determine phase composition of the alloy. Solid state phase transformations were monitored by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (131 SETARAM calorimeter) within a temperature range of $50-550\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at a heating rate of $5\ ^\circ\text{C min}^{-1}$ under argon atmosphere. Mechanical properties of the powder and extruded rod were determined by room temperature hardness measurements and tensile tests (loading machine FPZ100/l, loading rate 3 $\text{mm min}^{-1}$). Hardness, tensile properties and microstructure were examined also after long-term annealing of the powder and extruded rod at 350 and $400\ ^\circ\text{C}$, in order to reveal structural stability.

### Results and Discussion
#### Rapidly Solidified Powder.
SEM micrograph of the RS Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti powder (particle size $<45\ \mu\text{m}$) is shown in Fig. 2. It is seen in Fig. 2a that the particles are nearly of spherical shape with a relatively smooth surface. These attributes are typical for powders prepared by the melt atomization by inert gases. Image of sectioned powder particles in Fig. 2b indicates that the rapidly solidified alloy contains intermetallic phases finely dispersed in the $\alpha(\text{Al})$ matrix. Morphology of intermetallics varies from irregular to almost spherical and detailed observation

proves that the latter shape predominates in the powder. In some particles, sites where nucleation of the solid phase starts can be clearly recognized (marked by arrows in Fig. 2b). A very fine structure corresponds to these sites, which is in accordance with deep melt undercooling. As the solidification proceeds, released heat (recalescence) slows down the solidification, which results in coarsening of the microstructure.

DSC curve of the RS powder shown in Fig. 3 illustrates a degree of metastability of the alloy. A weak exothermal effect is clearly seen at temperatures between $450-500\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. This effect can be attributed to transformations of metastable phases present in the structure. XRD pattern in Fig. 4 (pattern A) proves that phase composition of the RS powder is dominated by crystalline $\alpha'(Al)$ supersaturated solid solution and $\text{Al}_{13}(\text{Cr,Fe})_2$ phase, but also quasicrystalline (Q) icosahedral phases $\text{Al}_{84.6}\text{Cr}_{15.4}$ and $\text{Al}_{95}\text{Fe}_4\text{Cr}$ are present. It is assumed that the Q phases given above contain ternary or quaternary additives present in the alloy; hence their chemical formulae do not exactly correspond to real chemical compositions [7]. The presence of Q phases and the supersaturated $\alpha'(Al)$ solid solution is a direct consequence of rapid solidification. At a high cooling rate, i.e. at a deep supercooling of the melt, the decreased diffusivity of atoms results in a reduced order of the atoms in the clusters which are sites of future quasi-crystalline nuclei. The supersaturated solid solution is formed due to a solute trapping [8] which occurs at a rapidly moving solid/liquid interface. High growth rate of a solid phase reduces the transportation rate of solute elements from a solid to a liquid phase and forms a solid solution supersaturated by elements present in the alloy.

At elevated temperatures, e.g. at $350\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, the Q phases transform to thermodynamically more stable crystalline intermetallic phases - mainly to $\text{Al}_{13}(\text{Cr,Fe})_2$, as is seen by comparing XRD patterns A and B in Fig. 4. The $\alpha'(Al)$ supersaturated solid solution decomposes to produce fine $\text{Al}_{13}(\text{Cr,Fe})_2$ precipitates and $\alpha(Al)$ solid solution with low concentrations of Cr and Fe. The precipitation brings about a hardening of the powder alloy during annealing, see Fig. 5.

![](./images/811865318082215936_4.jpg)

Fig. 4 XRD patterns of the Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti alloy: A - rapidly solidified, B - annealed at 350 °C / 50 h.

![](./images/811865318082215936_5.jpg)

Fig. 5 Room temperature hardness HV0.005 of the Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti powder alloy versus annealing time at 350 °C.

Solid state phase transformations occurring in the powder alloy at elevated temperatures that cause the thermal effect on DSC trace in Fig. 3 and hardening in Fig. 5 can be summarized by following equation:

$$\mathrm{Al}_{95} \mathrm{Fe}_{4} \mathrm{Cr}+\mathrm{Al}_{84.6} \mathrm{Cr}_{15.4}+\alpha^{\prime}(\mathrm{Al}) \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}+\alpha(\mathrm{Al}) \tag{1}$$

The difference between Figs. 3 and 4 in terms of temperatures at which phase transformations occur can be attributed to a thermal hysteresis of these transformations upon progressive heating during DSC analysis. The DSC analysis and hardness measurement also indicate that the preheating at 450 °C / 40 min of the powder before the hot extrusion leads to complete transformation of metastable phases present in the original RS microstructure, according to Eq. 1.

Hot Extruded Alloy. Cross-section and longitudinal section of the as-extruded material can be seen in TEM images shown in Fig. 6.

![](./images/811865318082215936_6.jpg)

Fig. 6 TEM micrographs of the hot extruded Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti alloy: a) cross section, b) longitudinal section.

The images demonstrate that structure is very fine and is composed of recrystallized $\alpha(\mathrm{Al})$ grains (light) and spheroids of $\mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}$ phase (dark). The phase composition was verified also by XRD. DSC trace in Fig. 7 confirms absence of metastable phases, since no thermal effects corresponding to their transformations are observed. The average $\alpha(\mathrm{Al})$ grain size, $\mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}$ spheroid diameter and interparticle spacing are 0.6 $\mu \mathrm{m}$, 0.12 $\mu \mathrm{m}$ and 0.3 $\mu \mathrm{m}$, respectively. The refined structure is a result of rapid solidification of the melt on the atomization. The recrystallized grains form upon the hot extrusion of the rapidly solidified powder.

![](./images/811865318082215936_7.jpg)

Fig. 7. DSC trace of the hot extruded Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt%Ti alloy.

Due to the plastic deformation at a relatively high temperature, new recrystallized grains with low concentration of lattice defects nucleate at a number of sites in the structure, e.g. at boundaries of original deformed grains. New grains tend to grow, but migration of grain boundaries is strongly suppressed by $Al_{13}(Cr,Fe)_2$ particles. Therefore, the observed fine grained structure of the as-extruded alloy is a result of high density of very fine $Al_{13}(Cr,Fe)_2$ particles originating from rapid solidification and from decomposition of metastable phases, see Eq. 1. The Cr-rich intermetallic phases are very stable, since they dissolve in solid solution extremely slowly and only at high temperatures close to the alloy melting point. Additionally, very low diffusion coefficient of Cr in solid Al, see Fig. 1, makes these phases resistant to coarsening.

The hardness and strength values reflect two main contributions: 1. Hall-Petch strengthening, 2. Orowan strengthening. The Hall-Petch contribution to the yield strength $\Delta \sigma_{\text{HP}}$ can be described as follows:

$$
\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{HP}}=k d^{-1 / 2}, \tag{2}
$$

where $k$ is the Hall-Petch coefficient and $d$ is the grain size. If the $k$ value is equal to $171 \mathrm{MPa} \mu \mathrm{m}^{1 / 2}$ [9], and the average grain size of $0.6 \mu \mathrm{m}$ is used, then Eq. 2 gives the value of $\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{HP}}=221 \mathrm{MPa}$. This value accounts for approx. $84 \%$ of the yield strength value. The Orowan strengthening is caused by finely dispersed $\mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}$ particles, see Fig. 6, with reported hardness of ~700 HV [10]. This contribution $\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{O}}$ can be roughly estimated by using the following equation:

$$
\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{O}}=3 G b / L, \tag{3}
$$

where $G$, $b$ and $L$ are the shear modulus of the matrix (26 GPa), the Burgers vector (0.286 nm) and the average interparticle spacing, respectively [11,12]. Based on $L=0.3 \mu \mathrm{m}$, see above, the Orowan contribution could be of the order of 74 MPa, i.e. much weaker than the Hall-Petch contribution. If we take into account that a large fraction of the $\mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}$ particles is located along the grain boundaries where they do not cause any significant strengthening, the Orowan contribution is probably much lower than the estimated 74 MPa.

An important feature of the investigated alloy is its excellent ductility. It can be attributed mainly to low concentrations of solute elements and absence of precipitates in the $\alpha(\mathrm{Al})$ matrix.

![](./images/811865318082215936_8.jpg)

Fig. 8 Room temperature mechanical properties of the hot extruded Al-6.0wt.%Cr-2.1wt.%Fe-0.5wt.%Ti alloy versus annealing time at 400 °C (HV-hardness, A-elongation, UTS- ultimate tensile strength, YS-yield strength).

Fig. 8 illustrates development of room temperature tensile properties and hardness of the investigated alloy caused by a long-term annealing at 400 °C. It is observed that the alloy shows excellent thermal stability that is much higher as compared to commercial casting or wrought aluminium alloys. The room temperature mechanical properties of the Al-Cr alloy do not deviate from original values even after 200 hours annealing at 400 °C. It is primarily caused by:
1. very slow diffusivities of chromium and iron in solid aluminium, see Fig. 1, and
2. low equilibrium solubility of Cr and Fe in solid Al (0.4 wt.% at 661°C and 0.06 wt.% at 655°C for Cr and Fe, respectively [10]).

Consider for a simplicity that an average diffusion distance $X$ of a diffusing atom, its diffusion coefficient $D$ and diffusion time $\tau$ are related to each other by a simple equation:

$$
X^{2}=2 D \tau \tag{4}
$$

By using $D_{(\mathrm{Cr}, 400{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C})}=4.3 \times 10^{-17} \mathrm{~cm} 2 \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ [1], it can be estimated that a diffusion distance of a Cr atom at $400{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C} / 200 \mathrm{~h}$ is about $0.08 \mu \mathrm{m}$ which is significantly less than the average $\mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}$ interparticle spacing $(0.3 \mu \mathrm{m})$. Since coarsening and coalescence of the finely dispersed $\mathrm{Al}_{13}(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{Fe})_{2}$ spheroids in the as-extruded structure would demand much larger diffusion distance of Cr atoms than $0.08 \mu \mathrm{m}$, these spheroids are not prone either to coarsening or to coalescence. Moreover, their dissolution in $\alpha(\mathrm{Al})$ at $400{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ is also negligible, due to small equilibrium solid solubility of $\mathrm{Cr}$, see above. As a result, the Hall-Petch strengthening, see Eq. 2, operates to a great extent after annealing due to the ability of the spheroids to block the grain growth efficiently.

## Summary
In this work, it is illustrated that the melt atomization produces Al-Cr-Fe-Ti powder alloy in a highly metastable state. This state is characterized by presence of quasicrystalline phases and supersaturated solid solution. Upon further treatment by the hot extrusion, the metastable phases transform to form stable crystalline phases. It may be accompanied by hardening of the alloy and by thermal effects detectable by DSC. The resulting hot extruded alloy has a refined recrystallized structure with a large fraction of dispersed intermetallics. Mechanical properties result mainly from the Hall-Petch strengthening. Due to a low diffusion coefficient and solubility of Cr in solid aluminium, the compacted alloy shows excellent thermal stability.
