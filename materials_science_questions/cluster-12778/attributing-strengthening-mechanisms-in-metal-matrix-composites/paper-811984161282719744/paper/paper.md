# Powder Metallurgy Ti-6Al-4V-xB Alloys: Processing, Microstructure, and Properties

S. Tamirisakandala, R.B. Bhat, V.A. Ravi, and D.B. Miracle

This article describes processing of Ti-6Al-4V-xB alloys via pre-alloyed and blended elemental powder metallurgy techniques. The influence of processing on the microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of the alloys produced by these two routes is summarized, and the thermo-mechanical response and optimization of processing parameters to produce defect-free engineering shapes in these alloys is also discussed. Potential applications and issues involved in the development of these materials for damage-critical components are highlighted.

## INTRODUCTION

The requirement for higher structural efficiency (a combination of stiffness and strength normalized by density) provides a significant motivation for the development of improved aerospace materials. Structurally efficient metallic materials provide a direct path for reducing mass, thus improving performance. A number of advanced aerospace missions, such as hypersonic flight and low-cost access to space, require dramatic reductions in flight mass. Materials with higher structural efficiency can provide enabling capabilities in these applications.

Relatively small boron additions to conventional titanium alloys provide important improvements in strength, stiffness, and microstructural stability. Because boron is essentially insoluble in titanium at all temperatures of interest, the intermetallic compound TiB is formed for even very small boron additions. The density of TiB is nearly equal to those of conventional Ti alloys, but its stiffness is over four times higher than conventional titanium alloys (~500 GPa for TiB vs. ~110 GPa for conventional titanium alloys). Thus,
TiB offers significant improvements in stiffness, tensile strength, creep, and fatigue properties.¹ Since TiB is in thermodynamic equilibrium with titanium alloys, there are no interfacial reactions to degrade properties with exposure at elevated temperature. Further, because the coefficient of thermal expansion of TiB is nearly equal to values for titanium alloys, residual stresses are nearly eliminated.

The TiB generally forms as elongated particles with an aspect ratio of ~10:1. Isotropic properties are obtained when the TiB particles are randomly oriented, but intentional alignment of the TiB through processes such as extrusion or rolling can produce higher strength and stiffness along the direction of TiB alignment. Most conventional processing techniques have been validated for boron-modified titanium (Ti-B) alloys.²

The TiB particles effectively pin grain boundaries so that a fine grain structure is retained even well above the beta transus and after cooling back into the α+β phase field. This feature has significant implications regarding the ability to devise new thermo-mechanical processing (TMP) schemes with dramatic improvements in affordability and manufacturability.

Although Ti-B alloys are now used commercially in automotive applications (e.g., valves),² the materials and processes have been optimized for reduced cost rather than for achieving the highest balance of properties necessary for fracture critical applications. These same materials and processes are therefore not likely to be adequate for high-performance materials and fracture-critical components in the aerospace industry. Thus, research and

## PROCESSING

A flow diagram for pre-alloyed (PA) and blended elemental (BE) powder processing of Ti-B alloys is shown in Figure 1. In the PA process, a boron source, typically $TiB_2$ or elemental boron powder, is added to the titanium alloy. Additional titanium is also added to the melt to compensate for the titanium scavenged from the titanium alloy to transform the boron or $TiB_2$ powder to TiB. The boron is completely dissolved in the liquid titanium alloy, which is subsequently converted to powder using conventional powder-conversion facilities and processes.³ The TiB particles form in the solid state, and are uniformly and randomly distributed in each powder particle (Figure 3a). Pre-alloyed powder produced via this route is subsequently outgassed and consolidated to a billet.

In the BE process, boron is added by intermixing solid particles. Either elemental or master alloy metal powders may be used, and boron is added as either elemental boron or $TiB_2$ powder. An appropriate blending process is required to ensure a uniform distribution of the powders. The material described below was produced by first wet-blending, then drying and dry blending. The blended powder must then be subjected to appropriate outgassing to remove volatile impurities. Consolidation is accomplished using standard equipment and techniques, and the actual consolidation parameters are optimized for Ti-B alloys. The BE compact is then subjected to a reaction heat treatment to convert boron or $TiB_2$ to TiB. The time and temperature for conversion to TiB depends upon the size of the phases prior to conversion. A typical process requires $1,300^\circ$C for 6 h.

In both the PA and BE processes, the consolidated Ti-B alloy billet may be subjected to further TMP to produce the desired shape and microstructure. Semi-finished or finished shapes can be produced via conventional metalworking techniques and facilities. However, optimization of the actual process parameters is required for Ti-B alloys.

The PA approach has the advantage of producing finer scale microstructural features due to rapid solidification, but is limited to hypoeutectic compositions to avoid the formation of large, primary TiB in the liquid metal phase. The BE approach extends the compositions to obtain higher volume fractions of TiB without the formation of primary TiB since processing is conducted in the solid state. However, the BE approach is less economical due to additional processing steps required, and the microstructural features are usually coarser compared to PA powder material.

## THERMO-MECHANICAL PROCESSING

The objective of this work is to guide optimization of processing parameters to avoid microstructural damage and to obtain desirable microstructures. Processing maps were developed and microstructural deformation mechanisms have been identified on the basis of hot compression experiments for wide temperature and strain rate ranges.⁴ This article describes the influence of TiB on the deformation mechanisms during TMP of Ti-64-xB alloys. (This nomenclature is used to emphasize that the composition of the metal phase in both alloys is within the specifications of Ti-6Al-4V. The actual overall alloy compositions are Ti-6.2Al-4.0V-1.6B and Ti-5.5Al-3.5V-2.9B.)

The processing map for PA Ti-64-1.6B compacts in the ranges $900–1,200^\circ$C and $10^{-3}–10$ s⁻¹ is shown in Figure 2a. The map reveals that at slow strain rates ($\sim10^{-2}$ s⁻¹) in the $\alpha+\beta$ range, PA Ti-64-1.6B exhibits conventional fine-grained superplasticity similar to Ti-64 with an equiaxed $\alpha+\beta$ microstructure. PA Ti-64-1.6B exhibits superplastic behavior in the monolithic beta phase field also with moderately large elongations (200–250%). The presence of TiB enables beta superplasticity by stabilizing a fine grain size, which otherwise rapidly grows to the order of a few millimeters in Ti-64. Grain boundary sliding as well as matrix/TiB interfacial sliding with simultaneous diffusional accommodation are found to contribute to the superplasticity mechanisms. At

The processing map for hot working of the BE Ti-64-2.9B compacts is shown in Figure 2b. Superplastic characteristics in the two-phase field are completely disabled in this material due to the presence of TiB. The safe window for deformation processing of this alloy is restricted to temperatures above the beta transus and strain rates below $\sim1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$, where either superplasticity or dynamic recovery mechanisms are operative. A large regime of instability manifested as cavitation at the matrix/TiB interfaces and TiB fracture occurs because of insufficient accommodation of the stresses at the interfaces. The intensity of these defects decreases with increasing temperature and decreasing strain rate, both of which enhance the accommodation processes. The ability to achieve superplasticity in the beta phase due to the presence of TiB could be advantageously used to form complex shapes with controlled microstructures using low-capacity presses at much faster strain rates $(10^{-1}-10^{-3}\mathrm{s}^{-1})$ than are typically employed in the superplastic forming of titanium alloys. The increased complexity in processing of BE Ti-64-2.9B compared to PA Ti-64-1.6B is attributed to the higher volume fraction of coarser TiB.

## MICROSTRUCTURES

A backscattered electron image of the cross-section of a PA Ti-64-1.6B powder particle is given in Figure 3a, which shows the presence and distribution of TiB representative of each powder particle. Figure 3b shows the microstructure after consolidation of a PA powder billet by blind-die compaction at $1,200^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for 180 s under a pressure of 1,400 MPa followed by air-cooling. TiB whiskers are uniformly distributed and randomly oriented in three dimensions (hereafter this condition is referred to as 3-D). Two distinct length scales of TiB whiskers are present in the PA material: eutectic TiB of about 8 vol.% with widths in the range 1–2 $\mu$m and aspect ratios in the range 8–12 (Figure 3a–d), and nanometer sized TiB of about 2 vol.% with average length of 500 nm and diameter of 50 nm (Figure 3d). Rapid solidification of the PA powder causes supersaturation of boron in the solid solution, which precipitates in the form of these fine TiB whiskers upon exposure to elevated temperature during powder consolidation. A few isolated coarse particles of primary TiB are also present, and their source is identified to be associated with the melting practice. Based on the Ti-B binary phase diagram, Ti-64-1.6B is expected to be a hypoeutectic alloy, and so primary TiB is not expected to form from inspection of the Ti-B binary phase diagram. However, calculated quaternary phase diagrams show a three-phase (liquid+$\beta$+TiB) region that extends the entire compositional range between the titanium terminal solid solution and TiB phase just below the eutectic reaction. This phase field is narrow along the temperature axis. Thus, formation of primary TiB may be avoided by application of sufficient superheat and a modest quench rate through this phase field.

The matrix exhibits a fine-grained ($\sim3\ \mu$m) equiaxed alpha microstructure with the beta phase at the boundaries. The presence of TiB not only restricts grain growth at high temperatures but also assists in the particle-stimulated nucleation of the alpha phase from

The microstructures of the material in Figure 3b after extrusion at 1,100°C in the longitudinal and transverse directions are shown in Figure 3c and d, respectively. After extrusion, TiB is aligned along the extrusion axis (this microstructural condition is hereafter referred to as 1-D). The transverse micrograph shows the presence of uniformly distributed nanometer-sized TiB. The microstructure of BE Ti-64-2.9B after compaction at 1,200°C is shown in Figure 3e and, in Figure 3f, after extrusion at 1,300°C in the longitudinal direction. The TiB particles occupy a volume of ~20%, with lengths of about 20–50 µm and aspect ratios of about 5–15 in a relatively coarse-grained matrix (~15 µm). Comparison of PA and BE microstructures reveals that relatively finer matrix grain sizes and significantly finer TiB sizes are obtained with the PA method. The microstructures in Figure 3 illustrate that a variety of microstructures can be obtained in Ti-64-xB alloys, depending on processing conditions, producing a wide range of mechanical property combinations.

## MECHANICAL PROPERTIES

Room-temperature mechanical properties of PA Ti-64-1.6B and BE Ti-64-2.9B are compared with those of Ti-64 in the mill annealed condition⁵ in Figure 4a. In PA Ti-64-1.6B 3-D, the stiffness is 24% higher and the strength is 35% higher than in Ti-64. In the longitudinal direction of the extruded product (PA Ti-64-1.6B 1-D), the stiffness increased by 38% and strength by 46% compared to Ti-64. The increase in stiffness is due to more effective load transfer when the TiB particles are oriented along the loading axis.

Detailed strength modeling revealed that the observed strengthening is reasonably consistent with predictions based on continuum and dislocation models when appropriate length scales of the various micro-constituents are considered.⁶ The majority of the strengthening (~80%) derives from the load sharing mechanism by the micrometer-sized TiB. Hall-Petch strengthening (~14%) due to grain refinement and Orowan (~6%) strengthening due to interactions of nanometer-sized TiB whiskers also contribute to the overall strength. In the case of BE Ti-64-2.9B 1-D, stiffness increased by about 82% over Ti-64 while the strength increase was 34%. These results indicate that while the stiffness approximately scales with the volume fraction of TiB per the rule of mixtures, the strength depends on the microstructural parameters, which in turn are influenced by the processing method. Both alloys exhibited a 2.5–3.5% elongation, with the exception of BE Ti-64-2.9B 3-D, which had poor ductility. In the PA material, primary TiB particles were responsible for failure initiation, while ductile dimpling and cup-and-cone fracture on all fracture surfaces indicated intrinsic ductile failure. Thus, elimination of primary TiB is expected to produce important improvements in tensile ductility. The fracture toughness of PA Ti-64-1.6B 3-D has been measured as 40–55 MPa√m. Elevated temperature strengths of both alloys up to 600°C are compared with those of Ti-64 and a high-temperature titanium alloy Ti-6Al-2Sn-4Zr-2Mo (Ti-6242)⁵ in Figure 4b. These values show that strength enhancements due to boron addition to Ti-64 are maintained at elevated temperatures and strengths are superior compared to Ti-6242.
