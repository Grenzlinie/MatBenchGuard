# Structure and Composition of Nanometer-Sized Nitrides in a Creep-Resistant Cast Austenitic Alloy

NEAL D. EVANS, PHILIP J. MAZIASZ, JOHN P. SHINGLEDECKER, and MICHAEL J. POLLARD

The microstructure of a new and improved high-temperature creep-resistant cast austenitic alloy, CF8C-Plus, was characterized after creep-rupture testing at 1023 K (750 °C) and 100 MPa. Microstructures were investigated by detailed scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Principal component analysis of EDS spectrum images was used to examine the complex precipitate morphology. Thermodynamic modeling was performed to predict equilibrium phases in this alloy as well as the compositions of these phases at relevant temperatures. The improved high-temperature creep strength of CF8C-Plus over its predecessor CF8C is suggested to be due to the modified microstructure and phase stability in the alloy, including the absence of $\delta$-ferrite in the as-cast condition and the development of a stable, slow-growing precipitation hardening nitride phase—the tetragonal Z-phase—which has not been observed before in cast austenitic stainless steels.

## I. INTRODUCTION

A new cast austenitic stainless steel designated CF8C-Plus, ASTM HG10MNN, has improved high-temperature creep properties relative to its predecessor, alloy CF8C. $^{[1-3]}$ Cast CF8C, nominally an 18Cr-10Ni stainless steel precipitation hardened by NbC, is the equivalent of wrought 347H stainless steel. It has a relatively low nickel balance, which results in the as-cast microstructure containing ~15 pct $\delta$-ferrite. $^{[3]}$ However, $\delta$-ferrite can transform relatively rapidly to $\sigma$ phase, $^{[4]}$ which embrittles the alloy and reduces creep ductility. Therefore, modifications to the chemistry of CF8C were sought to improve high-temperature creep properties by making it fully austenitic while maintaining precipitation hardening mechanisms and preventing the formation of other embrittling phases. The alloy design methodology used to modify the CF8C steel is described in detail elsewhere. $^{[1,2,5]}$ The new alloy, which is designated CF8C-Plus, contains additions of N, Mn, and Ni relative to standard CF8C; Mn is a substitutional element for Ni and increases the solubility of N in austenite. $^{[6]}$ Nominal compositions of CF8C and CF8C-Plus are given in Table I. This article investigates the microstructures observed in as-cast and creep-rupture tested alloys that are proposed to result in the improved high-temperature creep properties of the new alloy. Microstructures were investigated by detailed scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

## II. THERMODYNAMIC MODELING

Regions of equilibrium phase stability of CF8C-Plus were determined computationally using the thermodynamic program JMatPro (version 4.1; Sente Software, Guildford, United Kingdom). $^{[7]}$ The nominal alloy composition (weight percent) used for this calculation is given in Table I. Presented in Figure 1 is the calculated temperature-dependent relative amount of each equilibrium phase predicted to form in this alloy. The equilibrium phases predicted to solidify or precipitate are the cubic $\gamma$ parent phase, with precipitation that includes cubic M(C,N), $M_{23}C_{6}$, trigonal $M_{2}(C,N)$, and the tetragonal Z-phase and $\sigma$ phase. Additionally, the temperature-dependent compositions of these phases were computed; compositions at relevant temperatures are compiled in Table II.

## IV. RESULTS

### A. As-Cast Microstructure
A representative view of the CF8C-Plus as-cast microstructure is shown within the SEM image of Figure 2. The microstructure consists of primary austenite and cubic, Nb-rich, MX-type interdendritic phase ($X = \text{C,N}$), which will be referred to in this study as $\text{Nb(C,N)}$. The presence of some Mn-based inclusions also is noted. No other precipitate phases were observed in either the austenite or the interdendritic regions. As intended, the additions of N, Mn, and Ni were successful in stabilizing the austenite phase; no $\delta$-ferrite was observed in the as-cast material. The compositions of the observed phases, measured within the SEM by EDS, are reported in Table III.

### B. Microstructure Development During Creep-Rupture Testing in Air at $1023\ \text{K}\ (750\ ^{\circ}\text{C})$ and $100\ \text{MPa}$
Precipitation readily occurred in CF8C-Plus during creep-rupture testing in air at $1023\ \text{K}\ (750\ ^{\circ}\text{C})$ (684.4 hours, 100 MPa). The image in Figure 3 was obtained by summing SE and BSE images acquired from the same field of view at 5 kV. Here, the high-Z Nb-rich $\text{M(C,N)}$ phase was observed readily in the BSE signal, whereas the darker phase was observed easily in relief within the SE signal because it polishes at a different rate than other phases. EDS measurements revealed that the metallic composition of the darker phase is consistent with a Cr-carbide. These carbides have precipitated most heavily in the interdendritic regions of the alloy and grow during creep testing at $1023\ \text{K}\ (750\ ^{\circ}\text{C})$.

Detailed microcharacterization of the alloy's interdendritic and intragranular regions was conducted in the TEM. Shown in Figure 4 is an area containing both primary austenite and some interdendritic features in the upper portion of the image. Diffraction patterns acquired at low index zones from both the blocky, faceted features and the lamellar features in Figure 4 are consistent with these precipitates being the face-centered cubic $\text{M}_{23}\text{C}_{6}$ carbide and $\text{Nb(C,N)}$ phases, respectively. These features are so labeled in the figure. The compositions of the two phases present after creep testing were measured by EDS and are listed in Table IV. Within this interdendritic region, the austenite seems to lack any finely dispersed precipitate phase that could strengthen the alloy. Carbides that decorated grain boundaries tended to have a cellular morphology, whereas intragranular carbides had a faceted morphology. Although not expressly investigated, it is likely that precipitation of these phases within boundaries helps to impede grain boundary sliding (or its equivalent in the cast grain structure) relative to the equiaxed recrystallized grain structures present in wrought alloys, thereby contributing to the creep strength of this alloy. Beyond the interdendritic region, as shown in the lower portion of Figure 4 and with more detail in Figures 5(a) and (b), the austenite contains finely dispersed precipitates that are decorating dislocations. At the creep conditions of stress and temperature used in this study, dislocation climb is expected to be the operative creep mechanism. Although not reported here, stress exponents (n) of five and greater have been measured for this alloy and most other stainless steels of this type (e.g., 347H and 304H). Therefore, these fine precipitate structures were subjected to additional analysis for evaluating sources of strengthening.

These precipitates seem bimodal in size. The larger precipitates, being somewhat cuboidal in morphology, are typically 50 nm to 100 nm in diameter. These larger precipitates seem to form networks consistent with the decorated dislocations observed in Figure 4. The smaller precipitates either are equiaxed in morphology, with diameters being 10 nm to 20 nm, or seem to be short rods of about this same length scale. The average diameter of the smaller precipitates seen within Figure 5(b) is 16 nm, with 9-nm standard deviation. Assuming the local specimen thickness of this region is 100 nm, the precipitate density N_V is approximately 6 × 10²⁰/m³. The SAD pattern shown in Figure 5(c) was obtained from the cluster of smaller precipitates encircled in Figure 5(b). Although diffraction effects from the surrounding austenite dominate the pattern, weak reflections attributed to the precipitate phase can be indexed and are consistent with the tetragonal Z-phase, B = [1\(\bar{1}\)0]_z. The diffraction patterns here were indexed using the crystal structure and lattice parameters of Z-phase given by Jack and Jack (Nb₂Cr₂N₂; a₀ = 0.3037 nm, c₀ = 0.7391 nm).⁽⁹⁾ Z-phase precipitation has been observed in wrought austenitic steels containing both niobium and nitrogen.⁽¹⁰⁻¹²⁾ The crystallographic orientation relationship between these precipitates and the austenite is revealed in Figure 5(c) as follows:

$$[1\bar{1}0]_z||[110]_y;(001)_z||(001)_y$$

This same orientation relationship has been reported by others.⁽¹¹⁻¹³⁾ In this study, the crystal structure of the Z-phase, its morphology in the austenite, and its orientation relationship with the austenite are consistent with earlier investigations. Although the crystal structure of Z-phase has been accepted as being tetragonal,⁽⁹⁾ a variant based on vanadium rather than niobium and having a cubic structure has been reported in a 9 pct Cr martensitic steel.⁽¹⁴⁾ In the present study, only the Nb-containing tetragonal form of the Z-phase was observed. The diffraction patterns obtained from one of the larger cuboidal precipitates (arrowed) in Figure 5(b) are consistent with this being cubic Nb(C,N), in a cube-on-cube orientation relationship with the surrounding austenite (Figure 5(d)).

The compositions of the fine-scale Z-phase and Nb(C,N) precipitates were measured by EDS. However, because the size of the Z-phase precipitates was less than the thickness of the TEM foils, all spectra acquired from these specimens also contained a contribution from surrounding material. This made quantification by conventional means problematic. Consequently, EDS spectrum images were acquired, and multivariate statistical analysis was performed to separate the as-acquired spectrum images into principal component spectra and their corresponding amplitude maps. Figure 6 shows a dispersion of Z-phase precipitates within an intragranular region of the crept alloy. A spectrum image (64 × 64 pixels, 3 nm spatial resolution, 2 seconds dwell per pixel) was acquired from the highlighted region in the figure, which contains two precipitates. The PCA of the spectrum image determined that this data set contained three real nonnoise component spectra, which are referred to as SPT1 to SPT3 in Figure 7. ## V. DISCUSSION

High-temperature creep strength can be improved by modifying alloy chemistry to eliminate or mitigate the

precipitation of deleterious embrittling intermetallic phases and/or to promote solid-solution strengthening and age-hardening mechanisms. $^{[3]}$ The modifications in the chemistry of CF8C to produce CF8C-Plus have significantly improved the creep properties of the alloy by using all three of these strengthening mechanisms. The addition of nickel, nitrogen, and manganese as austenite stabilizers resulted in the as-cast alloy being fully austenitic, with no $\delta$-ferrite. Because no $\delta$-ferrite was present as a source for rapid transformation to $\sigma$ phase (Cr diffusion is 10 times higher in ferrite than in austenite), no $\sigma$ phase was observed in this alloy after creep-rupture testing 684 hours at $1023 \mathrm{~K}\left(750{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}\right)$. Although the amount of nitrogen held in solid solution by the austenite was not explicitly discerned in this study, nitrogen has been reported to be a potent solid- solution strengthener for austenite $^{[15]}$ and can improve other mechanical properties of austenitic stainless steels as well. $^{[16]}$ For the CF8C-Plus sample examined in this work, rupture elongation was 28.1 pct compared with only 5.9 pct for CF8C tested at the same condition. In CF8C, Nb(C,N) is well known and generally assumed to be a precipitation-hardening phase. In CF8C-Plus, in addition to Nb(C,N) precipitation, the nitrogen addition clearly resulted in the formation of a second age-hardening phase, Z-phase. Z-phase precipitation during high-temperature creep has been observed in martensitic steels such as the 9 pct to 12 pct Cr alloys, if both Nb (or V) and N are added.$^{[14,17–21]}$ Thermodynamic modeling predicts Z-phase to be the most stable nitride in 9 pct to 12 pct Cr steels that contain the constituent elements.$^{[22]}$ Its formation is known to degrade severely the high-temperature creep properties of this type of steel because Z-phase growth occurs quickly and at the expense of the precipitation-hardening V-rich MX precipitates.$^{[23–25]}$ This results in there being fewer and larger (micron-sized) precipitates, which then removes their age-hardening effect during creep, and weakens the 9 pct Cr steels. However, the behavior of Nb-rich Z-phase is entirely different in the Fe-Cr-Ni austenitic steels. The Z-phase that has been observed in wrought austenitic steels generally is reported to be only nm-sized

and slow growing. $^{[26-28]}$ In a recent numerical simulation study investigating precipitate nucleation and growth in heat-resistant austenitic steels, Shim *et al.* calculatedthat CF8C-Plus aged 100,000 hours at $973 ~K(700^{\circ} C)$  would contain Z-phase precipitates less than $20 ~nm$ in diameter and concluded that because Z-phase in austenitic steels grows relatively slowly, it should not have the deleterious effect on the high-temperature creep strength as it does in the 9 pct to 12 pct Cr steels. $^{[29]}$ In this study, the Z-phase precipitates observed in the castCF8C-Plus after creep 684 hours at $1023 ~K(750^{\circ} C)$  and $100 MPa$ were small in size (10 nm to $20 ~nm$ ) and were dispersed throughout the austenite grains. They, therefore, would contribute significantly to the observed creep resistance by impeding dislocation climb and glide.

The contribution of the dispersed nitride phase to the alloy's creep strength and yield strength at $1023 ~K$ (750 °C) can be assessed from a calculation of the Orowan stress needed for a dislocation to bypass impenetrable particles at the creep condition. With values of the average particle diameter $d$ and particle density $N_{V}$ estimated from TEM images as $16 ~nm$ and6.8 x 1020/m3, respectively, and the mean planar inter-particle separation, $\lambda_{S}$ , expressed as follows $^{[30]}$ :

$$
\lambda_{S}=\frac{1}{2 \sqrt{\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{V}} d}} \tag{1}
$$

where $\lambda_{S}$ for the Z-phase nitrides is estimated to be152 nm. The temperature-dependent yield and ultimate stresses of cast CF8C-Plus were reported from tensile testing by Shingledecker et al., $^{[2]}$ and from these same tension tests, a temperature-dependent elastic modulus is derived and shown in Figure 11. The linear best-fit relation for the elastic modulus E (GPa) with tempera- ture $T(^{\circ} C)$ is $E=140-0.07 ~T$ , so that a reasonable estimate of the elastic modulus at $1023 ~K(750^{\circ} C)$ is87.5 GPa. As Poisson's ratio $v$ for this alloy is not known explicitly, an assumed value of 0.25 was used to calculate the bulk modulus $G$ from classic elasticity theory as $35 GPa$ . The relation between $d_{S}$ , the mean diameter of intersection between a random plane and a spherical particle of diameter $d$ , has been given as ds = d(2/3)0.5,[30] Assuming the nitride particles are impenetrable, the Orowan stress $\tau$ needed bypassthe particle can be estimated as follows $^{[30,31]}$ :

$$
\tau=\frac{0.81 M G b}{2 \pi(1-v)^{1 / 2}} \frac{\ln \left(d_{S} / r_{0}\right)}{\lambda_{S}-d_{S}} \tag{2}
$$

where $M$ is a Taylor factor relating shear stress on slip planes to the macroscopic flow stress in the polycrystaland is taken as $3.06,^{[31]} b$ is the Burgers vector $(2.53 \times$  $10^{-10} ~m$ ), and $r_{0}$ is the dislocation "core" radius, taken here as being equal to the Burgers vector. Using the derived value of the elastic modulus at $1023 ~K(750^{\circ} C)$ , the Orowan stress is estimated to be $78 MPa$ , which is approximately50 pct of the stress estimated for the new alloy that is strengthened by the dispersed Z-phase.

The Orowan calculations support the hypothesis that the differences in strength between the CF8C and CF8C- Plus alloys are in part a result of fine precipitate structures, including the Z-phase, which develop in CF8C-Plus. This detailed microstructural examination and the simple Orowan calculations suggest that the very fine Z-phase nitride is an important component in the high-temperature strengthening of this stainless steel. This is the first instance of a cast austenitic stainless steel being strengthened by a nitride phase dispersed throughout the casting. The examination of specimens from interrupted creep tests to evaluate dislocation bowing between precipitates would have helped to evaluate the creep-strengthening mechanism and provided more explanation of the strengthening difference between the two alloys. However, the speci- mens available for microstructural analysis were taken from an engineering test program in which all specimens were run to failure. Additional fundamental studies on Z-phase strengthening compared with other precipitates or in conjunction with carbides/nitrides clearly are needed but are outside the scope of this study.

Because a conventional EDS measurement and quan- tification erroneously would have included contribu- tions from the surrounding austenite, the composition of Z-phase precipitates was estimated by quantifying the pure component spectrum for Z-phase, which was obtained from PCA. Quantification of the component spectrum revealed significant $Fe$ , and some $Ni$ is present in the Z-phase. Although these species actually may be present in the nitride, their reported presence also may be a result of limitations in the PCA routine. Further- more, close inspection revealed that the component spectra contained artifacts (i.e., nonphysically real features) in some background regions, illustrating the difficulty and challenges to such analysis in strong, engineering alloys. A better measure of composition for these small Z-phase precipitates may come from

combined EDS and electron energy-loss spectroscopy studies of these precipitates from extraction replicas.

The complex morphology of the Nb(C,N) precipitate revealed in Figure 10 suggests $M_{23}C_6$ and Z-phase nucleated on or adjacent to the Nb(C,N) carbide. The Nb(C,N) may be decomposing at this temperature, which is consistent with the thermodynamic modeling that predicts that both $M_{23}C_6$ and Z-phase can form as Nb(C,N) decomposes at 1023 K (750 °C) (Figure 1). The decomposition of MX into Z-phase has been observed in a Nb-containing austenitic stainless steel, which was crept in air at 1073 K (800 °C)$^{[28]}$ as well as in an aged martensitic 12 pct Cr steel.$^{[25]}$ The formation of clusters such as those observed in this study also may contribute to the longterm stability of the nanoscale precipitation structures observed in other CF8C-Plus steel creep specimens tested at higher temperatures (23 kh in air, at 1123 K (850 °C), 35 MPa).$^{[32]}$ They also may form by sympathetic nucleation of the adjacent phase as a result of solution enrichment or depletion zone formation caused by the nucleation of the first phase.

## VI. CONCLUSIONS

1. The addition of nickel, nitrogen, and manganese (which are less expensive alternatives to nickel as austenite stabilizers) to CF8C results in the new as-cast CF8C-Plus alloy being fully austenitic, with no $\delta$-ferrite present to act as a source for rapid $\sigma$-phase formation.

2. In the as-cast condition, the alloy contains primary Nb(C,N) found within the interdendritic regions; some Mn-based inclusions are present within the austenite matrix.

3. Three phases precipitated during creep at 1023 K (750 °C) 100 MPa and are thought to improve creep strength. Both $M_{23}C_6$ and Nb(C,N) precipitated in interdendritic regions, which may help stabilize these regions and impede grain growth. In addition, Nb(C,N), typically 50 nm to 100 nm in size precipitated throughout the primary austenite to act as an age-hardening mechanism. Finally, a stable slow-growing nitride phase, the tetragonal Z-phase, which previously has been observed only in wrought alloys, was observed throughout the primary austenite. The orientation relationship between the Z-phase and the austenite was identical to that previously reported by others.

4. The improved high-temperature creep strength of CF8C-Plus over CF8C is proposed to be a result of a combination of the new alloy being free of $\delta$-ferrite in the as-cast condition, which promotes creep ductility, and the precipitation of additional age-hardening phases during creep testing. Calculation of the Orowan shear stress required for a dislocation to bypass the nitride precipitates indicates that these are making significant contribution to the alloy's high-temperature strength.

5. Thermodynamic modeling was used to predict equilibrium phases in CF8C-Plus as well as their regimes of thermal stability, solubility, and compositions at relevant temperatures. Here, agreement generally was observed between the experiment and the prediction if the predicted $M_2$(C,N) phase does not form.

6. PCA of EDS spectrum images was used successfully to differentiate the chemistries of complex morphological features within the microstructure of the alloy CF8C-Plus. The resulting component spectra were not quantified successfully because of the presence of spectral artifacts that were introduced by the PCA.

7. The complex interaction proposed between the carbides, carbo-nitrides, and Z-phase should be studied in more detail with interrupted creep-test specimens or quantitative evolution of precipitate size and distribution with high-temperature creep exposure. This study strongly suggests that complex nanostructured nitrides, previously only observed in wrought alloys, can be a potent strengthening phase in heat-resistant cast austenitic stainless steels.
