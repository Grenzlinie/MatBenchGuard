# Ti-CONTENT AND ANNEALING TEMPERATURE DEPENDENCE OF DEFORMATION CHARACTERISTICS OF $\mathbf{Ti}_{X} \mathbf{N i}_{(92-X)} \mathbf{C u}_{8}$ SHAPE MEMORY ALLOYS

S. M. TAN, V. H. NO and S. MIYAZAKI

Institute of Materials Science, University of Tsukuba, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-8573, Japan

(Received 15 September 1997; accepted 14 November 1997)

Abstract-The Ti-content and annealing temperature dependence of the deformation characteristics of $\mathrm{Ti}_{X}-\mathrm{Ni}_{(92-X)} \mathrm{Cu}_{8}$ (at.\%) $(X=49.0-51.0)$ alloys was investigated by constant load thermal cycling tests by varying the annealing temperature between 673 and 1273 K. It was found that the stress-induced martensite transformation strain of a 50.0 at.% Ti alloy is larger than those of Ti-poor and Ti-rich alloys due to the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ and $\mathrm{Ti}_{2}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-Y} \mathrm{Cu}_{Y}\right)$ existing in the Ti-poor and Ti-rich alloys, respectively, acting as obstacles to the growth of preferential martensite variants. By annealing at 1273 K to cause the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ being dissolved into the matrix of the Ti-poor alloys, the stress-induced martensite transformation strain exhibits little dependence on Ti-content in the composition range of 49.0-50.0 at.% Ti. The plastic strain, critical stress for slip and recovery strain also show a unique Ti-content and annealing temperature dependence. (C) 1998 Acta Metallurgica Inc.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Ternary Ti-Ni-Cu alloys have attracted consider- able interest in recent years due to their narrow transformation temperature and stress hysteresis as- sociated with the transformation from B2 (parent phase) to B19 (orthorhombic martensite) [1-11]. Among the previous studies, the alloys of $\mathrm{Ti}_{50^{-}}$ $\mathrm{Ni}_{(50-X)}-\mathrm{Cu}_{X}$ (at.\%), having constant 50.0 at.% Ti-content, have been well investigated. However, the Ti-content dependence of the transformation behavior, pseudoelasticity and shape memory effect has not been investigated systematically in the Ti- Ni-Cu ternary alloys with constant Cu-content which show narrow transformation temperature hysteresis.

In the recent studies [12,13] of the present authors, the Ti-content dependence of transform- ation behavior and pseudoelasticity of the alloys of $\mathrm{Ti}_{X} \mathrm{Ni}_{(92-X)} \mathrm{Cu}_{8}$ (at.\%) $(X=49.0-51.0)$ have been systematically investigated. In the present study, Ti- content dependence of deformation characteristics (stress-induced martensite transformation strain, recovery strain, plastic strain and critical stress for slip) of the alloys were investigated by thermal cycling tests under various constant stresses for the alloys annealed at temperatures from 673 to 1273 K. The internal structures of the alloys were investigated by using transmission electron mi- croscopy (TEM). The roles of the composition of the matrix, dislocations and precipitates were also discussed in detail.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL

The deformation characteristics of five kinds of $\mathrm{Ti}_{X} \mathrm{Ni}_{(92-X)} \mathrm{Cu}_{8}$ (at.\%) $(X=49.0,49.6,50.0,50.6$, 51.0) alloys were investigated in the present study. The alloys were prepared by vacuum induction melting. The ingots were forged and extruded at 1173 K and then cold-drawn to wires of 1.0 mm in diameter. In the process of manufacturing the wires, intermediate annealing was carried out at about 973 K for 10 min in air. The reduction of the final cold work of the wires was $30 \%$. Tensile speci- mens, cut from the wires, with a straight shape were annealed at temperatures varying from 673 to 1273 K for 3.6 ks in vacuum and then quenched in water at room temperature. The surface of the spe- cimens were electropolished in a solution of acetic anhydride and $7.5 \%$ perchloric acid. The specimens used for TEM observations and X-ray diffraction analyses were prepared by cold-rolling the wire spe- cimens to thin plates with a $20 \%$ final cold work re duction. To observe the precipitates in the alloys, the thin plates used for TEM observations were annealed at 873 K for 3.6 ks. To investigate the Ti- content dependence of lattice parameters, the thin plates used for X-ray diffraction analyses were sol- ution treated at 1173 K for 3.6 ks.

The constant load thermal cycling tests were car- ried out using an Instron type tensile machine, Shimadzu Autogragh AG-A type, with a constant strain rate of $4.2 ×10^{-4} / \mathrm{s}$. TEM observations were made using JEM-2000 FX II operated at 200 kV.

X-ray diffraction analyses were made using RIGAKU X-ray diffractometer, RINT 2000 equipped with a heating and cooling stage. The slit systems were operated under the condition that the divergence slit (DS), scattering slit (SS) and receiv- ing slit (RS) were $0.5^{\circ}, 0.5^{\circ}$ and $0.15 ~mm$, respectively. Data collections were done by the step scan mode with intervals of $0.01^{\circ}$ for each step. Operation conditions were $40 kV$ and $30 ~mA$ with $Cu$ radiation $(\lambda=1.540562 \AA)$ . The measurements were carried out at 373 and $93 ~K$ , respectively, to determine the lattice constants of the parent phase(B2) and the monoclinic martensite (B19').

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Before presenting the experimental results, the quantitative evaluation method for deformation characteristics in a strain vs temperature curve under a constant load will be mentioned briefly in the following. A typical strain vs temperature curve under a constant load is shown in Fig. 1. A speci- men was first heated to above $OA_{f}$ (finish tempera ture of the reverse martensite transformation from B19 (orthorhombic martensite) to B2) under zero stress. A load was applied and the specimen was cooled to below $M_{f}$ (finish temperature of the mar tensite transformation from B19 to B19'), resulting in the full formation of stress-induced martensite. Then the specimen was reheated to above $OA_{f}$ to finish the reverse transformation. $\varepsilon_{M}$ in Fig. 1 con sists of stress-induced martensite transformation strain and plastic strain produced during the cool-ing cycle. Although the alloys of $Ti_{X} Ni_{(92-X)} Cu_{8}$  transform in two stages, i.e. from B2 to B19 and then from B19 to B19' under zero stress [12] or under low applied stresses, only one stage trans- formation was observed on strain vs temperature curves under high applied stresses [14]. In this case, it is difficult to distinguish the strain corresponding to orthorhombic martensite transformation from the strain corresponding to monoclinic martensite transformation. Therefore, $\varepsilon_{M}$ is here called the stress-induced martensite transformation strain, which includes both of the orthorhombic and monoclinic martensite transformation strains. $\varepsilon_{A}$ is the recovery strain corresponding to the reverse martensite transformation upon heating. $\varepsilon_{P}$ , obtained after removing the load, is the plastic strain produced during each cooling and heating cycle. In the present study, a set of thermal cycling tests were carried out under constant stresses of 30,50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 300 and 400 MPa, respect- ively, in a same specimen. $\varepsilon_{Pt}$ is here defined as a total plastic strain which is the sum of the plastic strains $(\varepsilon_{P})$ induced by the preceding and present thermal cycling tests.

Figure 4 shows the Ti-content dependence of lattice constants for the specimens annealed at 1173 K. The lattice constant ($a_{0}$) of the parent phase (B2), measured at 373 K, shows little dependence on Ti-content. The lattice constants of B19' martensite, measured at 93 K, show a weak dependence on the Ti-content, i.e. $a$ (B19') slightly decreases with increasing Ti-content and $c$ (B19') remains constant, whereas $b$ (B19') and $\beta$ (B19') slightly increase with increasing Ti-content. The standard error for the measurement of the lattice constants ($a_{0}$, $a$, $b$, $c$) is less than $7.53 \times 10^{-5}$ nm and the standard error for the measurement of $\beta$ (B19') is less than $1.16 \times 10^{-1}$ degrees. Using the lattice constants in Fig. 4, the martensite transformation strain ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$) produced by lattice distortion due to the B2-B19' transformation can be calculated. If it is assumed that the most favorable martensite grows to induce the maximum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ in each grain, the lattice distorsion matrix $\boldsymbol{T}'$ is expressed in the coordinates of the martensite as follows using the lattice constants of the parent phase ($a_{0}$) and those of the martensite phase ($a$, $b$, $c$, $\beta$):

$$
\boldsymbol{T}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
\frac{a}{a_{0}} & 0 & \frac{c^{\prime} \gamma}{\sqrt{2} a_{0}} \\
0 & \frac{b}{\sqrt{2} a_{0}} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \frac{c^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2} a_{0}}
\end{array}\right],
$$
where $c^{\prime}=c \sin \beta$ and $\gamma=1 / \tan \beta$.

Then, the lattice distortion matrix $\boldsymbol{T}$ which is expressed in the coordinates of the parent phase can be obtained as follows:
$$
\boldsymbol{T}=\boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{T}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{t}},
$$
where $\boldsymbol{R}$ is the coordinate transformation matrix from the martensite to the parent phase and $\boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{t}}$ is the transpose of $\boldsymbol{R}$. $\boldsymbol{R}$ corresponding to the most favorable martensite variant is expressed as follows:
$$
\boldsymbol{R}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
-1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}} \\
0 & \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{array}\right].
$$

Since any vector $\boldsymbol{x}$ in the coordinates of the parent phase is transformed to $\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}$ due to the martensite transformation using the following equation
$$
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{T} \boldsymbol{x},
$$
the maximum transformation strain $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}^{i}$ in each grain can be calculated as follows:
$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}^{i}=\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|-|\boldsymbol{x}|}{|\boldsymbol{x}|}.
$$

By averaging $\varepsilon_{M}^{i}$ for representative 36 orientations which locate periodically in a stereographic standard triangle, the transformation strain for a polycrystal can be estimated as follows:
$$
\bar{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{M}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{36} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}^{i}}{36}.
$$

Since the model used here assumes the most weak interaction between grains, $\bar{\varepsilon}_{M}$ is always larger than experimental results. Therefore, the calculated $\bar{\varepsilon}_{M}$ will not agree with experimental data $\varepsilon_{M}$. However, the composition dependence of $\varepsilon_{M}$ can be well estimated qualitatively by $\bar{\varepsilon}_{M}$. This is the present purpose for calculating $\bar{\varepsilon}_{M}$ as a function of composition as shown in Fig. 5. Figure 5 shows the calculated $\bar{\varepsilon}_{M}$ as a function of Ti-content for the specimens annealed at 1173 K. It can be seen that the calculated $\bar{\varepsilon}_{M}$ almost remains constant irrespective of Ti-content. Therefore, the Ti-content dependence of $\varepsilon_{M}$ in Fig. 3(a) is not mainly caused by the change of the lattice constants.

As the precipitates hinder the formation and growth of martensite variants, $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ will decrease with increasing amount of precipitates. The amount of precipitates depends on the Ti-content and annealing temperature. From the phase diagram of the alloys in Fig. 6, it can be understood that the precipitates of ${\rm Ti_{2}(Ni_{1 - Y}Cu_{Y})}$ exist in the Ti-rich alloys at any temperature and the amount of precipitates increases with increasing Ti-content. Therefore, $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ decreases with increasing Ti-content from 50.0 to 51.0 at.% Ti irrespective of the annealing temperature as shown in Fig. 3(a). From the phase diagram of the alloys, it can also be understood that the precipitates of ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ exist below a solution limit line in the Ti-poor alloys. Thus, the amounts of the precipitates of ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ is determined by not only the Ti-content but also by the annealing temperature. In the two-phase region of B2 and ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ as shown in the phase diagram, the amount of precipitates of ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ increases with decreasing Ti-content but decreases with increasing temperature. Therefore, $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ decreases with decreasing Ti-content from 50.0 to 49.0 at.% Ti as shown in Fig. 3(a). Moreover, the Ti-content dependence of $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ in the composition range of 49.0–50.0 at.% Ti is greatly affected by the annealing temperature. $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ of the specimens annealed at 873 K exhibits the strongest dependence on Ti-content because the effective aging temperature of the Ti-poor alloys is about 873 K. With increasing annealing temperature from 873 to 1273 K, the Ti-content dependence of $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ becomes weaker because the amount of precipitates of ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ in the matrix of the alloys decreases with increasing annealing temperature. The fact that $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ of the Ti-poor alloys annealed at 1273 K shows only a little dependence on Ti-content can be clearly explained only by considering the effect of lattice constants on $\bar{\varepsilon}_{\rm M}$ as shown in Fig. 5, since ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ precipitates do not exist at 1273 K. Additionally, in Fig. 3(a), it can also be seen that the Ti-content dependence of $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ for the specimens annealed at 673 K is not so strong as that for the specimens annealed at 873 K in all the composition range. This is attributed to the high density of remaining dislocations in the specimens annealed at 673 K, i.e. the effect of the dislocations is so strong that the effect of the precipitates appears weaker.

It can also be seen from Fig. 3(a) that $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ shows stronger dependence on Ti-content in the composition range of 49.0–50.0 at.% Ti than in the composition range of 50.0–51.0 at.% Ti for the specimens annealed at temperatures below 973 K. Therefore, it is understood that the precipitates of ${\rm Ti(Ni_{1 - X}Cu_{X})}_{2}$ exert more influence on $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ than the precipitates of ${\rm Ti_{2}(Ni_{1 - Y}Cu_{Y})}$ do.

Figure 3(b) shows the annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ induced under a stress of 75 MPa for various alloys. The alloys of 50.0, 50.6 and 51.0 at.% Ti exhibit a similar annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$. In the temperature range of 673–1073 K, $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ increases with increasing annealing temperature due to the recovery, recrystallization and grain growth occurring in the temperature range. Although the recrystallization temperature of the alloys was not determined by TEM observation in the present study, it is considered to be about 773 K because the recrystallization occurs at 773 K in Ti–Ni binary alloys [15]. In the temperature range of 1073–1273 K, $\varepsilon_{\rm M}$ remains constant irre-

spective of the annealing temperature. This indi- cates that the internal structures do not change sig- nificantly in the temperature range for the alloys. However, Ti-poor alloys show a slightly different relationship between the annealing temperature and $\varepsilon_{M}$ . For the alloy of 49.0 at.\% Ti, $\varepsilon_{M}$ decreases with increasing annealing temperature from 673 to 773 K even though recovery or recrystallization occurs in this temperature range, then $\varepsilon_{M}$ increases slightly with increasing annealing temperature from 773 to873 K and increases rapidly with increasing anneal- ing temperature from 873 to 1273 K. It is con- sidered that the effect of the precipitates of Ti(Ni1 -xCux)2 is responsible for the annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{M}$ for the Ti-poor alloys. $\varepsilon_{M}$ of the alloy of 49.0 at.\% Ti annealed at773 K is lower than that of the alloy annealed at673 K. This might be explained as follows. Since the process of drawing the wire specimens includes intermediate annealing at about 973 K, the density of $Ti(Ni_{1-x} Cu_{x})_{2}$ precipitates included in as-drawn wires is lower than that included in an equilibrium state at 673 or 773 K. Therefore, the density of the precipitates will increase by annealing at 673 or773 K. However, annealing at 773 K is more effec- tive in increasing the density of the precipitates than annealing at 673 K, so that $\varepsilon_{M}$ for the speci mens annealed at 773 K will be smaller than that for the specimen annealed at 673 K. The slight increase of $\varepsilon_{M}$ with increasing annealing tempera ture from 773 to 873 K is caused by recrystalliza-tion and grain growth. The rapid increase of $\varepsilon_{M}$  with increasing annealing temperature from 873 to1273 K results from the fact that the amount of precipitates of $Ti(Ni_{1-x} Cu_{x})_{2}$ decreases with increasing annealing temperature according to the phase diagram, the effect of grain growth also being involved. Similar phenomena were also observed for the alloy of 49.6 at.% Ti. However, as the amount of precipitates of $Ti(Ni_{1-x} Cu_{x})_{2}$ in the alloy of 49.6 at.% Ti is less than that in the alloy of49.0 at.\% Ti, $\varepsilon_{M}$ is always larger than that of the latter. Because all of the precipitates of $Ti(Ni_{1-x} Cu_{x})_{2}$ in the alloy of 49.6 at.\% Ti are dissolved into the matrix of the alloy at $1173 K, \varepsilon_{M}$  becomes constant irrespective of the annealing tem- perature above 1173 K.

### 3.2. Plastic strain
3.2.1. Stress-dependence. Figure 8(a), (b) and (c) show the stress dependence of $\varepsilon_{Pt}$ (total plastic strain as previously defined) for various alloys annealed at 1173, 873 and 673 K, respectively. In Fig. 8(a), it can be seen that the stress dependence of $\varepsilon_{Pt}$ is strongly affected by the Ti-content for the specimens annealed at 1173 K. The slope of the re- lationship between stress and $\varepsilon_{Pt}$ reflects the work hardening rate of the alloys. The steeper the slope is, the lower the work hardening rate is. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the work hardening rate increases with increasing Ti-content from 50.0 to 51.0 at.% Ti. This phenomenon is explained as follows. There exist precipitates of $Ti_{2}(Ni_{1-\gamma} Cu_{\gamma})$ in the Ti-rich alloys and the amount of the precipitates increases with increasing Ti-content. As the precipitates are the obstacles to the movement of dislocations, the dislocation structure hardens and the density of dis- locations increases with increasing amount of the precipitates, resulting in the work hardening rate increasing with increasing Ti-content for the Ti-rich alloys. The alloys of 49.6 and 50.0 at.% Ti also show the different work hardening rate, i.e. the work hardening rate of the latter is lower than that of the former. This is attributed to the solid sol- ution hardening, since there are no precipitates in

3.2.2. Ti-content and annealing temperature depen- dence. As mentioned in Section 3.2.1, the plastic strain shows stress dependence. Thus, to investigate the Ti-content and annealing temperature depen- dence of plastic strain systematically, the plastic strains of all the specimens should be measured under same constant stresses. Figure 9 shows Ti- content dependence of strain vs temperature curves under a stress of 150 MPa for the specimens annealed at 673, 873 and 1173 K, respectively. Since the applied stress in this case is higher than that in Fig. 2, the effect of stress on plastic strain is more prominently observed. The specimens annealed at673 K exhibit the strongest resistance to plastic de- formation due to the high density of remained dis- locations. Comparing the curve of the alloy of49.0 at.% Ti annealed at 1173 K with the curve of the same composition alloy annealed at 873 K, it can be seen that the stress-induced martensite trans- formation strain of the former is much larger than that of the latter, but the plastic strain of the two specimens is almost the same. This is attributed to the fact that both of the alloys have their own specific strengthening mechanisms but they are quite different. The strengthening mechanism of the former is solution hardening caused by the high Ni- content in the matrix. The solution hardening can suppress the plastic deformation by raising the criti- cal stress for slip, but it can not prevent the for- mation and growth of martensite variants under enough amount of applied stresses. However, the strengthening mechanism of the latter is the particle hardening caused by the precipitates of $Ti(Ni_{1-x}Cu_{x})_{2}$ . The precipitates of $Ti(Ni_{1-x}Cu_{x})_{2}$ can suppress the plastic deformation. In the mean- time, they are also the obstacles to the growth of martensite variants. Therefore, it is concluded that the solution hardening and the particle hardening exhibit the same effect on suppressing plastic defor- mation but a different effect on the martensite transformation strain.

The thermal cycling at each stress level was performed only once. $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ of the specimens annealed at temperatures above 873 K shows a strong dependence on the Ti-content as shown in Fig. 10(a). $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ increases with increasing Ti-content from 49.0 to 50.0 at.% Ti and then decreases with increasing Ti-content from 50.0 to 51.0 at.% Ti. For the Ti-rich alloys, the increase of the amount of precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}_{2}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-Y} \mathrm{Cu}_{Y}\right)$ is responsible for the decrease of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ with increasing Ti-content. For the Ti-poor alloys, the increase of the amount of precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ or the increase of Ni-content in the matrix is responsible for the decrease of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ with decreasing Ti-content. However, no apparent plastic deformation occurs in all of the alloys annealed at 673 or 773 K under the stresses below 150 MPa. This fact indicates that the introduction of a high density of dislocations is a more effective strengthening method than the precipitates or solid-solution hardening methods.

In Fig. 10(b), it can be seen that $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ of the alloys of 50.0, 50.6 and 51.0 at.% Ti increases with increasing annealing temperature from 773 to 1073 K and then becomes constant above 1073 K. For these alloys, the annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ is similar with the annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ as shown in Fig. 3(b). However, for the Ti-poor alloys, e.g. the alloy of 49.0 at.% Ti, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ slightly increases with increasing annealing temperature from 773 to 873 K and then remains a low constant value above 873 K as shown in Fig. 10(b). This phenomena is quite different from the annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ for the same composition alloy as shown in Fig. 3(b). This is caused by the fact that the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ dissolve into the matrix of the alloy with increasing annealing temperature above 873 K, causing the solution hardening to increase. As mentioned above, the solution hardening has the same resistance to plastic deformation as the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ do, but it can not hinder the growth of martensite variants. Therefore, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ remains constant but $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ increases rapidly with increasing annealing temperature above 873 K. For the Ti-rich alloys, as the composition in the matrix of the alloys and the amount of the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}_{2}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-Y} \mathrm{Cu}_{Y}\right)$ keep constant irrespective of increasing annealing temperature, both the annealing temperature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ are only affected by the remained dislocations and grain size which are dependent on annealing temperature. Therefore, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ show similar qualitative dependence to each other on annealing temperature for the Ti-rich alloys.

### 3.3. Critical stress for slip

The critical stress for slip $(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}})$ is determined from the data of plastic strain vs applied stress relationship shown in Fig. 8, i.e. $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ can be obtained by extrapolating the plastic strain to zero. As the plastic strain below $0.5 \%$ was neglected in Fig. $8, \sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ measured by the above method indicates the critical stress for macroscopic plastic deformation. Figure 11(a) and (b) show, respectively, the Ti-content and annealing temperature dependence of $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$. For the specimens annealed at 673 and $773 \mathrm{~K}, \sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ exhibits little dependence on Ti-content, whereas for the specimens annealed at the temperatures above 873 K, $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ decreases with increasing Ti-content in the composition range of 49.0-50.0 at.% Ti, but remains constant in the composition range of 50.0-51.0 at.% Ti. The above phenomena indicate that $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ of the specimens annealed at 673 and 773 K is mainly affected by the high density of remaining dislocations which is the same for all of the alloys. Therefore, the effect of the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ and $\mathrm{Ti}_{2}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-Y} \mathrm{Cu}_{Y}\right)$ hardly appears on $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ in these specimens. The fact that $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ remains constant in the composition range of 50.0-51.0 at.% Ti irrespective of the annealing temperature indicates that the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}_{2}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-Y} \mathrm{Cu}_{Y}\right)$ in the Ti-rich alloys do not exhibit significant influence on $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$. However, the fact that $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ of the Ti-poor alloys annealed above 873 K increases with decreasing Ti-content is attributed to that the amount of the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$ or the Ni-content in the matrix of the alloys increases with decreasing Ti-content in these specimens, resulting in the particle hardening or solution hardening. From the above facts, it is understood that the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}\left(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X} \mathrm{Cu}_{X}\right)_{2}$

### 3.4. Recovery strain

#### 3.4.1. Stress dependence.
As shape memory effect is evaluated by recovery strain $(\varepsilon_{A})$, it is important for practical applications to investigate the factors affecting $\varepsilon_{A}$. Figure 12(a), (b) and (c) shows, respectively, the stress dependence of $\varepsilon_{A}$ for various alloys annealed at 1173, 873 and 673 K. For all the specimens, $\varepsilon_{A}$ firstly increases until a maximum value and then decreases gradually with increasing stress. The stress corresponding to the maximum $\varepsilon_{A}$ depends on Ti-content and annealing temperature. For the specimens annealed at 1173 and 873 K as shown in Fig. 12(a) and (b), the stress corresponding to the maximum $\varepsilon_{A}$ of the alloy of 50.0 at.% Ti is lower than that of the Ti-poor or Ti-rich alloys. However, for the specimens annealed at 673 K as shown in Fig. 12(c), the stress corresponding to the maximum $\varepsilon_{A}$ is almost the same for all the alloys. The above phenomena are discussed in the following. As $\varepsilon_{A}=\varepsilon_{M}-\varepsilon_{P}$, both of the factors affecting $\varepsilon_{M}$ or $\varepsilon_{P}$ will affect $\varepsilon_{A}$. $\varepsilon_{M}$ increases with increasing stress and $\varepsilon_{P}$ also increases with increasing stress after the stress exceeds the critical stress for slip $\sigma_{s}$, as shown in Fig. 13, where $\varepsilon_{A}$, $\varepsilon_{M}$ and $\varepsilon_{P}$ are plotted against applied stress for the 49.0 at.% Ti alloy annealed at 1273, 873 and 673 K, respectively. In the stress range lower than $\sigma_{s}$, as $\varepsilon_{P}$ is zero or very small, $\varepsilon_{A}$ is equal to $\varepsilon_{M}$. So $\varepsilon_{A}$ increases with increasing stress in the low stress range. However, in the stress range higher than $\sigma_{s}$, as $\varepsilon_{P}$ increases more rapidly than $\varepsilon_{M}$ does with increasing stress, $\varepsilon_{A}$ starts to decrease. As shown in Fig. 11(a), as $\sigma_{s}$ increases with decreasing Ti-content from 50.0 to 49.0 at.% Ti for the specimens annealed above 873 K, higher stresses are required for the Ti-poor alloys to produce enough $\varepsilon_{P}$, which causes the decrease of $\varepsilon_{A}$, than that for the alloy of 50.0 at.% Ti. Therefore, higher stresses are also required for the Ti-rich alloys to produce enough $\varepsilon_{P}$ which causes the decrease of $\varepsilon_{A}$ for the alloy of 50.0 at.% Ti. ## 4. CONCLUSIONS

The deformation characteristics were investigated for $\mathrm{Ti}_{X}\mathrm{Ni}_{(92-X)}\mathrm{Cu}_{8}$ (at.%) ($X=49.0$–51.0) alloys annealed at temperatures from 673 to 1273 K. The stress-induced martensite transformation strain ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$), plastic strain ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{P}}$ or $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pt}}$), critical stress for slip ($\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$) and recovery strain ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$) were found to depend on Ti-content and annealing temperature. The fol- lowing conclusions were obtained:

(1) $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ of the alloy of 50.0 at.% Ti is larger than that of Ti-poor and Ti-rich alloys. However, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ exhibits little dependence on the Ti-content in the composition range of 49.0–50.0 at.% Ti for the spe- cimens solution-treated at 1273 K.

(2) $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ of the alloys of 50.0, 50.6 and 51.0 at.% Ti increases with increasing annealing temperature from 673 to 1073 K and then remains constant above 1073 K, whereas $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ of the Ti-poor alloys slightly decreases with increasing annealing tem- perature from 673 to 773 K and then increases with increasing annealing temperature from 773 to 1273 K (for the alloy of 49.0 at.% Ti) or to 1173 K (for the alloy of 49.6 at.% Ti). The annealing tem- perature dependence of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}$ was explained by the effects of the precipitates, remained dislocations and grain size.

(3) The Ti-poor alloys and Ti-rich alloys show a higher resistance to plastic deformation than the alloy of 50.0 at.% Ti does. Accompanying the recovery, recrystallization and grain growth, the plastic strain increases with increasing annealing temperature.

(4) For the specimens annealed at 673 and 773 K, $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ exhibits little dependence on Ti-content, whereas for the specimens annealed above 873 K, $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ decreases with increasing Ti-content from 49.0 to 50.0 at.% Ti and then remains constant from 50.0 to 51.0 at.% Ti.

(5) For all of the alloys, $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ decreases with increas- ing annealing temperature from 673 to 873 K and then remains constant from 873 to 1273 K. High density of remained dislocations is effective for increasing $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ in all of the alloys investigated. Particle hardening caused by the precipitates of $\mathrm{Ti}(\mathrm{Ni}_{1-X}\mathrm{Cu}_{X})_{2}$ and solution hardening caused by high Ni content in the matrix are also effective to increase $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$ in Ti-poor alloys.

(6) In the composition range of 50.0–51.0 at.% Ti, maximum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ decreases with increasing Ti-con- tent irrespective of annealing temperature. However, in the composition range of 49.0–50.0 at.% Ti, the Ti-content dependence of maxi- mum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ is greatly affected by annealing tempera- ture. At annealing temperatures below 973 K, maximum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ exhibits positive dependence on Ti- content. With increasing annealing temperature from 973 to 1273 K, the Ti-content dependence of maximum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ gradually changes from positive dependence to negative dependence, resulting in maximum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ simply decreasing with increasing Ti- content from 49.0 to 51.0 at.% Ti for the specimens annealed at 1273 K.

(7) For the alloys of 50.0, 50.6 and 51.0 at.% Ti, maximum $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ decreases with increasing annealing temperature from 673 to 773 K and then almost

remains constant with increasing annealing tem- perature from 773 to 1273 K. For the Ti-poor alloys, maximum $\varepsilon_{A}$ also decreases with increasing annealing temperature from 673 to 773 K and then remains constant from 773 to 973 K, but it increases with increasing annealing temperature from 973 to 1273 K. The above phenomena are considered to be the results of combined effects of $\varepsilon_{M}, \varepsilon_{P}$ and applied stress on $\varepsilon_{A}$.
