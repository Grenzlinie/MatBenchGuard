## ABSTRACT

The site preference of Re in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases and the impact of Re-additions on the ideal shear strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ of $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases are investigated by the first-principles calculation. A correlative energy function $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ has been adopted to evaluate the interaction between point defects caused by Re-additions, *i.e.*, $\text{Re}_A$ and $\text{Re}_B$. The results show either attraction or repulsion don't change the site preference of Re at Al sites, and Re atoms tend to stay away from each other in the double Re-addition system. The $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in $\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B$ complexes is not only a function of distances $d$ between $\text{Re}_A$ and $\text{Re}_B$ defects, but also depends on the direction $[\theta,\phi]$ from $\text{Re}_A$ to $\text{Re}_B$ defects. The double Re-addition at preferential Al-Al sites may further elevate the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in single Re-addition systems, but an extra substitution of Re for Ni atoms, especially the clustering of Re atoms, is harmful to the strengthening of $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phase. A careful analysis of $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ reveals the improvement of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ of $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases induced by Re-addition can be attributed to a week repulsive interaction between $\text{Re}_{A\text{I}}$ and $\text{Re}_{A\text{I}}$ defects, and a strong attractive interaction caused by Re-clustering should be responsible for the detrimental effect of excess Re-additions to some extent.

### 1. Introduction

Ni-based single crystal (SC) superalloys with superior high-temperature mechanical properties are strategic for the blade applications in the advanced aero engines. Their typical microstructure involves $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases with L1₂ structure embedded coherently in fcc $\gamma$-Ni matrices [1]. These $\gamma'$ phases with high volume fraction up to 70% are a dominated phase [2]. Their high-temperature strength is strongly dependent on the concentration of refractory elements, *e.g.*, Re. Corresponding to the second and third generation superalloys containing about 3 wt% and 6 wt% Re, their temperature resistant capacity increases by 30 °C and 60 °C [3], respectively. However, it is also reported that a high Re-addition tends to yield Re-rich topologically closed packed (TCP) phase precipitations which will return to reduce the creep rupture strength of Ni-based SC superalloys [4].

In order to deeply understand the partitioning behavior of Re in Ni-based SC superalloys, several investigations [5–11] have been reported in the past decades. Miyazaki [5,6] firstly investigated the partitioning ratio of Re atoms between $\gamma$ and $\gamma'$ phases in Ni-Al-Re ternary alloys and Ni-Al-Re-X (X = Cr, Mo, W, Ti, Ta, Nb, Co) quaternary alloys. The results show the partitioning of Re atoms into $\gamma'$ phases decreases with increasing Re in Ni-Al-Re ternary alloys [5], and an extra addition of X makes the tendency of Re partitioning into $\gamma$ phases to become stronger in Ni-Al-Re-X quaternary alloys [6]. By atom-probe tomography (APT) and first-principles calculations, Zhou et al. [7] found that Re strongly partitions to $\gamma$ phases in Ni-Al-Cr model alloys because the substitution energy of Re in $\gamma$ phases is smaller than that in $\gamma'$ phases. But a preferential partitioning of Re into $\gamma'$ phases were also reported [8–11], in which the partitioning behavior of Re in $\gamma$ and $\gamma'$ phases even can be reversed by other alloying elements. For example, Liu et al. [8] found that Re preferentially partitions to $\gamma'$ phases in Ni-Al-Re ternary alloys, however, a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) based energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis by Carroll et al. [9] revealed that Re partitioning to the $\gamma$-matrix in Ru-addition superalloys is much less than in Ru-free superalloys. A similar phenomenon was also observed in polycrystalline Ni-base superalloys with Ir-addition [10]. These results distinctly indicate a complicated correlation and synergetic effect between alloying elements could exist in Ni-based SC superalloys.

Aimed at the interplay between alloying elements, many researchers [12–14] further investigated the site preference of Re in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phase. It is found Re prefers to occupy Al sub-lattice sites in Al-rich and Ni-rich $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases as well as stoichiometric Ni₃Al crystals [12–14]. However, only 53–78% of Re atoms are detected at Al sub-lattice sites in the Ni₃Al-2%Re compound [5]. Furthermore, some Re-clusters were also observed by atom-probe microanalyses [2], *e.g.*, Re-clusters about 1 nm in Re-modified versions of CMSX-2 and PWA 1480 superalloys. Wang

It is speculated that Re-clusters play a key role in the high temperature creep of superalloys due to their more efficient obstacles against dislocation motion compared to isolated solution atoms [2,16]. However, an extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis shows that only Ni atoms are detected around Re in a Ni-Re binary alloy [17]. And consequently Mottura et al. [18] pointed out Re-clusters are likely to be unstable in $\gamma$ solid solution phases because of a strong repulsive interaction between nearest neighboring Re atoms. This conflict seems to imply that the clustering is not a sole reason for the strengthening effect of Re-additions.

As for the creep behavior of Ni-based SC superalloys with Re-addition, it is found dislocations firstly move in $\gamma$-matrix channels and then shear into $\gamma'$-phase. The $\langle 110\rangle$ super-dislocation shearing into $\gamma'$-phase may be decomposed on (111) planes to form the configuration of $1/3\langle 112\rangle$ super Shockleys partials plus stacking faults, and some $\langle 110\rangle$ super-dislocations shearing into $\gamma'$-phase can be cross-slipped on $\{111\}$ planes to $\{100\}$ planes to form Kear-Wilsdorf (K-W) locking configuration of $1/2\langle 110\rangle$ and antiphase boundary (APB), which may hinder dislocation movement and restrain cross-slipping of dislocations [19]. The deformation feature of Re-free superalloy is $\langle 110\rangle$ superdislocation on $\{111\}$ planes shearing into rafted $\gamma'$-phases at the later stage of creep at $980\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ [20]. 2%Re Ni-based SC superalloy at $980\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ displays a lower strain rate and better creep resistance, and the K-W locks are formed within $\gamma'$-phases [20]. Compared to the 2%Re alloy system, 4.2%Re superalloy possesses a better creep strength in the range of $1040$-$1100\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, and the dislocation climbing over $\gamma'$-phases dominates the creep process of superalloys during the steady state creep [16]. Therefore, a dominant shearing mode of $\gamma'$-phases is crucial to high-temperature creep properties of Ni-based SC superalloys with Re-addition.

It is well known that the creep strength mainly depends on the obstructive degree of various defects against dislocation motion. In order to understand the shearing behavior of Ni-based SC superalloys, several density functional theory (DFT) calculations have been reported [21-23]. It was found that the substitution of Re for Al atoms enables to enhance the bonding between Re and its nearest neighboring Ni atoms [15]. As a result, both of ideal shear strengths $\sigma_{max}$ at $[\overline{1}10](111)$ and $[11\overline{2}](111)$ directions in Re-addition systems [22,23] are improved relative to the Re-free system [21]. Moreover, an interplay between Re and other alloying elements were also investigated [24,25]. For example, a multiple addition of Re and Mo at preferential Al-Al sites, i.e., a $\text{Re}_{Al}+\text{Mo}_{Al}$ complex, is demonstrated to have an excellent basic mechanical properties at high temperatures compared with their single Re-addition or Mo-addition systems [24]. As a result of Re-d/Ru-d, Re-d/Ni-d and Ru-d/Ni-d hybridizations, Chen et al. [25] found that a synergistic effect of Re and Ru can further enhance the fracture strength of $\gamma$-Ni/$\gamma'$-Ni$_3$Al interfaces. However, a strong repulsive interactions between Re and P is revealed to be deleterious to the Griffith rupture works of $\gamma$-Ni/$\gamma'$-Ni$_3$Al interfaces [26]. These results further indicate the correlation between alloying elements is a very important factor for the dislocation movement from $\gamma$-Ni phases to $\gamma'$-Ni$_3$Al phases, and consequently a detailed investigation on the interplay between Re-Re atoms in $\gamma'$-Ni$_3$Al phases is desired.

## 2. Method and model of calculation

The first-principles calculations are performed by the Vienna $ab$ $initio$ simulation package (VASP) [27,28] based on the DFT. In which a plane-wave basis set with the projector augmented wave (PAW) [29] is used to model the ion-electron interaction, and the exchange correlation term is described within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) parameterized by Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [30]. In our self-consistent field (SCF) calculation, the cutoff energy of plane wave functions is set at 350 eV, and the sampling of irreducible wedges of Brillouin zones is carried out with $6\times6\times6$ and $4\times4\times4$ regular Monkhorst-Pack grids of special k-points [31], corresponding to $2\times2\times2$ and $3\times3\times3$ supercells, respectively. A conjugate gradient method [32] has been used to optimize the geometry and all atomic positions in the supercell are relaxed until the force on each atom is less than 0.009 eV/Å. The calculation of total energies and electronic structures is followed by cell optimization with SCF tolerance of $1\times10^{-5}$ eV under GGA-PBE potential.

A series of $3\times3\times3$ supercell models consisting of 108 atoms are constructed to investigate the site preference of Re in Ni$_3$Al-Re systems, referred to Fig. 1a. In which Arabic numerals 1, 2, 3 represent three non-equivalent sites of Ni sublattices, respectively. The shadow area is a (1 1 1) slip plane. The two dotted lines represent $\langle110\rangle$ and $\langle112\rangle$ slip directions. In the case of single Re-addition, Re$_\text{Al}$ and Re$_\text{Ni}$ mean one Al atom and one Ni atom are substituted by Re, respectively. As a couple of Re atoms being simultaneously added into the Ni$_3$Al crystal, $i.e.$, Re$_\text{A}$ + Re$_\text{B}$ complexes, they are remarked as M$^\text{Re}_\text{Re}^\text{A}_\text{B}(d_0)$, in which A and B represent the species and type of non-equivalent sites, $i.e.$, Ni1, Ni2, Ni3 and Al sites. $d_0$ represents the distance between A and B sites before relaxation, which can be denoted as 1st, 2nd and 3rd, corresponding to the first, the second and the third nearest neighbors (NNs) of assigned sites, respectively. For example, M$^\text{Re}_\text{Re}^\text{Al}_\text{Ni}(3$rd) represents a Al atom and its 3rd NN Ni atom are simultaneously replaced by Re atoms. In this L1$_2$-Ni$_3$Al crystal, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th and 6th are $\sqrt{2}a_0/2$, $a_0$, $\sqrt{6}a_0/2$, $\sqrt{2}a_0$, $\sqrt{10}a_0/2$ and $\sqrt{3}a_0$, respectively, in which $a_0$ is the equilibrium lattice parameter.

To calculate the ideal shear strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ along $[\overline{1}10](111)$ and $[11\overline{2}](111)$ direction, several $2\times2\times2$ supercells of 32 atoms are also built, as shown in Fig. 1b. In which the shear direction $[\overline{1}10]$ or $[11\overline{2}]$ and the slip plane (1 1 1) have been rotated to be parallel to the lattice vectors $\overrightarrow{a}$ and normal to the lattice vectors $\overrightarrow{c}$ as proposed by Roundy et al. [33,34], respectively. In the shear process of Ni$_3$Al-Re crystals, a same rotation means and particle swarm optimization algorithm as reported in Refs. [35,36] has been adopted to yield their curves of stress $\sigma$ vs. strain $\varepsilon$. For single Re-addition system, different local atom configurations of doped non-equivalent sites have been distinguished by a Re$_\text{Al}$ model and three Re$_\text{Ni}$ models, $i.e.$, Re$_\text{Ni1}$, Re$_\text{Ni2}$ and Re$_\text{Ni3}$ models. In the case of double Re-additions, the position $(h_1$ and $h_2)$ of Re$_\text{A}$ and Re$_\text{B}$ defects and the orientation $(\theta$ and $\phi)$ from A to B sites are also taken into account besides their distance $d_0$, referred to Fig. 1c. For simplicity, only two kinds of typical modes are investigated in this work. That is $h_1=h_2=0$ and $h_1=0$ but $h_2=h_0$, in which $h_0=a_0/\sqrt{3}$ is the separation between adjacent (1 1 1) planes. For $h_1=h_2=0$, an intra-layer arrangement of Re atoms at a same (1 1 1) atomic layer is remarked as H$^\text{Re}_\text{Re}^\text{A}_\text{B}(\overrightarrow{d}_0)$, in which $\overrightarrow{d}_0=d_0[hkl]$ represents a directional arrangement of A-B sites along $[hkl]$ direction. In the case of $h_1=0$ and $h_2=h_0$, an interlayer arrangement of Re atoms is remarked as V$^\text{Re}_\text{Re}^\text{A}_\text{B}(\overrightarrow{d}_0)$, which means another Re atom situates at an adjacent (1 1 1) atomic layer of the first Re atom, referred to Fig. 1d.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Influence of an extra Re-addition on site preference of Re in $\gamma'$-Ni$_3$Al

The calculated equilibrium lattice parameter of L1$_2$-Ni$_3$Al crystals is 3.566 Å, which is in good agreement with the experimental value [37] of 3.572 Å. Further, the formation enthalpy $H_i$ of point defects [38] in Ni$_3$Al-Re systems is calculated by:
$$
\begin{aligned}
H_{i} & =E(\mathrm{Ni}_{3x-n}\mathrm{Al}_{x-m}\mathrm{Re}_{n+m})-E(\mathrm{Ni}_{3x}\mathrm{Al}_{x})-(n+m)\cdot E(\mathrm{Re})+n\cdot E(\mathrm{Ni}) \\
& \quad +m\cdot E(\mathrm{Al})
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$
where $i=\{\text{Ni}_\text{Al}, \text{Al}_\text{Ni}, \text{Re}_\text{Al}, \text{Re}_\text{Ni}\}$ represents the type of point defects, corresponding to an antisite at Al or Ni sublattices as well as the substitution of Re for host atoms, respectively. $E(\text{Ni}_{3x-n}\text{Al}_{x-m}\text{Re}_{n+m})$ and $E(\text{Ni}_{3x}\text{Al}_{x})$ are the total energies of Ni$_3$Al-Re supercells with $(n+m)$ Re atoms and perfect Ni$_3$Al crystals, respectively. $x=3\times3\times3$ denotes the size of supercells. $E(\text{Ni})$, $E(\text{Al})$ and $E(\text{Re})$ are the energies per atom in fcc-Ni, fcc-Al and hcp-Re unit cells, respectively. The calculated results are listed in Table 1. A negative $H_{\text{Al}_\text{Ni}}$ means the formation of isolated Al antisite defects is an exothermic reaction in Re-free Ni$_3$Al intermetallic compounds [39]. For ternary Ni$_3$Al-Re systems, $H_{\text{Re}_\text{Al}}$ and $H_{\text{Re}_\text{Ni}}$ are positive, indicating the substitution of Re for host atoms is an endothermic process [40].

To examine the site preference of Re in $\gamma'$-Ni$_3$Al phases, an energy factor $E_\text{Re}^{\text{Ni}\rightarrow\text{Al}}$ in the reaction of $\text{Re}_\text{Ni}+\text{Al}_\text{Al}\rightarrow\text{Re}_\text{Al}+\text{Al}_\text{Ni}$ [12–14] is further calculated by:
$$
E_{\text{Re}}^{\text{Ni}\rightarrow\text{Al}}=E(\mathrm{Ni}_{3x}\mathrm{Al}_{x-1}\mathrm{Re})+E(\mathrm{Ni}_{3x-1}\mathrm{Al}_{x+1})-E(\mathrm{Ni}_{3x-1}\mathrm{Al}_{x}\mathrm{Re})-E(\mathrm{Ni}_{3x}\mathrm{Al}_{x})
\tag{2}
$$

In which $E(\text{Ni}_{3x}\text{Al}_{x-1}\text{Re})$, $E(\text{Ni}_{3x-1}\text{Al}_{x+1})$, $E(\text{Ni}_{3x-1}\text{Al}_{x}\text{Re})$ and $E(\text{Ni}_{3x}\text{Al}_{x})$ are total energies of Re$_\text{Al}$, Al$_\text{Ni}$, Re$_\text{Ni}$ and defect-free supercells, respectively. As above definition of point defects, this expression can be further described to:
$$
E_{\text{Re}}^{\text{Ni}\rightarrow\text{Al}}=(H_{\text{Re}_\text{Al}}+H_{\text{Al}_\text{Ni}})-(H_{\text{Re}_\text{Ni}}+H_{\text{perfect}})
\tag{3}
$$

In which $H_{\mathrm{AlAl}}=H_{\mathrm{NiNi}}=H_{\text {perfect }}=0$ denotes a perfect crystal. Obviously, $E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}$ is equal to the difference in total defective formation enthalpies between products $(H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}+H_{\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Ni}}})$ and reactants $(H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}+H_{\text {perfect }})$ in the reaction of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}+\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Al}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$. In the framework of antisite-based substitution schema [12-14], this energy factor $E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}$ which characterizes the energy required to transfer a $\mathrm{Re}$ atom from initial $\mathrm{Ni}$ site to final $\mathrm{Al}$ site can be used to evaluate the preferred site of $\mathrm{Re}$ in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases by following criterions: (1) as $E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}<0$, Re prefers to occupy $\mathrm{Al}$ sublattices. (2) the preferred site is $\mathrm{Ni}$ sublattices in the case of $E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}>(H_{\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}+H_{\mathrm{Ni}_{\mathrm{Al}}})$. (3) for $0<E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}<(H_{\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}+H_{\mathrm{Ni}_{\mathrm{Al}}})$, the site preference of $\mathrm{Re}$ is strongly composition-dependent. That is, Re prefers to occupy $\mathrm{Ni}$ sites in Al-rich $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases and $\mathrm{Al}$ sites in Ni-rich $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases, and no site preference exists in stoichiometric $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases. In our $3 \times 3 \times 3$ supercells with single Re-addition, the calculated $E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}$ is $-1.05 \mathrm{eV}$. A negative energy factor indicates Re prefers to occupy $\mathrm{Al}$ sites in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases. This finding is consistent with previous theoretical [12-14] and experimental [5,41,42] results, but it is unclear if this site preference exists in $\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Re}$ systems with double Re-addition.

To explore the impact of an extra Re-addition, the point defect formation enthalpies $H_{i}(d)$ in double Re-addition systems are also calculated. Herein, $i=\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ represents various arrangements of Re $\mathrm{Re}$ atoms up to 6th NN. $d$ is the distance between A and B sites in $\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ Re supercells after optimization. Thus, their interactions, i.e., the correlative energy $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ between $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ defects, can be further calculated by following expression [38]:

$$
\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)=H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)-H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}}-H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}
\tag{4}
$$

The calculated $H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ and $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ are also listed in Table 1. Compared with single Re-addition systems, the magnitude of $H_{i}$ in double Re-addition models is significantly high, which means the addition of an extra $\mathrm{Re}$ atom in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases is difficult, and the difficulty depends on the distance $d$ between two substituted sites. Fig. 2 further plots the variation of $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ vs. $d$. One can see that the correlative energy $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ is negative at $d \approx d_{0}=1$ st, which means an attractive interaction [18,43] exists between $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ defects as both of $\mathrm{Re}$ atoms locates at the 1st NN sites of one another. This attractive interaction is undoubtedly favorable for the clustering of $\mathrm{Re}$ atoms in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases [2,18,43]. With the increase of $d$, the $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ becomes positive values and then gradually drops up to approximate zero from $d \approx 2$ nd to $d \approx 6$ th, indicating most of interactions between $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ defects are repulsive and this repulsive interaction can be ignored as $d$ being beyond the 6th NN of one another.

Eq. (2) shows $E_{\mathrm{Re}}^{\mathrm{Ni} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al}}$ is in fact the latent heat in the reaction of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}+\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Al}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Al}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$. Using a similar method to Eq. (3), the energy factors $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ in the double Re-addition models are also calculated. In which the superscript represents a transformation from initial to final configures of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ complex, i.e., a reaction from reactants to products in the antisite-based substitution schema [12-14]. For example, as $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})$ and $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(1 \mathrm{st})$ being regarded as the initial and the final configurations of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ complexes, respectively, the reaction can be described by following expression:

$$
\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})+\mathrm{Ni}_{\mathrm{Ni}} \rightarrow \mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(1 \mathrm{st})+\mathrm{Ni}_{\mathrm{Al}}
\tag{5}
$$

Correspondingly, its energy factor $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ can be deduced from:

$$
E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}=H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}(1 \mathrm{st})+H_{\mathrm{Ni}_{\mathrm{Al}}}-H_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})-H_{\text {perfect }}
\tag{6}
$$

It is worthy of pointing out that Eq. (5) isn't a reversible reaction and the $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ is a $d$-dependent parameter. For example, the $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ is $1.90 \mathrm{eV}$ in Eq. (6), while it is $-0.86 \mathrm{eV}$ in its reverse process, i.e., from $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(1 \mathrm{st})$ to $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})$, and even becomes $-0.98 \mathrm{eV}$ in the transformation from $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(3 \mathrm{rd})$ to $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})$. Although the energy factor $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ isn't a good indicator for the spontaneous transformation from initial configurations to final configurations, but that which a negative $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ means the final configuration is more stable than the initial configuration should be undoubted in terms of antisite-based substitution schema [12,13].

Table 2 tabulates the calculated $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ in double Re-addition systems. One can see that all $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ are negative values in the transformations from $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Ni}$ sites to $\mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Ni}$ sites and from $\mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Ni}$ sites to $\mathrm{Al}-$ $\mathrm{Al}$ sites, which means the stability of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ complexes is high compared with $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ complexes, and the $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}$ complex is the most stable among various $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}}(d)$ models. That is said, the Re-Re pair prefers to occupy $\mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Al}$ sites, followed by $\mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Ni}$ sites, and then $\mathrm{Ni}-$ $\mathrm{Ni}$ sites. Corresponding to the substitutions of $\mathrm{Re}$ atoms at $\mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Al}$ sites, $\mathrm{Al}-\mathrm{Ni}$ sites and $\mathrm{Ni}-\mathrm{Ni}$ sites, the weight percents of $\mathrm{Re}$ atoms in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases are $5.06 \mathrm{wt} \%, 5.09 \mathrm{wt} \%$ and $5.13 \mathrm{wt} \%$, respectively. This result clearly indicates the site preference of $\mathrm{Re}$ at $\mathrm{Al}$ sublattices is scarcely changed by an extra Re-addition, but the substitution of Re for Ni atoms is also energetically permissive as Re content being high enough. And consequently only $53-78 \%$ Re atoms at Al sublattices are detected in $\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}-2 \% \mathrm{Re}$ intermetallic compounds [5].

Further, as the $d$ between A and B sites being considered, it is found that the interaction between $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ defects has a significant impact on the distribution of $\mathrm{Re}$ atoms in a same $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ complex. For example, Table 1 shows the $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ are $0.120 \mathrm{eV}$ and $0.014 \mathrm{eV}$, respectively, in $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})$ and $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(6 \mathrm{th})$ models. As $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})$ and $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(6 \mathrm{th})$ are regarded as the initial and final configurations, respectively, its $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ is $-0.10 \mathrm{eV}$, which means the stability of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}$ complexes with $d \approx 6$ th is high relative to the $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}$ complex with $d \approx 2$ nd. That is said, a big repulsive interaction is unfavorable for the formation of stable $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}$ complex, and the stronger the repulsive interaction, the lower the stability of $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}$ complex. As a result, $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ defects tend to separate from each other in the case of preferential sites. In contrast with repulsive interactions, the attractive interaction between $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ defects is found to be beneficial to the formation of stable $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}$ complexes. For example, the $E_{\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Re}}^{\text {initial } \rightarrow \text { final }}$ are $-0.38 \mathrm{eV}$ and $-0.12 \mathrm{eV}$, respectively, in the transformations from $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}(2 \mathrm{nd})$ to $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}(1 \mathrm{st})$ and from $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(3 \mathrm{rd})$ to $\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}}^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}}(1 \mathrm{st})$, indicating $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ and $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ complexes with negative $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$ can be easily generated relative to those with positive $\Delta E^{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{B}}}(d)$. Therefore, this formation ability of first NN Re$\mathrm{Re}$ pairs prior to the second and the three NN Re-Re pairs further indicate Re-clusters are likely to exist in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases [2,5,15] as the substitution of Re for Ni atoms takes place.

Thus, we can conclude that although Re prefers to occupy Al sublattice sites and is prone to stay away from each other, but the substitution for $\mathrm{Ni}$ atoms is also energetically permissive, and tends to together with its near $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Al}}$ or $\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ defect to form a Re-cluster in $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases.

### 3.2. Impact of interactions between defects on ideal shear strengths

The ideal shear strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$, *i.e.*, a critical stress required to break a perfect crystal [34,44], is an important parameter in the design and reliability analysis of advanced materials, although this strength in a real crystal can be affected by dislocations, grain boundaries, cracks and other microstructural defects. The $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ provides an upper bound of creep strengths which the material can achieve [33,44,45], and the magnitude of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ associated with the stress necessary for the movement of a dislocation [44] is a key parameter to predict the Peierls-Nabarro stress [46], *i.e.*, the minimum stress required to move an individual dislocation [44,47]. Hence, an in-depth research of ideal shear strengths in defect-free crystals could provide some crucial information for understanding their creep properties. As this reason, the ideal shear strengths $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at $[\overline{1}10](111)$and$[11\overline{2}](111)$ slip directions in Re-free Ni₃Al crystals and Ni₃Al phases with Re_Al and Re_Ni defects are firstly calculated. Herein, the lattice vector $\vec{a}$ in rotated supercells is parallel to the $[\overline{1}10]$ or $[11\overline{2}]$ directions and the lattice vector $\vec{c}$ is parallel to the normal direction of (111) plane, *i.e.*, $[111]$ direction. After sheared, the deformed lattice vectors $R$ can be obtained by:

$$
R=R_0{\cdot}D \tag{7}
$$

where the matrix $R_0$ represents the un-deformed lattice vector, and the deformation matrix $D$ [23,48] is

$$
D=\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\varepsilon&0&1
\end{bmatrix} \tag{8}
$$

In which $\varepsilon$ is the shear strain, *i.e.*, the ratio of displacement $\Delta a$ with respect to the height $c$ of the rotated supercell. In the first-principles calculation, the atom positions are represented by fractional coordinates. At each step of shear deformation, the configuration of previous steps is used as a starting point [44]. The atom positions and cell shape are relaxed under the constraint of fixed shear strains $\varepsilon$.

Fig. 3 exhibits the curves of stress $\sigma$ *vs.* strain $\varepsilon$ in the shear process of Re-free and single Re-addition Ni₃Al supercells. With the increase of $\varepsilon$, a continuous rise followed by a gradual drop in $\sigma$ can be seen. For Re-free Ni₃Al crystal, Fig. 3a shows the shear stress $\sigma$ along $[\overline{1}10]$ direction reaches its maximum of 4.83 GPa at $\varepsilon=0.16$, which means the ideal shear strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in the $[\overline{1}10](111)$ slip direction is 4.83 GPa. Similarly, the $\sigma_{\text{max}}=4.23$ GPa at $\varepsilon=0.18$ in the $[11\overline{2}](111)$ slip direction can be also deduced, referred to Fig. 3b. The $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in $[\overline{1}10](111)$ slip directions is big relative to $[11\overline{2}](111)$ slip directions, which means the dislocation movement at $[\overline{1}10](111)$ direction is more difficult. As a result, a dislocation decomposition $[\overline{1}01]\rightarrow\frac{1}{6}[\overline{1}\overline{1}2]+\frac{1}{6}[\overline{2}11]+\frac{1}{6}[\overline{2}11]+\frac{1}{6}[\overline{1}\overline{1}2]$ used to happen under shear deformation [44], although the dislocation at $[\overline{1}10](111)$ directions easily nucleates owing to its low unstable stacking fault energy relative to $[11\overline{2}](111)$ directions [1,49].

Compared with Re-free Ni₃Al crystals, Fig. 3 shows the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in Re_Ni models are low. They decrease by 0.49 GPa, 0.73 GPa and 0.33 GPa at the $[\overline{1}10](111)$ direction and 0.45 GPa, 0.70 GPa and 0.70 GPa at the $[11\overline{2}](111)$ direction, respectively, corresponding to Re_Ni1, Re_Ni2 and Re_Ni3 models. This lower $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ seems to imply the movement of dislocations in $\gamma'$-phases easily takes place in the case of Re-addition. However, it is noticed that the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in Re_Al model increase to 7.47 GPa and 5.59 GPa in $[\overline{1}10](111)$ and $[11\overline{2}](111)$ directions, respectively. Since the $\langle110\rangle$ super-dislocation shearing into $\gamma'$-phase can be decomposed to the configuration of $1/3(112)$ super Shockleys partials plus stacking faults, and some $\langle110\rangle$ super-dislocations can even form K-W locking configuration of $1/2(110)+$ APB by cross-slip from $\{111\}$ planes to $\{100\}$ planes [20], a increasing $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ caused by Re-addition at preferential Al sites undoubtedly may enhance the resistance against dislocation motion in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases, and consequently can improve the creep strength of Ni-based SC superalloys [2,20].

It is noticed that most of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ are different from each other in Re_Ni1, Re_Ni2 and Re_Ni3 models except for the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at the $[11\overline{2}](111)$ direction in Re_Ni2 and Re_Ni3 models. This result distinctly indicates the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ is a parameter sensitive to sites. For three non-equivalent sites of Ni sub-lattices as shown in Figs. 1a and 4 further illustrates their local atom configurations in the case of Re-addition, in which $h_0=a_0/\sqrt{3}$ is the separation between adjacent (111) planes, and $l_0=a_0/\sqrt{2}$ is the distance between Re and its first NN host atoms. One can see that the atom arrangement of three host atoms upon the doped (111) slip plane at $[\overline{1}10](111)$ directions is obviously different from that at $[11\overline{2}](111)$ directions. In the shear deformation, these host atoms have a same displacement $\Delta x=\varepsilon{\cdot}h_0$ along slip directions, but the change from $l_0$ to $l=[l_0^2+(\Delta x)^2-2l_0{\cdot}\Delta x{\cdot}\mathrm{cos}\delta]^{1/2}$ is different from each other owing to various distribution angles $\delta$, referred to Fig. 4g. As a result, a complicated variation in bonding strengths $Q_{\text{Re-Ni/Al}}(l)$ between Re and host atoms will consequently take place [26,50]. At $[\overline{1}10](111)$ slip directions, the host atoms situate at three different non-equivalent sites with various distribution angles $\delta$, and the species of host atoms at these non-equivalent sites is different from each other. Therefore, Re_Ni1, Re_Ni2 and Re_Ni3 models exhibit various $\sigma_{\text{max}}$. However, only two $\delta$ can be observed at $[11\overline{2}](111)$ slip directions, and the overall effect caused by changed bonding lengths $\Delta l=l-l_0$ in Re_Ni2 model cannot be distinguished from Re_Ni3 model, and consequently the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at $[11\overline{2}](111)$ directions in Re_Ni2 model shows a same value as Re_Ni3 model.

These results distinctly indicate only a preferential substitution of Re for Al atoms is favorable for improving the ideal shear strength of $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases. As a result, a longitudinal arrangement of Re<sub>A</sub> and Re<sub>B</sub> defects should be of high $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ compared with their horizontal layout. For the shear deformation at $[1\ 1\ \overline{2}](1\ 1\ 1)$ directions, Table 3 shows $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 4.34$ GPa at $\phi = 0^{\circ}$ is indeed less than $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 4.64$ GPa at $\phi = 120^{\circ}$ in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(1st) models, and $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 3.71$ GPa at $\phi = 90^{\circ}$ is more than $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 3.13$ GPa at $\phi = 150^{\circ}$ in H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sup>(1st) models. In the same $[\theta,\phi]$, the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni3</sub> complex are equal to those in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni2</sub> complex, e.g., $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 4.68$ GPa at $[\theta,\phi] = [90^{\circ}, 30^{\circ}]$ and $d \approx$ 1st and $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 5.09$ GPa at $[\theta,\phi] = [61.87^{\circ}, 100.89^{\circ}]$ and $d \approx$ 3th. However, not all $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at $[1\ 1\ \overline{2}](1\ 1\ 1)$ directions follow the principle. For example, $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 4.52$ GPa at $\phi = 90^{\circ}$ is lower than $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 4.69$ GPa at $\phi = 30^{\circ}$ in H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(1st) models. In fact, most of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>($\overrightarrow{d}$) and H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>($\overrightarrow{d}$) models, especially the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at $[\overline{1}\ 1\ 0](1\ 1\ 1)$ directions, is failure in the above orientation rule. For example, for same $[\theta,\phi] = [35.26^{\circ}, 30^{\circ}]$ at $d \approx$ 1st and $[90^{\circ}, 30^{\circ}]$ at $d \approx$ 3th, $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 5.51$ GPa and 5.79 GPa at the $[\overline{1}\ 1\ 0](1\ 1\ 1)$ direction in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni2</sub> complexes are different from their corresponding $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 5.07$ GPa and 6.22 GPa in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni3</sub> complexes. Obviously, this deviations mainly arises from the different orientation dependence of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ between Re<sub>Ni2</sub> and Re<sub>Ni3</sub> models, i.e., a different $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ of Re<sub>Ni2</sub> from Re<sub>Ni3</sub> at the $[\overline{1}\ 1\ 0](1\ 1\ 1)$ direction and the same $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ of Re<sub>Ni2</sub> as Re<sub>Ni3</sub> at the $[1\ 1\ \overline{2}](1\ 1\ 1)$ direction.

With respect to $d$-dependence of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$, Table 3 shows the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>($\overrightarrow{d}$) and H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>($\overrightarrow{d}$) models ascend with the increase of $d$. For example, most of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at $[1\ 1\ \overline{2}](1\ 1\ 1)$ directions in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(3th) and H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sup>(3th) models are larger than those in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(1st) and H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sup>(1st) models except for $\sigma_{\text{max}} = 4.47$ GPa in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni1</sub> complex with intra-layer arrangement of Re atoms at $d \approx$ 3th. And a similar variation of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ versus $d$ can be also seen at the $[\overline{1}\ 1\ 0](1\ 1\ 1)$ direction. This result distinctly indicates the $d$ between Re<sub>A</sub> and Re<sub>B</sub> defects has a important influence on the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ of $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases, and the bigger the $d$, the higher the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$. However, as $d \approx$ 1st, it is noticed that all $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni</sub> complex are higher than those of Re<sub>Ni</sub> + $Re_{\text{Ni}}$ complexes either at the $[\overline{1}\ 1\ 0](1\ 1\ 1)$ direction or at the $[1\ 1\ \overline{2}](1\ 1\ 1)$ direction. Undoubtedly, $d$ is merely a very important factor but is not dominant for the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in double Re-addition systems.

As this reason, Fig. 5 further illustrates the variation of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ versus correlative energies $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ between Re<sub>A</sub> and Re<sub>B</sub> defects. From Fig. 5b, one can see that with the increase of $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ from $-0.30$ eV to $0.20$ eV, the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ ascends firstly, and then gradually falls down, and the biggest $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ emerges at $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d) \approx 0.044$ eV. Moreover, the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in V<sub>Re<sub>Al</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(2nd) models with $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d) = 0.120$ eV is far higher than the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sup>(1st) and H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sup>(1st) models with $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d) = -0.212$ eV and the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in V<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(1st) and H<sub>Re<sub>Ni</sub></sub><sup>Re<sub>Al</sub></sup>(1st) models with $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d) = -0.079$ eV. These results distinctly indicates that the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ of $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al-Re phases cannot benefit from a strong interaction between Re<sub>A</sub> and Re<sub>B</sub> defects regardless attraction or repulsion, similarly to the influence of point defects caused by P-Re or S-P pairs on the rupture strength of $\gamma$-Ni/$\gamma'$-Ni₃Al interfaces [26,51], and a weak positive correlation is more profitable than a weak negative correlation for the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in Re<sub>Al</sub> + Re<sub>Ni</sub> complexes. Thus, relative to $\overrightarrow{d}$, $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ provides a simple and direct criterion for the $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al-Re phases to be evaluated. That is, a week repulsive interaction between Re<sub>Al</sub> defects should be responsible for the improvement of creep strengths of Ni-based SC superalloys to some extent, and the detrimental effect caused by excess Re-addition can be attributed to a strong attractive interaction between Re<sub>Al</sub> and Re<sub>Ni</sub> defects.

## 4. Conclusion

Using the first-principles calculation method, the site preference of Re in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phase and the impact of Re-addition on the ideal shear strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ at $[\overline{1}\ 1\ 0](1\ 1\ 1)$ and $[1\ 1\ \overline{2}](1\ 1\ 1)$ slip directions have been investigated, and a correlative energy function $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ has been adopted to evaluate the interaction between institutional point defects caused by Re-additions. The analysis of energy factors $E_{\text{Re-Re}}^{\text{initial}\rightarrow\text{final}}$ associated with the formation enthalpy of point defects reveals Re prefers to occupy Al sublattice sites in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phase, but the substitution of Re for Ni atoms is also energetically permissive. In the case of double Re-additions, this site preference is scarcely affected by $\Delta E^{\text{Re}_A+\text{Re}_B}(d)$ between Re<sub>A</sub> and Re<sub>B</sub> defects. That is, the Re-Re pair prefers to occupy Al-Al sites, and then Al-Ni sites, followed by Ni-Ni sites, and Re atoms at preferred Al sites are prone to stay away from each other, but the substitution for Ni atoms tends together with its near Re<sub>Al</sub> or Re<sub>Ni</sub> defect to form a Re-cluster in $\gamma'$-Ni₃Al phases. As excessive Re atoms are added into $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases to replace a part of $\mathrm{Ni}$ atoms, especially to form Re-clusters, the $\sigma_{\max }$ at $[\overline{1} 10](111)$ and $[11 \overline{2}](111)$ slip directions in Re-free $\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ crystals are even weaken. Thus, the improvement of $\sigma_{\max }$ of $\gamma^{\prime}-\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ phases caused by preferential substitution of $\mathrm{Re}$ for $\mathrm{Al}$ atoms can be attributed to a week repulsive interaction, and the strong attractive interaction induced by Re-clusters should be responsible for the detrimental effect of excess Re-addition to some extent.
