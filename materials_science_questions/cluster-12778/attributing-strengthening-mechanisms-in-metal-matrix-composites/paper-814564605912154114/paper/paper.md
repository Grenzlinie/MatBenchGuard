# Synthesis and Mechanical Behavior of Nanostructured Al 5083/ n-TiB₂ Metal Matrix Composites

Meijuan Li$^{\text{a,b}}$, Kaka Ma$^{\text{b,c}}$, Lin Jiang$^{\text{b,c}}$, Hanry Yang$^{\text{b,d}}$, Enrique J. Lavernia$^{\text{b,c}}$, Lianmeng Zhang$^{\text{a}}$ and Julie M. Schoenung$^{\text{b,c*}}$

## Abstract:

Nanostructured Al 5083-based composites with nano-TiB₂ reinforcement particles were fabricated via cryomilling and spark plasma sintering (SPS). TEM observation revealed that the Al matrix consists of equiaxed nano-grains (average size, ~74 nm), and the reinforcement, TiB₂ nanoparticles (n-TiB₂), was distributed discretely and homogeneously in the Al matrix. The interface between the Al-matrix and n-TiB₂ appears to be free of defects, and no obvious discontinuities were observed. The composite exhibits a compressive strength of 817 MPa with 6.0% strain-to-failure. The strength is 20% higher than that of an equivalent SPS consolidated Al 5083 without reinforcement. Nanoindentation was used in our study to provide fundamental insight into the local microscopic mechanical properties. The strengthening mechanisms of the composites are analyzed taking into account the grain boundaries, the Orowan strengthening from the n-TiB₂ particles and dispersoids such as Al₂O₃, AlN and Al₆Mn, as well as geometrically necessary dislocations induced in the matrix by the nano-TiB₂ particles.

Keywords: Nanostructure; Al metal matrix composite; Spark plasma sintering; Mechanical properties.

### 1. Introduction

Particulate reinforced metal matrix composites (MMCs) can be engineered for enhanced specific stiffness, specific strength and creep resistance, as well as a wide array of other desirable material attributes [1]. Among the family of available MMCs, aluminum based composites provide low density, superior physical and mechanical properties, at a relatively low cost [2]. Al-based composites, reinforced with hard ceramic particles such as $TiB_2$, $SiC$, $Al_2O_3$ and $B_4C$, provide improved mechanical behavior, such as stiffness, strength and wear resistance, over their unreinforced counterparts [3-7]. In recent years, researchers working with MMCS have used nanostructured matrices (generally defined hereafter as having a nominal grain size smaller than 100 nm) to synthesize materials that are technologically and scientifically interesting. In some cases, the behavior of such nanoscale materials can be very different from those of the same substance with microscale features [8]. Bulk nanostructured Al 5083 alloys [9] and $Al$-$B_4C$ composites with high yield strength (~755 MPa) [10] have been successfully fabricated previously through a combined approach involving two steps: first, cryomilling to prepare the nanocrystalline Al alloy powder and second, spark plasma sintering (SPS) to consolidate the cryomilled powder.

Cryomilling is a mechanical attrition technique used to fabricate nanocrystalline powder within a cryogenic medium (such as liquid $N_2$ or Ar) [11]. It takes advantages of the extremely low temperature environment, which restrains recovery and recrystallization, and leads to finer grain structure and more rapid grain refinement kinetics [9, 12]. Cryomilling is particularly effective at creating a clean, well-bonded matrix-reinforcement interface and a uniform distribution of reinforcement particles within the matrix [13]. To take advantage of the unique properties of bulk nanostructured materials, the nanocrystalline powders need to be consolidated into bulk samples with defined geometry and size. One critical objective during the consolidation process is to achieve full density with minimal microstructural coarsening and/or undesirable microstructural transformations. However, review of the literature shows that some conventional consolidation methods lead to significant grain growth. For example, an average grain size of 116 nm was observed in a hot isostatically pressed (HIPed) cryomilled Al-Mg alloy [14], and the grain size in a cryomilled Al 5083 material consolidated by cold isostatic pressing (CIP) and extrusion was 207 nm, whereas the grain size of the initial powder was ~30 nm [15].

Spark plasma sintering (SPS) is commonly employed to minimize grain growth during

consolidation [16]. Therefore, this technique, one of several potential field-activated consolidation processes, has been widely employed for the fabrication of various nanostructured metallic materials and composites [17]. In the SPS process, a pulsed direct current is used concurrently with uniaxial pressure to consolidate powders into fully dense, bulk materials. Compared with traditional consolidation methods, the SPS technique is capable of achieving an extremely high heating rate (up to 1000K/min) under high pressure. Consequently, samples can be sintered at a relatively low temperature and in a shorter period of time, making it more likely to retain the starting microstructure.

Among all the ceramic particles used as reinforcements in Al-based composites, titanium diboride ($\text{TiB}_2$) has emerged as an outstanding option because of its excellent resistance to mechanical erosion, plus high hardness (960 HV), high melting point ($2790^\circ\text{C}$) and good thermal stability [18]. In addition, $\text{TiB}_2$ has been used as a grain refiner in Al alloys to suppress the growth of Al grains [19]. In general, sub-micron size $\text{TiB}_2$ particles have been introduced into the matrix using in-situ techniques through the reactions between $\text{K}_2\text{TiF}_6$ and $\text{KBF}_4$ salts [20], Ti and B elements [21], or mixing of molten Al-8Ti and Al-4B alloys [22]. In situ formation of $\text{TiB}_2$ within the melt itself reduces the opportunity for particle surface oxidation and subsequently improves the interfacial integrity [20, 23]. However, the distribution and concentration of $\text{TiB}_2$, residual reactants and by-products in the composites are difficult to control accurately, and grain growth of the as-produced $\text{TiB}_2$ and the Al matrix is unavoidable under the required high reaction temperatures ($850$-$1080^\circ\text{C}$) [20-24]. Therefore, there is a need to further explore this material system, but with a focus on two key aspects: introducing nano-sized, rather than sub-micron sized, $\text{TiB}_2$ reinforcing particles, and to introduce them by ex-situ addition, rather than through in situ reactions.

Motivated by the above discussion, the present study was undertaken in an effort to provide insight into two questions. First, is it possible to use a field assisted sintering technique to synthesize a bulk nanostructured composite in which the matrix grain size remains under 100 nm? Second, what are the mechanisms that govern the mechanical behavior of such a nanostructured composite? To address these questions, we used the following approach. First, an aluminum alloy 5083, which is a non-heat-treatable, work-hardenable and corrosion resistant alloy, was selected as the metal matrix due to the aforementioned attributes, as well as its high strength and relevance as a structural material for various applications [25]. Second, $\text{TiB}_2$ nanoparticles are introduced into the Al alloy powder via cryomilling, and then the cryomilled $\text{Al/TiB}_2$ nanopowder was consolidated by

SPS.

## 2. Experimental procedure

Gas atomized Al 5083 powder (Al-4.5Mg-0.57Mn-0.25Fe in weight percent, wt.%) was cryomilled with 5 wt.% titanium diboride nanopowder (designated n-TiB₂ hereafter). The average particle size of the n-TiB₂ is approximately 31 nm with a standard deviation of 14 nm. Cryomilling was carried out in a liquid nitrogen environment for 12 hours using a modified Szegvari attritor with a stainless steel vessel and balls (~6.4 mm in diameter). The attritor was operated at a rotational rate of 180 rpm and a temperature of approximately -185°C. The ball-to-powder ratio was 32:1, with 0.2 wt.% stearic acid added as a process control agent to prevent agglomeration. The cryomilled powders were loaded into a graphite die with an inner diameter of 20 mm. They were then fast sintered at 723 K (450 °C) in a Dr. Sinter SPS-825S system with a heating rate of 100 K (100 °C) per minute and held for 3 minutes. The 88 MPa loading pressure was applied within 1 minute when the sintering temperature approached 723 K (450 °C) and was held constant until the end of the 723 K dwell. Finally, the bulk samples were naturally cooled to room temperature. The temperature was controlled by a K-type thermocouple inserted into the graphite die from the side. The chamber pressure was kept at a near vacuum level of 4 Pa to avoid oxidation during sintering. In the rest of the manuscript, the SPS consolidated bulk composite sample is denoted as Al/n-TiB₂.

The density of the Al/n-TiB₂ specimens was measured with an AG204 balance (Mettler-Toledo (Schweiz) AG, Greifensee, Switzerland). Phase composition of the composites was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Scintag XDS2000 diffractometer (Thermo Optek Corp., Franklin, MA) with Cu K_α radiation. A mode of step scan was used with a step-size of 0.01° and a scan rate of 0.5 seconds per step. The mechanical behavior was characterized by means of compression testing and nano-indentation. 3×3×3 mm compression cubes were sectioned from the SPS-consolidated bulk materials by electric discharge machining. Quasi-static compression testing was performed at a strain rate of $10^{-3}$/s at room temperature using an Instron (Norwood, MA) 8801 servohydraulic testing system equipped with Bluehill software. The compressive load direction was parallel to the direction of the pressure loading during SPS consolidation.

Nanoindentation tests were performed on the bulk samples via a MTS Nano Indenter® XP equipped with a diamond Berkovich tip. In this work, an indentation depth of 2000 nm and a method of XP-G series continuous stiffness measurement (CSM) standard hardness, modulus and tip calibration were used to determine the elastic modulus and hardness. With

the CSM option, the indenter applies a load to the tip to force it into the surface while simultaneously superimposing an oscillating force with a force amplitude generally several orders of magnitude smaller than the nominal load. The CSM option offers a means of separating the in-phase and out-of-phase components of the load-displacement history. This separation provides an accurate measurement of the location of initial surface contact and continuous measurement of contact stiffness as a function of depth or frequency, thus eliminating the need for unloading cycles. Since the contact stiffness is determined directly, no assumptions (such as mechanical equilibrium) are required to correct for elasticity [26]. For the purpose of comparison, a sample of Al 5083 alloy (without reinforcement), which was also fabricated through cryomilling and SPS consolidation [27], was studied by nanoindentation as well.

The morphology of the compressive fracture surface of the $Al/n-TiB_2$ composite was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Quanta 250 FEG, FEI, Czech). The microstructure was observed using a JEOL JEM 2500 SE transmission electron microscope (TEM). The TEM specimen was prepared by mechanically grinding the bulk material to a thickness of $<60\ \mu$m, then dimpling from one side to a thickness of approximately $15\ \mu$m. Further thinning to a thickness of electron transparency was carried out using a Gatan PIPS 691 ion milling system at a voltage of 4.5 kV. The grain and particle sizes of the Al and the $n-TiB_2$, respectively, were determined by TEM observation and estimated by measuring Feret diameters of the strongly diffracting grains using ImageJ$^\circledR$ image analysis software. More than 200 measurements were performed for each average value to assure the statistical accuracy.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Microstructure

The density of the $Al/n-TiB_2$ is $2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$, which is approximately 98.0% of the theoretical density ($2.75\ \text{g/cm}^3$), as calculated via the rule of mixtures, according to the density of Al 5083, $2.67\ \text{g/cm}^3$ [27], and the density of $TiB_2$, $4.50\ \text{g/cm}^3$ from JCPDS card 35-0741. Thus the global porosity in the sample was approximately 2%. The volume fraction of $TiB_2$, f, is assumed to be 0.03, based on the conversion from its weight percent (5 wt.%). Figs. 1a and 1b show the optical microstructure of the sintered composite and its XRD pattern, respectively. A small volume fraction of porosity (designated as black dots) was observed in the sample. The pores were distributed randomly and exhibited an average size in the submicron regime. The XRD pattern reveals that in addition to the Al matrix phase and the

$TiB_2$, a small amount of intermetallic compound $Al_6Mn$ exists in the sintered bulk sample; this phase is commonly observed in Al 5083 after solidification [28].

### 3.2 Mechanical behavior

The compressive stress-strain curves for the Al/n-TiB₂ composite are shown in Fig. 4. Two samples were used for testing. The average yield strength was 717±4 MPa and the compressive strength was 818±4 MPa, which are approximately 20% higher than a cryomilled-plus-SPS-consolidated Al 5083 sample described in previous study, which had a compressive strength of approximately 670 MPa [27]. The Al/n-TiB₂ samples reveal work hardening and plasticity with a compressive strain-to-failure of approximately 6.0%. Fig. 5 presents the fracture surface produced after compression testing. Dimples, which indicate ductile failure of the matrix, are not observed. The fracture surface is irregular and displays some necking regions and voids, suggesting that the composite failed due to brittle fracture, while some plastic deformation occurred before fracture. There is no evidence of pull-out of the TiB₂ nanoparticles from the Al matrix, which supports the suggestion of good cohesion between the reinforcement particles and the matrix.

The average values of E and H are 85.8 ± 2.5 GPa and 3.0 ± 0.4 GPa, respectively. The variation in values from point to point is due to local inhomogeneity of the microstructure. ## 4. Discussion
### 4.1 Formation of Nanostructure

It is noteworthy that the Al/n-TiB₂ synthesized in the present study retained a nanostructured matrix. The grain size (average ~ 74 nm) of the composite is relatively small

compared to that in the Al 5083 alloy (average ~ 192 nm) that was also fabricated by cryomilling and SPS [30]. Prior work suggests that the grain size of the Al matrix in cryomilled Al 5083 or Al 5083 composite powders is approximately 30 nm for a cryomilling time of 8 hours [17, 31-32]. The increase in grain size observed in the consolidated sample is attributed to the grain growth during SPS consolidation, where the material experienced thermal exposure at 450 °C for a few minutes. Despite of the limited grain growth, the average grain size in the materials remains in nano-regime (< 100 nm) from the TEM observation (Figs. 2a-c). The presence of the nano-TiB₂ reinforcing particles contributes to the preservation of the nanostructure via Zener pinning of the grain boundary, because a higher volume fraction of reinforcement and a shorter interparticle spacing result in a stronger pinning force [33]. The inter-particle spacing, L, is given by $L=\sqrt{2/3}D_{p}(\sqrt{\pi/4f}-1)$ , assuming the reinforcement, n-TiB₂ particles are distributed homogeneously in the composite [6]. For the Al/n-TiB₂ composite in the current study, $D_p$ is 38 nm and f is 0.03. Therefore, L is calculated to be 127 nm approximately. The value of the interparticle spacing corresponds to the grain size of the Al matrix.

### 4.2 Strengthening Mechanisms

The Al/n-TiB₂ composite exhibited notably high strength (compressive yield strength ~717 MPa) and high hardness (~3 GPa). In this section, we describe an approach to provide insight into the individual mechanisms that affect strengthening. In general, the presence of porosity leads to a decrease in strength but does not significantly influence the strain to failure in compressive properties for Al alloys and composites. The size of the pores in the sample studied in the current work is at submicron scale. The volume fraction of the porosity in the Al/n-TiB₂ composite is less than 0.02. Thus, the influence of pores on the compressive properties in the current work can be considered to be negligible. If tensile properties were considered, the presence of the pores would lead to a decrease in the strength and the ductility of the materials. Pores tend to be the nucleation sites for cracks, and a network of pores would lead to easier crack propagation during tensile deformation. Consequently, the tensile strength and ductility of a porous sample are usually lower than the values for a fully dense sample of the same material. The scope of the present work, however, was focused on the compressive strength of the Al/n-TiB₂ composite. Given the complex interactions that are likely to be present amongst the various mechanisms, it is not our objective to "predict" the overall strength. Instead, by calculating the individual contributions to strength from a mechanistic perspective, we intend to provide insight into their relative magnitudes. In the

case of conventional MMCs with micron-sized or larger reinforcing particles, the strengthening mechanisms are usually divided into two distinct categories: (i) strengthening from the metal matrix, such as grain boundary strengthening described by the Hall-Petch relationship and Orowan strengthening from dispersoids present in the alloy matrix; (ii) strengthening associated with the reinforcement phase, including direct strengthening that results from load transfer [34] and indirect strengthening through the creation of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs), which are produced to accommodate mismatch of the elastic modulus and coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) between the matrix and reinforcement particles [35]. However, given the volume fraction (f = 0.03) of the $TiB_2$ reinforcement particles and their nanosize (~38 nm) in the current study, it is unlikely that load transfer is the primary strengthening mechanism for the reinforcement phase. Instead, the $TiB_2$ nanoparticles act as obstacles to dislocation motion through Orowan loop formation [36].

The Al metal matrix processed by cryomilling usually possesses a large fraction of high angle grain boundaries (HAGBs), which impede continuous dislocation motion through the metal matrix [10]. As the average grain size decreases, i.e., the volume fraction of HAGBs increases, impedance to dislocation motion is increased and the stress required to deform the material is increased. The strengthening resulting from the nanostructured Al matrix can be approximated by the Hall-Petch relationship [37]:

$$
\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{GB}}=\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{HP}} / \sqrt{\mathrm{d}} \tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{GB}}$ is the strengthening contribution from the HAGBs, $\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{HP}}$ is a constant called the Hall-Petch slope, and d is the mean grain size. The Hall-Petch slope for cryomilled Al alloys has been reported to be $0.09 \mathrm{MPa} \mathrm{m}^{1 / 2}$ [38]. Substituting the mean grain size of the aluminum matrix (d = 74 nm), the strength increment due to the nano-grain size is calculated to be 332 MPa.

The flow stress of engineering alloys affected by dispersed particles, e.g., ceramic reinforcements, which are structurally incoherent with the matrix at the onset of yielding, can be predicted by the Orowan equation [39]. Redsten et al. revealed that the strengthening effect of $\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ particulate reinforcement (~ 0.28 µm in diameter) on a commercially pure Al,could be estimated from the Redsten version of the Orowan strengthening equation [6]:

$$
\sigma_{\alpha}=\frac{0.4 M}{\pi \sqrt{1-v}} \cdot \frac{G b}{L} \cdot \ln \frac{\sqrt{2 / 3} D_{p}}{b} \tag{2}
$$


where M is the mean orientation factor for face-centered cubic Al, G is the shear modulus, b is the Burgers vector, $\nu$ is the Poisson's ratio, f is the concentration of the secondary phase (or reinforcement) in the material and $D_p$ is the average diameter of reinforcement particles. The inter-particle spacing, L, is already calculated in Section 4.1, which is approximately 127 nm. For Al 5083, the following parameter values are given for the Redsten version of the Orowan strengthening equation: M = 3.06, G = 25.6 GPa, b =0.286 nm, and $\nu$= 0.33. Consequently, $\sigma_{or}$ is estimated to be 128 MPa. However, Ferguson et al. found an appreciable deviation between experimental and simulated data when the inter-particle free path, L, approached the sub-micrometer regime and the reinforcement phase was in a low volume fraction regime, especially when the particle size of the reinforcement was decreased to the nanometer scale [39]. Accordingly, the actual value of $\sigma_{or}$ is overestimated here, given the average diameter of n-TiB₂ reinforcement particles is only 38 nm.

In addition, it has been demonstrated that small concentrations of alumina, aluminum nitride and intermetallic compound $Al_6Mn$ were formed in the cryomilled Al 5083 powder and remained as nano-dispersoids in the consolidated bulk material [27, 30]. The concentration of these dispersoids was estimated on the basis of the content of O, N and Mn elements in the composite using the method reported in [27]. The total volume fraction of the dispersoids was approximately 0.03. Based on our previous studies [27, 30], these dispersoids are generally present in grain interiors and their size ranges from 10-40 nm. These nano-dispersoids also improve the strength of the Al/n-TiB₂ composites via the Orowan strengthening mechanism since their crystal structure is incoherent with the Al matrix. According to Equation (2), the strength increase due to the Orowan mechanism is independent of the chemical composition of the dispersoids. Instead, it is correlated to the dimension, volume fraction and inter-particle spacing of the dispersoids. Therefore, it is feasible to combine the Orowan strengthening from the dispersoids with the strengthening from the TiB₂ nano-reinforcement. The presence of the dispersoids decreased the inter-particle spacing, L, in Equation (2). As a result, the Orowan strengthening value increased.

Geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) induced by the reinforcement particles have been a subject of extensive discussion in the studies on MMCs [40-43]. GND is a concept that represent an extra storage of dislocations required to accommodate the lattice distortion or curvature arising from non-uniform plastic deformation. In addition to the

dislocations created by the severe plastic deformation during cryomilling, GNDs are present in the $Al/n-TiB_2$ composite to accommodate the non-uniform deformation due to mismatch of elastic modulus and CTE between the Al matrix and the $TiB_2$ reinforcement. Gao et al reported that the critical length scale to consider the influence of GNDs in continuum plasticity is below 1 $\mu$m [40]. Previous studies on a cryomilled Al composite reinforced with 1-7 $\mu$m $B_4C$ particles reported that an interfacial layer of nanocrystalline Al grains coats the reinforcement particles and effectively traps the punched GNDs [42, 43]. Consequently, their influence on mechanical properties is limited. In contrast, the average particle size of the $TiB_2$ reinforcement in the current study is approximately 38 nm in the sintered $Al/n-TiB_2$ composite, while the Al matrix grain sizes varied from 20 to 210 nm with an average cross-sectional diameter of 74 nm. The microstructure (Figs. 3d-f) suggest that the interfaces between the Al matrix and the $n-TiB_2$ particles are clean and well-bonded. Therefore, it is important to take into account the role of GNDs in the current study. GNDs do not necessarily have different atomic structures from dislocations created by uniform plastic deformation. It would be impossible to identify an individual dislocation as a geometrically necessary one or otherwise. Thus, it is yet a challenge to experimentally determine the density of the GNDs from the other dislocations in the homogeneous state [40]. However, the density of the GNDs due to CTE mismatch can be approximated by [41]:

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{CTE}}=\frac{12 \mathrm{f}_{\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{CTE}}}{\mathrm{bD}_{\mathrm{p}}} \tag{3}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{CTE}}=\Delta \alpha \Delta \mathrm{T}$ is the strain due to CTE mismatch, $\Delta \alpha$ ($\sim 15 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~K}^{-1}$) is the difference in CTE between the Al and the $TiB_2$, and $\Delta$T ($\sim$ 420 K) is the difference between the processing temperature and room temperature; $f_r$ is the volume fraction of the reinforcement particles, 0.03; and b is the Burgers vector. Furthermore, the density of GNDs due to elastic modulus mismatch can be approximated by [41]:

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{EM}}=\frac{6 \mathrm{f}_{\mathrm{r}} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{bD}_{\mathrm{p}}} \tag{4}
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the misfit strain due to elastic moduli mismatch, which is assumed to be the same as the strain due to CTE mismatch $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{CTE}}$.

The strengthening contribution from the GNDs can be estimated by the following equation [41]:

$$
\Delta \sigma_{\mathrm{GND}}=\sqrt{3} \eta \mathrm{Gb} \sqrt{\rho_{\mathrm{EM}}}+\sqrt{3} \beta \mathrm{Gb} \sqrt{\rho_{\mathrm{GND}}} \tag{5}
$$


where $\eta = 0.5$ and $\beta = 0.7$ are geometric constants. This strength increment is estimated to be 192 MPa.

From the analysis above, it is clear that the higher strength and hardness achieved in the $Al/n$-TiB₂ composite than that in the Al 5083 alloy can be principally attributed to the finer grain size. This grain refinement is a consequence of the addition of the nano-TiB₂ reinforcement particles. Meanwhile, the TiB₂ particles also strengthen the composite through Orowan strengthening mechanism and the creation of GNDs.

## 5. Conclusions

In this work, bulk nanostructured $Al\ 5083/n$-TiB₂ metal matrix composites were successfully synthesized by cryomilling and spark plasma sintering. The density of the bulk composite achieved 98% of the theoretical density. The characterization of the microstructure and the analysis on the mechanical behavior have revealed the following concluding remarks:

(1) In the composite microstructure, most of the Al grains are equiaxed and in the nanometer regime with an average size of 74 nm. The n-TiB₂ reinforcement particles are distributed discretely and homogeneously in the aluminum matrix.

(2) The $Al/n$-TiB₂ composite fails due to brittle fracture during the compression testing, exhibiting a yield strength of 717 MPa, compressive strength of 818 MPa, and a compressive strain-to-failure of 6.0%. Compared to the unreinforced Al 5083 material, which was also fabricated by cryomilling and SPS, the compressive strength of the composite is 20% higher.

(3) Nanoindentation results demonstrate that the elastic modulus (E) and hardness (H) of the $Al/n$-TiB₂ composite are ~85.8 GPa and ~ 3 GPa, respectively. Variation in values from one indent location to another is observed, indicating local inhomogeneity of the microstructure.

(4) The n-TiB₂ particles play a critical role in the mechanical properties of the $Al/n$-TiB₂ composite through the following mechanisms: Orowan strengthening, creating GNDs, and retaining the superfine grain structure, which provides significant grain boundary strengthening.
