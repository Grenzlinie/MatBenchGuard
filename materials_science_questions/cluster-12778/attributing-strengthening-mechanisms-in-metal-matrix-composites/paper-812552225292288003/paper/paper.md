# Corrosion Behavior in Nitric Acid Solution and Tensile Properties of (CuFeNiMn)${_{1-x}}$Cr${_x}$ HEAs

Xu Chen$^{1,2}$ • Jiaxuan Hu$^{1,2}$ • Ye Liu$^{1,2}$ • Feng Xiang$^{3}$

Received: 28 April 2020 / Accepted: 24 September 2020
© The Korean Institute of Metals and Materials 2020

## Abstract
The empirical prediction on the phase formation in as-cast (CuFeMnNi)${_{1-x}}$Cr${_x}$ alloys was consistent with the previous experimental results, which indicated that the BCC phase formed in the alloys was in the thermodynamically unstable state. The corrosion resistance of the alloys in $2\ \text{wt}\%\ \text{HNO}_3$ solution was increased with the increased Cr content which was mainly related to the formation of large anode and small cathode, increasing total volume fraction of Cr-rich FCC1 and BCC phases, the more uniform distribution of Cu and decreasing relative Cu content. The (CuFeMnNi)$_{0.75}$Cr$_{0.25}$ alloy exhibited an excellent corrosion resistance dominated by localized corrosion, while the CuFeMnNi alloy showed the worst corrosion resistance. Moreover, the strength of the investigated alloys increased while the ductility decreased with the increasing of Cr content, revealing that the extrinsic features played a more important role in the fracture behavior than the intrinsic features.

Keywords CuCrFeMnNi HEAs · Corrosion behavior · Nitric acid solution · Tensile properties

## 1 Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) had been investigated owing essentially to the unexpected microstructures and excellent properties since the alloys were reported [1–5]. Previous experimental results also revealed that HEAs possess high mixing entropy, lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion and cocktail effect resulting from the additions of non-principal alloying elements [6–8]. It was generally believed that two kinds of effects were obtained in HEAs with the additions of alloying elements. One was promoting the formation of FCC phase, and the other was beneficial to the achievement of BCC phase [9]. Consequently, it was the common methods of alloy design that microstructure and properties were controlled by adding alloying elements [10–13].

The CuCrFeMnNi HEAs had attracted more and more attentions due to its good performance and low cost. The microstructure characteristic of CuCrFeNiMn HEAs had been studied by Ren et al. [9, 14], and it was found that the alloys had FCC phase or FCC + BCC phases. Moreover, they pointed out that the achievement of solid solution phase was related to a small difference between their atomic size and an appropriate mixed enthalpy. Segregated Cu, Cr, Mn and Fe in dendrite and interdendrite regions were also obtained; however, Ni was distributed homogeneously in those alloys. Hyun et al. [15] revealed that the as-cast CuCrFeNi alloy contained FCC phase, and the microstructure transformed from FCC phase to FCC1 + FCC2 + BCC phases when Mn element was added. Furthermore, when Al was added into CuCrFeNiMn alloy, the constituent phases changed from three phases to two phases. The phase formation, microstructures, mechanical properties and tensile fracture morphologies of the Fe$_{0.4}$Cr$_{0.4}$NiMn$_x$Cu (x < 1.4) HEA were investigated systematically [16]. It could be inferred that more attentions have been paid on the microstructural evolution of the HEAs. Seldom work reported the systematic influence of alloying elements on mechanical properties. ## 2 Experimental Process

### 2.1 Materials

The $(CuFeMnNi)_{1-x}Cr_x$ alloy which x from 0 increased to 0.25 (at.%) were prepared by means of standard arc-melting technique. The alloys used for the investigation were CuFeMnNi alloy (Logogram: 0%Cr), $(CuFeMnNi)_{0.95}Cr_{0.05}$ alloy (Logogram: 5%Cr), $(CuFeMnNi)_{0.9}Cr_{0.1}$ alloy (Logogram: 10%Cr), $(CuFeMnNi)_{0.85}Cr_{0.15}$ alloy (Logogram: 15%Cr), $(CuFeMnNi)_{0.8} Cr_{0.2}$ alloy (Logogram: 20%Cr) and $(CuFeMnNi)_{0.75}Cr_{0.25}$ alloy (Logogram: 25%Cr), respectively. The elements used in the melted alloys were Cu, Cr, Fe, Ni, and Mn, all of which were high purity (99.99 wt%) elements. The alloys used in the test were refined many times to make the chemical homogeneity better. SEM–EDS maps and chemical composition of the as-cast specimens were detected using a ZEISS-EVO MA10 SEM with an EDS. The alloys for electrochemical tests were cut with the size of 1 cm×1 cm×0.5 cm, and then the specimens were mounted by epoxy. In order to electrical contact, the cold-mounted alloys were welded by copper lines. The exposed surfaces in solution were ground and then cleaned by alcohol and deionized water carefully before electrochemical experiment.

### 2.2 Electrochemical Measurements and Tensile Test

Potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were carried out at room temperature in a typical three-electrode cell, which consisted of reference electrode (saturated calomel electrode (SCE)), working electrode (specimen) and auxiliary electrode (platinum sheet). Polarization curves of corresponding alloys were obtained potentiodynamically coupled with the sweep rate of $1\ \text{mV s}^{-1}$. EIS was carried out by the aid of Corrtest electrochemical workstation at open circuit potential (OCP) with a sinusoidal potential amplitude of 10 mV, running from 10 kHz to 10 MHz. The specimens after the polarization experiment were cleaned using deionized water immediately. Moreover, flowing hot air was also conducted to dry those surfaces. Afterwards, the morphologies of those surfaces for the specimens were observed at once by SEM (AJSM-6360LV).

Tensile tests were conducted at room temperature in a CMJ5105 universal electronic testing machine. Tensile samples with the cross-section of $1×2$ mm and the gauge length of 8 mm were prepared by means of electro-discharge machining, then carefully ground in sequence on #280, #400, #800, #1200, #2000 and #4000 SiC paper, respectively. The morphology of fracture surfaces for tensile samples was characterized by SEM (ZEISS-EVO MA10).

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Phase Selection in As-Cast Alloys

In general, the configurational entropy of HEA was higher than that of conventional alloy due to the fact that there were multiple principal components. That was the main reason why the new type alloys were called HEAs. Based on the expression of $\Delta G_{mix}$, higher $\Delta S_{mix}$ would result in lower $\Delta G_{mix}$, which was benefit to the stability of the system. Meanwhile, it was found that only solid solution (SS) could form with the additions of multiple principal components [18]. However, the following studies manifested that $\Delta S_{mix}$ probably was not the major factor on the phase selection in HEAs [19]. In this work, the high values of $\Delta S_{mix}$ were obtained in the as-cast $(CuFeMnNi)_{1-x}Cr_x$ alloys, as shown in Table 1. To ensure the accuracy of prediction on the phase formation, several parameters including $\Delta H_{mix}$, $\Omega$ (the ratio of $T\Delta S_{mix}/\Delta\text{H}_{mix}$), $\delta$ (atomic-size difference) and $VEC$ (valence electron concentration) were proposed successively [20]. The statistical results indicated that $SS$ tended to be obtained when $-15\ \text{kJ/mol} \leq \Delta H_{mix} \leq 5\ \text{kJ/mol}$, $\Omega\geq1$ and $1\leq\delta\leq6$. Meanwhile, researches showed that the stable phases were the mixture of FCC and BCC $SS$ when the parameter for $VEC$ ranged from 6.87 to 8.0. The stable FCC solid solution can be obtained if the $VEC$ is above 8.0, while the smaller $VEC$ value ($\leq6.87$) is related to the stable BCC solid solution [21].

Previous experimental researches had found that FCC1 and FCC2 phases were formed when the Cr content was 0%, 5% and 10% (at.%), while FCC1、FCC2 and BCC phases were obtained in 15%Cr, 20%Cr and 25%Cr alloys [6]. From Table 1, it could be found that the parameters of $\Delta H_{mix}$, $\Omega$ and $\delta$ in this work satisfied the demands to form $SS$, which was also in agreement with the above mentioned data. However, the values of $VEC$ in the as-cast $(CuFeMnNi)_{1-x}Cr_x$ alloys were higher than 8.0, indicating that FCC1 and FCC2

### 3.2 Potentiodynamic Polarisation Curves and EIS Study

Figure 1a plotted the potentiodynamic polarisation curves of as-cast (CuFeMnNi)₁₋ₓCrₓ alloys in 2 wt% HNO₃ solution. Corrosion potential ($E_{corr}$) and corrosion current density ($I_{corr}$) obtained from those curves could be used to estimate the corrosion behavior in the view of dynamics and thermodynamics, respectively. The passivation area in the curves illustrated that the specimen interior was far away from continual dissolution because of the protective oxide film formed on the surface. As illustrated by those curves shown in Fig. 1a, the (CuFeMnNi)₁₋ₓCrₓ alloy was directly changed to active dissolution region from Tafel region while the active to passive transition was not obtained as no passivation protective film was achieved. The spikes showed in current before the appearance of sustained breakdown perhaps due to the formation of metastable pitting. The changes in the current were resulted from the fact that the pits repassivated finally [22].

Electrochemical parameters were given in Table 2, in which the corrosion rate ($CR$) was mainly determined by corrosion current ($I_{corr}$). The corrosion rate and $I_{corr}$ decreased with the increasing Cr, indicating the corrosion resistance was generally strengthened. The (CuFeMnNi)<sub>0.75</sub>Cr<sub>0.25</sub> alloy had the best corrosion resistance, and the annual corrosion thickness was only 0.032 mm. The CuFeMnNi alloy can corrode 1.089 mm in one year, and the corrosion rate of CuFeMnNi alloy was 34 times higher than that of (CuFeMnNi)<sub>0.75</sub>Cr<sub>0.25</sub> alloy. When the alloy contained 5 at.% Cr, its corrosion resistance was significantly improved, which was almost an order of magnitude higher. As the Cr increased, the corrosion resistance steadily increased.

### 3.3 Tensile Properties

The engineering tensile stress–strain curves at room temperature, and the ultimate tensile strength (UTS), yield strength (YS) and elongation-to-failure (EL) for as-cast (CuFeMnNi)₀.₉₅Cr₀.₀₅ alloy (Logogram: 5%Cr), (CuFeMnNi)₀.₉Cr₀.₁ alloy (Logogram: 10%Cr), and (CuFeMnNi)₀.₇₅ Cr₀.₂₅ alloy (Logogram: 25%Cr) were depicted in Fig. 2. The 5%Cr alloy showed an ultimate tensile strength of 596 MPa, a yield strength of 347 MPa and an elongation-to-failure of 27.5%. With the increas- ing Cr content, both ultimate tensile strength and yield strength increased. The ultimate tensile strength increased dramatically to 1017 MPa and the yield strength increased to 489 MPa in the as-cast 25%Cr alloy. Along with the improved strengths, there was a reduction in the elon- gation-to-failure of the alloys. The elongation-to-failure of the as-cast 25%Cr alloy decreased to 11%, however, the alloy was still ductile. Therefore, it could be deduced that increasing of the Cr content resulted in an obvious strengthening effect.

## 4 Discussions

### 4.1 Corrosion Behavior In 2 Wt% HNO₃ Solution

Figure 3 illustrated the morphologies of as-cast (CuF- eMnNi)_{1-x}Cr_{x} alloys after the polarization experiments. The corroded surfaces of the alloys displayed numerous dendritic ($DR$) and interdendritic ($ID$) structures. The cor- rosion extent of (CuFeMnNi)₀.₇₅Cr₀.₂₅ alloy was slight, while that of CuFeMnNi alloy was worse. The CuFeMnNi alloy had a great degree of corrosion both in $DR$ and $ID$, and the boundaries between the $DR$ and $ID$ were blurred due to large-area corrosion. It should be noted that with the addition of Cr, the boundaries between $DR$ and $ID$ became clear, and the corrosion morphology changed from the general corrosion to the $ID$ corrosion. The results showed that the (CuFeMnNi)₀.₇₅Cr₀.₂₅ alloy had a strong corrosion resistance. It could be seen from the Fig. 3 that only the $ID$ was corroded and the $DR$ was not corroded.

It was found that Cr together with Fe and Ni were mainly segregated in the DR to form the FCC1 phase in the 5%Cr and 10%Cr alloys, and Cu element coupled with Mn were mainly segregated in the ID to form the FCC2 phase. When the Cr content reached to 25%, BCC phase appeared. Moreover, the DR was composed of FCC1 and BCC phases, and the ID contained the FCC2 phase. However, it should be noted that the area of Cu segregation was reduced with the increasing Cr as the distribution of Cu became more uniform in Fig. 4d-f. In addition, with the increasing Cr, the relative content of Cu also decreased and resulted in the improvement of the corrosion performance. The segregation of Cu caused the formation of Cu-rich and Cr-depleted region, at the same time caused the enrichment of Cr element and forming Cr-rich and Cu-depleted region, which constituted a significant potential difference and formed galvanic corrosion. It usually existed in the form of Cu⁺ in the solution during the oxidation process of Cu. Many Cu⁺s piled up to play two roles. On the one hand, Cu⁺s could effectively reduce the critical current density in the active dissolution process and played a role in reducing the corrosion tendency. On the other hand, Cu⁺s accelerated the pitting corrosion rate. Pitting corrosion, coupled with small anode and large cathode, resulted in the considerable anodic dissolution rate of the etched holes, thereby accelerating the intergranular corrosion. In addition, the formation of a large number of defects during the solidification at grain boundary accumulated a large amount of strain energy, making the grain boundary unstable. Once the corrosion channel formed at the grain boundary, the dendrites were preferentially corroded.

Ni/Cr-contained protective film tended to form if the content of Ni/Cr was high. The formation of the protective film could prevent the alloy interior from getting in touch with the acid solution and restrained the continued attack from the solution during anodic polarization [23-25]. It could be seen from Fig. 5 that the volume fraction of each phase in different alloys changed with the increasing Cr. The volume fraction of Cr-rich FCC1 phase decreased while that of the Cr-rich BCC phase increased as the Cr content increased. However, the volume fraction of Cu-rich FCC2 phase increased firstly and then decreased when the Cr content is above 10%. It was well known that the Cr-rich FCC1 and BCC phases served as the cathode and the Cu-rich FCC2 phase was anode [17]. As the Cr content increased, the Cr-rich corrosion-resistant BCC phase appeared and the potential of the cathode (DR) increased [26]. ### 4.2 Effects of Cr content on the fracture behavior

The tensile fracture surfaces of as-cast 5%Cr, 10%Cr and 25%Cr alloys at room temperature were shown in Fig. 6.

There were many dimples on the surfaces of the alloys, indicating a good ductility which was consistent with the tensile data in Fig. 2. The characteristic mode of ductile fracture was not changed obviously as the Cr content increased from 5 to 10%, as shown in Fig. 6a and b. The enlarged morphologies in Fig. 6a and b were presented in Fig. 6d and e, respectively. Compared with the dimples in 10%Cr alloy (Fig. 6b and e), the distribution of those in 5%Cr alloy (Fig. 6a and d) was deeper and more uniform in size which gave rise to the improved ductility. However, it should be noticed that the fracture mode changed into a mixed mode when the Cr content increased to 25%. Together with many dimples, there were a small number of quasi-cleavage facets as shown in Fig. 6c marked by blue A and B. The enlarged view of the facet marked by A in Fig. 6c was presented in Fig. 6f. The chemical composition of the facets marked by A and B in Fig. 6c were shown in Table 4. It was interesting to note that the facets were enriched in Cr and Fe, which was consistent with the composition of BCC phase as shown in Table 3. Therefore, it could be deduced that the faceted fracture phase in 25%Cr alloy was the Cr/Fe-riched BCC phase.

Both the ultimate tensile strength and yield strength increased with the increasing of Cr content as shown in Fig. 2. The improved strength was obtained when the Cr content increased from 5 to 10% due to the solution strengthening or obstacle hardening related to aggregations of some elements as there were only FCC1 phase and FCC2 phase in those alloys [27]. Compared with the 5%Cr and 10%Cr alloys, there was BCC phase besides FCC1 and FCC2 phases in the 25%Cr alloy. Moreover, the microhardness of FCC phase was lower than that of BCC phase [6]. The appearance of BCC phase in the 25%Cr alloy, on the one hand, improved the strength as compared with 5%Cr and 10%Cr alloys because of the higher hardness of the BCC phase. On the other hand, the formation of BCC phase gave rise to a decreasing in ductility. This was consistent with the fact that there was a contradiction between strength and ductility. It was generally believed that the ductility of BCC phase was lower than that of FCC phase owing to the less slip direction. The facet of the fracture BCC phase appeared in Fig. 6c and f supported the above analysis.

In addition, according to the study of Qi and Chrzan [28], two aspects of material's ductility could be addressed during alloy design. One was so-called extrinsic features of the alloy, for example, grain size and precipitate distribution, etc. The extrinsic features could be modified to passivate and blunt cracks before they propagated and leaded to failure of the macroscopic part. The other was the intrinsic ductility of its perfect crystal. It was generally believed that the local shear stress on the slip plane for the dislocation nucleation should be near the corresponding ideal shear strength, while the local tensile stress for the crack initiation on the direction perpendicular to the cleavage plane should be larger than the ideal tensile strength. Further research had proved that decreasing VEC by alloying helped to improve the intrinsic ductility because the critical strain of shear instability and the corresponding intrinsic failure mode could be adjusted by alloying, which could shift the Fermi level [28, 29]. The above-mentioned theory had been supported by the experiments that are carried out on the Nb/Hf/Mo-containing HEAs [29-31]. However, the value of VEC in this paper decreased with the increasing of Cr content, while the ductility decreased. Although it seemed contradiction between previous investigations and this work, it was worth noting that only BCC solid solution was obtained in those alloys and mixed microstructure (FCC1+FCC2)/(FCC1+FCC2+BCC) phases were found in this study. Therefore, the extrinsic features seemed played a key role in the fracture behavior as compared with the intrinsic features in this investigation.

## 5 Conclusions
The corrosion behavior of as-cast $(CuFeMnNi)_{1-x}Cr_x$ alloys was investigated by means of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements and potentiodynamic polarization measurements which conducted in $2\ wt\% \ HNO_3$ solution at room temperature. The $(CuFeMnNi)_{0.75}Cr_{0.25}$ alloy displayed a better corrosion resistance, however, the CuFeMnNi alloy without Cr exhibited the worst corrosion resistance in nitric acid solution, indicating the corrosion resistance was enhanced with the increasing Cr content. Moreover, the strength properties for the investigated alloys increased while the elongation-to-fracture decreased as Cr increased. Furthermore, the empirical prediction on the phase formation of the as-cast $(CuFeNiMn)_{1-x}Cr_x$ alloys agreed with the previous experimental results.
