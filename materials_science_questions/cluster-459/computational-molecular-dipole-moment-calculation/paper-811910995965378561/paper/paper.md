ABSTRACT: Atomic multipoles from the Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules
(QTAIM), up to quadrupoles, and CHELPG charges are employed in the description of
electrostatic properties of some small linear molecules: $H_2$, HF, HCl, HBr, HCN, HNC, and
CO. A proton is placed on the molecular axis in distances within the range 3–8 Å from the
terminal atoms. The polarization of molecular electronic densities induced by the proton is
readily included by the calculation of atomic multipoles in each proton-molecule
arrangement. Electrostatic potentials at the proton position as given by QTAIM multipoles
are always in better agreement with the reference results, calculated directly at the
B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) level, than those predicted with CHELPG charges, particularly for
cases in which QTAIM multipoles showed that the contribution of atomic charges to these
potentials is not clearly predominant over other contributions as in $H_2$, HCl, HBr, HNC,
and CO. The lone pair at the carbon atoms in CO and HNC, as described by QTAIM
atomic dipoles, is indispensable in studies of electrostatic properties of these molecules.
Moreover, QTAIM multipoles are also able to describe properly the large polarizations
induced by the proton along the proton-molecule distances studied.

## Introduction
The Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules
(QTAIM) [1, 2] presents a unique way of par-
titioning regions of a molecule that belong to any
of its atoms, clearly defining the limits of atoms in
molecules. In this way, a region of space associated
to an atom is called a basin. This procedure is carried
out through a topological analysis of the observ-
able three-dimensional molecular electronic density,
searching for zero-flux surfaces that are observed in
a map of the gradient vector field of the electronic
density. In this context, after the determination of
zero-flux surfaces that divide atomic basins,different
atomic properties are accessible and, among all them,

atomic multipoles can be highlighted. For instance, the atomic charge is given as a difference between the atomic number of the atom considered and the electronic population obtained by integrating the electron density belonging to that basin. A basic char- acteristic of QTAIM is that molecular properties can be given as sums of atomic quantities [3] such as the molecular dipole moment that is composed of two sums: one with respect to atomic charges and another for atomic dipoles. Experimental molecu- lar dipole moment values are readily reproduced with QTAIM [3, 4]. Moreover, QTAIM quantities are shown to be highly transferable among molecular systems [5]. However, despite the access to differ- ent atomic multipoles from QTAIM [1, 2, 6], a large number of studies emphasizes only on the use of atomic charges. Although this way of dealing with common problems involving molecular systems should be correct, the results are often incomplete due to the neglect of other important electrostatic contributions.

An example of property that depends on contribu- tions of higher-order atomic multipoles is the force exerted on atoms of a molecule by the presence of an external charge [4, 7–9]. These forces can be pre- dicted by QTAIM multipoles [4] as well as by atomic multipoles based on derivatives of molecular electric moments [7–9]. Another example is given by infrared fundamental intensities [4, 10–12], which result from charge, charge flux, and dipole flux atomic con- tributions (CCFDF model) to the molecular dipole moment derivative of each normal mode of vibration [3, 4]. Hence, as an atom or a group of atoms vibrates it causes a deformation in the molecular electronic density, deformation that can be quantified by these atomic multipoles and their fluxes as calculated by QTAIM. The CCFDF model is based only on clas- sically interpretable contributions from atoms in contrast to the old charge - charge flux - overlap (CCFO) model [13]. Finally, QTAIM multipoles usu- ally show a fast convergence in the determination of electrostatic potentials around molecules as noticed by Kosov and Popelier [14], in order that the inclu- sion of a few different low-order atomic multipoles is generally enough to a proper description of this property.

Another current method to obtain atomic charges in molecules is given by the CHELPG formalism [15]. In this approach, atomic charges are adjusted in order to reproduce the electrostatic potentials calculated in a grid of points selected around the molecule. The CHELPG model furnishes values capable to reproduce molecular dipole moments in some extent though it can lead to charges in dis- agreement with chemical intuition based on simple electronegativity arguments, as seen in the case of the fluorochloromethanes [16]. An improvement to this simple model, that is not commonly used, is the addition of atomic dipoles, which can also be deter- mined along with the charges during the adjust- ment process of electrostatic potentials. However, this procedure can still lead to unrealistic atomic quantities because there are no guaranties that mul- tipole expansions will converge at this level. More- over, there are also difficulties in obtaining CHELPG charges for inner atoms in large molecules. Despite these limitations, electrostatic potential charges as CHELPG are widely employed in the parametriza- tion of popular force fields [17, 18].

However, other alternatives have also been con- sidered to develop force fields recently as, for exam- ple, multicentered multipolar expansions [19–22]. Such multipolar expansions are used into the polar- izable molecular mechanics force field SIBFA [23], that has been used in the treatment of enzyme residues [24]. Other force fields that include multi- centered multipolar expansions are AMOEBA [25] and NEMO [26]. This fact illustrates that the interest in precise ways of predicting electrostatic interac- tions involving molecular systems is considerable.

The objectives pursued here are the analysis and comparison of electrostatic properties of some small linear molecules ($\mathrm{H_2}$, HF, HCl, HBr, HCN, HNC, and CO) as predicted by QTAIM multipoles (charges, dipoles, and quadrupoles) and by CHELPG charges. Hence, electrostatic potentials at the proton position are investigated in proton-molecule arrangements in which a proton is located at points along the molecular axis in distances ranging from 3 to $8\ \mathring{\mathrm{A}}$ from the terminal atoms. Polarization effects caused by the proton on molecular electronic densities are easily included by the recalculation of atomic multi- poles for each proton-molecule arrangement. More- over, these polarization effects are also studied and characterized.

## Results and Discussion

### ATOMIC MULTIPOLES

Because a large number of results in this work depends on vectorial quantities, as dipole moments, the orientation chosen for the molecules is firstly presented. Hence, it was decided to orient all these molecules along the Cartesian $z$ axis, placing hydrogen in the negative side for almost all the molecules except CO, in which the carbon atom is at this position.

Molecular equilibrium geometries and dipole moments calculated in this work with the B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) approach, Table I, are in good agreement with experimental results [29], deviating by $0.01$ Å and $0.18$ D at most. The largest deviation of molecular moments is observed for HF and excluding this molecule the deviation is lowered to only $0.06$ D. In this table, one can also find the molecular dipole moments calculated by means of atomic charges and atomic dipoles obtained at the B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) level from QTAIM, as presented in Table II. The $z$ component of a molecular dipole moment ($\mu_z$) is given as

$$
\mu_z = \sum q_i z_i + \sum m_{z,i}, \tag{1}
$$

where the first sum is the atomic charge ($q_i$) contribution and the second sum is the atomic dipole moment ($m_{z,i}$) contribution (both sums go over all atoms $i$ in the molecule). These sums (labeled as $c$ for charge and $d$ for dipole contributions) and the total molecular dipole moments from QTAIM are observed in Table I. These molecular dipole moments are in satisfactory accordance with both experimental [29] or directly calculated B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) values, also in Table I. It should also be noticed that the direction of the molecular dipole moments in HNC and CO is a result from the sums involving the large atomic dipole moment components ($-5.76$ and $6.23$ D, respectively for HNC and CO) surpassing the atomic charge contributions ($2.69$ and $-6.08$ D, respectively, for HNC and CO) to these molecular quantities. However, even for the other molecules studied in which charge contributions are dominant, atomic dipole moment contributions are comparatively quite large and cannot be neglected. It is interesting to observe that HBr is the only molecule in which both atomic charge ($-0.50$ D) and atomic dipole ($-0.35$ D) contributions have the same sign, what may be a result from the smallest QTAIM charges found here in Table II (except of course for $\text{H}_2$) that are unable to cause large polarizations of atomic basins. The molecular dipole moments obtained from CHELPG charges are also presented in Table I. The agreement of values derived from CHELPG with the reference B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) ones is always worse than those given by QTAIM quantities, except for HF. Of course, molecular dipole moments given by QTAIM should be exact, except by numerical limitations. For HF, small numerical inaccuracies in QTAIM quantities given by Gaussian 98 seem to be the reason for the observed deviation in its dipole moment but these inaccuracies are not large enough to compromise the following investigations in this molecule.

Table II shows the QTAIM multipoles determined at the B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) level for the representative atoms in all molecules. It is easy to verify that hydrogen atomic charges follow the electronegativity tendency when this atom is bonded to halogens, reflecting the ability of halogens to withdraw electronic charge. One can also see that, despite the large variation of hydrogen atomic charges from a molecule to another, hydrogen atomic dipole moments are almost constants, with values ranging from $0.07$ to $0.12$ a.u., and have the same directions in all cases (pointing to the center of the molecules). On the other hand, focusing at atomic traceless quadrupole moments also in Table II, one can see that the largest values are observed for the largest halogen atoms, chlorine (3.8 a.u.) and bromine (5.5 a.u.). Surprisingly, the carbon atom in HCN and the nitrogen atom in HNC also show large atomic quadrupole moments (respectively, 3.0 and 1.5 a.u.) while in all the remaining cases the largest value is only 0.8 a.u. These central atoms in such linear triatomic systems are involved in multiple bonds and the quadrupole moments are probably reflecting differences between $\sigma$ and $\pi$ electronic populations [30]. Moreover, quadrupole moments of hydrogen seem to be negatively correlated to its atomic charge, which may be explained by a decrease in hydrogen basin sizes as this atom becomes more positively charged.

Table II also contains the CHELPG charges. The qualitative agreement of CHELPG charges with those obtained from QTAIM is satisfactory for HF, HCl, HBr, and HCN. But, even in HCN both formalisms disagree in which atom is predicted to be more positively charged: carbon for QTAIM and hydrogen for CHELPG. However, the charge values are quite different in CO and HNC: (1) CHELPG charges in carbon monoxide are almost zero contrasting with the large QTAIM ones; (2) CHELPG gives a negative carbon charge and an almost zero but positive nitrogen charge in HNC. Both these CHELPG results are unlikely based on simple electronegativity arguments. The electronegativity difference between carbon and oxygen is large and this should lead to significant atomic charges in CO. Moreover, nitrogen is the most electronegative atom in HNC and should acquire a negative charge in this molecule. Finally, some criticisms have been raised because of the large QTAIM charge values obtained in certain cases, but they are rebutted by Bader and Matta [5] and the carbon monoxide molecule is also discussed in details. Of course, CO is a peculiar molecule and its dipole moment was already pointed to result from two large and opposite contributions in textbooks, one from net charges (with a negatively charged oxygen atom that is in line with electronegativity arguments) and another dominated by the lone pair on carbon [31].

## ELECTROSTATIC PROPERTIES OF MOLECULES

The main objective of this work is a study of electrostatic properties of the molecules analyzed here in presence of a proton. The proton-molecule arrangements selected to investigation were those in which a proton is located on the molecular axis, but with distances to the terminal atoms ranging from 3 to 8 Å. Two distinct situations are considered because there are two different terminal atoms in each linear molecule (except $\text{H}_2$). Of course, the presence of a proton within this distance range will cause a large polarization of molecular electronic densities, but this polarization can be easily included in our analysis by the determination of atomic multipoles in all proton-molecule arrangements.

![](./images/811910995965378561_1.jpg)

FIGURE 1. Electrostatic potential at the proton position in the presence of $\text{H}_2$ along proton-terminal atom distances as given by QTAIM atomic multipoles ($\triangle$), CHELPG charges (+), and directly by B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) calculations ($\bullet$).

The electrostatic potential ($V_p$) at the proton position in proton-molecule arrangements, truncated up to the atomic quadrupole contribution, is given by [32, 33]

$$
V_{p}=\sum \frac{q_{i}}{r_{i, p}}+\sum \frac{\left(\mathbf{m}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{i, p}\right)}{r_{i, p}^{2}}+\sum \frac{\left(\mathbf{r}_{i, p} \cdot \mathbf{Q}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{i, p}\right)}{2 r_{i, p}^{3}}, \text{ (2)}
$$

where $r_{i,p}$ is the distance between atom $i$ and the proton, $\mathbf{r}_{i,p}$ is the unitary vector pointing from atom $i$ to the proton, $\mathbf{m}_{i}$ is the atomic dipole moment vector, and $\mathbf{Q}_{i}$ is the atomic traceless quadrupole moment matrix. All these atomic multipoles are accessible within the QTAIM formalism, whereas only the first sum is evaluated by CHELPG charges. For comparison, electrostatic potentials are also calculated directly with the B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) approach at the position occupied by the proton. Figures 1–7 show all these electrostatic potentials for the molecules treated here along the distance range.

CHELPG would predict null electrostatic potentials because atomic charges for isolated H₂ are zero (see Table II). On the other hand, QTAIM quantities as atomic dipole and quadrupole moments would give origin to non-zero electrostatic potentials but with incorrect values. However, the polarization due to a proton promotes a redistribution of electronic charge along the molecule resulting in a reorganization of the molecular electron density that modifies all the QTAIM and CHELPG quantities. It is easy to notice in Figure 1 that QTAIM multipoles predict electrostatic potentials in much better agreement with the reference B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) results than those potentials obtained with CHELPG, particularly for smaller proton-molecule distances. Moreover, atomic charge, dipole, and quadrupole contributions from QTAIM (see the three sums in eq. 2) show similar magnitudes in the determination of these potentials along the distance range, as can be seen in terms of relative values in Table III. It can be observed in this table that both atomic charge and dipole contributions are negative whereas the quadrupole one is positive but not as large as the sum of the first two, resulting in negative total potentials. This indicates that a proton is stabilized when it is located at these positions.

The next molecule, HF, is a case in which both atomic formalisms, QTAIM and CHELPG, predict almost the same pattern of electrostatic potentials along the entire distance range, Figure 2, although their atomic charge estimates are quite different. # POLARIZATION EFFECTS INDUCED BY A PROTON ON ATOMIC MULTIPOLES

The change in QTAIM quantities of hydrogen along the proton-molecule distance range, studied for arrangements in which a proton is closer to the hydrogen end, is used for a simple analysis of polarization effects caused by the proton. Figure 8 shows the ratio between hydrogen charges under influence of a proton (at different proton-hydrogen distances) and the hydrogen charge for isolated molecules as given by QTAIM. It can be noticed in this figure that the hydrogen charge becomes less positive when a proton is closer to this atom. Hence, an intramolecular charge flux is induced by the proton, with electronic charge being accumulated on hydrogen. Moreover, hydrogen bromide is the most affected molecule by this polarization, result- ing even in a sign change for the hydrogen charge in the smallest proton-hydrogen distance. Further, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen cyanide are also significantly polarized by the proton, with hydrogen charge variations of roughly 40 to 60 % for the small- est proton-hydrogen distance. On the other hand, hydrogen fluoride is the less sensitive molecule to such polarization, with a charge variation smaller than 10 %. Hence, the order observed for the polar- ization influence on the hydrogen charge given by QTAIM is: HBr > HCN > HCl > HNC > HF. It can also be mentioned that CHELPG charges work well in predicting the electrostatic potentials in HCN, that is significantly polarized by the proton, what seems to point that CHELPG charges are able to deal with the polarization effect in this molecule.

## Conclusions

As expected, QTAIM multipoles are better in
reproducing the electrostatic potentials at the proton
position in proton-molecule systems than CHELPG
charges for all the molecules studied in this work,
with large differences between both formalisms for
H₂, HCl, HBr, HNC, and CO. However, CHELPG is
comparable to QTAIM for HF and HCN. The per-
centual deviation observed for electrostatic poten-
tials calculated with QTAIM multipoles in compari-
son with the reference B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) values
is generally below 10% in the smallest proton-
molecule distance. The exceptions are noticed when
a proton is near hydrogen in HCl (14%) and in
HBr (38%) and when a proton is near oxygen
in CO (17%). Hence, higher-order atomic multi-
poles, not considered here, are expected to explain
these deviations with QTAIM. The relative contri-
butions from these higher-order multipoles in HCl
and HBr are probably significant because of large
quadrupole contributions shown in Table III for
these molecules. Moreover, the relative contribu-
tions of higher-order atomic multipoles for CO are
not expected to be large but, as the contributions
from charge and dipole almost cancel each other,
the percentual error observed was above 10%. How-
ever, the convergence of the electrostatic potential
expansion in terms of QTAIM multipoles is very
satisfactory as can be observed in the overall per-
formance of such multipoles truncated at the first
three terms (up to atomic quadrupoles). On the other
hand, the percentual deviations observed for electro-
static potentials obtained with CHELPG charges in
comparison with the B3LYP/6-311G(3d,3p) data are
almost always much larger than 10% in the smallest
proton-molecule distance, except for HF and HCN.
The worst percentual deviations are observed for
H₂ (37%), HCl (25 and 44%), and CO (37 and 60%).
Moreover, CHELPG charges even predict electro-
static potentials in qualitative disagreement when a
proton is in the closest approach to the hydrogen end
in HBr (negative instead of positive values).

Hence, this work shows that contributions of
higher-order atomic multipoles beyond charges are
required to deal with the electrostatic properties
of molecules even in apparently simple systems as
those treated here. Such multipoles introduce correc-
tions that take into account anisotropies of atoms in
molecules because these atoms are deformed from
spherical distributions of electronic charge during
the formation of chemical bonds. In this way, QTAIM
multipoles present advantages that indicate their use
in the development of accurate force fields. However,
other effects as charge overlap between monomers'
electron clouds should also be taken into account
during the development of force fields [34].

The need for higher-order atomic multipoles in
electrostatic studies of molecules can be assessed by
QTAIM. Molecules for which CHELPG charges give
comparable electrostatic potentials to QTAIM multi-
poles, HF and HCN, are cases where QTAIM shows
that the atomic charge contribution is clearly dom-
inant over the other atomic contributions, dipoles
and quadrupoles. It can be noticed that H₂, HCl,
