PHYSICAL REVIEW B 75, 113404 (2007)

# Electronic structures of the self-assembled monolayer of molecules of symmetric disulfides of benzoic acid

Y.-H. Tang and M.-H. Tsai

Department of Physics, National Sun Yat-Sen University, Kaohsiung, 80424 Taiwan

(Received 5 May 2006; revised manuscript received 28 November 2006; published 22 March 2007)

The first-principles calculation method has been used to obtain electronic structures of self-assembled monolayer of molecules of symmetric disulfides of benzoic acid with functional elements of H, F, and Br. It is found that these functional elements strongly alter the dipole moment of the molecule depending on their electronegativity and sizes. The molecular dipole moments and energy gaps are found to have nonlinear response to the high applied electric field typically used in nanoscience and nanotechnology applications.

DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.75.113404

PACS number(s): 81.16.Dn, 31.15.Ar

## I. INTRODUCTION

The self-assembled monolayer (SAM) of biomolecules has a nonvanishing dipole moment. The absorption of the SAM of biomolecules on semiconductors and ferroelectrics can modify their transport and dielectric properties, respectively. The former basically utilizes the controllable and predicable dipolar property of the organic molecules by the functional group to tune the potential barrier at the SAM/semiconductor interface and the current in the conduction channel in the semiconductor. The latter is potentially applicable as a biosensor for detecting bacterial or viral particles, because the interaction of these bacterial particles with their corresponding antibodies can induce a change in the dielectric property of the ferroelectrics. The SAM of molecules are also potentially applicable in molecular electronics, $^{1-3}$ optoelectronic devices, $^{4,5}$ and the semiconductor based sensor. $^{6}$

In comparison with the energy bands of semiconductor and metal films, the energy bands of the SAM's are relatively flat, so that its transport properties are different from those of semiconductors and metals. On the other hand, the electronic structures of the SAM's are different from those of a single molecule, in which the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels are well defined. Couplings between molecules in the SAM are expected to disperse or broaden the LUMO and HOMO bands, which will alter the electronic property of the molecular electronic structures. Thus, it is interesting to understand the effect of couplings between molecules in the SAM. In addition, the understanding of the dipolar property is essential for the application of SAM/semiconductor and SAM/ferroelectrics systems.

Recently, the electronic transport properties of metal-molecule-metal systems have attracted intensive theoretical investigations $^{7-14}$ by using the transmission probability/Green function or nonequilibrium Green function approach. Earlier works assumed that the potential drop is dominantly present at the molecule-electrode interfaces and the molecular states can be treated as fixed when the applied voltage is less than about $1\ \text{eV}.^{7-12}$ However, recently, Ke, Baranger, and Yang $^{14}$ showed that the most part of the potential drop is across the molecule. In a separate calculation for the Au-film-benzene-1,4-dithiol-molecule-Au film system, the potential drop is also found across the molecule $^{15}$ in agreement with the calculation of Ke, Baranger and Yang. Thus, the neglect of the response of the electronic states to the external field may not be adequate, which has been recognized and considered by Brandbyge et al. $^{13}$ and Ke, Baranger, and Yang $^{14}$ in their self-consistent first-principles calculations.

In this study, the symmetric disulfides of benzoic acids with various functional elements $X$ as shown in Fig. 1, are chosen as the biomolecules in the SAM as an example. The reason for the choice of this molecule is that the absorption of the disulfide ligand on the conduction surface [i.e., Au(111) (Ref. 16)] can control the surface potential and that the C-C bridge between their two arms can prevent chain segregation.

## II. MOLECULAR STRUCTURES AND THE CALCULATION METHOD

In this study, the functional element $X$ of symmetric disulfides of benzoic acid is chosen to be H, F, and Br, and the optimized positions of atoms in the molecule for the three cases are obtained from the CHEMOFFICE 2002 software in the energy minimization application until the magnitudes of the forces are less than $0.1\ \text{eV}/\mathring{\text{A}}$, which are shown in Fig. 1(b) for $X{=}\text{H}$. The two H atoms bonded to the two S atoms are used to represent the semiconductor or ferroelectric substrate surface atoms bonded to the two S atoms. When the two S atoms are bonded to substrate surface atoms, they are no longer bonded to each other as depicted by Haick et al. $^{17}$

method implemented with the local density approximation (LDA) of Hedin-Lundqvist formulation²⁰ and the linear theory of Andersen²¹ is used to obtain the electronic structures. The pseudofunctions are smooth mathematical functions, which have the same spherical Hankel and Neumann tailing functions in the interstitial region as the basis wave functions. These pseudofunctions can be expanded by a minimal set of plane waves, which enables the calculation of the interstitial and nonspherical parts of the Hamiltonian matrix elements efficiently using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) technique. For the calculation of these SAM's of molecules, the PSF method uses the single-slab structural models, which is different from the infinitely repeated-slab supercell model used in the pseudopotential method. The Coulomb potential along the z axis is calculated by solving the Poisson equation numerically.

The constant external electric field, $E$, is perpendicular to the plane of SAM, i.e., along the $z$ axis. In the current PSF formulation, the electric potential function of $z$ due to $E$ is expanded in plane waves with a periodicity of $2d$ with $d$ sufficiently larger than $b$, which is one half of the film thickness. To improve the convergence of the plane wave expansion, the electric potential is extended between $-d$ and $-b$ and between $b$ and $d$ by a linear combination of smooth sine and cosine functions, such that the resultant function and its first derivative are continuous at $-b$ and $b$ and function values are equal at $-d$ and $d$. It has been tested that the linear external potential can be accurately expanded by plane waves. Note that this linear potential is an external potential such as the potential due to the positive and negative charges in the two electrodes of a capacitor with the SAM lying between the two electrodes. The total potential seen by an electron in the molecules is the sum of this external potential and the Kohn-Sham effective potential, which includes Coulomb or Hartree potential due to ion cores and valence electrons and the exchange-correlation potential within the LDA. The Kohn-Sham potential depends on the linear external potential through self consistent response or rearrangement of electrons to this external potential.

### III. CALCULATED ELECTRONIC PROPERTIES

The calculated energy bands of the SAM's with the three functional elements $X$=H, F, and Br with a periodicity of 7.81 Å are shown in Figs. 2(a)-2(c), respectively. The zero energy is chosen to be the Fermi level ($E_F$), i.e., the highest occupied energy level. The $\Gamma$, $X$, and $M$ $k$ points represent the Brillouin zone center $(\frac{\pi}{a},0)$ and $(\frac{\pi}{a},\frac{\pi}{a})$, respectively, where $a$ is the periodicity of the molecular lattice in the $x$-$y$ plane. For all three cases, the lowest two unoccupied bands near $E_F$ are mainly composed of S $3p$ orbitals, and the two top occupied bands near $E_F$ are mainly composed of $C{=}O$ bond orbitals. The energy gaps are 1.04, 0.84, and 0.97 eV for $X$=H, F, and Br, respectively. Since the energy bands near $E_F$ are not dominated by the orbitals of the functional elements, the energy gaps do not have large deviations for the functional elements with drastically different electronegativities and sizes.

In the following, the discussion will be divided into two parts. One is the effect of intermolecular couplings. Another is the response of the SAM of molecules to an external electric field.

#### A. Dependence of the electronic property on $a$ or intermolecular separation

With a larger periodicity of 11.7 Å, i.e., a larger intermolecular separation, the energy gaps are increased to 1.32, 1.29, and 1.43 eV for $X$=H, F, and Br, respectively, and dispersion of energy bands are reduced as expected. The calculated net dipole moments per molecule with respective to the Pauline electronegativities of H, Br, and F are shown in Fig. 3 for both $a$=7.81 and 11.7 Å cases. The black and grey lines are for $a$=7.81 and 11.7 Å, respectively. Here, a positive dipole moment is defined as pointing in the +$z$ direction. Because H has a smaller electronegativity than that of C, the dipole moment associated with the C-H bond points in the +$z$

On the contrary, F and Br have larger electronegativities than that of C, the dipole moments associated with the C-F and C-Br bonds point in the $-z$ direction, so that the dipole moment per molecule, which includes contributions from the C-$X$ bond and the rest of the molecule, for the $X$=H case is much larger than those of $X$=F and Br cases for $a$=11.7 Å. It is interesting to note that the dipole moment per molecule for $X$=Br is smaller than that for $X$=F, although Br has a smaller electronegativity, which indicates the size of the functional element also affect the dipole moment, since the dipole moment is proportional to the distance between C and $X$. (The electronegativities of H, C, Br, and F are 2.20, 2.55, 2.96, and 3.98,²² respectively, and the covalent radii of Br and F are 1.14 and 0.72 Å,²² respectively.) When the separation between molecules is decreased to 7.81 Å, the dipole moments per molecule for all three cases are decreased, which indicates that intermolecular couplings reduce the net dipole moment of the molecule. In other words, the dipole moment of the molecule and its effect on the electronic or dielectric property of the substrate are sensitive to the density of the SAM, which unfortunately is unavailable to our knowledge. However, the effect of the functional element $X$ has been confirmed to alter the current-voltage ($I$-$V$) curve significantly.¹·²

### B. Dependence on the external electric field

The calculated dipole moments per molecule for the SAM for $a$=7.81 and 11.7 Å with functional elements $X$=H and F as functions of the external electric field, $E$, in unit of V/Å are shown in Fig. 4. Note that the positive external electric field is defined from the $X$ end to the $S$ end, i.e., in the $-z$ direction. The dipole moment per molecule can be approximately described as $p(E)\cong p_0+\alpha E+\beta E^2$. For $X$=H, $p_0$=4.22 D and $\alpha$=$-1.36×10^{-6}$ D cm/V for $a$=7.81 Å and $p_0$=8.13 D and $\alpha$=$-1.82×10^{-6}$ D cm/V for $a$=11.7 Å. For $X$=F, $p_0$=0.86 D and $\alpha$=$-1.37×10^{-6}$ D cm/V for $a$=7.81 Å and $p_0$=2.44 D and $\alpha$=$-1.90×10^{-6}$ D cm/V with for $a$=11.7 Å, respectively. The $\alpha$'s are nearly the same for both $x$=H and F with the same intermolecular separation. The larger $p_0$ for $x$=H is due to its smaller electronegativity as described above. Both $p_0$ and $\alpha$ are reduced for both $x$=H and F as the intermolecular separation is decreased due to increased intermolecular couplings. The nonlinear coefficients $\beta$'s are $-1.35×10^{-14}$ and $-4.43×10^{-14}$ D cm²/V² for $x$=H and F with $a$=7.81 Å, respectively, and $-1.69×10^{-15}$ and $-2.68×10^{-14}$ D cm²/V² for $x$=H and F with $a$=11.7 Å, respectively. The increase of the absolute value of the nonlinear coefficient $|\beta|$ with the reduction of the intermolecular separation results from the enhancement of the intermolecular couplings.

The calculated energy gaps for $a$=7.81 Å with functional elements $X$=H and F as functions of $E$ in unit of V/Å are shown in Fig. 5. For both $x$=H and F, the energy gaps decrease monotonically as the external electric field is increased. The effective charges of the atoms in the molecule are also found to vary monotonically with the external electric field. These results suggest that the conventional approaches for calculating the electric current of a molecular junction, which assume constant molecular electronic states, may not be adequate.

The energy gaps between HOMO and LUMO bands are found to be sensitive to the external elec- tric field, which suggests that the response of the molecular electronic states to the external field should be taken into account in the calculation of the electric current of a molecu- lar junction.
