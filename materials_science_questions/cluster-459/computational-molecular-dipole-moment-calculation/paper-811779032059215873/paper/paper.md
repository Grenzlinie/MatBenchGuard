## A B S T R A C T

(4Z)-4-(4-N,N-dimethylaminobenzylidene)-1-methyl-2-phenyl-1,4-dihydro-5H-imidazolin-5-one (DMPI), an analog of the green fluorescent protein (GFP) luminophore has been investigated by steady state absorption and emission spectroscopic studies. The polarity and viscosity dependent fluorescence solvatochromic studies along with time dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations support a molecular rotor property of DMPI and not the 'hula-twist mechanism'. The results suggest that the protein environment around the GFP luminophore could be playing a similar role by increasing the viscosity around the luminophore leading to the enhanced quantum yield of GFP.

### 1. Introduction

(4Z)-4-(4-N,N-Dimethylaminobenzylidene)-1-methyl-2-phenyl-1,4-dihydro-5H-imidazolin-5-one (DMPI) is an analog of the green fluorescent protein (GFP) [1] luminophore (Fig. 1) that has recently found application in the fabrication of organic solar cells [2,3]. The GFP luminophore is formed via a post-translational inter- nal cyclization of the $Ser^{65}-Tyr^{66}-Gly^{67}$ tripeptide followed by $\alpha,\beta$ – dehydrogenation of the tyrosine in the protein without the aid of any cofactor or substrates other than oxygen [4,5]. Moreover, genetically fusing the DNA sequence of GFP with another protein of interest does not seem to affect the function and fluorescence of this protein. Furthermore, the amazing ability of wild type GFP to emit efficiently intense visible green light makes it tremen- dously valuable for monitoring expression, location and transloca- tion of proteins within cells [6,7], in gene expression studies [8,9] and in fluorescence microscopy [10]. The wide applicability of GFP and its mutants [11] inspired us to synthesize several analogs of GFP luminophore with an intention to evaluate their potential as synthetic extrinsic fluorophores.

Intramolecular twisting is a very common excited state phe- nomenon, especially when the molecules consist of an electron do- nor and acceptor pair linked by a single bond. In polar solvents, such a molecule on excitation, undergoes a fast intramolecular electron transfer from donor to acceptor, followed by intramolecu- lar twist about the single bond to produce a relaxed perpendicular structure and emits dual fluorescence. The higher energy emission band was called normal fluorescence or locally excited fluores- cence (LE) and the lower energy emission band was assigned as a 'twisted intramolecular charge transfer' (TICT) band by Grabowski et al. [12,13]. 4-Dimethylamino benzonitrile (DMABN) and its ana- logs [14] exhibit dual fluorescence in polar solvents due to the phe- nomenon of TICT. DMPI is structurally analogous to DMABN except that the electron withdrawing nitrile functional group in DMABN is replaced by the electron withdrawing imidazolin-5-one ring bear- ing an exocyclic C4–C7 double bond (Fig. 1). While DMABN emits LE fluorescence in non-polar solvents and dual fluorescence in po- lar solvents DMPI displays only a locally excited emission band in all solvents, with vibrational splitting being observed in non-polar solvents (Fig. S1, Supporting information). The fluorescence emis- sion intensity gradually decreases with increasing polarity and hydrogen bonding ability of the solvents.

Most of the reported GFP luminophore analogs are very poorly fluorescent [15–17] relative to the native protein (wtGFP). A twisted excited state structure, familiarly known as the 'hula-twist' [18], has been proposed to be involved in the excited state dynam- ics of some GFP luminophore analogs [19,20]. The mechanism of the hula-twist involves a concerted movement of the dihedral angles, N3–C4–C7–C8 and C4–C7–C8–C9 (Fig. 1), where they both rotate simultaneously in order to conserve space. However, a hula- twist is an energetically uphill process [21] and it is observed in sterically constrained systems where the conventional single bond rotation is restricted [18]. The excited and relaxed twisted struc- ture undergoes ultrafast internal conversion through conical inter- section or avoided crossing of the ground state potential energy ($S_0$) surface with that of the first excited state ($S_1$). This non-radia- tive decay has been proposed to be responsible for the weak fluo- rescent nature of the analogs [15,19,20]. The non-radiative decay of the twisted excited state of the reported GFP luminophore ana- logs, thus account for the inefficient fluorescence quantum yields.

## 2. Experimental

### 2.1. Materials

Reagent grade glycine, anhydrous sodium acetate, acetic anhy- dride, anhydrous zinc chloride, 4-dimethylamino benzaldehyde and methylamine solution, HPLC grade cyclohexane, acetonitrile, ethylacetate, chloroform and dichloromethane were purchased from SDFine Chemicals, India. Acetic anhydride was distilled before use. Anhydrous sodium acetate and zinc chloride were fused to re- move moisture before use. Solvents ethyl acetate, petroleum ether (b.p. 60-80 °C) and methanol were distilled before use. Laboratory research grade toluene was purchased locally and purified by fol- lowing established procedure [23]. The dielectric constant and vis- cosity values of the solvents were taken from Ref. [24]. Glycerol was purchased from Qualigens Fine Chemicals, India and was used as such. The DMPI was synthesized according to previously pub- lished procedures by us [22]. All spectral work was performed in HPLC grade solvents.

### 2.2. Measurement

Absorption spectra were recorded in a Jasco V-550 UV-Visible spectrophotometer. The steady state fluorescence spectra were re- corded in Cary Eclipse (Varian) and FluoroLog 3 (FL-3.21, Horiba Jo- bin Yvon) spectrofluorimeters. For the solvatochromic studies, typically a $10\ \mu M$ solutions of DMPI were prepared in different sol- vents. The viscosity dependence of the fluorescence property of DMPI was studied in methanol-glycerol mixtures. The viscosities of the methanol-glycerol mixtures were calculated by using Eq. (1) [25]

$$
\ln \eta_{\text{mix}} = \sum_{i=1}^{2} w_{i} \cdot \ln \eta_{i} \tag{1}
$$

where $\eta_{i}$ is the viscosity of the individual component and $w_{i}$ is the weight component of the individual components ($0 < w_{i} < 1$). Fluo- rescence quantum yields of DMPI were measured in methanol- glycerol mixtures with respect to rhodamine 101 in ethanol.

## 3. Quantum mechanical calculations

Electronic structure calculations were carried out using GAUSSIAN 03 software package [26] using time dependent density functional theory (TDDFT). The molecular geometry was optimized by B3LYP/6-31g(d) method. TDDFT analysis was performed on the optimized geometry [27].

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Steady state absorption

Absorption spectra of DMPI in a series of solvents with increas- ing polarities are shown in Fig. 2. DMPI shows a single absorption band in the visible region which is increasingly red shifted with increasing polarity and hydrogen bonding ability of the solvents. In cyclohexane absorption maximum is observed at 435 nm. In acetonitrile the absorption maximum shifts to 450 nm. In metha- nol, there is further bathochromic shift of the absorption maxi- mum to 460 nm due to better stabilization of the excited state than the ground state through hydrogen bonding. The observed positive solvatochromic shift indicates that the dipole moment of the Frank-Condon excited state is larger than its ground state ($\mu'_{e} > \mu_{g}$, where $\mu'_{e}$ stands for dipole moment of the Frank-Condon excited state). The oscillator strength of the absorption band in cyclohexane is found to be 0.4.

### 4.2. Steady state emission

Emission spectra of DMPI in five solvents with similar viscosities ($\eta \sim 0.5$ mPa s) and varying polarities are shown in Fig. 3. It is readily observed that the emission spectra are appreciably red shifted on increasing the polarity of the solvents. The increased shift in emission maxima with increasing solvent polarities indicates increased dipole moment in the excited state. The absorption and emission characteristics of DMPI in different solvents are summarized in **Table 1**. The steady state absorption and emission characteristics of DMPI was measured as a behaviour of solvent polarity functions $f(\varepsilon, n)$ and $f(\varepsilon, n)+2g(n)$ ($\varepsilon$, dielectric constant; $n$, refractive index) [28]. As seen in table 1, there is a gradual increase of Stokes shift with increase in the solvent polarity function $f(\varepsilon, n)$, which indicates that $\Delta\mu(=\mu_e - \mu_g)$ is positive and small. Fig. 4 shows the correlations of the Stokes shift ($\bar{v}_a - \bar{v}_f$) and the sum of the absorption maxima and emission maxima ($\bar{v}_a + \bar{v}_f$) with the solvent polarity functions, $f(\varepsilon, n)$ and $f(\varepsilon, n)+2g(n)$, respectively, for the solvents listed in **Table 1**. The slopes and the correlation coefficients are $m_1 = 1545$ cm$^{-1}$, $R_1 = 0.98$ and $m_2 = -3788$ cm$^{-1}$, $R_2 = 0.89$. Protic solvents were not considered in order to exclude specific solvent effect and thereby non-linearity of the solvatochromic plots. The high correlation coefficients, as shown in Fig. 4, indicates a linear dependence of the Stokes shift ($\bar{v}_a - \bar{v}_f$) and sum of the absorption and emission maxima ($\bar{v}_a + \bar{v}_f$) on dielectric constant ($\varepsilon$) and refractive index ($n$) in the immediate environment of DMPI. The ground and excited dipole moment of DMPI was determined by using Eqs. (2) and (3) as suggested by Inamdar and coworkers [29]. For DMPI, the radius of the Onsager cavity '$a$' of DMPI was calculated to be half the distance between the dimethyl amine nitrogen atom and the carbonyl oxygen atom [30] because these two atoms provide the strongest vector of dipole moment

$$
\mu_{g}=\frac{m_{2}-m_{1}}{2}\left(\frac{h c a^{3}}{2 m_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \tag{2}
$$

$$
\mu_{e}=\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{2}\left(\frac{h c a^{3}}{2 m_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \tag{3}
$$

The radius of the Onsager cavity of DMPI was calculated to be 4.33 Å. Using the values of $m_1$ and $m_2$ from Fig. 4, Eqs. (2) and (3) gives the dipole moment of DMPI in the ground and excited state as $\mu_g = 2.56$ $D$ and $\mu_e = 6.09$ $D$, respectively. The change in dipole moment ($\Delta\mu = \mu_e - \mu_g$) was found to be 3.53 $D$. TDDFT calculations gave excited state dipole moment of 4.78 $D$ in vacuum. The reasonable agreement in the changes of dipole moment observed by experiments and also similar values of excited state dipole moments obtained by experiment and theoretical methods justify the assumption of the Onsager cavity radius of the DMPI molecule (4.33 Å). The small dipole moment change $\Delta\mu = 3.53$ $D$ and strong correlations of the spectral shifts with the solvent polarity functions of both polar and non-polar solvents clearly indicate that the excited state being dealt with, is a locally excited state (LE) of DMPI. TDDFT calculations suggest that in the ground state the dipole moment is oriented along the XY plane from the benzene ring towards the imidazolin-5-one ring. However in the excited state, there is a change in the orientation of the dipole moment and it now points from the dimethylamine group towards the imidazolin-5-one ring on the XY plane (Fig. S3, Supporting information).

A gradual loss of fluorescence intensity with increasing polarity may indicate the possible involvement of another highly polar excited state which probably does not emit. In polar solvents a highly polar excited state (e.g., charge transfer) is stabilized more than the LE state and formation of a highly polar non-emissive state can account for the gradual loss of fluorescence intensity of DMPI in solvents with increasing polarity. GFP luminophore analogs have been suggested to undergo an intramolecular twist in the excited state to a non-emissive state which accounts for the weak emissive nature of the analogs [15]. Supporting evidence of the involvement of a twisted non-emissive excited state can be obtained from the studies of influence of viscosity on the emission spectra of a fluorophore.

To study the effect of viscosity on the emission property of DMPI, a series of solutions of DMPI in methanol–glycerol mixtures with varying composition of the two solvents were prepared. The viscosities of the methanol–glycerol mixtures increase with increasing composition of glycerol as shown in table 2. Fluorescence emission spectra of DMPI in methanol and glycerol are shown in Fig. 5 and the measured quantum yields in the methanol–glycerol mixtures are shown in table 2. It is clearly seen from Fig. 5 that there is no appreciable shift of the emission maxima of DMPI on changing the solvent from methanol to glycerol, however fluorescence intensity and subsequently the quantum yields (see **Table 2**) increases appreciably with the viscosity of the solvent. Quantum yield of DMPI in glycerol is enhanced by about 160 times than that in methanol. The viscosity dependent quantum yield of DMPI strongly indicates that probably the twisting of DMPI in excited state is prevented, blocking the non-radiative deactivation channel of DMPI. The intramolecular rotation of the N3-C4-C7-C8 dihedral angle is proposed to be the key process for the rapid excited state relaxation in low viscous solvents. Glycerol being viscous, cannot provide the requisite 'free volume' [33,34,39] for torsional relaxa- tion of the DMPI molecule and hence quantum yield of DMPI in- creases enormously. Thus the formation of the twisted excited state is prevented in a viscous medium (e.g., glycerol), and DMPI predominantly emits from the LE state. This effect becomes more pronounced as the composition of glycerol increases in the meth- anol-glycerol mixtures, resulting in the increase in fluorescence intensity and quantum yield of DMPI. The higher dielectric con- stant of glycerol (47.2) than methanol (32.63) will not have the ef- fect of reducing fluorescence intensity because formation of the twisted excited state is not favored in methanol-glycerol mixtures with higher proportion of glycerol. The strong viscosity dependent fluorescence property of DMPI clearly suggests the reduction of torsional freedom in DMPI in viscous media. This is further sup- ported by the observation of strong fluorescence, by locking the dihedral freedom in several synthesized GFP chromophore model compounds, by Wu and Burgess [35]. The fluorescence property of DMPI is thus unambiguously viscosity dependent and it strongly suggests that DMPI is probably acting as molecular rotor [36]. The viscosity controlled fluorescence property of DMPI is analogous to the role of the protein matrix in GFP. In GFP, twisting motion of any type may be prevented by the protein matrix through the network of hydrogen bonds, thus blocking the non-radiative decay channel, leading to the enhanced fluorescence of GFP.

## 5. Discussion
The results of the solvatochromic studies, viscosity dependent emission studies and TDDFT calculations indicate that in polar solvents, DMPI undergoes twisted intramolecular charge transfer to form a more stable TICT state, than the LE state, which then undergoes non-radiative decay. Thus the excited state dynamics of DMPI can be explained by a simple schematic (Scheme 1). In polar solvents, the LE state of DMPI undergoes a fast charge transfer from the donor dimethylaminobenzene to the acceptor imidazolin-5-one ring and C2-substituted benzene ring followed by C7-C8 single bond rotation to form the TICT state. A polar solvent favors charge transfer by strong adiabatic coupling of the LE and the TICT state [37] and lowering the dipolar TICT state by solvation. The TICT state can undergo a radiative decay to the ground state resulting in the observation of a charge transfer band or dual fluorescence (e.g., dual fluorescence of DMABN). Alternatively, the TICT state can undergo non-radiative decay to the ground state, resulting in the observation of a single emission band (LE emission) with diminished intensity (e.g., emission from molecular rotor) [36,40]. Since fluorescence intensity of DMPI gradually decreases with increasing polarities of the solvents and there is only one emission band (Fig. 3) in all the polar solvents, it favorably indicates that probably the LE state equilibrates with a TICT state as shown in Scheme 1 and that the TICT state undergoes a non-radiative decay to the ground state [36,38]. This suggests that DMPI behaves like a 'molecular rotor' in polar and non-viscous solvents. In non-polar solvents, the formation of TICT is unfavored and DMPI decays radiatively from the LE state leading to enhanced fluorescence intensity. In viscous solvents, formation of the TICT state is prevented and DMPI predominantly decays radiatively from the LE state.

## 6. Conclusions
DMPI shows solvent dependent absorption and emission spectra. On increasing the solvent polarity the absorption and emission spectra undergo red shift (Fig. 3) with gradual decrease in fluorescence intensity. A perceptible change in dipole moment ($\Delta\mu = \mu_e - \mu_g$) and strong linear correlation of Stokes shift with solvent polarity functions clearly shows the evidence of emission from a locally excited (LE) state. Strong dependence of fluorescence quantum yield on viscosity indicates the evidence of involvement of a twisted excited state through a single bond rotation in polar solvents which undergoes a non-radiative decay. TDDFT calculation suggest charge transfer from the donor dimethylaminobenzene ring to the acceptor imidazolin-5-one and the C2-substituted benzene rings. Thus the excited state is a twisted intramolecular charge transfer (TICT) state which undergoes a non-radiative decay in polar solvents, resulting in very low quantum yields of DMPI. In non-polar solvents, formation of the TICT state is unfavored and DMPI decays radiatively from the locally excited state leading to a higher fluorescence intensity as compared to the polar solvents. The evidence of the involvement of a TICT state and strong viscosity dependent fluorescence suggests that DMPI would probably behave like a molecular rotor.
