# Dipole Moment Dictates the Preferential Immobilization in Gel Electrolytes for Ah-level Aqueous Zinc-Metal Batteries

Shaojie Zhang, Huaisheng Ao, Jingjing Dong, Dongdong Wang, Chenggang Wang, Xijin Xu, Zhiguo Hou,* and Jian Yang*

In memory of Prof. Yitai Qian

## Abstract
Aqueous Zn-metal batteries are of great interest due to their high material abundance, low production cost, and excellent safety. However, they suffer from severe side reactions and notorious dendrite growth closely related to electrolytes. Here, in situ generated zwitterionic polymers are used as gel electrolytes to overcome these problems. It is shown that anions and $\ce{H_{2}O}$, but not anions and cations, are preferentially immobilized at different sites of zwitterionic polymers, facilitating the free migration of $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ and reducing the side reactions. This immobilization can be associated with the dipole moment of zwitterionic polymers. As a result, poly[3-dimethyl(methacryloyloxy ethyl) ammonium propane sulfonate] (PDMAPS) stands out from a series of zwitterionic polymers and outperforms the other candidates in electrochemical performance. The symmetric cells using PDMAPS smoothly operate ~9000 h at 0.5 mA cm⁻² for 0.5 mAh cm⁻², much better than the controls. Moreover, PDMAPS enables an Ah-level pouch cell for continuous cycling. These results not only benefit the rational molecular design of advanced electrolytes, but also demonstrate the promising potential of zwitterionic polymers in aqueous Zn-metal batteries.

## Introduction
Aqueous zinc metal batteries (AZMBs) have attracted extensive interest in both academia and industry in recent years, $^{[1]}$ due to their advantages in material abundance, production cost, intrinsic safety and environmental friendliness. However, they also face some problems, such as irreversible structural transition and significant dissolution of cathode materials, $^{[2]}$ dendritic growth and corrosion reactions on Zn anodes, $^{[1d,3]}$ and limited electrochemical stable window (ESW) of electrolytes as well. $^{[4]}$ Therefore, various strategies have been explored to overcome these problems. $^{[5]}$ One strategy is to develop novel electrolytes capable of enhancing the charge-transfer kinetics, $^{[6]}$ inhibiting the side reactions, $^{[7]}$ and extending the ESW for AZMBs. $^{[8]}$ To date, the reported electrolytes always use small organic molecules or giant organic polymers as electrolyte co-solvents or electrolyte additives, which remarkably improves the electrochemical performance. $^{[9]}$ But they are still far from being satisfied for practical applications. More importantly, the correlation between molecular structure and electrochemical performance is rarely investigated.

Taking zwitterionic polymers as an example, they have distinct properties well suited as a hydrogel electrolyte for AZMBs. $^{[10]}$ 1) Zwitterionic polymers contain both positively and negatively charged groups that can interact with cations and anions in theory. Therefore, they can effectively regulate the ion flux of both cations and anions near the electrode/electrolyte interface. $^{[11]}$ 2) These charged groups also show good affinity to water molecules, facilitating electrolyte permeation and charge transfer. $^{[5d]}$ Meanwhile, this interaction immobilizes $\ce{H_{2}O}$, reducing the amount of free water molecules in the electrolyte. This significantly reduces the associated side reactions, such as hydrogen evolution, dendrite growth, and dissolution of transition metal ions. $^{[12]}$ 3) The charged groups enable the polymer to adhere firmly to the surface of Zn foils. Meanwhile, the excellent flexibility of the polymer makes it well adapted to the volume/surface change during cycling, thereby maintaining the good stability. $^{[13]}$ 4) Last but not least, the diverse molecular structures of zwitterionic polymers offer us an effective way to modulate the interaction between zwitterionic polymers and electrolyte/electrode for advanced performance. $^{[14]}$ These unique features make zwitterionic polymers interesting in AZMBs.

However, they still face the following challenges: 1) To date, zwitterionic polymers are usually pressed onto the Zn foils. In this case, the physical contact between the zwitterionic polymers and the Zn foils cannot guarantee the fast charge transfer across the interface. $^{[15]}$ This problem can be solved by in situ polymerization on the Zn foils. 2) There are many types of zwitterionic polymers available for this application. How to identify the appropriate polymers has not been explored in the past. To answer this question, it is necessary to reveal the underlying relationship between molecular structures and electrochemical performance. $^{[16]}$ 3) The electrochemical performance of AZMBs is always limited to coin cells and small capacity pouch cells (< 100 mAh), which is far from satisfying the practical applications. $^{[17]}$ Even as a proof of concept, the Ah-level pouch cells are more convincing for the development of AZMBs.

In this work, the in situ polymerization is adopted for zwitterionic polymers to facilitate the intimate contact with Zn foils in AZMBs (Figure 1a). The formation of zwitter- ionic polymers is confirmed by ex situ FT-IR, in situ Raman spectra and electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS). Three zwitterionic polymers, poly(2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphoryl choline) (PMPC), poly (carboxyl betaine meth- acrylate) (PCBMA) and poly[3-dimethyl(methacryl oylox- yethyl) ammonium propane sulfonate] (PDMAPS), are investigated to unravel the relationship between molecular structure and electrochemical performance theoretically and experimentally, which provides an insightful guidance for zwitterionic polymers in molecule design. As a result, PDMAPS exhibits the best electrochemical performance among these polymers. Using this gel electrolyte enables us to realize an Ah-level cell for continuous cycling. ## Results and Discussion

Zwitterionic polymers are prepared on the Zn anodes by in situ polymerization in the electrolyte. Using the case of 3-dimethyl (methacryloyloxyethyl) ammonium propane sulfonate (DMAPS) as an example, the polymerization was carried out using DMAPS as the monomer, N,N-methylene bisacrylamide (MBA) as the linker, carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CMC) as the binder and potassium persulfate ($\text{K}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8$) as the initiator (Figures 1a, see Supporting Information for details). Figure S1 shows the ex situ Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the electrode before and after cycling. The peaks located at 1485, 1630, 1727, 2978 and $3484\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ originate from the bending/stretching vibrations of $\text{C-N}$, $\text{C=C}$, $\text{O-C=O}$, $\text{CH}_3$ of DMAPS and $\text{O-H}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O}^{[18]}$ During the cycling process, the peak of $\text{C=C}$ at $1630\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ gradually weakens and finally disappears (Figure 1b), indicating the successful polymerization of DMAPS. This conclusion is supported by the in situ Raman spectra (Figure 1c), where the scattering band of $\text{C=C}$ at $1631\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ is constantly attenuated (Figure S2).$^{[19]}$ The in situ polymerization of DMAPS on the Zn foils is directly supported by post-mortem check after cycles (Figure S3). The abundant groups of DMAPS, especially the sulfonate group, have strong binding with Zn foils, as evidenced by $\text{Zn-O}$ and $-\text{SO}_3^-$ on the surface (Figure S4).$^{[20]}$ The binding constructs a robust interface, reduces the interfacial impedances (Figure 1d). The distribution of relaxation time (DRT) is derived from in situ EIS, which allows us to accurately identify different electrochemical processes.$^{[21]}$ As shown in Figure 1e, there are several local maxima of the relaxation time. The peak at low frequencies ($\text{log}(\tau)\ \sim -0.13$) can be attributed to Zn-ion diffusion on the surface.$^{[22]}$ The peaks at medium frequencies ($\text{log}(\tau)$: $-0.6$--$-4.0$) can be associated with the double-layer relaxation, the passivation layers ($\text{R}_{\text{SEI}}$), and the charge-transfer process ($R_{\text{ct}}$).$^{[23]}$ The complex processes result in multiple peaks in this range. The peak at high frequencies ($\text{log}(\tau)\ \sim -4.67$) is related to the contact resistance.$^{[24]}$ In our case, all these resistances decrease during the discharge process. It confirms that the polymerization obviously lowers the interfacial resistance, which benefits the rate performance.

To gain insight into this polymerization, several controls were conducted. If the cells were simply aged without the dis-/charging process, the characteristic peak of $\text{C=C}$ was still present after 150 min (Figure S5a). It indicates that the polymerization slows down due to the lack of current. The similar conclusion is drawn for the cells without a chemical initiator (Figure S5b). These results reveal that the chemical initiator and the discharging current work together to promote the in situ polymerization in this case. This conclusion is supported by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figures 1f-1i and Table S1). Initially, the monomer (DMAPS) distribution is relatively uniform (Figure 1f), although small aggregates appear after aging. When only chemical initiation or dis-/charging current is applied to the solution, the molecule aggregation is aggravated (Figures 1g and 1 h). But the polymerization degree is not comparable to the case with both simultaneously (Figure 1i). This result indicates the synergy of chemical initiation and dis-/charging current for in situ polymerization.

In order to elucidate the relationship between molecular structures and physicochemical properties of zwitterionic polymers, different zwitterionic polymers, PMPC, PCBMA and PDMAPS, were synthesized using the same protocol. It should be noted that three polymers are elaborately selected. First of all, they are all typical zwitterionic polymers. However, the positions and types of positively and negatively charged groups in the polymers vary from one to another (Figure S6). For example, PCBMA and PDMAPS share the same positively charged groups but differ in the negatively charged groups, carboxyl vs. sulfonate. The comparison between them can illustrate the influence of the negatively charged groups on the physicochemical properties of zwitterionic polymers. Another comparison between PMPC and PCBMA, in which the positively and negatively charged groups switch positions, can illustrate the influence of the locations of positively and negatively charged groups on the physicochemical properties of zwitterionic polymers. To simplify the calculations, the monomers are computed (Figure 2a), instead of the whole polymer. Using the binding energies of these structure units with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ as an example, it is noted that DMAPS has the lowest binding energies with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in three monomers (Figures 2b-2e), suggesting the strong interaction between DMAPS and the electrolyte. Further examination of the data reveals many interesting and important pieces of information. Firstly, the binding energy of DMAPS with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ($-3.52\ \text{eV}$) is much lower than that with $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ ($-0.77\ \text{eV}$) (Figures 2b and 2d). It implies that DMAPS prefers to bind with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ rather than $\text{Zn}^{2+}$, although $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ bind at different sites on the molecules. In this context, anion migration in the gel electrolyte is inhibited. A similar scenario occurs in the cases of MPC and CBMA. Secondly, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ competes with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ to bind quaternary ammonium and sulfonate of DMAPS. As displayed in Figures 2c and 2e, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ loses in the competition with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ($\text{H}_2\text{O}\ -1.49\ \text{eV}$ vs. $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ $-3.52\ \text{eV}$) near quaternary ammonium, but wins in the competition with $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ ($\text{H}_2\text{O}\ -1.07\ \text{eV}$ vs. $\text{Zn}^{2+}\ -0.77\ \text{eV}$) near sulfonate. The results indicate that water in the electrolyte does not affect the binding of $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ to DMAPS, but weakens the interaction of $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ with DMAPS. It facilitates the free migration of $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ in the gel electrolyte. The same conclusion also applies to the cases of MPC and CBMA. This is very different from the intuitive recognition, where $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ dictate the interaction with the positively and negatively charged groups respectively. Thirdly, the binding between $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and DMAPS is stronger than that between $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and CBMA (MPC) (Figures 2d and 2e), which enhances the electrolyte permeation in PDMAPS. This result is supported by the contact angles of the electrolyte on three polymers (Figure S7).

Based on these results, we propose a protocol to theoretically evaluate the transfer kinetics of $Zn^{2+}$. The large difference in the binding energy ($\Delta$BE1=$BE_{SO4^{2-}}$-$BE_{Zn^{2+}}$, and $\Delta$BE2=$BE_{SO4^{2-}}$-$BE_{H2O}$) suggests the preferential binding of the polymer with $SO_{4}^{2-}$ over $Zn^{2+}$ and $H_{2}O$ (Figure 2f), which effectively limits the anion movement and suppresses the related parasitic effects. The large difference in the binding energy ($\Delta$BE3=$BE_{H2O}$ - $BE_{Zn^{2+}}$) suggests the preferential binding of the polymer with $H_{2}O$, which effectively lowers the water content near the electrode and inhibits the related side reactions. It also shields the interaction between the polymer and $Zn^{2+}$, thereby promoting the free migration of hydrate $Zn^{2+}$ in the electrolyte. In other words, the binding energy of the polymer with the electrolyte species follows an order of $SO_{4}^{2-}>H_{2}O>Zn^{2+}$, which is important for the rational design of the polymer electrolyte. Although this order occurs for MPC, CBMA and DMAPS, DMAPS shows the largest differences in $\Delta$BE1, $\Delta$BE2, $\Delta$BE3 (Figure 2f). Then, the advantages arising from these differences become more significant in DMAPS. The theoretical predictions are fully supported by the experimental results. For example, the preferential binding of DMAPS to $SO_{4}^{2-}$ leads to an increase in the transference number ($t_{Zn^{2+}}$) and ion conductivity of $Zn^{2+}$ ($\sigma_{Zn^{2+}}$). PDMAPS has a $t_{Zn^{2+}}$ of 0.76 (Figures 2g, S8, and Table S2), higher than PMPC (0.51) and PCBMA (0.41). The weak binding of DMAPS with $Zn^{2+}$ facilitates the migration of $Zn^{2+}$ ions, as confirmed by the diffusion coefficient of $Zn^{2+}$ ($D_{Zn^{2+}}$, Figure S9). PDMAPS exhibits the largest diffusion coefficient of $Zn^{2+}$ ($2x10^{-4}\,cm^{2}s^{-1}$), higher than those of PMPC and PCBMA (7.6 and $6.3\,x10^{-5}\,cm^{2}s^{-1}$). Therefore, the fast diffusion and high transference number of $Zn^{2+}$ significantly enhance the Zn-ion conductivity. As displayed in Figure 2h, PDMAPS presents the highest $\sigma_{Zn^{2+}}$ of $12.5\,mScm^{-1}$ among the three polymers. This data is almost comparable to that in the aqueous electrolyte ($16.9\,mScm^{-1}$) (Figure S10). The high Zn-ion conductivity can reduce the electrode overpotential and improve the electrochemical performance.

The origin of the difference in the binding energy must be associated with the molecular structure of zwitterionic polymers. Since the interactions between zwitterionic polymers and anions/cations are primarily electrostatic attraction, electrostatic potential (ESP) maps of these structures are computed. Using CBMA as an example, replacing the negatively charged group at the end of the molecule from carboxyl to sulfonate only slightly changes the positive charge density maximum (PCDMa) and negative charge

density minimum (NCDMi) (Figures 3a and 3b). While one more $\text{CH}_2$ between the quaternary ammonium and the carboxyl group greatly elevates the PCDMa and lowers the NCDMi. The results indicate that the distance between the positive charge and the negative charge, rather than the functional group, dominates the charge density maximum/minimum. More interestingly, swapping the positions of the positive and negative charges in zwitterionic polymers does not obviously affect the PCDMa either, but reduces the NCDMi using MPC as an example (Figures 3c and 3d). It can be attributed to the different local structures around the negative charge in two cases. The results provide useful guidelines for the structure design of the zwitterionic polymers, which can be summarized as follows. 1) The positive charge is concentrated on the nitrogen atom of quaternary ammonium, while the negative charge is distributed on multiple oxygen atoms of sulfonate and carboxylate. Therefore, the quaternary ammonium in the zwitterionic polymers usually has the higher absolute charge density compared to the negatively charged group. Then, these polymers exhibit stronger binding to anions than that with $\text{Zn}^{2+}$. 2) The good separation between the negative charge and positive charge of the zwitterionic polymers increases the PCDMa and decreases the NCDMi, thus strengthening the interaction between the polymers and anions/$\text{H}_2\text{O}$. 3) If the negatively charged group is located in the center of the molecule, it would elevate the NCDMi and weaken the interaction with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. The relationship between the molecular structure and their interaction with $\text{Zn}^{2+}$, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ could be roughly evaluated by the dipole moment ($\mu$=q×d) as shown in Figure 3e. Here, q is the separated charge in the molecule, and d is the distance between the positive and negative charges. When the dipole moment is larger, the monomer interacts more strongly with anions and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Figure 3f), favoring the transfer kinetics of $\text{Zn}^{2+}$.

As discussed above, the zwitterionic polymers preferentially immobilize anions and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. In other words, the zwitterionic polymers not only regulate the anion flux, but also affect the $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ migration in the electrolyte. In order to gain insight into the latter, MD simulations were performed (Table S3). Figures 4a-4d show the typical snapshots of $\text{ZnSO}_4$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in different electrolytes. As displayed in Figures 4f-4 h, the content of free H₂O molecules in the electrolyte decreases from 81.7 % of PCBMA to 78.2 % of PMPC and 66.2 % of PDMAPS (Figures S12-S14). For example, there are three types of hydrogen bonds in the Raman spectra, the strong hydrogen bond at ~3230 cm⁻¹, the medium hydrogen bond at ~3418 cm⁻¹ and the weak hydrogen bond at ~3553 cm⁻¹.

These hydrogen bonds arise from water molecules interacted with polymers, neighboring $H_2O$ and $Zn^{2+}$, respectively. $^{[25]}$ The largest proportion of strong hydrogen bond appears in the case of PDMAPS, again confirming its intense interaction with $H_2O$. It is in agreement with the MD simulations. These changes in the conformations of $H_2O$ strongly suppress the HER, which is directly confirmed by in situ gas chromatography (GC) (Figures 4i-4 l). Hydrogen evolution in the symmetric cells was monitored at $10\,\text{mA}\,\text{cm}^{-2}$ for 120 minutes. The $H_2$ signal is remarkably attenuated, when PDMAPS is used as the gel electrolyte. Therefore, the formation of by-products (ZHS) is inhibited, as supported by the Raman spectra (Figure 4m) and the XRD patterns (Figure 4n). The same conclusion is also achieved by in situ pH detection (Figure S17), Tafel plots and LSV curves (Figures 4o and 4p). The corrosion current decreases from 8.81 in BE to $0.05\,\text{mA}\,\text{cm}^{-2}$ in PDMAPS. The onset potential of HER is extended from $-1.699\,\text{V}$ at $5\,\text{mA}\,\text{cm}^{-2}$ (vs. SCE) in $1\,\text{M}\,\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$ to $-1.854\,\text{V}$ in PDMAPS. These results again confirm that the zwitterionic polymers are effective in suppressing HER and that PDMAPS is the best in this respect. The strong binding of the polymer to $H_2O$ also facilitates the desolvation process of $Zn^{2+}$, as supported by the activation energy $(E_a)$. $E_a$ decreases from $42.5\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ of PCBMA to $36.9\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ of PMPC, and $32.1\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ of PDMAPS (Figure S18 and Tables S4-S6), which can be explained by the binding energy of the zwitterionic polymers with $H_2O$. The charge transfer resistance also follows the same order (Figure S19). All these results indicate the excellent charge-transfer kinetic at the PDMAPS/Zn interface. Therefore, uniform Zn deposition is preferred in the case of PDMAPS, as revealed by the chronoamperometry (CA) curves. As depicted in Figure S20, the current density in the case of PDMAPS rapidly increases at the beginning, followed by a plateau only after 20s. The rapid stabilization of the current density is regarded as a sign of uniform growth. In contrast, the current densities for PMPC, PCBMA and BE increase continuously over time. This result implies the unstable electrode/electrolyte interface, a typical sign of dendrite growth.

Benefiting from the above advantages, PDMAPS exhibits its superior electrochemical performance in AZMBs. The symmetric cells using PDMAPS as the gel electrolyte show a cycle life of 750 h at $1\ \text{mA}\text{cm}^{-2}$ for $1\ \text{mAh}\text{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure 5e), much better than those of PMPC (360 h), PCBMA (~240 h) and BE (~90 h). The enlarged voltage profiles suggest that PDMAPS presents the smallest voltage hysteresis among the three zwitterionic polymers (Figure S23), which is consistent with the predictions from theoretical calculations and experimental results. The same conclusion about the enhanced reaction kinetics in PDMAPS is also supported by the rate performance (Figure 5f). The reversibility of Zn plating/stripping can be ascertained using a so-called ion reservoir test developed by Aurbach et al. based on the asymmetric cells of $Cu||Zn.^{[26]}$ The cell using PDMAPS as the electrolyte achieves an average Coulombic efficiency of 97.5 % (Figure 5g), higher than those using MPC (93.1 %) and CBMA (87.7 %). This result directly supports the reduced side reactions on the Zn anodes. The cycling performance of the asymmetric cells is also evaluated. As shown in Figure 5h, the cell using PDMAPS has an initial Coulombic efficiency of 84.1 %, which is higher than that of PMPC (71.1 %) and PCBMA (69.1 %). Moreover, the high Coulombic efficiency of PDMAPS remains throughout the cycling. Therefore, the cell using PDMAPS runs for over 500 cycles, while those using PMPC and PCBMA only survive only 150 cycles. The cell using BE as the electrolyte dies after 60 cycles. These results not only demonstrate the promotion of electrochemical performances by zwitterionic polymers, but also verify the relationship between molecular structures and electrochemical performance. The latter is particularly important to understand the underlying mechanism and design rational molecules for advanced aqueous zinc metal batteries in the future.

To simplify the following discussion, only two electrolytes, PDMAPS and BE, are tested further as a typical zwitterionic polymer and conventional electrolyte. At a current density of $0.5\ \text{mA}\text{cm}^{-2}$ for an areal capacity of $0.5\ \text{mAh}\text{cm}^{-2}$, the symmetric cell using PDMAPS as the electrolyte can be cycled smoothly over 9000 h (~one year) in Figure 6a. The cell using BE only works for ~330 h (~14 days). Since PDMAPS is formed in situ in the electrolyte, some monomer must remain in the electrolyte. In order to clarify if these leftovers enhance the performance, we remove the chemical initiator from the electrolyte formulation. The effect of the monomer on the electrochemical performance is explored under the same conditions. As displayed in Figure S24, the cell fails after ~350 h, close to the case of BE. It indicates that the effect of the monomer is almost negligible. The excellent performance of PDMAPS is also observed at $3\ \text{mA}\text{cm}^{-2}$ for $3\ \text{mAh}\text{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure 6b). The symmetric cell using PDMAPS operates for over 800 h, while that using BE survives 100 h. These results are much better than the reported data based on gel electrolytes (Figure S25). This excellent electrochemical performance may be related to the effective inhibition of dendrite growth by PDMAPS, as confirmed by in situ optical microscope (Figure S26).

The electrochemical performance of the gel electrolytes was further investigated using full cells with Zn foils as the anode and commercial $\text{LiMn}_2\text{O}_4$ powders (LMO, Figure S27) as the cathode material. LMO is chosen due to good availability and high operation voltage (~1.8 V vs. $\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}$). There are two pairs of redox peaks in the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of LMO||Zn (Figure S28), which are thought to result from the two-step $\text{Li}^+$ insertion/extraction in LMO. The similar contour of the voltage profiles indicates that different electrolytes do not alter the electrochemical reactions in the electrode materials. The use of gel electrolytes extends electrochemical stable window (ESW, Figure S29), facilitating the stable cycling. Figure S30 compares the self-discharge behaviors, which is important for the practical applications of the cells. After a rest period of 48 hours, the full cell using PDMAPS as the electrolyte retains ~86.4 % of its charge capacity, significantly higher than that using BE (55.6 %). Figure S31 shows the voltage profiles of LMO||Zn using PDMAPS or BE as the electrolyte in the first cycle at $3\ \text{C}\ (1\ \text{C}=110\ \text{mA}\text{g}^{-1})$. The voltage hysteresis decreases with PDMAPS as the electrolyte, reflecting the good charge transfer kinetics in the full cells. The reversible capacity of LMO||Zn using PDMAPS is $85.3\ \text{mAh}\text{g}^{-1}$, much higher than that using BE $(51\ \text{mAh}\text{g}^{-1})$. This advantage is maintained over cycling. After 2000 cycles at $3\ \text{C}$, there is still a capacity of $54.1\ \text{mAh}\text{g}^{-1}$ for LMO||Zn using PDMAPS (Figure 6c). The full cell using BE quickly drops to $20\ \text{mAh}\text{g}^{-1}$ after 400 cycles. In addition to the improved cycling stability, PDMAPS also improves the rate capability of the full cells. As illustrated in Figure 6d, the reversible capacities in the case of PDMAPS are always higher than those of BE at different current densities. Encouraged by these results, the packing cell of LMO||Zn was assembled with plastic plates to reduce the dead weight and address the issue of gas generation (Figure S32). The cell delivers a capacity of 1.2 Ah (Figure 6e), but decays to 0.64 Ah after 125 cycles. The specific energy density of the pouch cell is calculated to be $35\ \text{Wh}\text{Kg}^{-1}$ (Figure S33 and Table S7, calculation details in Supporting Information).

Because the thermal effect of the cell is closely related to the intrinsic reactions, infrared thermal imaging is developed as a non-destructive tool to check the thermal effect in pouch cells. To the best of our knowledge, it has not been previously explored for AZMBs. The pouch cells with PDMAPS or BE as the electrolyte were assembled and charged at a current density of 1 C (Figure 6f). Initially, the surface temperature of the cell is approximately ~17°C. After 5 minutes, the cell temperature increases in both cases. In the case of BE, the temperature varies drastically from 17.3°C to 18.1°C. The high temperature is concentrated in a few areas, suggesting the intense reactions at these locations. In general, the high local temperature would readily exacerbate serious side reactions, e.g. dendrite growth and

HER on the anode side, Mn-species dissolution from the cathode side, etc. Then, it lowers the Coulombic efficiency, reduces the active materials, and shortens the cycle life. In contrast, the temperature in the case of PDMAPS is uniform (~17.6°C), reflecting the uniform reactions in the cells. As the charging process proceeds, the difference between the two cases is maintained. After 60 min, the cell using BE as the electrolyte shows the local temperature maxima up to 38°C, while the cell using PDMAPS has the temperature at 32.4°C. The low temperature reduces the side reactions to some extent, thereby extending the cycle life. These results here provide a useful and simple technique to detect the reactions inside the cells.

## Conclusion
In summary, zwitterionic polymers are in situ formed and used as gel electrolytes for aqueous Zn metal batteries. Using three zwitterionic polymers (PDMAPS, PMPC and PCBMA) as an example, we investigate the underlying mechanism for enhanced electrochemical performances. They preferentially bind anions and $H_2O$ around the positive and negative charges, limiting their migration and reducing their contribution to charge transfer and side reactions. In contrast, their effect on $Zn^{2+}$ is limited so that $Zn^{2+}$ could move freely. More importantly, it is found that the interaction between zwitterionic polymers and the electrolyte can be correlated with the dipole moment, which is very useful for structure design and molecule selection. The larger the dipole moment, the stronger the interaction. Therefore, PDMAPS shows the strongest preferential binding with anion and $H_2O$ than the other polymers, and results in the best electrochemical performance among three zwitterionic polymers. The symmetric cells using PDMAPS as the electrolyte exhibit an ultralong cycle life, ~9000 h (375 days) at a current density of $0.5 mA cm^{-2}$ with an areal capacity of $0.5 mAh cm^{-2}$. The pouch cells of LMO||Zn using PDMAPS deliver a reversible capacity of up to 1.2 Ah, much better than those using basic electrolytes. These results clearly demonstrate the promising potential of zwitterionic polymers in aqueous batteries. In situ infrared thermal imaging was developed to monitor the cell temperature, providing a non-destructive tool to investigate the reactions inside the pouch cells.
