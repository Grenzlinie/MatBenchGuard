## Abstract

The atop interaction of halogens (Cl, Br and I) above a finite surface cluster model has been studied at the ab initio Hartree-Fock level of theory employing non-empirical pseudopotentials and large basis sets. Two different electronic states have been considered corresponding formally to neutral and ionic interactions. The physical nature of the interaction emerging from the analysis of the resulting wavefunction can be different for each of these two electronic states. However, the use of a set of theoretical techniques, such as the study of the dipole moment curves or the effects induced by an external uniform electric field, allows us to show that in both cases the chemisorption bond between halogens and the surface model can be described as an ionic bond.

## INTRODUCTION

Chemisorption of halogens on mercury has usually been chosen as a test system to modelize the interaction of simple adsorbates on a mercury surface. This model has been treated by both semiempirical [1-3] and ab initio techniques [4,5], always from the cluster model approach. For halogens on mercury, ab initio cluster model calculations using an $Hg_{7}$ cluster surface model have been able to show the existence of two different electronic states corresponding formally to two different bonding mechanisms. Thus the electronic ground state appeared to be of a rather ionic nature, corresponding formally to the interaction between the charged subsystems $(X^{-}$ and $Hg_{7}^{+})$ , whereas the physical nature of the interaction arising

from the first excited electronic state can be viewed formally as a neutral, rather covalent, interaction. A detailed analysis of the interaction is not yet available. In fact, from the cluster model approach one obtains directly local properties such as geometries, vibrational frequencies or interaction energies, but a detailed analysis of the interaction requires additional theoretical techniques to extract the desired information from the ab initio cluster wavefunction.

Recently, Bagus et al. [6] have introduced a set of theoretical techniques, such as the study of the dipole moment curves or the effects induced by an external uniform electric field on the bonding parameters, which can be used to study the degree of ionicity of a given chemisorption bond. These techniques have been further applied successfully to the study of the nature of the chemisorption bond in a wide variety of systems involving semiconductor, oxide and metal surfaces [7-16]. In this work we will use some of these theoretical techniques to gain further insight to the modelization of the interaction of adsorbates on mercury. Results obtained from the application of these theoretical techniques to the analysis of the cluster wavefunctions corresponding to the halogen-surface interaction will allow us to show unequivocally that halogens (Cl, Br and I) are effectively adsorbed on mercury as halides, the interaction being described as an ionic bond. This will be shown to be the case for the formally ionic electronic ground state and also for the formally neutral first excited electronic state.

## METHOD OF CALCULATION

As in our previous works [4,5], we have used a finite cluster model with a central Hg atom surrounded by its first six nearest neighbours in the first layer maintain- ing the experimental geometry [17,18]. Potential energy curves were obtained by computing the total energy of the supersystem, the cluster model plus the adsor- bate moving perpendicularly above the central atom of the model, as a function of the distance from the surface to the adsorbate. The total energy of the system was computed in the well-known restricted open-shell Hartree-Fock (ROHF) approxi- mation.

In order to reduce the dimension of the atomic basis, the internal cores of Hg and halogen atoms were substituted by a non-empirical pseudopotential. In this work we have used the pseudopotentials reported by Hay and Wadt [19]. The electrons treated explicitly are the $5d^{10}6s^{2}$ of each cluster mercury atom and the $ns^{2}np^{5}$ of each halogen. The inclusion of the d-shell of all mercury atoms in the Hg cluster model is necessary in order to obtain a correct description of the electronic ground state [4,5]. The basis sets used in this work are similar to those used previously as series C in ref. 5 but contain one more "d" function in each one of the Hg atoms surrounding the central cluster atom. The Hg basis set can be denoted as (3s3p3d/2s1p2d). For the halogen atoms we have used a basis set of the same quality as that used previously in ref. 5. Basically it is a double-$\zeta$ plus polarization basis set augmented with one diffuse "s" and one diffuse "p" function; the final basis set can be denoted as (4s4p1d/3s3p1d). Hay and Wadt

pseudopotentials were also used to describe the effect of the inner cores of the halogen atoms in the valence shell. Calculations were carried out using the HONDO7 computer program from the MOTECC package [20].

As in our previous works [4,5], structural parameters such as the equilibrium distance above the surface, the vibrational frequency for the perpendicular mode with respect to the surface plane and the interaction energy with respect to the neutral separated systems have been obtained from the computed potential energy (ROHF) curve. In this work we will also use some other techniques to study the degree of ionicity associated with the interaction between the halogen and the mercury surface model.

Finally let us point out that the cluster electronic ground state is $^1$A$_{1g}$ (in the $D_{6\text{h}}$ point group of the isolated cluster model), corresponding to a closed-shell occupation. However, there is a difference with respect to the electronic wavefunc- tions reported in our previous works. In fact we have found that substitution of the highest doubly occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of $b_{1\text{u}}$ symmetry by the first virtual molecular orbital of $a_{2\text{g}}$ symmetry leads to a lower energy and hence the latter appears to be the ground state. Thus the overall symmetry of the cluster wavefunction remains unchanged although there is a change in the electronic configuration. This is the reason why sometimes it is possible that the self-con- sistent field (SCF) procedure leads to an excited state even for a closed-shell system. Such a possibility is unfortunately more common than previously thought and it is worth performing a series of calculations exploring different possible electronic configurations. In this particular case we will show that this change in the cluster electronic wavefunction does not alter the conclusion of our previous works. In fact, upon interaction with the halogen, two electronic states are again possible. The first one corresponds to a formally ionic interaction dissociating into the charged subunits ($\text{Hg}_7^+$ and $\text{X}^-$). However, because the HOMO has changed, the symmetry of this electronic state is now $^2$A$_2$ (in the $C_{6\text{v}}$ point group of the supersystem) and it corresponds to the $^2$B$_1$ electronic state described earlier in refs. 4 and 5. Likewise, the second electronic state studied in this work corre- sponds to a formally neutral interaction of $^2$A$_1$ symmetry as in refs. 4 and 5, although again the highest doubly occupied orbital of $b_{1\text{u}}$ symmetry has been substituted by a virtual molecular orbital of $a_{2\text{g}}$ symmetry. It is worth pointing out that neither the $b_{1\text{u}}$ nor the $a_{2\text{g}}$ molecular orbital can interact with the atomic orbitals of the adsorbate. As a result the description arising from the new electronic states is quantitatively the same as that found previously in refs. 4 and 5, as will become clear from the calculated structural parameters.

The results for the equilibrium distance and vibrational frequency are in very good agreement with the previous values reported in ref. 5 using a similar approach. Once again the equilibrium distance corresponding to the formally ionic ($^2\text{A}_2$) state is larger than that corresponding to the formally neutral interaction described by the ($^2\text{A}_1$) electronic state. The reason for such a behaviour has already been discussed in our previous works [4,5]. It is worth pointing out that the agreement between the present results and those reported in ref. 5 is quantitative, although the electronic states considered in the present work correspond to a slightly different electronic configuration where a doubly occupied purely cluster orbital has been replaced by another purely cluster orbital. Since these molecular orbitals do not interact directly with the adsorbate, there are no changes in the bonding parameters in either of the two electronic states considered.

Next we consider the interaction energies for halogens in our surface cluster model. Again the results are very close to those reported in ref. 5. Thus the electronic ground state appears to be the ($^2\text{A}_2$) one corresponding formally to an ionic interaction. Moreover, the energy difference between the two electronic states calculated here for Cl on Hg₇, is 40 kJ mol⁻¹, compared with a value of 32 kJ mol⁻¹ reported in ref. 5. Thus the only difference between the present calculations and those of ref. 5 concerns the absolute value of the interaction energies, which are somewhat larger here than those reported in ref. 5. At this point we have to remark that interaction energies obtained from the ab initio Hartree-Fock cluster model approach are not directly comparable with experi- mental values such as the adsorption heat. This is due to the approximations in the wavefunction and also to the choice of the surface cluster model. The first problem can be solved by using a more accurate wavefunction as was done for instance in refs. 21-27. However, the second problem is more difficult to solve because of the poor convergence of interaction energies with respect to cluster size [28], though

some rules have been given recently to obtain interaction energies which are stable with respect to cluster size [29]. However, stable energies do not necessarily mean that the calculated values agree with the experimental ones [30]. Owing to all these reasons, interaction energies have to be handled with care: they provide useful information about trends but cannot be taken as a measure of the real interaction energy.

From the results presented in this section we can extract two main conclusions. One is that even using SCF wavefunctions that correspond to different electronic states, one gets the same description if the only change in the electronic structure involves a purely surface cluster model orbital. This is a result of the local nature of the interaction upon which the cluster model approach relies. This fact appears from the comparison between the present results and those reported in our previous works [4,5]. The second conclusion implies that the numerical values for bonding parameters such as $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ and $\nu_{\mathrm{e}}$ do not depend strongly on the level of the calculation. In the next section we will be concerned more specifically with the nature of the chemisorption bond of halogens on mercury.

## ANALYSIS OF THE INTERACTION

Two different theoretical techniques to investigate the character of the chemisorption bond of halogens above a mercury surface model have been chosen in this work. These two techniques are the analysis of the dipole moment curves and the effects on the equilibrium distance $(r_{\mathrm{e}})$ induced by a uniform external electric field.

The idea behind the analysis of the dipole moment curve is very simple. For an electric dipole the representation of the dipole vs. the distance gives a straight line and the slope of this line gives the charge on the dipole. For an adsorbate above a surface the slope of the surface dipole is also related to the charge on the adsorbate [10]. Moreover, the degree of ionicity of a given bond can be related to the curvature of the dipole moment curve. In principle, a small value for the curvature of the dipole moment curve indicates that the adsorbate-surface interac- tion is largely ionic. However, owing to bulk polarization effects, it is not so clear how to relate the slope of the dipole moment curve to the adsorbate charge. One good technique is the substitution of the adsorbate by a given point charge (in this case a -1 point charge) and the subsequent comparison of the resulting dipole moment curves.

In order to study the dipole moment curves, the dipole moment has been computed as an expectation value from the ab initio Hartree-Fock SCF wavefunc- tion and then expanded in a Taylor series around the equilibrium distance:

$$
\mu=M_{0}+M_{1}\left(z-z_{\mathrm{e}}\right)+M_{2}\left(z-z_{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{2} \tag{1}
$$

In expansion (1) the $M_{1}$ value is the slope of the dipole moment curve and $M_{2}$ is its curvature. In principle, the larger the $M_{2}$ value, the larger is the covalent contribution to a given chemisorption bond. However, this statement is based on

the assumption that the charge above the adsorbate is only a test charge, which is the case if the adsorbate-surface distance is large enough. For very large distances the quadratic term vanishes and one obtains the usual classical image law be- haviour. For halogens on mercury at the atop position the distance is moderately large and a non-zero value for $M_{2}$ is not due to the distortion induced by the charge near the surface but to the possible covalent contribution to the chemisorp- tion bond.

The second technique that we have applied to study the nature of the chemisorption bond of halogens on mercury consists of obtaining the equilibrium distance of the adsorbate perpendicular to the surface $(r_{e})$ in the presence of a uniform external electric field. The idea is again very simple: if the adsorbate-sub- strate interaction is ionic, there will be large effects on $r_{e}$ due to the presence of the electric field; on the other hand, the electric field effects will be very small for a neutral adsorbate. In order to introduce the electric field effects, we consider that the total hamiltonian in the presence of the field, $H(F)$ , can be obtained from the hamiltonian at zero field, $H(0)$ , by simply adding the operators associated withthe external electric field:
$$\boldsymbol{H}(F)=\boldsymbol{H}(0)+F \sum \boldsymbol{r}_{i}-F \sum \boldsymbol{R}_{i}\qquad(2)$$

In eqn. (2) $r_{i}$ is the operator associated with electron positions and $R_{i}$ repre sents that corresponding to nuclear positions. The Hartree-Fock (SCF) energy in the presence of the field can be obtained simply by including the corresponding one-electron integrals due to the $r_{i}$ operator. The values used for the uniformexternal field are the same as those used in previous works $( \pm 0.01$ a.u. $\equiv \pm 5.7 \times$ 107 V cm-') and are close to those applied in electrochemical cells (see e.g. ref.12).

In fact, the shape of the dipole moment curves of halogens interacting with the surface cluster model in the $^2$A$_2$ electronic state is almost a straight line. The small value of $M_2$ and the large value of $M_1$ are a clear indication of the ionicity of the bond. The value of $M_1$ seems to indicate that the net charge on the adsorbate is smaller than $-1$, a value with no physical meaning. This is because the surface model is polarized in response to the charge on the adsorbate and the value of $M_1$ cannot be taken directly as a measure of the charge on the adsorbate [12]. A simple way to estimate the effect due to the polarization of the surface consists of comparing the slope of the dipole moment curve for the adsorbate with that obtained when the adsorbate is substituted by a $-1$ point charge. For a $-1$ point charge above the surface we obtain a value $M_1 \approx -1.4$ in all cases. This result is a clear indication that the net charge on the adsorbate is very close to $-1$ and the bond can be viewed effectively as an ionic bond.

Now let us turn our attention to the dipole moment curves for the adsorbates above the surface in the formally neutral $^2$A$_1$ electronic state. In this case the value of $M_2$ is larger than when the $^2$A$_2$ electronic state is considered. This is an indication that the interaction occurring in this electronic state contains some degree of covalent character. However, the large value of $M_1$ indicates that the bond is still predominantly ionic. In fact the value of $M_1$ in this case is only about 10% lower than that corresponding to the previous case where we have seen that the interaction corresponds to an ionic bond.

The above analysis of the dipole moment curves permits us to conclude that the interaction of halogens on mercury can be described as an ionic bond in both electronic states. In the $^2$A$_1$ state there is some small degree of covalent character but the interaction is still largely ionic. The results in Table 3 show that the equilibrium distance perpendicular to the surface $(r_{\mathrm{e}})$ is greatly affected by the external electric field. The sense of the field is such that a positive field will attract a negative point charge towards the surface. At negative values of the electric field there is a large increase in $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ as will occur if the adsorbate is negatively charged. The magnitude of the increase in $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ is larger for the $^{2}\mathrm{A}_{2}$ electronic state as expected from the analysis of the dipole moment curves. However, the $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ value is also somewhat affected if the interaction occurs in the $^{2}\mathrm{A}_{1}$ electronic state. The variation is smaller because there is some degree of covalent character but also because the electrostatic interaction is different. The electrostatic interaction can be obtained approximately by first-order perturbation theory and is given simply by the expectation value of the dipole moment $(M_{0})$ times the value of the electric field $(F)$. For the $^{2}\mathrm{A}_{2}$ state we have a larger value for the dipole moment (see Table 2) and hence a larger electrostatic interaction. This explains in part why the field effects on $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ are larger for the $^{2}\mathrm{A}_{2}$ electronic state. When the external field is positive, there is a decrease in $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ because the adsorbate is attracted towards the surface. The magnitude of the decrease in $r_{\mathrm{e}}$ is smaller because now there is a Pauli repulsion between the electronic distribution of the adsorbate and that of the surface cluster model, i.e. a surface wall effect [31,32]. Finally we point out the close similarity between the electric field effects reported here for halogens on mercury and those obtained for halogens on silver [12]. In both case halogens appear to be chemisorbed as halides and the chemisorption bond is in fact an ionic bond.

## CONCLUSIONS

In this work we have used ab initio Hartree-Fock cluster model wavefunctions to study the physical nature of the interaction between halogens and a mercury surface model. Two electronic states have been considered corresponding formally to ionic and neutral interactions. The values for the structural parameters $(r_{\mathrm{e}},\ \nu_{\mathrm{e}}$ and binding energy (BE)) obtained here for both electronic states are in very good agreement with those reported in our previous works [4,5], although there is a change in electronic configuration between the electronic states considered here and those studied in refs. 4 and 5. This change in electronic configuration corresponds to the substitution of a doubly occupied purely cluster molecular orbital by a purely cluster virtual molecular orbital. The results for the chemisorp- tion parameters are then almost unaffected.

The nature of the bonding between halogens and the mercury surface has been explored by using two different theoretical techniques, namely the analysis of the dipole moment curves and the effects induced by a uniform external electric field. Both techniques lead to the same conclusion, i.e. chemisorption of halogens on mercury corresponds to the formation of an ionic bond. This is the case for both electronic states considered even though one of them is formally neutral. Our conclusions have been obtained for a small cluster model and only one active site has been considered. However, because ionic bonds are not directional, it is very likely that the interaction mechanism will be the same for other sites as well.
