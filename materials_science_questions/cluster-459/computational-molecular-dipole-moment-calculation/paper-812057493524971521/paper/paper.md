## Abstract
The diagonal components and the trace of tensors which account for chiroptical response of the hydrazine molecule $\text{N}_2\text{H}_4$, that is, static anapole magnetizability and frequency-dependent electric dipole-magnetic dipole polarisability, are a function of the $\phi \equiv \angle \text{H-N-N-H}$ dihedral angle. They vanish for symmetry reasons at $\phi = 0^\circ$ and $\phi = 180^\circ$, corresponding respectively to $C_{2v}$ and $C_{2h}$ point group symmetries, that is, cis and trans conformers characterized by the presence of molecular symmetry planes. Nonetheless, vanishing diagonal components have been observed also in the proximity of $\angle \text{H-N-N-H} = 90^\circ$, in which the point group symmetry is $C_2$ and hydrazine is unquestionably chiral. In the boranylborane molecule $\text{B}_2\text{H}_4$, assuming the B-B bond in the $y$ direction, the $a_{yy}$ component of the anapole magnetizability tensor approximately vanishes for dihedral angles $\angle \text{H-B-B-H}$ corresponding to chiral rotamers which belong to $D_2$ symmetry. Such anomalous effects have been ascribed to physical achirality of these conformers, that is, to their inability to sustain electronic current densities inducing either anapole moments, or electric and magnetic dipole moments, about the chiral axis connecting heavier atoms, as well as perpendicular directions. In other terms, the structure of certain *geometrically chiral* rotamers may be such that neither toroidal nor helical flow, which determine chiroptical phenomenology, can take place in the presence of perturbing fields paral- lel or orthogonal to the chiral axis.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The Pasteur affirmation "L'univers est dissymétrique," p. 4 of Reference 1, is frequently recalled to state that chirality is pervasive in our Universe and omnipresent at all levels of magnitude, from ele- mentary particles, proteins and helical bacteria up to macroscopic systems like helical climbing plants and sea-shells, as well as giant galaxies. $^{2-4}$ Therefore, the Pasteur idea of cosmic dissymmetry has been considered "the first enunciation of the principle that an asymmetrical phenomenon must have an asymmetrical cause," and a prediction of the discovery of parity nonconserving phenomena in Nature. $^{5}$

Optical rotation, or *optical activity*, $^{6-10}$ is the rotation of the plane of polarization of linearly polarized light traveling through chiral mate- rials. Sometimes, within the chemical community, chirality is colloqui- ally defined according to the behavior of a molecule in the laboratory, for example, simply observing if it is provided with optical rotatory power or not. However, characterizing the concept of chirality with precision, via physical and mathematical criteria, requires careful and sophisticated analysis. $^{11,12}$

A current approach relies on the authority of William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, who coined the term "chirality"¹³ and proposed it in his lectures on "Molecular Dynamics and the Wave Theory of Light," given in 1884 at Johns Hopkins University of Baltimore and published in 1904.¹⁴ He writes: "I call any geometrical figure, or group of points, chiral, and say that it has chirality if its image in a plane mirror, ideally realized, cannot be brought to coincide with itself."

The terminology suggested by Thomson would replace that of Pasteur in a few years, thus, according to his novel proposal, an achiral object is, within the common acceptation, one which is identical with its mirror image.

As a matter of fact, Lord Kelvin statement provides a general definition of geometrical chirality, which characterizes molecules as rigid objects belonging to point groups that do not possess improper rotations, that is, symmetry planes, inversion and roto-reflection axes.

In a note published in 1955, Mislow and Bolstad reported the synthesis of (dextro)-menthyl (levo)-menthyl 2,6,2',6'-tetranitro-4,4'-diphenate, "an optically inactive and configurationally pure compound whose individual molecules are asymmetric."¹⁵ Such a molecule appeared to the authors "unique in that its enantiomeric conformations are interconverted by rotameric changes which cannot involve passage through any symmetric conformation."¹⁵

In a previous paper, Mislow had argued that, for compounds characterized by similar structure, "the sole necessary criterion for optical inactivity is the presence, in the molecular system, of an equal number of conformational and/or configurational enantiomers."¹⁶ Therefore, the asymmetric compound investigated in Reference 15 is pragmatically designated as chemically achiral, since it is not frozen in any rigid structure. In other words, its optical activity "was zero, as measured in pyridine and benzene solutions at 589, 578, 546, and 435 mμ," because "rotameric interconversions ensure statistically equal populations of enantiomeric conformations."¹⁵

The papers by Mislow¹⁶ and Mislow and Bolstad¹⁵ laid the foundations of axial chirality, a notion appropriate for systems not endowed with a stereogenic center, thus implying stereoisomerism which results from the nonplanar arrangement of substituent groups, ordered according to the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules about a chiral axis.¹⁷

Moreover, these works¹⁵,¹⁶ prompted mathematicians to propose precise specifications of geometrical achirality, meant for molecules as rigid objects. According to the general definition given above, also within the restricted domain of axially chiral species, the notion of geometrical chirality is typical of any rotamer which belongs to a symmetry group not having improper rotations: if such a species is geometrically chiral, then its only rigid symmetries are rotations.

Therefore throughout this work, we will be using the following definition proposed by Flapan,¹⁸ fully consistent with that of Lord Kelvin:¹⁴

A molecule is geometrically achiral if, as a rigid object, it can be superimposed on its mirror image. Otherwise it is geometrically chiral.

Geometrical achirality is distinct from chemical achirality, which is assessed by experimental criteria, for example, via lack of optical activity.¹⁵,¹⁶ In fact, according to Flapan, "there is no mathematical definition of chirality that corresponds to chemical chirality for all molecules, since some molecules are more or less rigid than others."¹⁸

On the other hand, we can adopt the opposite point of view and assume that axially chiral molecules are not clamped in a rigid configuration. As they are free to rotate according to Mislow,¹⁶ Hougen,¹⁹,²⁰ and Longuet-Higgins,²¹,²² can they legitimately be called chiral?

One can even postulate that molecules are completely flexible, and consequently propose the designation of topological achirality:

A molecule is said to be topologically achiral if, assuming complete flexibility, there is an isotopy which takes it to its mirror image. If there is no such isotopy, the molecule is said to be topologically chiral.¹⁸,²³ In geometric topology, for example in knot theory, the idea of isotopy is used to construct equivalence relations. The relationships involving different types of chirality and achirality have been discussed by Flapan¹⁸ and Walba.²⁴

The importance of topological approaches to chirality cannot be overemphasized as shown by the increasing interest of several authors,¹¹,²⁵⁻²⁸ the spread of new concepts which followed References 15,16, and the coinage of neologisms as "Euclidean rubber glove molecules" and "topological rubber glove molecules."¹⁸

Thus the need for a global approach to molecular chirality, one that cannot help but apply topological modus operandi, has been emphasized by Mezey.¹¹

Group-theoretical methods had also been applied, proposing an alternative and for many aspects complementary avenue to axial chirality. In fact, both topological and group-theoretical procedures provide criteria and suggest operations for manipulating molecular structures.

After some preliminary investigations by Hougen,¹⁹,²⁰ Longuet-Higgins advanced the concept of feasible permutations and permutation-inversions, which may be arrived at without passage over a high potential barrier, for semi-rigid molecules, and suggested that workable operations form groups which could be understood as the molecular groups of both rigid and nonrigid molecules.²¹,²² This proposal was further explored in a number of papers and reviews.²⁹⁻⁴¹

Some ambiguous aspects arising from the difference between a geometrical symmetry operation and a permutational symmetry operation, have been discussed,⁴²,⁴³ and the existence of a symmetry group paradox for non-rigid molecules has been recently pointed out.⁴⁴

At any rate, axial chirality is rather appealing for other, specifically physical, reasons. In a recent paper it has been shown that even a molecule as small as hydrogen peroxide presents interesting properties that merit to be investigated for understanding mechanisms which come into play in optical rotatory power and anapolar response of the electron cloud of chiral systems.⁴⁵

The trace of two tensors, the electric dipole-magnetic dipole polarisability (MEMDP) and the anapole magnetizability, both of which embody chiroptical character of the $\mathrm{H_2O_2}$ molecule as a function of the $\angle$ H–O–O–H dihedral angle $\phi$, vanishes for $\phi = 0^\circ$ and $\phi = 180^\circ$, corresponding to $\mathrm{C_{2v}}$ and $\mathrm{C_{2h}}$ point group symmetries, respectively. It also vanishes for values of $\phi$ in the range 90–100°, where the molecule, as a rigid object, is undeniably chiral.

This unexpected feature is determined by contributions of diagonal tensor components with nearly maximal magnitude but opposite sign, which offset each other on summing.⁴⁵ The magnitude of these diagonal components is determined by electronic currents which flow in open or closed helical paths, giving rise to induced electric and magnetic dipole moments,⁴⁶ as well as toroidal flow generating anapole moments.⁴⁷

One may ask if analogous mechanisms are at work in other compounds exhibiting axial chirality, for example, the isoelectronic hydrazine system N₂H₄. The present paper aims at investigating its axial chirality as a function of the dihedral angle ∠ H–N–N–H. It is organized as follows: in Section 2 we describe the theoretical tools employed in our research. Main questions concerning axial chirality of hydrazine and related boranylborane B₂H₄ and ethane C₂H₆ are discussed in Section 3 and details on the computational approaches adopted are given in Section 4. The results arrived at are discussed in Section 5 and conclusions are presented in Section 6.

## 2 | OUTLINE OF NOTATION AND THEORETICAL METHODS

Within the Born-Oppenheimer (BO) approximation,⁴⁸ for a molecule with n electrons and N clamped nuclei, charge, mass, position, canonical, and angular momentum of the kth electron are indicated, in the configuration space, by $-e$, $m_{\mathrm{e}}$, $\boldsymbol{r}_{k}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{l}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{r}_{k} \times \hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k}$, $k=1,2,...n$, using boldface letters for electronic operators.

Analogous quantities for nucleus $I$ are $Z_{I}e$, $M_{I}$, $\boldsymbol{R}_{I}$, and so forth, for $I=1,2,...,N$. The imaginary unit is represented by a Roman i. Troughout this paper, the International System units are used and standard tensor formalism is employed, for example, the Einstein convention of implicit summation over two repeated Greek indices is applied. The third-rank Levi-Civita skew-tensor is indicated by $\epsilon_{\alpha \beta \gamma}$.

Capitals denote n-electron vector operators, for example, for position, canonical, and angular momentum,

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n} \boldsymbol{r}_{k}, \hat{\boldsymbol{P}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n} \hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k}, \hat{\boldsymbol{L}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n} \hat{\boldsymbol{l}}_{k},
$$

except for the electric and magnetic dipoles,⁴⁹⁻⁵¹

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=-e \hat{\boldsymbol{R}}, \tag{1}
$$

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}=-\frac{e}{2 m_{\mathrm{e}}} \hat{\boldsymbol{L}}. \tag{2}
$$

Tensor operators are expressed specifying the components, for example, for the second-rank tensor operator corresponding to the magnetic quadrupole,⁵²,⁵³

$$
\hat{m}_{\alpha \beta}=-\frac{e}{6 m_{\mathrm{e}}} \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\hat{l}_{\alpha} r_{\beta}+r_{\beta} \hat{l}_{\alpha}\right)_{k}, \tag{3}
$$

and for its antisymmetric part, which defines the parity-odd, time-odd, anapole vector operator,⁵³

$$
\hat{a}_{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\alpha \beta \gamma} \hat{m}_{\beta \gamma}=\frac{e}{6 m_{\mathrm{e}}} \sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\left(r^{2} \delta_{\alpha \beta}-r_{\alpha} r_{\beta}\right) \hat{p}_{\beta}+\mathrm{i} \hbar r_{\alpha}\right]_{k}. \tag{4}
$$

The static anapole magnetizability $a_{\alpha \beta}$ is a nonsymmetric parityodd, time-even, second-rank tensor, expressed as the sum of paramagnetic and diamagnetic contributions,⁵¹,⁵³⁻⁵⁸

$$
a_{\alpha \beta}=a_{\alpha \beta}^{\mathrm{p}}+a_{\alpha \beta}^{\mathrm{d}}, \tag{5}
$$

$$
a_{\alpha \beta}^{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{1}{\hbar} \sum_{j \neq a} \frac{2}{\omega_{j a}} \mathfrak{R}\left(\left\langle a\left|\widehat{a}_{\alpha}\right| j\right\rangle\left\langle j\left|\widehat{m}_{\beta}\right| a\right\rangle\right), \tag{6}
$$

$$
a_{\alpha \beta}^{\mathrm{d}}=\frac{e^{2}}{12 m_{\mathrm{e}}} \epsilon_{\alpha \beta \gamma}\left\langle a\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(r^{2} r_{\gamma}\right)_{k}\right| a\right\rangle. \tag{7}
$$

In these equations it is assumed that the eigenvalue problem for the time-independent BO electronic Hamiltonian $\hat{H}^{(0)} \Psi_{j}^{(0)}=E_{j}^{(0)} \Psi_{j}^{(0)}$ has been solved, determining a set of eigenfunctions $\Psi_{j}^{(0)}$ and corresponding energy eigenvalues $E_{j}^{(0)}$. The reference (ground) state is indicated by $\Psi_{a}^{(0)}$ and the natural transition frequencies are $\omega_{j a}=\left(E_{j}^{(0)}-E_{a}^{(0)}\right) / \hbar$. $|j\rangle$ is shorthand for the eigenvector $\left|\Psi_{j}^{(0)}\right\rangle$.

In a change of coordinate system, or more generally in a gauge transformation, the contributions (6) and (7) interchange in such a way that their sum remains the same.⁵⁴ The paramagnetic part (6) can be considered the limit for the frequency $\omega \to 0$ of the optical anapole magnetizability.⁴⁷ It is easily checked, from Equations (6) and (7), that the average anapole magnetizability contains only the paramagnetic contribution, that is,

$$
\overline{a}=(1 / 3) a_{\alpha \alpha} \equiv(1 / 3) a_{\alpha \alpha}^{\mathrm{p}}, \tag{8}
$$

since $a_{\alpha \alpha}^{\mathrm{d}}$ vanishes identically. The induced anapole moment is a timeodd polar vector, given by

$$
\Delta\left\langle\widehat{a}_{\alpha}\right\rangle \equiv \mathcal{A}_{\alpha}=a_{\alpha \beta} \beta_{\beta}. \tag{9}
$$

The sum rule⁵¹ arrived at by summing the terms in the numerator of Equation (6),

$$
\sum_{j \neq a} \mathfrak{R}\left(\left\langle a\left|\widehat{a}_{\alpha}\right| j\right\rangle\left\langle j\left|\widehat{m}_{\alpha}\right| a\right\rangle\right)=0, \tag{10}
$$

corresponds to the Condon sum rule⁵⁹ for rotational strengths $R_{a j}$,

$$
\sum_{j \neq a} R_{a j}=\sum_{j \neq a} \mathfrak{I}\left(\left\langle a\left|\widehat{\mu}_{\alpha}\right| j\right\rangle\left\langle j\left|\widehat{m}_{\alpha}\right| a\right\rangle\right)=0, \tag{11}
$$

useful to check the accuracy of computed mixed electric-dipole magnetic-dipole polarisability, defined by the nonsymmetric parityodd, time-even, second-rank tensor,⁴⁹⁻⁵¹

$$
\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{\hbar} \sum_{j \neq a} \frac{2 \omega}{\omega_{j a}^{2}-\omega^{2}} \Im\left\{\left\langle a\left|\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\right| j\right\rangle\left\langle j\left|\hat{m}_{\beta}\right| a\right\rangle\right\}.
\tag{12}
$$

The electric and magnetic dipole moments, induced respectively by the time derivative of the electric and magnetic fields of monochromatic radiation with frequency $\omega$, are

$$
\Delta\left\langle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\right\rangle=\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime} \dot{B}_{\beta} \omega^{-1},
\tag{13}
$$

$$
\Delta\left\langle\hat{m}_{\alpha}\right\rangle=-\kappa_{\beta \alpha}^{\prime} \dot{E}_{\beta} \omega^{-1}.
\tag{14}
$$

The trace $\kappa_{\alpha \alpha}^{\prime}(\omega)$ of the MEMDP tensor is related to the angle $\phi$ of natural optical rotation per unit path length through the Rosenfeld equation. $^{60,61}$ In SI units

$$
\phi=-\frac{1}{3} \omega \mu_{0} L \mathcal{N} \kappa_{\alpha \alpha}^{\prime},
\tag{15}
$$

where $\mu_{0}$ is the permeability of free space, $L$ is the path length; and $\mathcal{N}$ is the number density of optically active molecules. $^{62}$

Since $a_{\alpha \beta}$ and $\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}$ are time-even and parity-odd, that is, axial tensors, their nonvanishing and unique components are the same as for $\left\{\widehat{L}_{\alpha}, \widehat{P}_{\beta}\right\}_{-1}$ in table I of Reference 63.

In a translation of coordinate system represented by the shift of origin

$$
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime} \rightarrow \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime \prime}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{d},
\tag{16}
$$

the anapole magnetizability transforms according to the relation

$$
a_{\gamma \delta}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime \prime}\right)=a_{\gamma \delta}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)+\frac{1}{2} \epsilon_{\alpha \beta \gamma} \xi_{\delta \alpha} d_{\beta},
\tag{17}
$$

where $\xi_{\delta \alpha}$ is the molecular magnetizability. Thus, according to Equation (17), diagonal components and trace of (5) are origin independent in the principal axis system of the symmetric $\xi_{\alpha \beta}$ tensor. $^{53}$

An analogous equation was found for the MEMDP,

$$
\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}\left(\omega, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime \prime}\right)=\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}\left(\omega, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{2} \omega \epsilon_{\beta \gamma \delta} \alpha_{\gamma \alpha}(\omega) d_{\delta},
\tag{18}
$$

where $\alpha_{\alpha \gamma}(\omega)$ is the frequency-dependent electric dipole polarisability. Hence diagonal components and trace of $\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}(\omega)$ are origin independent in the principal axis system of $\alpha_{\alpha \beta}(\omega) .^{64}$

Simple models of fundamental physical processes underlying response of chiral molecules to time-independent $\boldsymbol{B}$ or optical electro-magnetic fields are presented in the Graphical Abstract and in Figure 1. Induced toroidal or sinusoidal electronic currents are the fundamental constituents for rationalizing chiroptical properties, as well as the quite unpredictable behavior observed in Sections 3 and 5. These models are to be interpreted as mathematical idealizations, useful to describe basic morphological features of electron flow in chiral molecules. In practice, the actual current density vector field $\mathcal{J}$ is expected to deviate from those represented in Figure 1, thus appearing as topological distortions of a geometric torus or helix.

## 3 | AXIAL CHIRALITY OF HYDRAZINE, BORANYLBORANE AND ETHANE MOLECULES

In the light of the arguments raised by Longuet-Higgins, $^{21} \mathrm{~N}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}$, the hydrazine molecule displayed in Figure 2, could not be regarded as a chiral object. In fact, rotation about the $\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}$ internuclear axis inhibits separation of enantiomers in the gas phase, or in solution, at room temperature. Nonetheless, the existence of a hindering potential permits to define a preferred equilibrium configuration as that energetically more stable.

The skew conformer represented in Figure 2, for the $\angle$ $\mathrm{H} 1-\mathrm{N} 1-\mathrm{N} 2-\mathrm{H} 3$ dihedral angle $\phi=90^{\circ}$, corresponds to calculated values for the minimum energy state, ${ }^{65-68}$ and it is even more stable than the trans conformer, which may have the least steric repulsion between the $\mathrm{NH}_{2}$ moieties, allowing for a simple repulsion model. ${ }^{67}$ The dihedral $\phi$ angle $\angle \mathrm{H} 1-\mathrm{N} 1-\mathrm{N} 2-\mathrm{H} 3$ is the same as the dihedral angle $\angle \operatorname{lp}(\mathrm{N})-\operatorname{lp}(\mathrm{N})$, denoting by $\operatorname{lp}(\mathrm{N})$ a lone pair of each $\mathrm{N}$ atom, see figure 1 of Reference 68.

The rotational barrier estimated via a natural bond orbital analysis $^{68}$ is approximately twice that of ethane. $^{69-71}$ Hydrazine passes through two transition states, cis (eclipsed) TS1 at $\phi=0^{\circ}$ and trans (staggered) TS2 at $\phi=180^{\circ}$, in the course of internal rotation about the $\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}$ bond; the ground state conformation corresponds to $\phi=91.2^{\circ} .{ }^{68}$ Most accurate $\operatorname{CCSD}(\mathrm{T})$ values are in the range 89.6$90^{\circ}$, with a recommended best estimate of $89.6^{\circ} .^{66}$

The hydrazine molecule frozen in a rigid geometry is an object belonging to the same symmetry group of molecules as $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{2}$, investigated in a previous paper, $^{45}$ that is, $C_{2 v}$ for the dihedral angle $\phi=0^{\circ}$, $C_{2 h}$ for $\phi=180^{\circ}$, and $C_{2}$ for any intermediate value within this range.

As for other axially chiral systems, the rigid configurations of hydrazine are classified via the stereochemical labels $R_{a}$ or $S_{a}$ : the chiral axis is viewed end-on and the sense of either configuration is identified as that of the clockwise or anticlockwise rotation of a group (the $\mathrm{H}$ atom of an $\mathrm{NH}$ bond for $\mathrm{N}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}$ ), starting with the end of the molecule that is closest, then finishing with the end that is farthest. $^{45}$

An alternative specification, $P$ (plus) or $M$ (minus), is based on the helicity of a configuration, for the reason that an axially chiral structure is characterized by a helical, that is, screw-shaped geometry. As a result, $P$, or $\Delta$, is used for a right-handed helix, whereas $M$, or $\Lambda$, is used for a left-handed helix. ## 4 | COMPUTATIONS

The equilibrium geometries of $\mathrm{N}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}, \mathrm{~B}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}$, and $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{6}$ molecules represented in Figure 2 in the text, and in Figure S2, were optimized at the B3LYP $^{76}$ level of accuracy via the DALTON code. $^{77}$

A large uncontracted (13s10p5d2f/11s7p4d) basis set, previously tested for predicting anapole magnetizabilities of other chiral molecules, $^{53}$ has been employed to compute $a_{\alpha \beta}$ and $\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}$ for a series of molecular conformations corresponding to different values of the HX-XH dihedral angle, with X = N, B, and C, using the DALTON code. $^{77}$

Calculations of the molecular tensors, Equations (6), (7), and (12), have been carried out at the coupled SCF-HF level of accuracy, equivalent to the random-phase approximation, $^{78}$ for the M rotamers. The effects of electron correlation have been estimated via density functional theory, taking into account the B3LYP functional. $^{76}$

Computed values reported in the figures appearing in the text, in Tables S1-S4 and in Figures S3-S11 are given in atomic units. Conversion factors from a.u. to SI units, obtained by an updated compilation, $^{79}$ are $e^{2} a_{0}^{3} / m_{\mathrm{e}}=4.175756730 \times 10^{-39} \mathrm{JT}^{-2} \mathrm{~m}$ for the anapole magnetisability $a_{\alpha \beta}$ and $e^{2} a_{0}^{3} / \hbar=3.607015578 \times 10^{-35}$ $\mathrm{Fm}^{3} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ for the MEMDP tensor $\kappa_{\alpha \beta}^{\prime}$.

An attempt at testing the quality of B3LYP functional for $a_{\alpha \beta}$ was made by comparison with coupled cluster singles and doubles (CCSD) computations of anapole magnetizability reported in Figure 3. ## 5 | DISCUSSION

The curves represented in Figures 3, and in Figures S3 and S4, for the anapole magnetizability of $\mathrm{N}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{4}$ at CCSD, HF, and B3LYP levels are characterized by some common features:

- The diagonal components and the trace of computed $a_{\alpha \beta}$ tensors vanish for values 0 and $180^{\circ}$ of the dihedral angle $\phi \equiv \angle$ $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{H}$, corresponding respectively to $C_{2 v}$ and $C_{2 h}$ point group symmetries, that is, cis and trans configurations, identified by the presence of $\sigma_{v}$ and $\sigma_{h}$ symmetry planes.
- In a wide range of configurations with $C_{2}$ symmetry, a quite small maximum of $a_{y y}$ is observed in the proximity of $\phi=90^{\circ}$, between two null values, possibly coinciding with saddle points at $\approx 60^{\circ}$ and $\approx 120^{\circ}$; minima occur at $\phi \approx 30^{\circ}$ and $\phi \approx 150^{\circ}$. The vanishing values of $a_{y y}$ evaluated in these regions cannot be related to the existence of improper rotations: the corresponding conformations are by all means chiral. At any rate, the magnitude of $a_{y y}$ is much smaller than that of $a_{x x}$ and $a_{z z}$ for $0^{\circ}<\phi<180^{\circ}$, excluding $\phi \approx 90^{\circ}$. A similar trend had been observed for computed anapole magnetizabilities of hydrogen peroxide, see figure 3 of Reference 45, clearly showing that $a_{y y}$ is the smallest diagonal component. This seems to suggest that toroidal flow about the $\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{O}$ bond of hydrogen peroxide and $\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{N}$ bond of hydrazine is comparatively unfavoured.

- Maximum (minimum) value of $a_{xx}$ and minimum (maximum) value of $a_{zz}$ are found for $\phi \approx 50^{\circ}$ ($\phi \approx 140^{\circ}$).

- The contributions provided by electron correlation effects do not seem relevant enough to determine a change of shape of curves and magnitude of computed diagonal components. The maximum value of $a_{xx} \approx 0.17$ a.u. predicted at B3LYP level is slightly higher than the corresponding CCSD estimate, see Figure 3, and Figure S4.

The diagonal components of the MEMDP $\kappa'_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ and the isotropic $\kappa'_{\alpha v}(\omega)$ computed at the B3LYP level are displayed in Figure 4 and Figure S5, for two values of $\omega$. We observe that, in both figures,

- All the curves intersect at $\phi = 90^{\circ}$, where $\kappa'_{xx} \approx \kappa'_{yy} \approx \kappa'_{zz} \approx \kappa'_{\alpha v} \approx 0$, which cannot be ascribed to the presence of improper rotations for a conformer of $C_2$ point group symmetry, reasonably expected to be chiral. This feature can legitimately be considered anomalous and quite interesting from the physical point of view.

- Null values of $\kappa'_{yy}$ and $\kappa'_{zz}$, which cannot be explained in terms of symmetry, are also found within the range $40^{\circ} \leq \phi \leq 60^{\circ}$ in Figure 4, for $\omega$ coincident with the sodium D line.

- The diagonal components of $a_{\alpha\beta}$ computed for $B_2H_4$ are displayed in Figure 5, and Figures S6 and S7. They are qualitatively similar to those obtained for $N_2H_4$, evidencing null values also for $\phi = 90^{\circ}$, that is for a geometrically achiral conformer of $D_{2d}$ symmetry.
Quite remarkably, the $a_{yy}$ component is much smaller than that observed for hydrazine, and nearly vanishing, on the scale of Figure 5, and Figures S6 and S7, all over the intervals $0^{\circ} < \phi < 90^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ} < \phi < 180^{\circ}$, that is, for rotamers of $D_2$ symmetry.
By magnifying, an oscillating curve vanishing for $\phi = 90^{\circ}$ is observed. However, in the proximity of a maximum for $\phi = 140^{\circ}$, $a_{yy}$ of boranylborane molecule evaluated at HF, B3LYP, and CCSD levels is approximately 150 times smaller than $a_{xx}$. Thus, for the $a_{yy}$ component of boranylborane, left and right of the dihedral angle $\phi = 90^{\circ}$, one can properly advocate the notion of cryptochirality first introduced by Mislow. $^{80-82}$

- Diagonal components and average value of $\kappa'_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ for $B_2H_4$ are shown in Figure 6 and in Figure S8. All these quantities vanish for the geometrically achiral rigid conformer corresponding to $\phi = 90^{\circ}$.
At variance with $a_{yy}$, the magnitude of $\kappa'_{yy}(\omega)$ in Figure 6, for $\omega = 0.077278$ a.u., is quite large for $\phi \neq 90^{\circ}$, with a maximum (minimum) in the vicinity of $\phi = 40^{\circ}$ ($\phi = 150^{\circ}$). Extremum points of $\kappa'_{yy}(\omega)$ for $\omega = 0.128347$ a.u., are found approximately in the same range of $\phi$ values.

Large oscillations of $\kappa'_{xx}(\omega)$ and $\kappa'_{zz}(\omega)$ are observed in Figure S8 for $\phi \approx 35^{\circ}$ and $\phi \approx 145^{\circ}$, suggesting the presence of a resonant frequency, which was confirmed by a computation with the SYSMOIC code. $^{83}$ We determined a singlet excitation at 3.5197 eV, corresponding to 352.26 nm, quite close to 355.001 nm, corresponding to 0.128347 a.u., with $\omega = E_h/\hbar = 4.134137336 \times 10^{16}$. Angular frequency in a.u. and wavelength in nm are connected by $\omega$(a.u.) $= 45.563352\ 6/\lambda$(nm).

- The curves displayed in Figures 7 and 8, and Figures S9-S11 for $0^{\circ} < \phi < 60^{\circ}$, corresponding to $D_3$ conformations of ethane bounded by $D_{3h}$ and $D_{3d}$ rotamers, indicate absence of anomalous achirality. This finding implies that the molecular structure of rigid configurations within that range of dihedral angles does not inhibit the onset of electronic currents inducing either static anapole moments or dynamic electric and magnetic dipole moments.

An explanation can now be attempted for manifestation of nearly vanishing diagonal components of anapole magnetizability of the hydrazine rotamer corresponding to the dihedral angle $\phi = 90^{\circ}$, and for the physical causes determining a very small - or virtually null - $a_{yy}$, with $y$ the B-B bond direction, all over $0^{\circ} < \phi < 180^{\circ}$ in boranylborane.

Bearing in mind a useful model for magnetic field-induced toroidal flow $^{53}$ applied in a previous study of hydrogen peroxide, $^{45}$ and that displayed in Figure 1, one can tentatively assume that a magnetic field $B_y$ induces electronic currents with the shape of a topological torus about the $y$ axis, and consequently an anapole moment, $^{51}$

$$\Delta\langle\widehat{a}_y\rangle = a_{yy}B_y,$$

along this direction, analogous to that found in dithiins. $^{84}$

Thus, from Figure 3, and Figures S3 and S4, one can infer that induction of anapole moments along x, y, and z directions in hydrazine is almost completely hampered for the $C_2$ conformation corresponding to $\angle\ \text{H-N-N-H} = 90^{\circ}$. For boranylborane, the $a_{yy}$ component of the anapole magnetizability is virtually vanishing, as displayed in Figure 5, and Figures S6 and S7, over the interval $0^{\circ} \leq \phi \leq 180^{\circ}$, including also conformations with $D_2$ symmetry. Therefore, according to these findings, rotamers chiral from the geometrical point of view, are *physically achiral*, since the outset of toroidal flow, which would determine nonvanishing anapole magnetisability components and chiral response, is not possible.

The different trends observed for $a_{yy}$ in $N_2H_4$ and $B_2H_4$ could possibly be imputed to different hybridization of atomic orbitals at N and B, that is, to different geometric structures, characterized by pyramidal arrangement of a lone pair and two N-H bonds about N-N||y in hydrazine. Planarity of two B-H and B-B bonds in boranylborane seems to preclude induction of toroidal currents about B-B almost completely, for any value of $\phi$.

Noticeably, the $a_{yy}$ component is much smaller than $a_{xx}$ and $a_{zz}$, and comparatively less affected by a change of dihedral angle also in the $H_2O_2$ molecule, for which it vanishes in the proximity of $\phi = 100^{\circ}.^{45}$

Figure 4 and Figure S5 for hydrazine, as well as Figure 6 and Figure S8, for boranylborane, can be interpreted allowing for classical models proposed by Kauzmann, $^{46}$ showing that the electrons of chiral molecules in the presence of optical fields move along helical paths. A beam of monochromatic plane-polarized light oscillating with frequency $\omega$, propagating in the z direction, and carrying sinusoidal fields, for example, magnetic field $B_x(\omega)$ and

electric field $E_y(\omega)$, shone on a chiral molecule, induces frequency dependent dipole moments

$$
\begin{align*}
\Delta\langle\hat{\mu}_x(t)\rangle &=\kappa_{xx}'(\omega)\dot{B}_x(t)\omega^{-1}, \\
\Delta\langle\hat{m}_y(t)\rangle &=-\kappa_{yy}'(\omega)\dot{E}_y(t)\omega^{-1}.
\end{align*}
$$

The time-dependent electric field $E_y$ (magnetic field $B_x$) generates an oscillating magnetic moment $\Delta\langle\hat{m}_y\rangle$ (electric moment $\Delta\langle\hat{\mu}_x\rangle$) determined by the time derivative $\dot{E}_y$ ($\dot{B}_x$) via the $\kappa_{yy}'$ ($\kappa_{xx}'$) component of the MEMDP, $^{49,50,85}$ in a spiraling path, assimilated to a solenoid, whose axis lies in the $y$ ($x$) direction. $^{46}$

According to Figure 6, and Figure S8, the diagonal components of the MEMDP of boranylborane vanish only for $\phi=0^\circ$, $\phi=90^\circ$ and $\phi=180^\circ$, that is, the intrinsic chirality of rotamers with $D_2$ symmetry is not thwarted, at variance with hydrazine for $\phi=90^\circ$.

Nonetheless, a substantial difference is noticed with respect to the hydrogen peroxide molecule, characterized by a null value of the isotropic $\kappa_{av}'\equiv\bar{\kappa}'=\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}'/3$ in the proximity of $100^\circ$, due to mutual off- setting of diagonal components with different sign. As such, it cannot be related to the existence of improper rotations. The analogous distinctive feature has been reported also for $\mathrm{H_2S_2}$. $^{86}$

## 6 | CONCLUDING REMARKS AND OUTLOOK

In Section 1 the concept of axial chirality, "used to refer to stereo- isomerism resulting from the non-planar arrangement of four groups in pairs about a chirality axis," $^{17}$ which distinguishes molecular systems not endowed with a stereogenic center, has been reviewed, emphasizing its importance in chemistry, physics and mathematics.

Allowing for the results arrived at in this study, we introduce the notion of physical achirality as the quality of an axially chiral molecule marking a specific geometrically chiral rotamer, characterized by the unexpected null value of a given chiroptical property, for some dihedral angle $\phi$. In other words, the anomalous status of physical achirality is made manifest by the presence of accidental zeros in plots of components of two axial tensors, odd under parity and even under time reversal, at a particular $\phi$.

According to this definition, such a rotamer is non- superimposable to its mirror image. Its point group does not include pseudorotations. Hence, its chiroptical response, expected to be that typical of an object frozen in a given rigid geometry, is frustrated by physical reasons.

Two peculiar physical facts are the hallmark of chiral behavior: induction of (i) electronic anapole moment in the presence of static homogeneous magnetic field, (ii) oscillating electric and magnetic dipole moments operated by the optical fields of a beam of monochromatic light. These induced moments, sustained by electronic currents, are rationalized in terms of intrinsic molecular response properties, static anapole magnetisability and MEMDP, respectively.

However, induction of different current regimes, which drive molecular response properties $a_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\kappa_{\alpha\beta}'$, may be impeded to a different extent all over the range of a chiral pathway, causing accidental zeros to appear at $\phi$ values that do not coincide for both quantities. Actually, we found that in the rotamer of hydrazine which corresponds to a dihedral angle $\phi\equiv\angle\ \mathrm{H-N-N-H}=90^\circ$, belonging to $C_2$ symmetry, only the $a_{xx}$ and $a_{zz}$ (and the isotropic value) of the static anapole magnetizability tensor vanish, whereas the three diagonal components of frequency-dependent MEMDP $\kappa_{\alpha\beta}'(\omega)$ are exactly zero.

For the boranylborane molecule, assuming the B-B bond in the $y$ direction, computed $a_{yy}$ values are nearly vanishing over the entire range $0^\circ<\phi<180^\circ$ of dihedral angles $\angle\ \mathrm{H-B-B-H}$, including also conformations with $D_2$ symmetry.

Remarkably, no accidental zero was found for diagonal components and isotropic value of both $a_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\kappa_{\alpha\beta}'$ in ethane, implying no physical achirality in this molecule. Vanishing values were found only for rotamers of $D_{3d}$ and $D_{3h}$ symmetries.

To summarize, both $a_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\kappa_{\alpha\beta}'(\omega)$ provide different, distinctive signatures of chiral molecules. Their unforeseeable null value for geometrically chiral rotamers can only be explained by inferring that some physical mechanisms, which give rise to the typical phenomenology of chiroptical response, are inhibited for certain conformations.

A time-independent magnetic field produces anapole moments associated to currents flowing on the surface of a torus, whereas electrons moving backward and forward along helical paths, under the influence of the fields of a plane-polarized light beam, induce oscillating electric and magnetic dipole moments.

Only a small $a_{yy}$ component of the anapole magnetisability is different from zero. Thus, as explained in detail in Section 5, we suppose that onset of toroidal electron flow about the N-N bond of $\mathrm{N_2H_4}$, as well as induction of current density along helical paths, are abated in the $\phi=90^\circ$ rotamer.

That's the root of their physical achirality.

This hypothesis should be tested and possibly validated in future investigations, by spatial maps of streamline and modulus of electronic current densities induced by a static magnetic field and by a monochromatic plane wave, for different values of dihedral angle. Hopefully, these maps would document progressive shrinking of toroidal and helical pathways, for example, for hydrazine, on approaching the critical value $\phi=90^\circ$.

Nonetheless, the statement that "the sole necessary criterion for optical inactivity is the presence, in the molecular system, of an equal number of conformational and/or configurational enantiomers," $^{16}$ should be carefully tested on investigating axial chirality, to exclude additional possibilities.

As a matter of facts, the rotation angle of plane-polarized light, Equation (15), depends on the trace $\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}'(\omega).^{60,61}$ It may vanish in the vicinity of the equilibrium geometry, on account of hindered rotation, in

- rotamers characterized by the phenomenology documented for a dihedral angle $\phi\equiv\angle\ \mathrm{H-O-O-H}\approx100^\circ$ in $\mathrm{H_2O_2},^{45}$ where diagonal components of opposite sign offset one another, so that $\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}'(\omega)\approx0$,

- rotamers in which the three diagonal components of $\kappa'_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)$ exactly vanish, as for hydrazine at the dihedral angle $\phi=90^\circ$, as found in the present work.

Eventually, attempts could be made to detect the condition of physical achirality experimentally in nearly rigid rotamers, whose geometry is known with sufficient accuracy. Leucoindigo and biphenyls carrying substituent groups which preclude free rotation about the internuclear axis seem to be promising candidates. If the substituents are different, a chiral biphenyl exists as a pair of axially chiral atropisomers, for example, 6,6'-dinitro-2,2'-diphenic acid, which may exhibit features analogous to those observed for rotamers considered in this work.
