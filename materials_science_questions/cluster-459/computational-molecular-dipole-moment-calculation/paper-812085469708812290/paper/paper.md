# An analysis of the hydrogen bond in BASE-HF complexes using the theory of atoms in molecules

by MARSHALL T. CARROLL and RICHARD F. W. BADER

Contribution from Department of Chemistry,
McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada L8S 4M1

(Received 7 March 1988; accepted 9 May 1988)

The nature, energetics and mechanism of BASE-HF hydrogen bonding (where BASE = OC, SC, $\mathrm{N}_{2}$, HCN, $\mathrm{H}_{3}\mathrm{N}$, $\mathrm{O}_{3}$, SCO, $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$, $\mathrm{N}_{2}\mathrm{O}$, $\mathrm{SO}_{2}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{CO}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}$, HF, $\mathrm{H}_{3}\mathrm{P}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{S}$ and HCl) are examined using the theory of atoms in molecules. The results are obtained from RHF/6-311++G**//6-31G** calcu- lations. A quantitative description of the electron redistribution and changes in atomic properties, including populations, energies, volumes and moments upon hydrogen bond formation are given by the theory, information which in turn provides a qualitative understanding of the hydrogen bond. A hydrogen bond results from the interaction of two closed-shell systems and the theory quan- tifies the concept of the mutual penetration of the van der Waals envelopes of the acid and base molecules. It is found that the extent of this penetration and the factors which govern it are of paramount importance in determining the strength of the interaction.

## 1. Introduction

The theory of atoms in molecules [1] is used to study the nature, mechanics and energetics of gas-phase intermolecular hydrogen-bonded complexes of the type BASE-HF. Through a partitioning of the total charge distribution into atomic subdomains as determined by a quantum boundary condition, atomic properties such as electron populations, energies, first moments and volumes can be calculated. The way in which both the charge density and these properties change upon complex formation provides a physical understanding of hydrogen bonding.

The hydrogen bond was first postulated in G. N. Lewis's laboratory in 1920 [2]. Simple electrostatic models were subsequently developed by Pauling [3] that could qualitatively reproduce trends in observed H-bond energies. Tsubomura [4] and Coulson [5] were the first to use perturbation theory to estimate the contribution of different energy components to the H-bond energy. This theory was later refined by Daudey [6], among others [7-9], and is still the most direct way to obtain estimates of the electrostatic exchange, charge-transfer, polarization and dispersion energy contributions. A widely used alternative involves the decomposition of the inter- action energy using the Hartree-Fock SCF formalism, as developed by Morokuma and others [10, 11]. Kollman has used this analysis to rationalize geometries and interaction energies of a wide variety of H-bonded complexes [12]. However, both the virial theorem and Pauli exclusion principle are violated in this procedure [13]. Mulliken populations [12, 14], distributed and point multipole analyses [12, 14, 15], density difference maps [16], localized molecular orbital [17] and HOMO-LUMO descriptions [13], and charge transfers calculated using the Townes-Dailey model

[18] have also been used in attempts to further the understanding of hydrogen bonding.

No models are employed in the present work. Instead, a theory is used that is an extension of quantum mechanics to subdomains—the theory of atoms in molecules. This theory is based upon a generalization of quantum mechanics to a subsystem of a total system, a generalization that is possible only if the subsystem is bounded by a surface through which the flux in the gradient vector field of the charge density is zero,

$$
\nabla \rho(\mathbf{r}) \cdot \mathbf{n}(\mathbf{r})=0 \quad \text { for all points on the surface. } \tag{1}
$$

The operational statement of theory for a subsystem $\Omega$ is the variational statement of the Heisenberg equation of motion for the observable $G$. For a stationary state the corresponding statement is a variational expression for the hypervirial theorem [1]

$$
\delta E[G \phi, \Omega]=-(\varepsilon / 2)\left\{(i / \hbar)\langle\phi[H, G] \phi\rangle_{\Omega}+\text { complex conjugate }\right\} /\langle\phi, \phi\rangle_{\Omega}. \tag{2}
$$

The quantity $\delta E[G \phi, \Omega]$ is the subsystem projection of the variation in the total energy of the system as caused by the action of the generator of an infinitesimal $(\varepsilon)$ change, $-(i \varepsilon / \hbar) G$, on the state function $\phi$. In general, because of the topology of molecular charge distributions, the zero flux boundary condition leads to a disjoint partitioning of the space of a molecule into a set of subsystems each of which contains a single nucleus—a partitioning into atoms. The average value of every observable, together with its Heisenberg equation of motion is defined for every atom $\Omega$ in a molecule and the average value of a property $M$ for the total system is given by the sum of the atomic average $M(\Omega)$,

$$
\langle M\rangle=\sum_{\Omega} M(\Omega). \tag{3}
$$

The atomic statement of the hypervirial theorem as given in equation (2) yields a variational derivation of all the theorems derived from the Heisenberg equation of motion, including the virial and Ehrenfest theorems [1]. The atomic virial theorem is obtained by setting the operator $G$ equal to $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{p}$ and is of a form identical to that for a total system, namely

$$
2 T(\Omega)=-V(\Omega). \tag{4}
$$

This theorem serves to define the kinetic energy $T(\Omega)$ and potential energy $V(\Omega)$ of the electrons in $\Omega$. Both of these quantities are determined by the quantum mechanical stress tensor which is a functional of the one-electron density matrix and both are therefore expressible as corresponding densities which can be integrated over the basin of an atom. The total energy of atom $\Omega$, which also obeys the requirements of the virial theorem, is

$$
E(\Omega)=T(\Omega)+V(\Omega)=-T(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2} V(\Omega). \tag{5}
$$

In a preceding paper (hereafter referred to a Paper 1) [19], the theory of atoms in molecules and in particular, the laplacian of the charge density, the quantity $\nabla^{2} \rho$, were used to predict the structures and geometries of these same BASE-HF complexes. The laplacian of the charge density has the important property of determining where electronic charge is locally concentrated (where $\nabla^{2} \rho<0$ ) and where it is locally depleted (where $\nabla^{2} \rho>0$ ) [20]. The maxima in the local concentrations of

charge defined in this manner, recover the Lewis model of localized electron pairs while his model of a generalized acid-base reaction is recovered in terms of a local charge concentration of a base attacking a site of local charge depletion in the acid. The geometry of the resulting complex is predicted by the relative positions of the corresponding critical points in the laplacian distribution. The predictions of the geometries of the hydrogen-bonded complexes made in this manner are in good agreement with ab initio and experimental results.

## 2. Method of calculation
Restricted Hartree-Fock SCF 6-311++G**//6-31G** calculations† were performed using GAUSSIAN 82 [21]. The total SCF energies and the predicted dissociation energies $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ are given in table 1. Experimental values of $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ are known for only two cases and for these, the relative errors in the calculated values are 5 and 19 per cent. The results reported in paper 1 were obtained using RHF/6-31G**//6-31G** calculations. Contrary to these previous results and in agreement with experiment [22], the larger basis set correctly predicts the cis complex of $\mathrm{SO}_{2}$ with $\mathrm{HF}$ to be the global minimum, predicting it to be $6.5 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ more stable than the trans isomer. The predictions of the primary minimum energy structures for the remaining complexes were unchanged from those previously obtained [19]. Test calculations using geometries optimized at the larger of the two basis sets lowered the energy by negligible amounts $(0.04 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1})$ and were found to have only a small effect on the geometry (bond lengths change by at most 0.006 a.u.). Thus, the theoretical values given in tables 1 to 11 inclusive are obtained from RHF/6-311++G**//6-31G** calculations.

For a given basis, the calculated value of $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ is too large because of the basis set superposition error (BSSE) [16, 23-33]. This error arises from the fact that the basis set used to describe each reactant is larger for the complex than for the isolated molecule because in the complex use can be made of the basis functions centred on the other reactant.

In the function counterpoise method used to correct for BSSE [23], the energies of the separated reactants are calculated with the full complement of basis functions used in the calculation of the complex. To find the proper description of isolated HF for example, means that in addition to the basis functions centred on the acid nuclei, basis functions of the base molecule, called ghost orbitals, are centred at locations relative to the nuclei in HF that they have in the complex.

The full counterpoise corrections to the total energy of the reactants in HF-HF and $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{~N}-\mathrm{HF}$ are given in the Appendix (table A 1). It has been argued that the full counterpoise correction overestimates the error and that procedures which use only the polarizing functions or the virtual orbitals for the ghost orbitals reduce the correction [25-30]. Thus the results of table A 1 are upper bounds to BSSE. In the strongest of the BASE-HF complexes studied $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{~N}-\mathrm{HF}$, for which the correction will be relatively large, the full counterpoise correction to the energies of the base and acid are $2 \cdot 3$ and $1.0 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ respectively, compared to the calculated $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ of $46.2 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$. Bachrach and Streitwieser [25] examined the effect of BSSE on the electron density and found the corrections to be small when the basis set contains diffuse functions. We have also examined the effects of BSSE on the electron density of the reactants in HF–HF and $\text{H}_{3}\text{N}$–HF (table A 1). The corrections to the average electron populations of an atom $\Omega$, $\text{N}(\Omega)$ are small as are the changes in its first moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\Omega)$ and volume $V(\Omega)$. The largest of these changes are for nitrogen in $\text{H}_{3}\text{N}$. The values of the charge density and laplacian of the charge density at the bond critical points, $\rho(\mathfrak{r}_{c})$ and $\nabla^{2}\rho(\mathfrak{r}_{c})$ also change by small amounts. Thus the charge densities calculated from the $6\text{--}311++\text{G}^{**}$ basis set are relatively insensitive to the basis set superposition error.

BSSE is important when examining interaction energies in large biological systems where minimal basis sets must be used [32]. Attempts to improve corrections to BSSE in these cases have been made [31].

The PROAIM series of programs was used to carry out the charge density analysis [34]. The atomic properties are calculated numerically using gaussian quadrature and their accuracy is such that the sums of the integrated atomic populations and energies never differ from their corresponding values for the total systems by more than $0.0005$ e and $2\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ respectively.

The interaction of two atoms leads to the formation of a critical point in the charge density $\rho$, a point where $\nabla\rho(\mathbf{r}) = 0$, which is characterized by exhibiting one positive and two negative curvatures of $\rho$. The vectors defining the axes of these principal curvatures define the interatomic surface and the bond path. Within each set of H-bonded complexes, where the base atom $B$ is C, N or O, $D_{\text{e}}$ increases with increasing values of $\rho(\mathbf{r}_{\text{b}})$ (with the exception of OOO–HF). Also listed in table 2 under the headings $\rho^{0}(\mathbf{r}_{B})$ and $\rho^{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{H}})$, are the values of $\rho$ in the isolated base and acid molecules at the point of penetration, i.e. at the position determined by the vector corresponding to the bonded radius $r_{B}$ or $r_{\mathrm{H}}$. The sum of $\rho^{0}(\mathbf{r}_{B})$ and $\rho^{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{H}})$ is close in value to $\rho(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{b}})$, the value of the charge density at the hydrogen bond critical point, being either equal to it to within a few per cent or else slightly smaller in value. This analysis yields a picture of hydrogen bond formation which corresponds to the mutual penetration of the outer diffuse nonbonded densities of the base atom $B$ and of $\mathrm{H}$, with the final density at the bond critical point (the point of minimum density along the line of maximum density linking the $B$ and $\mathrm{H}$ nuclei) being equal to or slightly greater than the sum of the unperturbed densities. Unlike a shared or covalent interaction, there is not a large increase in charge density at the bond critical point and in the interatomic surface relative to the unperturbed densities. The final density is instead determined primarily by the extent of penetration-the greater the penetration the larger the value of $\rho(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{b}})$ and the stronger the resulting hydrogen bond. To obtain bonds with $D_{\mathrm{e}}>25 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$, the van der Waals envelope of the $\mathrm{H}$ atom must be penetrated by $\approx 1$ a.u. and that of the $B$ atom by a still larger amount. For these penetrations, both of the unperturbed densities are in excess of $0.01$ a.u.

The changes induced in the charge distribution of the HF bond are monitored by examining the corresponding changes in the properties of its bond critical point (denoted by the position vector $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{a}}$) as reported in table 3. The formation of each

complex results in a decrease in the value of $\rho(\mathbf{r}_a)$ relative to its value of 0.3923 a.u. in the HF monomer (i.e. $\Delta\rho(\mathbf{r}_a)$ is negative), indicating that charge density is removed from the H-F bond. Figure 2(c) shows that $\rho(\mathbf{r}_a)$ decreases linearly with increasing $D_e$. These data provide direct evidence that the HF bond is weakened through a loss of charge density upon formation of a hydrogen bond, a result in accord with the increase in length and decrease in stretching frequency of the $A$-H bond of the acid which is observed to accompany the formation of a hydrogen bond [22,41]. The value of $\nabla^2\rho(\mathbf{r}_a)$ is $-3.3695$ a.u. for the HF monomer, the negative sign implying a shared interaction. Upon complex formation, the curvatures both perpendicular and parallel to the H-F bond path at $\mathbf{r}_a$ decrease in magnitude as $D_e$ increases. This is in contrast to the three curvatures at $\mathbf{r}_b$ which all increase in mangitude as $D_e$ increases. The distance from $\mathbf{r}_a$ to the proton, the bonded radius $r_h$, decreases with increasing $D_e$. The value of $r_h$ in HF is unusually short. This is a consequence of the dominance of the fluorine nucleus in determining the form of the charge distribution, as reflected in the large extent of charge transfer present in this molecule and in the small value for the H nonbonded radius. Since the H-F bond length increases upon complex formation, the bonded radius $r_F$ in general increases with increasing $D_e$. Thus the weakening and lengthening of the H-F bond and the strengthening and shortening of the hydrogen bond as $D_e$ is increased, are reflected in the properties of the charge density at the corresponding bond critical points.

### 4. Atomic properties

An atomic property is determined by integrating the corresponding property density over the basin of an atom $\Omega$. The atomic properties given in tables 4-6 are defined as follows [34]: the average electron population of an atom, $N(\Omega)$, is found by integrating the charge density over the basin of an atom. The net charge $q(\Omega)$ is given by
$$
\mathrm{q}(\Omega)=Z_{\Omega}-N(\Omega), \tag{7}
$$
where $Z_{\Omega}$ is the nuclear charge.

The average kinetic energy of an atom in a molecule is readily obtained by integration of a corresponding kinetic energy density, defined in terms of the first order density matrix, over the basin of the atom. The atomic energy $E(\Omega)$ is then obtained using the virial relationship, equation (5). The atomic virial theorem is not exactly satisified at the $6-311++\mathrm{G}^{**}$ level of calculation, and the ratio $-V/T$ differs slightly from the correct value of 2 for an equilibrium geometry (table 1). To correct for the error in the virial, each atomic kinetic energy must be multiplied by the factor $V/T+1$ to obtain a set of atomic energies that correctly sum to the total energy of the molecule. The factor $|V/T+1|$ while close to unity, the value it should equal for an equilibrium geometry, is not the same for all of the molecules. Because of this, one cannot compare the atomic energies between the reactants and complexes in every case as the correction factors differ between molecules. There are however, two systems $\mathrm{O}_3$-HF and SCO-HF where the factors are similar enough to permit a meaningful comparison of the changes in the atomic energies on complex formation. The errors in the virial derive principally from the failure of the functions to satisfy the criterion of vanishing Hellmann-Feynman forces on the nuclei at the equilibrium configuration. (The situation is not noticeably improved if the geometry is optimized using the same basis set as used in the calculation of the state function.)

These errors increase with nuclear charge, as does the energy itself and the virial is very close to being satisfied for the hydrogen atoms. This result is not unexpected, since the calculated $(V/T + 1)$ ratios correct the energy of a hydrogen atom by less than $1\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and the difference between the largest and smallest of these corrections amounts to only $0.5\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. Thus the energies of the hydrogen atoms may be compared throughout the complete set of molecules. The energies of all the atoms in the two systems noted above may also be compared. Only the overall trends in the changes in the total energies of the base and acid molecules in forming the complex, as listed in table 4, will be discussed.

The dipolar polarization of an atomic distribution is measured by its first moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\Omega)$ which is defined as

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\Omega)=-\int_{\Omega} \mathbf{r}_{\Omega} \rho(\mathbf{r}) d \tau
\tag{8}
$$

where $\mathbf{r}_{\Omega}$ is the electronic position vector with origin at the nucleus. Its magnitude is denoted by $|\boldsymbol{\mu}(\Omega)|$. The atomic volume, $V(\Omega)$, is found by integrating the volume element $d\tau$ over the basin of an atom out to the intersection of the zero-flux surface with the 0·001 a.u. charge density envelope [40]. As stated above, this envelope yields molecular sizes in good agreement with measured van der Waals radii in the gas phase. Atomic properties are additive (e.g. $\sum_{\Omega} E(\Omega)=E(\text{molecule})$). This fact is essential to the prediction of observed additivity schemes for molecular properties based on the transferability of group properties [42, 43].

### 4.1. Isolated reactants
Table 5 listed $N(\Omega)$, $|\boldsymbol{\mu}(\Omega)|$ and $V(\Omega)$ for the atoms in the isolated reactants. Molecular graphs of the reactants showing the values of $q(\Omega)$ and the directions and magnitudes of $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\Omega)$ are presented in figure 4. In general, because of the fields created by the transfer of charge from one atom to another, the charge distributions of the atoms become polarized in a direction counter to the direction of charge transfer (figures 3 and 4). In isolated HF for example, 0·753 e are transferred from H to F. The fluorine, responding to the net positive electric field exerted upon it by hydrogen, polarizes towards its neighbour, while the latter atom polarizes in the same direction, away from F, in response to the negative field emanating from F.

These atomic moments are essential to the prediction and understanding of molecular dipole moments and their change upon nuclear displacements [43]. When the donor atom possesses relatively loosely bound, nonbonded charge density, such as C in OC or P or S in their hydrides, the atomic moment can become quite large. This back polarization of the nonbonded density resulting from the charge transfer field is reflected in the values of the nonbonded radii of these atoms. The nonbonded radius of C in OC for example, is larger than the atomic radius of the free C atom by 0·18 a.u. The large atomic moments on the atoms in OC, particularly that for C account for the near zero value for the dipole moment observed for this molecule in spite of a considerable degree of charge transfer. The value of $|\boldsymbol{\mu}(\text{P})|$ in $\text{H}_3\text{P}$ is the largest of the atomic moments found in this work. The directions of the dipole moments of $\text{H}_3\text{P}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ are determined by atomic rather than charge transfer moments.

### 4.2. Changes in atomic properties on hydrogen bond formation
The changes in the populations, energies and volumes of the acid and base molecules upon hydrogen bond formation are given in table 4 and the corresponding changes in these same properties, save the energies, are given in table 6 for the individual atoms.

In all cases there is a transfer of charge from the base to the acid, $\mathrm{HF}$, in agreement with Lewis' generalized definition of an acid and a base as an electron acceptor and donor, respectively. The strongest of the complexes studied here, $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{~N}-\mathrm{HF}$, has the greatest number of electrons transferred (0.046e) while the weakest complex, NN-HF has among the least (0.005e) (table 4). The former complex exhibits the greatest degree of interpenetration of the van der Waals envelopes of the acid and base molecules, and the latter among the least.

The magnitude of the transfer of charge from base to acid is relatively small compared to the changes in the individual atomic charges (table 6). There is therefore, a considerable redistribution of charge within the acid and the base. A consequence of this redistribution is a loss of electronic charge from the 'tail' of the base (the $X$ group) and a comparable gain in negative charge by the 'head' of the acid (the $\mathrm{F}$ of $\mathrm{HF}$ ). For example, in $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{~N}-\mathrm{HF}, 0.078 \mathrm{e}$ are lost by $X$ and $0.066 \mathrm{e}$ are gained by $\mathrm{F}$. As a result of this charge transfer, across the length of the complex, the dipole moment of the complex is greater than the vector sum of the moments of the isolated reactants, an effect experimentally observed and termed 'dipole moment enhancement' [22] $(\S 4.3) \dagger$.

As anticipated on the basis of the above discussion of the decreases in nonbonded radii of $B$ and $\mathrm{H}$, the volumes of these atoms decrease on hydrogen bond formation (figure 3). In fact, there is an overall decrease in volume for the entire base fragment and where $B$ is a second-row atom, for the entire acid fragment as well (table 4). There is a rough correlation between the decrease in volume upon complexation and increasing $D_{\mathrm{e}}$.

In general, the acid is stabilized ( $\Delta E$ (acid) is negative) except when the base contains third-row atoms $\mathrm{P}, \mathrm{S}$ or $\mathrm{Cl}$. The base is stabilized when $B$ is a third-row atom and in all other cases with the exceptions of OC, NN, HCN, OCO, NNO and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{CO}$.

The most important trends are to be found in the changes of the properties of the $B, \mathrm{H}$ and $\mathrm{F}$ atoms. The changes in the atomic properties of the acid $\mathrm{HF}$ are considered first. Fluorine gains charge in all cases, in amounts ranging from $0.012 \mathrm{e}$ for the weakest complex NN-HF to $0.066 \mathrm{e}$ for the strongest complex $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{~N}-\mathrm{HF}$. It acquires charge not only from the base, but also from $\mathrm{H}$ since the bond critical point in HF shifts closer to the proton upon complexation (table 3). $\mathrm{H}$ loses charge in all cases (except in $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{P}-\mathrm{HF}$ where $\Delta N(\mathrm{H}) \approx 0$ ) in amounts ranging from 0.002 to $0.024 \mathrm{e}$. In all complexes, the charge gained by $\mathrm{F}$ is greater than the charge lost by $\mathrm{H}$ and, therefore, $\Delta N$ (acid) increases.

The decrease in $V(\text{H})$ ranges between $1.0$ and $5.8$ a.u. The increase in $V(\text{F})$ ranges between $0.2$ and $3.8$ a.u. $V(\text{H})$ decreases mainly because of the creation of the $B$–$\text{H}$ interatomic surface which removes the outermost charge density from $\text{H}$ (and $B$) (figure 3). The removal of this density is a factor contributing to the change in $\boxed{\mu}(\text{H})$.

Although $|\Delta N(\text{H})|$ increases linearly with $D_{\text{e}}$ within each set, it does not do so overall. However, $\Delta E(\text{H})$ does exhibit such a relationship through the complete series of molecules (figure 2 (d)). The nonbonded density on $\text{H}$ is penetrated by the base, $\text{H}$ loses charge and is consequently destabilized. The linear relationship between $\Delta E(\text{H})$ and $D_{\text{e}}$ emphasizes the importance of the perturbation of the charge distribution of $\text{H}$ in hydrogen bonding and in particular it demonstrates again the dependance of $D_{\text{e}}$ on the degree of penetration of the nonbonded density on the $\text{H}$ atom. Table 7 lists the contributions to the changes in potential energy for the $\text{H}$ atom. The symbol $V_{\text{H}}(\text{H})$ denotes the potential energy of interaction of the charge density within the basin of the $\text{H}$ atom with its own nucleus. Because of the loss of charge by $\text{H}$, the change in this quantity is positive and the effect on the energy of $\text{H}$ is destabilizing. The quantity $V_{A}(\text{H})$, the total attractive potential energy, is the interaction of all the nuclei in the system with the charge density of the $\text{H}$ atom. This quantity decreases on the formation of the hydrogen bond and is stabilizing because of the interaction of the nuclei of the base, particularly that of the $B$ atom, with the density of the $\text{H}$ atom. The repulsive contributions to the energy of the $\text{H}$ atom, the electron–electron and nuclear–nuclear repulsions which are grouped under the heading $V_{\text{R}}(\text{H})$, increase by more than the attractive interactions decrease and overall, the $\text{H}$ atom is destabilized. (By the virial theorem, $\Delta E(\text{H}) = \frac{1}{2}[\Delta V_{A}(\text{H}) + \Delta V_{\text{R}}(\text{H})]$).

In all the systems studied, $\text{H}$ undergoes a reduction in polarization upon H-bond formation. This reduction is primarily the result of the loss of its non-bonded density. The moment $\boxed{\mu}(\Omega)$ is determined by the average value of $\boldsymbol{r}$ over the atomic charge distribution and its value is therefore, sensitive to the amount of density relatively far from the nucleus. This counter polarization of the charge density of the hydrogen atom is enhanced when the base atom $B$ is negatively charged as in the complexes with $\text{H}_{3}\text{N}$, $\text{H}_{2}\text{CO}$ and $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$. These three complexes possess the greatest extent of mutual penetration and the three largest values of $D_{\text{e}}$. In the complexes with $\text{OC}$, $\text{H}_{2}\text{S}$ and $\text{H}_{3}\text{P}$, the $B$ atoms are positively charged and consequently, the corresponding $\Delta|\boxed{\mu}(\text{H})|$ values are the smallest of those listed in table 6.

We now focus our attention on the atoms of the base molecule $XB$. Upon hydrogen bond formation, the base molecule loses charge in all instances as does the $X$ group. The $\text{B}$ atom gains charge if $B$ is a second-row atom but loses charge if $B$ is a third-row atom. Both $B$ and $X$ decrease in volume and, in general, $|\Delta V(B)| > |\Delta V(X)|$. In general, the greatest volume changes occur for those $B$ atoms with diffuse regions of nonbonded density.

The $XB$–$\text{HF}$ complexes are dividied into two categories: those which have the change in the first moment of $B$ directed away from $\text{H}$ and those which have this change directed towards $\text{H}$. The bases belonging to the first class are $\text{H}_{3}\text{N}$, $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$, $\text{HF}$, $\text{H}_{3}\text{P}$, $\text{H}_{2}\text{S}$, $\text{HCl}$, $\text{NN}$, $\text{HCN}$, $\text{OC}$, $\text{SC}$ and $\text{O}_{3}$. Those belonging to the second class all have oxygen as the base atoms $B$ and are $\text{SCO}$, $\text{OCO}$, $\text{NNO}$, $\text{OSO}$ and $\text{H}_{2}\text{CO}$. The signs of $\Delta|\boxed{\mu}(B)|$ need not be the same for two bases belonging to one class, since the original polarization of $B$ may be directed towards $\text{H}$ as in $\text{H}_{3}\text{N}$ where $\Delta|\boxed{\mu}(\text{N})| < 0$, or away from $\text{H}$ as in $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ where $\Delta|\boxed{\mu}(\text{O})| > 0$ (figure 4).

The change in $\boldsymbol{\mu}(B)$ away from $\mathrm{H}$ in the first class of complexes is rationalized as follows: The creation of the $B-\mathrm{H}$ interatomic surface removes diffuse density from $B$ and this decreases its atomic moment. This effect is most pronounced for those base molecules which initially possess diffuse regions of nonbonded density on the base atom $B$, such as found in $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{~N}, \mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{P}, \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{~S}, \mathrm{OC}$ and $\mathrm{SC}$. In each complex of the first class, with the exception of $(\mathrm{HF})_{2}$, the $X$ group loses more charge than $\mathrm{H}$ and this, coupled with the fact that the $X$ group is closer to $B$ means that the electric field generated by $X$ is dominant. Thus both effects cause a change in polarization of $B$ away from $\mathrm{H}$.

While at first surprising, this back polarization of the density on $B$ leads to an increase in $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ (table 8). Within each group of base atoms in this class, one finds a correlation of the magnitude of the change in the polarization of the base atom $B$ away from the proton with the extent of the mutual penetration of both the $B$ and $\mathrm{H}$ atoms and hence with $D_{\mathrm{e}}$. As noted above, the change in the polarization of $\mathrm{H}$ is also directed away from the bonded region. In other words, the removal of density from the bonded region resulting from these polarizations facilitates the approach of the two closed-shell systems.

The $B$ atoms of the second class of molecules are oxygen atoms and their nonbonded density is tightly bound. For example, the nonbonded radius of $\mathrm{C}$ in $\mathrm{OC}$ is much larger than that of $\mathrm{O}$ in SCO (table A2). The creation of a new $\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{H}$ interatomic surface upon complexation and the concomitant removal of diffuse density does not have a large effect on $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{O})$ owing to the tightness of the $\mathrm{O}$ charge distribution. Therefore, field effects play a dominant role in determining changes in $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{O})$. In OCO-HF and OSO-HF, the base atom neighbouring $B$ loses charge but less than that lost by $\mathrm{H}$ and the net field is such as to cause the change in $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{O})$ to be directed towards $\mathrm{H}$. In SCO-HF, NNO-HF and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{CO}-\mathrm{HF}$, the base atom neighbouring $B$ gains charge on complexation and the change in $\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{O})$ is again directed towards $\mathrm{H}$. Since the change in polarization of $B$ is directed towards $\mathrm{H}$ in this class of molecules, the extent of mutual penetration of the $B$ and $\mathrm{H}$ atoms and the value of $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ increase with a decrease in the value of $|\Delta \boldsymbol{\mu}(B)|$ (table 8). The value of $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ increases with a decrease in the extent to which the polarization of $B$ places density in the bonded region.

Unlike a shared or covalent interaction where charge is accumulated in the bonded region, these interactions are strengthened by polarizations of both $B$ and $\mathrm{H}$ which lead to a lessening of charge in this region, thereby facilitating their mutual penetration. While $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ does increase with increasing charge density at the bond critical point, the values of $\rho\left(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$, which are approximately the sum of the unperturbed densities, increase because of increasing penetration and not because of polarizations of charge into the bonded region. Further evidence of a relation between the polarization of density on $B$ and the extent of penetration is provided by the $\sigma$ to $\pi$ promotion of the density on $B$ which, as discussed next, accompanies the formation of a hydrogen bond. Other authors have used electrostatic and associated polarizability arguments to discuss the energy changes of the reactants upon H-bond formation [44-47].

The complexes listed in table 9 are linear and one may distinguish between the contributions of the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ orbitals to the atomic populations for each of these molecules [48]. The changes in these contributions, together with the total atomic $\pi$ population in the complex are given in table 9. In each complex, HF has a $\pi$ population which varies from its unperturbed value of four by only $\pm 0.002 \mathrm{e}$, and

there is essentially no direct transfer of $\pi$ density from the base to the acid. The loss and gain of charge by $X$ and $B$ respectively, is primarily a result of changes in their $\pi$ populations. In fact, the $\pi$ charge lost by $X$ is nearly equal to that gained by $B$ and it is possible to refer to a corresponding transfer of $\pi$ charge from $X$ to $B$. The $\pi$ population of the $B$ atoms increase, while their $\sigma$ populations decrease. This implies that the formation of the hydrogen bond causes a promotion of $\sigma$ into $\pi$ density. An exception to this behaviour is found for $N$ in $N_2$. Only in this molecule, which forms the weakest complex, is there an increase in the $\sigma$ density of the base atom $B$. This atom exhibits the smallest penetration of the nonbonded density of the $H$ atom of the acid (table 2). Those base atoms showing the largest $\sigma$ to $\pi$ promotion, $C$ of SC and $N$ of HCN, also possess the greatest extent of penetration of the nonbonded density of the $H$ atom. The $\sigma$ to $\pi$ promotion corresponds to the transfer of nonbonded density on $B$ from a distribution which attains its maximum value on the axis of approach of $B$ to $H$ to one which is a maximum in a torus-like distribution about this axis. This redistribution of charge facilitates the approach of two closed-shell systems and, like the polarizations of $B$ and $H$ discussed above, leads to a greater penetration of their charge distributions and to a stronger hydrogen bond. This same promotion of density out of the plane of the hydrogen bond to a $\pi$-like distribution concentrated above and below this plane has been found to occur for the base atoms in the cyclic formamide dimer [49].

These considerations make the differences in $D_e$ values between complexes understandable, between those of OC and SC, for example. The positively charged carbon in OC possesses a diffuse nonbonded density distribution, but the remaining density of OC is tightly bound and does not undergo significant relaxations on forming the complex. In SC, carbon is negatively charged and more readily polarized, in addition to possessing diffuse nonbonded density (table A 2). All of the charge relaxations accompanying the formation of the complex are greater for SC than for OC: S transfers nearly four times as much charge as does the more electronegative O and the carbon atom gains more charge in SC than in OC by close to a factor of ten; $C$ in SC is polarized towards the soft and positively charged $S$ and this is increased on complexation, while $C$ in OC undergoes only a very small change in polarization towards the hard, negatively charged $O$; the $\sigma$ to $\pi$ polarization is greatest for $C$ in SC. As a consequence of these differences, the mutual penetration of $C$ and $H$ is greater in SC than in OC by 0.34 a.u. and SC forms the stronger hydrogen bond.

Table 10 summarizes the changes in energy and population of each atom in the complexes with $O_3$ and SCO which have nearly equal values for $D_e$. A gain or loss of charge results in a corresponding increase or decrease in the stability of each atom. As noted above, the $H$ atom is destabilized in all systems. The $F$ and $B$ atoms are stabilized in both complexes, more so in the $O_3$ complex because of greater gains in charge, particularly for the $B$ atom. The change in energy per change in charge for both $F$ and $H$ are of the same order of magnitude for both complexes, but differ by a factor of three for the $B$ atom. The two molecules belong to different classes with regard to the change in polarization of the $B$ atom and both exhibit the least favourable type of change. The polarization of the $B$ atom towards $H$ is essentially unchanged in $O_3$ while in SCO, it undergoes the largest of the polarizations towards H (table 8). Consequently of the two, the penetration of the $B$ atom is greatest in the former complex. In the $O_3$ complex, both of the remaining atoms in the base lose charge and are destabilized by an amount almost equal to the

stabilization energy of the $B$ atom and overall the base fragment is stabilized by only $2.6\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$. In the SCO complex, the C atom gains more charge than does the base atom O and is considerably stabilized. The S atom on the other hand undergoes a loss in charge in excess of that gained by O and C and is destabilized so that the final base fragment is stabilized by $6.3\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$.

It is clear from this and the preceeding discussions, that all of the atoms of the acid and base molecules in these systems are perturbed by similar amounts on forming a hydrogen bond and all undergo comparable changes in charge and energy. Among the most important characteristics of an atom of theory is that its properties, including its energy, change in direct response to changes in its distribu- tion of charge in real space. Thus whether the form of an atom changes by a little or a lot, its energy changes by a corresponding amount. This relationship has been illustrated by the properties of the atoms making up the transferable groups in the normal series of hydrocarbons and their strained counterparts in cyclic members of the series [42]. Thus the changes in energy of the atoms on hydrogen bond forma- tion as found here are a direct response to corresponding changes in their charge distributions, changes for well defined spatial regions.

### 4.3. Dipole moment enhancement

Dipole moments of some of the BASE-HF complexes have been measured experimentally [22] and they, along with the theoretically determined values, are listed in table 11. The theoretical results overestimate the experimental values of the dipole moment with the exceptions of OC-HF, NNO-HF and $\text{H}_2\text{CO-HF}$. It is experimentally observed that the dipole moment of the complex is always greater than the vector sum of the dipole moments of the isolated reactants [22] and this result is predicted by the theoretical calculations. This dipole moment enhancement (DME) along with the theoretical values are listed in table 11. The theoretical values are in fair agreement with experiment. The theory of atoms in molecules demon- strates that the dipole moment enhancement is a result of a loss of charge by the the $X$ group (the tail) of the base and a gain by the F (the head) of the acid (table 6). This effective transfer of charge across the length of the complex creates a moment which adds to the head to tail alignment of the base and acid dipoles to yield a moment that is greater than the sum of the reactant dipoles. The competing moment arising from a gain of charge by atom $B$ of the base and a loss of charge by H, is of smaller magnitude since the distance over which this effective charge trans- fer occurs is much shorter than the head to tail distance between F and $X$.

### 5. Discussion

It has been claimed that a successful theory of the hydrogen bond has to explain the properties of H-bonded complexes listed below, for which extensive experimen- tal evidence is available [41]. Each of these observations is followed by its explana- tion as afforded by the theory of atoms in molecules.

(1) 'Molecules forming H-bonds approach much closer than the sums of the van der Waals radii of the nearest atoms would allow.' The theory of atoms in molecules quantifies the concept of the penetration of van der Waals envelopes and relates the extent of penetration to the strength of the hydro-

gen bond. The basis of this relation lies in the closed-shell nature of the hydrogen bond as characterized by the properties of the charge density at its critical point.

(2) 'The dipole moment of the complex is larger than the vectorial addition of the monomer moments.' This so-called dipole moment enhancement is readily accounted for in terms of the changes in the atomic populations as an effective transfer of charge from the tail of the base to the head of the acid.

(3) 'The electron density at H is decreased and so N.M.R. signals are shifted to lower magnetic fields.' Indeed, $\Delta N(\mathrm{H})$ is negative in all complexes studied in this work and also in other hydrogen-bonded complexes [40, 49]. The accompanying descreening of the proton lowers the energy of $\mathrm{H}$ in the complex.

(4) 'Upon hydrogen bond formation, the $\mathrm{H}-Y$ bond of the acid molecule $\mathrm{H} Y$ is lengthened and its I.R. stretching frequency is shifted to smaller wave numbers.' $A b$ initio calculations confirm the elongation of the bond (table 1).The redistribution of charge which leads to the weakening of the $\mathrm{H}-Y$ bond and the concomitant strengthening of the hydrogen bond are charac- terized by the properties of the charge density at the corresponding bond critical points.
