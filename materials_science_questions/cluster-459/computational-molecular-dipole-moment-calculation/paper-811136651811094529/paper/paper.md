# THE USE OF THE CNDO METHOD IN SPECTROSCOPY
## VII. CHANGES IN DIPOLE MOMENT UPON EXCITATION

GARY W. KUEHNLENZ AND CONSTANTIN A. MASMANIDIS
Department of Chemistry, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio 45221 (U.S.A.)

H. H. JAFFÉ*
Centre de Mécanique Ondulatoire Appliquée, 23, rue du Maroc, 75019 Paris (France)

(Received 18 September 1972)

## ABSTRACT

The results of the calculations of changes in dipole moment upon excitation of some organic molecules are discussed in connection with recent high resolution spectroscopic measurements of the same quantities. Calculations are reported for several substituted benzenes, several azines, and for formaldehyde, propynal, formyl fluoride, difluorodiazirine and azulene. The calculations were carried out within the CNDO/S parametrization scheme.

## INTRODUCTION

Recently, two new experimental methods have been reported which permit the direct measurement of changes in the dipole moment of molecules upon excitation $^{1,2}$. Both methods are based upon the Stark splitting of the rotational levels of the O-O vibrational band of the electronic transition. The first $^{1,3-11}$ consists in observing the electronic spectrum of a molecule in the gas phase, while an electric field is applied to the sample. The application of an external field removes the spatial degeneracy of the rotational sublevels of the O-O band, since all directions in space are no longer energetically equivalent, resulting in the observed splitting of the rotational lines. To the first order, the magnitude of this splitting is proportional to

$$W_{\text {Stark }}=-\Delta \mu \frac{E M K}{J(J+1)}$$

* Permanent Address: Department of Chemistry, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio45221, U.S.A.

where $\Delta \boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the modulus of the vector difference of the dipole moments of the two states, $\Delta \boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{\mu}^* - \boldsymbol{\mu}$, $E$ is the magnitude of the applied electric field, $J$ is the total angular momentum quantum number, and $K$ and $M$ are its components along the top axis and the field direction. The magnitude of this splitting is proportional to the difference in the dipole moments, $\Delta \mu$, between the two electronic states. Therefore, the measurement of the Stark splitting allows the determination of the change in dipole moment between the ground and the excited electronic state.

The second method, introduced by Hochstrasser and coworkers $^{2,12}$, is based on the removal of orientational degeneracy of molecules in a single crystal, or of molecules imbedded in a single crystal matrix, under the influence of a strong electric field. This results in the splitting of the O-O levels of the electronic bands. The electric field is applied along the main crystal axis of the sample, and the O-O levels of the desired electronic states are observed. The splitting depends on the crystal structure parameters and is proportional to $\Delta \boldsymbol{\mu} \cdot \boldsymbol{E}$, where $\boldsymbol{E}$ is the electric field vector.

The important point to note here is that both of these methods provide the modulus of the vector difference of the dipole moment. Since most of the ground state dipole moments are reported as positive numbers (that is, as the modulus of the dipole moment vector), confusion may arise when the dipole moment changes direction in the excited state. In such cases a simple algebraic difference is not applicable, and therefore, the modulus of the vector difference may differ from the magnitude of the algebraic difference by as much as $2|\boldsymbol{\mu}^*|$.

The determination of dipole moments has been of considerable value in the understanding of chemical properties of molecules in the ground electronic state. Therefore, it is expected that such measurements in the excited electronic states will lead to similar understanding of their nature and their physical and chemical properties.

## METHOD OF CALCULATION

In this paper we would like to report some calculations of dipole moments of ground and excited states of molecules for which experimental data exist or which are currently under experimental investigation. The calculations are carried out within the CNDO formalism $^{15}$.

In this formalism each Cartesian component of the dipole moment (in Debyes) is approximated as the sum of two parts:

(1) A contribution from the net atomic charge densities
$$
\mu_{q}(x)=4.8 \sum_{\mathrm{A}}\left(Z_{\mathrm{A}}-P_{\mathrm{AA}}\right) x_{\mathrm{A}}
$$
where $x_{\mathrm{A}}$ is the appropriate Cartesian coordinate in Ångstrom units and $Z_{\mathrm{A}}$ and

The method of calculation has one inherent difficulty. Since the excited states are constructed from virtual orbitals of the self-consistent ground state,they are neither themselves self-consistent, nor do they conform to the proper geometry of the excited state. In our opinion, serious as these approximations are, they are dictated by a desire to do calculations in a reasonable time. Given the great limitations of the experimental methods, we believe that the errors introduced by these approximations do not exceed the experimental uncertainty. Hochstrasser has studied molecules of $C_{2 v}$ symmetry. This presents no problems in correlating the experimental and the theoretical results, since in such molecules the $C_{2}$ axis is necessarily coincident with the principal axis and the dipole moment. On the other hand, Lombardi $^{18}$ has studied several molecules of lower symmetry. In molecules of point group $C_{\mathrm{s}}$ the gas phase method, in principle, permits determination of the change of two components of the dipole moment, one along the principal axis, the other along the minor axis. Here, the principal axis is the axis around which the moment of inertia is a maximum, the minor axis the in-plane axis normal to the principal axis; since, in most $C_{\mathrm{s}}$ molecules, these axes do not correspond to the usual Cartesian axes, we have made the appropriate coordinate transformations. Unfortunately, in many molecules, the minor axis components of the dipole moment changes have been too small to be obtained experimentally.

## DISCUSSION

### (A) Molecules of $C_{2 v}$ or higher symmetry

Pyridine, pyridazine and pyrimidine. The dipole moment change for the $n-\pi^{*}$ transition in pyridine has been reported $^{12}$ as $-3.2 \pm 0.1$; our calculated value of -3.32 is in excellent agreement with that value. This large $\Delta \mu$ can be attributed to the significant change in the charge distribution which results from the promo- tion of an electron from a lone pair $(n)$ molecular orbital predominantly localized on the nitrogen atom to a delocalized $\pi^{*}$ molecular orbital. Similar changes are calculated for pyridazine and pyrimidine, but of smaller magnitude since the dipole moments are vectorially reduced due to the presence of two nitrogen atoms. In pyrazine the cancellation is complete due to the $D_{2 \mathrm{~h}}$ symmetry. Measurements on these last three molecules have not been reported in the literature yet, but it has been mentioned that work is in progress $^{19}$.

Pyridine N-oxide. The dipole moment of the first electronically excited state of pyridine N-oxide has recently been reported by Hochstrasser and Wiersma $^{20}$. They conclude that the first excited state must be a $\pi-\pi^{*}$ state because of the small change in dipole moment in going from the ground to the excited state, $\Delta \mu=0.71 \pm 0.03$ D with a ground state dipole moment of 4.13 D. Their claim is further supported by polarization of the fluorescence excitation spectra of other $\mathrm{N}$-oxides $^{21}$, which gave indications by analogy that the state at $320 \mathrm{~nm}$ is most likely not $n-\pi^{*}$. Our CNDO/S calculations are in direct conflict with these con-

clusions, with the calculated results of $\mu(\text{calc.}) = 8.10$, $\mu^*(\text{calc.}) = -3.32$ and $\Delta\mu(\text{calc.}) = 11.42$ D. Analysis of the molecular wave functions indicates that the lowest calculated excited state is an $n-\pi^*$ state of $A_2$ symmetry with a transition energy of 3.6 eV ($f = 0.0$). The next two observed, moderately strong transitions at 280 nm ($f = 0.173$) and 215 nm ($\varepsilon_{\text{max}} = 18000$) are calculated as follows: the first, a pair of nearly degenerate transitions at 4.3 eV (0.000), $^1B_1$, and 4.4 eV (0.249), $^1A_1$, and the second at 5.6 eV (0.418), $^1B_1$; another transition is calculated at 6.3 eV (0.364), $^1A_1$. These calculated transition energies are in good agreement with the observed spectra. In view of the good results with the calculated transitions at 4.4 and 5.6 eV, of $A_1$ and $B_1$ symmetry, it seems improbable that the first calculated $\pi-\pi^*$ transitions at 4.3 eV, $B_1$, is the observed band at 320 nm. Still, this is the only remaining $\pi-\pi^*$ transition. It seems equally unlikely, assuming the 320 nm band to be an $n-\pi^*$ transition after all, that $\Delta\mu$ could be miscalculated by almost 11 D. The problem requires further attention.

Aniline and $p$-fluoroaniline$^{8,9}$. The calculations indicate a reduction in the $\Delta\mu$ in going from aniline to $p$-fluoroaniline because of opposing effects between the two substituents, fluorine and the amino group. The $\Delta\mu$ is still relatively small for the $\pi-\pi^*$ transition as compared to the $n-\pi^*$ transition in the case of pyridine.

Fluorobenzene$^{10}$. The calculated dipole moment for the ground state is in good agreement with the observed value, as is the dipole moment change. The small change is consistent with the observed changes in dipole moments for $\pi-\pi^*$ transitions.

Azulene$^{2b}$. The dipole moment changes for the first and second singlet-singlet transitions of azulene have been determined and are $1.21\pm0.05$ and $1.10\pm0.05$ D, as compared to the calculated dipole moment changes of 1.93 and 5.26, respectively.

### (B) Molecules of $C_s$ symmetry

Phenol and $p$-fluorophenol$^{8,9}$. In both compounds very good agreement is obtained between the calculated and the observed $\Delta\mu$'s. By analogy, we see that the dipole moment changes for $\pi-\pi^*$ states are relatively small in comparison to the $n-\pi^*$ states as in the case of the azines. Furthermore, this small change suggests that the two $\pi$ orbitals involved in the transition are very similar in nature.

The case of phenol is particularly interesting. Experimentally the change in dipole moment upon excitation is negligible ($|\Delta\mu| = 0.2\pm0.2$), and our calculated change for the total modulus dipole moment, $|\Delta\mu| = -0.1$, agrees with this observation. However, upon examination of the components of the total dipole moment we observe that the $\mu_{\text{b}}$ component, which is the dominant component, remains almost constant, whereas the $\mu_{\text{a}}$ component is reversed; however, since

the magnitude is almost unchanged, the modulus of the total moment remains nearly constant, only its direction changes. The $\mu_{\mathrm{b}}$ component seems to be almost entirely due to the oxygen lone pair. The dipole moment along the principal axis reflects a change in charge distribution upon excitation, which presumably is due to polarization of the $\pi$ system (that is, electron migration from the oxygen atom into the phenyl ring upon excitation).

Styrene$^{11}$. The small change in dipole moment further supports the observation that little change in dipole moment occurs upon excitation in agreement with the results of other $\pi-\pi^{*}$ transitions in substituted benzenes.

Formaldehyde$^{3}$, difluorodiazirine$^{18}$, formyl fluoride$^{4}$ and propynal$^{5}$. It appears appropriate to discuss the remaining four molecules together, regardless of their symmetry, because of their small size. In all these molecules the calculated dipole moments for both the ground and the excited states are overestimated.

The three-membered ring compound, difluorodiazirine, for which the lowest lying $n-\pi^{*}$ transition has been reported at 3.5 eV with an observed $\Delta \mu$ of $1.5 \pm 0.2$ D, has a ground state dipole moment of 0.00 D. The CNDO/S calculation indicates a ground state dipole moment of 0.26 D, the $n-\pi^{*}$ transition at 3.3 eV, with $\Delta \mu$ calculated as 3.12 D. The agreement here is similar to that of the other smaller molecules, such as formaldehyde, which also differ from the observed $\Delta \mu$'s by an approximate factor of 2.

Propynal and formyl fluoride are unique among the molecules discussed here, since the ground state dipole moments have been measured by microwave spectroscopy, providing the separate components. This would allow actual comparison between the individual components of the ground and the excited state dipole moments, were it not for the unfortunate circumstance that only the $\mu_{\mathrm{a}}$ components have been reported for the excited states. The experimental value for $\mu_{\mathrm{a}}$ for propynal is $0.7 \pm 0.2$ D, compared with our calculated value of 1.38 D. At first sight, our $|\Delta \mu_{\mathrm{a}}($ calc. $)|$ value is much greater than that experimentally determined. This is so because the authors claim that the ground and excited $\mu_{\mathrm{a}}$ components have the same direction. There is no basis for that assumption since only absolute values of the experimental ground state dipole moments were reported. We think that, on a chemical basis, our model with the significant dipole moment change is correct because a charge separation over the molecular framework would account for the large dipole moment change.

In our calculations on small molecules, we have noticed a consistent trend in the calculated transition energies as being too low. On examination of our method of calculation we concluded that this is probably due to insufficient configuration interaction, because frequently the number of singly-excited configurations of the proper symmetry is quite restricted and therefore insufficient freedom exists for the wavefunctions.

Our purpose has been to report some preliminary calculations in an effort to ascertain the feasibility of further calculations on additional molecules as their excited state dipole moments are being determined by high resolution spectros- copic investigations. Such calculations should prove useful in determining the sign of the dipole moment change.
