## Abstract
Film-like retained austenite was prepared by a quenching + intercritical annealing + tempering (QIAT) heat treatment to study its effect on the low temperature toughness of high strength offshore steel. Based on the Purdy model, the flat growth mechanism of film-like retained austenite was studied and the toughening mechanism of this kind of retained austenite was revealed. The results showed that the microstructure after the QIAT heat treatment was a multi-phase structure containing tempered bainite/martensite, intercritical ferrite and film-like retained austenite. The thickness of the film-like retained austenite was only 0.1 $\mu$m. The yield strength was 716 MPa, the tensile strength was 812 MPa and the low temperature toughness at $-40\ ^\circ$C reached 150 J. The low temperature toughness was significantly improved compared with the quenching + tempering (QT) treatment. Enrichment of Mn in the retained austenite formation region caused a surge in dissipated free energy induced by austenite interface migration, making it a one-dimensional film-like flat growth. The excellent stability of the film-like retained austenite and the large number of low-angle grain boundaries were the reasons for the excellent low temperature toughness of this offshore steel.

## 1. Introduction
Since the beginning of the 21st century, the development and utilization of marine resources have received increasing attention, with the need for the support of advanced marine equipment, which promotes the development of offshore steel with superior performance [1, 2]. The production of traditional high-strength offshore steel ($\geqslant$700 MPa) generally adopts the QT process, which can easily meet the performance requirements in strength, although the toughness index is unstable [3]. The offshore steel is used in the ocean, which has a bad service environment and the large temperature difference between day and night. The demand for low temperature toughness is high. Hence, improving the low temperature toughness of high strength offshore steel has become the focus of the current research and development of offshore steel, which is of great significance.

Studies [4–13] indicate that retained austenite formed by heat treatment in the $\alpha+\gamma$ two-phase region can improve the low temperature toughness of the steel. Initially, the technology of heat treatment in the two-phase region was generally used to produce high alloy low temperature steel. Nikura and Morris [6, 7] adopted fine grain strengthening and two-phase region tempering to form stable retained austenite, so that it can maintain ultra-high strength and toughness at $-196\ ^\circ$C. Kim *et al* [8] used intercritical annealing in the two-phase region to improve the low temperature toughness of 5.5Ni steel. Zhang [9] and Lei [10] obtained retained austenite by quenching at the two-phase region for 9Ni steel to improve its toughness. In recent years, Zhou and Xie *et al* [2, 11–13] achieved the purpose of regulating the plasticity and toughness of low-carbon and low-alloy steel by adopting multi-step intercritical heat treatment to obtain retained austenite. It solved the cost problem of high-alloy steel and provided a new idea for improving the low temperature toughness of high strength steel under a

However, the temperature range of intercritical heat treatment is narrow, and the multi-step intercritical treatment leads to a complicated production process, which is difficult to control in industrial production. Therefore, how to achieve the enhancement of low temperature toughness of high strength offshore steel under low alloy and simple heat treatment is still worthy of further study. In addition, there is no unified argument for the formation mechanism and toughening mechanism of film-like retained austenite, which requires further exploration and research.

In the present work, the composition design of low carbon low alloy was adopted and one-step intercritical annealing in the two-phase region was added between the traditional QT processes [quenching + intercritical annealing + tempering (QIAT)] to obtain retained austenite to produce high strength offshore steel with excellent low temperature toughness. SEM, XRD, EBSD, TEM, tensile and low temperature impact were used to study the microstructure, mechanical properties and retained austenite characterization of the steel after the QIAT heat treatment. The results were also compared with those after QT. The Purdy model was used to calculate the effect of Mn enrichment on the migration of the retained austenite interface to study the growth mechanism of film-like retained austenite. Furthermore, the toughness mechanism of film-like retained austenite generated by this growth mechanism was analyzed.

### 2. Experimental methods

The chemical composition (wt%) of the test steel was C 0.1, Si 0.42, Mn 2.44, Nb 0.08, Mo 0.30, Ni 1.27, Cu 1.23 and Fe balance. The test steel was smelted in a 50 kg vacuum induction furnace and cast into ingot. The ingot was heated to $1200\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 2 h and then rolled into a 25-mm-thick steel plate by seven passes. The sample taken from the hot-rolled plate was measured for phase change points on a LINSEISL 78 RITA phase changer. The sample was austenized at $850\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 10 min. The temperature at the equilibrium point of the test steel was measured as $\text{A}_{\text{c1}}$: $701\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$; $\text{A}_{\text{c3}}$: $762\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ $\text{B}_{\text{s}}$: $508\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$; $\text{B}_{\text{f}}$: $421\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. The heat treatment process was established based on the measured phase transition equilibrium temperature, as shown in figure 1. QT process: quenching at $830\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ + tempering at $640\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$; QIAT process: quenching at $830\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ + intercritical annealing at $720\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ (air cooling) + tempering at $640\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$.

After heat treatment, the plates of the two processes were processed into standard tensile specimens with a diameter of 5 mm and a gauge length of 25 mm, and subjected to normal temperature stretching on a UTM 5305 electronic universal testing machine at a tensile rate of $1\ \text{mm}\ \text{min}^{-1}$. The Charpy V-notch (CVN) impact specimens with dimensions of $10\ \text{mm} \times 10\ \text{mm} \times 55\ \text{mm}$ were intercepted from the slabs. The CVN impact and instrumented impact tests were performed on a ZBC2752 impact tester at $-40^{\circ}\text{C}$. Specimens were cut from the slabs after QT, QIAT and intercritical annealing without tempering. After grinding and polishing, they were etched by 4% Nital and the microstructure was observed under an EVOMA 10 scanning electron microscope (SEM). The volume fraction of retained austenite in the samples of different heat treatment stages was measured by an X'PERTPROX light diffractometer (XRD) using a Co target, a tube voltage of 35 kV and a tube current of 40 mA. The EBSD samples were mechanically grinded and polished, and then electropolished with a 10% perchloric acid-alcohol (volume ratio) solution. The retained austenite distribution in the matrix was observed by a JSM6480LV scanning electron microscope equipped with an Oxford-EBSD insert. A 0.3 mm thick sheet was cut by wire cutting, ground to 30 um and then thinned by 5% perchloric acid-alcohol. The austenite morphology and selected area electron diffraction were observed using a JEM-2000 field emission transmission electron microscope (TEM). The difference in the internal component content was detected by the EDS system.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Microstructure

Figure 2 shows the SEM images of the test steel after different heat treatments. It can be seen from figure 2(a) that the microstructure after the QT treatment is a fine tempered lath bainite/martensite structure with clear lath boundaries. The microstructure after quenching at $830\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ plus two-phase annealing at $720\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ is a complex phase composed of intercritical ferrite and bainite/martensite, as shown in figure 2(b). The quenched lath martensite before intercritical annealing was austenitized in part of the region after being heated and maintained by the two-phase region. The unaustenitized region still retained the original quenched lath structure, but the super-saturated C, Mn and other elements in the original quenched martensite were gradually desolvated and diffused into the boundaries of the austenitized region, forming a low C low Mn lath ferrite structure [5].
Santofimia *et al* [14] called it intercritical ferrite, which retained the high strength of lath martensite and had good toughness due to the redistribution of C and Mn. Then, the austenitized region transformed into lath bainite/martensite, which was rich in C and Mn at its boundaries, during the subsequent (air-cooling) process. After tempering at $640\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, C and Mn were further redistributed causing the lath structure boundaries to become blurred. Bainite/martensite and intercritical ferrite were decomposed into long strip or island $\alpha$-Fe phase due to the release of internal stress and the decrease of dislocation density [15]. Retained austenite was formed at the alloy rich boundary of the lath bainite/martensite. Hence, the structure after the QIAT process was tempered bainite/martensite, intercritical ferrite and retained austenite, as shown in figure 2(c).

### 3.3. Characterization of retained austenite
The XRD results showed that the main phases of the steel were $\alpha$-Fe and $\gamma$-Fe, and the diffraction peaks of different heat treatment stages are shown in figure 3. After the first quenching, only 1.3% of the supercooled austenite remained in the sample. Fresh reversed austenite was formed in the tissue during intercritical annealing after quenching, but due to its low stability, only 4% was left after cooling. After tempering at $640\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the volume fraction of retained austenite increased significantly to 18.1%. However, when tempering was directly performed after the first quenching (QT process), the sample contained 20.8% of retained austenite. It can be seen that the retained austenite content in QT steel was higher than that of QIAT steel, but its low temperature toughness was lower than that of QIAT steel. This suggested that the content of retained austenite in the tissue was not the primary factor in determining the low temperature toughness of the steel. The morphology, size and distribution of the single retained austenite were also important factors affecting the low temperature toughness [16, 17]. Hence, it was necessary to study the toughening mechanism of retained austenite in the QIAT process in the subsequent work.

EBSD phase distribution of the samples after QT and QIAT treatment are shown in figure 4, where the red part was body-centered-cubic (bcc) phase and the blue part was the face-centered-cubic (fcc) phase. After QT treatment, a large amount of large-sized blocky or strip-shaped retained austenite was observed in the trigeminal boundary of the prior austenite grain and in the crystal (figure 4(a)). But in figure 4(b), only a small amount of fine granular austenite around the boundary of prior austenite grain and the interface of the bainite/martensite phase was characterized. Combined with the XRD results found that the content of retained austenite in the sample after QIAT treatment was only 2% less than that after QT treatment, but the amount of austenite characterized by EBSD was much less than the latter. Therefore, it was inferred that the retained austenite size after the QIAT process was smaller than the EBSD step size so that it was not found by EBSD. The microstructure of the single grains of the test steel was further observed by a transmission electron microscopy.

Figure 5 shows the TEM images of retained austenite in the microstructure after different heat treatments. As shown in figure 5(a), retained austenite after QT treatment was distributed in a block form at the grain boundary

### 3.4. Flat growth mechanism of film-like retained austenite
The formation of retained austenite in the QIAT steel was mainly caused by the enrichment of C and Mn elements. According to the classical nucleation growth theory, grain growth is accompanied by boundary migration and elemental partitioning. Unlike the gap solute C, the substitutional solute Mn has a low diffusion coefficient. Therefore, the enrichment of Mn at the bainite/martensite boundary will inevitably affect the migration of retained austenite boundary during reverted transformation. Purdy [20] suggested that the substitutional alloy solute has a chemical potential at the α:γ interphase boundary, as shown in figure 6. Eᵐⁿ was the interfacial binding energy of Mn, 2δ was the diffusion thickness of Mn at the interface and 2ΔE was the chemical potential difference of Mn between ferrite and austenite, which was obtained by Thermo-Calc thermodynamics software.

Due to the influence of the enrichment of Mn at the boundary of bainite/martensite, the Gibbs free energy dissipation caused by the diffusion of Mn at the α:γ interface can be expressed as [20, 21]:

$$
\Delta G_{d i s}=-N_{v} V_{m} \int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\left[C(x)-C^{0}\right] \frac{\partial E(x)}{\partial(x)} d x \tag{1}
$$

where C(x) is the concentration of Mn at x, E(x) is the interface binding energy of Mn at x, Nᵥ is the number of atoms replaced by unit volume, Vₘ is the molar volume of Mn and C° is the nominal concentration of Mn.
When the phase interface moves at the rate v, the throughput of Mn at the α:γ interface can be expressed as [22]:

$$
\frac{\partial C(x)}{\partial x}+\frac{C(x)}{K T} \frac{\partial E(x)}{\partial x}+\frac{v}{D^{M n}}\left[C(x)-C^{o}\right]=0 \tag{2}
$$

K is the gas constant, T is the phase transition temperature, and Dᵐⁿ is the diffusion coefficient of Mn.
Simultaneous formulas (1) and (2), ΔG<sub>dis</sub> is expressed by the following parameter formula, and the boundary velocity and growth rate are normalized to $V^{Mn}$, such as the formula [20]:

![](./images/812737122917154816_8.jpg)

$$
a=\frac{\left(\Delta E-E^{M n}\right) D^{M n}}{K T v \delta} b=\frac{\left(\Delta E+E^{M n}\right) D^{M n}}{K T v \delta} V^{M n}=\frac{v \delta}{D^{M n}} \tag{3}
$$

The calculated result of free energy dissipation caused by Mn enrichment in this test steel is shown in figure 7(a), where $\mathrm{C}^{0}=2.4 \%, 4 \%, 5 \%, \mathrm{~T}=640{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{Mn}}\left(=8 \mathrm{~kJ} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}\right)$ is taken from the data measured in the literature [21]. It suggested that due to the drag of Mn at the interface, it caused a peak of free energy dissipation at a lower interface migration velocity $(\sim 1)$, and as the Mn element continued to enrich, the peak of free energy dissipation further increased. The reverse phase transition of bainite/martensite to austenite was a diffusion phase transition which resulted in a slow migration rate of the retained austenite grain boundaries. Thus, the appearance of this peak means that there was a great resistance at the initial stage of migration of the retained austenite interface, so that it cannot grow in equiaxed crystal form. The Gibbs free energy consumed by the bainite/martensite$\rightarrow$austenite reverse phase transition is divided into two parts: free energy of interface migration and free energy dissipation:

$$
\Delta G_{G i b b s}=\Delta G_{f r i c t i o n}+\Delta G_{d i s} \tag{4}
$$

The effective free energy for interface migration $(\Delta G_{friction})$ decreased with the increase of free energy dissipation $(\Delta G_{dis})$, leading to a reduction in the migration ability of retained austenite grain in the direction perpendicular to the $\alpha:\gamma$ phase interface and an elliptical growth of retained austenite (figure 7(b)). When the concentration of Mn in the $\alpha:\gamma$ phase interface reached the result measured by EDS, it caused the retained austenite to grow only one-dimensionally along the bainite/martensite lath without perpendicular to the $\alpha:\gamma$ phase interface, as shown in figure 5(c). Hence, compared with the QT process, the formation mechanism of the reverse transformation of retained austenite at the bainite/martensite boundary under QIAT process and the higher degree of Mn enrichment were the reasons for its film-like flat growth. This was consistent with the conclusion that Zhang [23] simulated the ternary alloy phase transition by the phase field method. Zhang suggested that the aggregation of Mn at the interface led to the dragging of the solute on the interface, resulting in the flattening of the product of phase transition.

### 3.5. Toughness mechanism of film-like retained austenite
The impact force-displacement and energy-displacement curves of QT and QIAT steel at an impact temperature of $-40{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ were compared, as shown in figure 8. It could be seen that the energy absorbed by the QT and QIAT samples at the elastic deformation and plastic deformation stages before the crack initial point was almost identical. However, after the crack initial point, the QT sample quickly entered the crack instable propagation, and the energy at the crack termination point was only $113.7 \mathrm{~J}$. In contrast, QIAT steel absorbed a large amount of energy during the crack initiation to fracture process, and had an ultra-high crack propagation absorption energy. It suggested that the reason why the low temperature toughness of QIAT steel was significantly higher than that of QT steel was that QIAT steel had higher crack propagation absorption energy in the crack propagation stage, which was attributed to the formation of film-like retained austenite in the QIAT steel.

On the one hand, the film-like retained austenite is small in size and has large resistance to twinning, which requires a larger driving force for the TRIP effect, so it has high stability [16, 24, 25]. This high retained austenite stability causes more energy to be absorbed by the TRIP effect when cracks pass through the matrix, effectively preventing crack propagation. The XRD result showed that 9.3% of the retained austenite still remained in the matrix near the fracture, demonstrating the excellent austenite stability of the film-like retained austenite.

On the other hand, the large amount of low-angle grain boundaries formed in the reverse phase transition region due to the formation of film-like retained austenite also hindered the crack propagation. Grain-subgrain boundary and misorientation angle distribution maps after QIAT were obtained by EBSD, as shown in figures 9(b) and (c). The interior of the grain is filled with high density low angle grain boundaries in figure 9(b). There were two peak distributions in the grain boundary misorientation angle and the angles of the peaks were

$2^\circ$–$6^\circ$ and $57^\circ$–$60^\circ$ (figure 9(c)), respectively. The first peak consisted of a large number of low angle grain boundaries, which accounts for up to 68%. Studies have shown that low-angle grain boundaries are formed by a large number of dislocations [26, 27]. The reverse phase transition caused the structure to change from bcc to fcc, and the transition region had lattice distortion and a large number of dislocations. Moreover, the pinning action of Mn atoms led to serious dislocation plugging, forming a high-density dislocation wall, then forming the low angle grain boundary. This generation mechanism caused a stress field at the low angle grain boundary due to the disorder of internal atomic arrangement [28]. When the crack passed through the stress field, energy loss was caused, which effectively hindered the crack tip propagation, thereby greatly improving the material strength and toughness [28].

The excellent stability of film-like retained austenite formed by QIAT process and the large number of low-angle grain boundaries formed during the reverse phase transition make the QIAT steel have excellent crack propagation absorption energy, resulting in an increase in low temperature toughness.

## 4. Conclusions

This work compared the microstructure and mechanical properties after QT and QIAT heat treatments to study the effect of film-like retained austenite on the low temperature toughness of high strength off-shore steel and described the flat growth mechanism and toughening mechanism of the retained austenite:

(1) After QIAT heat treatment, the microstructure of this new offshore steel was composed of tempered bainite/martensite, intercritical ferrite and film-like retained austenite. The film-like retained austenite was located between the bainite/martensite laths and had a thickness of only $0.1\ \mu\text{m}$. The yield strength was 716 MPa, the tensile strength was 812 MPa and the low temperature toughness of $-40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ reached 150 J. The QIAT process played a role in enhancing low temperature toughness.

(2) The enrichment of Mn at the lath bainite/martensite boundaries after QIAT caused a surge in dissipated free energy of retained austenite during the reverted phase transformation, which hindered the migration of the retained austenite interface. Hence, the retained austenite grains grew in a one-dimensional flat film-like along the boundaries of the bainite/martensite laths.

(3) The volume fraction of retained austenite was not the first factor in determining the low temperature toughness of offshore steel. The shape and size of retained austenite also played a decisive role in the influence of low temperature toughness. As described herein, the formation of film-like retained austenite was the reason for the improvement of low temperature toughness of this offshore steel. Its toughening mechanism was excellent austenite stability and the large number of low angle grain boundaries formed during the reverse phase transition, which improved its crack propagation absorption energy.
