## Abstract:
In photocatalytic hydrogen production via water splitting, noble metal alloy nanoparticles exposed to specific crystal facets can be highly effective cocatalysts in comparison with noble metal nanospherical particles. In this research we have investigated, for the first time, the {110} facet-dependent efficiency of a Pt₃Sn nanocubes cocatalyst for solar photocatalytic hydrogen production. Under identical conditions and with the same cocatalyst loading, the hydrogen production rate over excavated {110} facets exposed Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS is 2 times higher than that of {100} facets exposed Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS and 3.5 times higher than that of {100} facets exposed Pt nanocubes/CdS, respectively. The quantum efficiency of photocatalytic hydrogen production over the {110} facets exposed Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS can be as high as 86% at 420 nm, exceeding previously reported efficiencies. Theoretical computations and experimental characterizations have revealed that excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes exposed to high-energy {110} crystal facets are more favorable for hydrogen evolution reactions than other cocatalysts studied, leading to excellent photocatalytic performance. Tuning the exposed facets of a metal cocatalyst can greatly promote its photocatalytic activity. This work provides an alternative strategy for synthesizing highly active photocatalysts for water splitting/reducing.

**Keywords:** Photocatalysis; Hydrogen production; Cocatalysts; Pt₃Sn nanocubes; DFT.

### 1. Introduction

Hydrogen is a most promising renewable energy carrier because of its zero greenhouse gas emission.¹⁻⁵ In recent years, hydrogen production via photocatalytic water splitting has captured intensive research attention.⁶⁻⁸ The great challenge, however, is the development of a highly efficient and low cost visible light photocatalyst.⁷⁻¹⁰ Various semiconductor-based photocatalysts have been employed in hydrogen production via photocatalytic water splitting.², ⁶⁻¹⁰ However, semiconductor photocatalysts alone have limited efficiencies due to the rapid recombination of photo-generated electron-hole pairs within the photocatalysts.¹, ⁶⁻¹⁰ In order to overcome this major obstacle in photocatalysis an efficient and stable proton-reducing noble metal cocatalyst must be loaded onto the surface of semiconductor photocatalysts.⁷, ¹⁰, ¹¹ Platinum (Pt) is the most effective proton reduction catalyst and has been used as a cocatalyst for photocatalysis.⁸, ¹²⁻¹⁵ However, due to its high cost and natural scarcity, reducing Pt loading while improving the performance of Pt loaded photocatalysts has been both a challenge and an opportunity for the solar hydrogen economy.¹⁴⁻¹⁷

As reported in literature, modifying the morphology of precious metal particles can change the crystal plane structures of a material. The presence of high-energy crystal facets in catalyst nanoparticles reduces the reaction activation energy, thus improving catalyst performance.¹⁸⁻²⁰ The main effects of the morphology of metal catalysts are the differences in the surface atom structures of catalyst particles. Atoms in a catalyst's crystal edges or corners are highly active reaction centers. As such, they are extremely effective in the improvement of catalytic properties. Based on this understanding, research into the preparation of noble metal cocatalyst nanoparticles with specific crystal exposed facets could be a new means of increasing the efficiency of hydrogen production via photocatalytic water splitting.

Our group has recently studied the effects of low index crystalline facets, such as {111} ²¹and {100}²², on the photocatalytic activities of noble metal particle loaded photocatalysts for solar hydrogen production. We have found that Pt, Pd and Pt-Pd nanocubes with {100} crystal surface facets result in much greater photocatalytic activity for hydrogen production than do {111}-facet-bounded nanooctahedra or spherical metal particles loaded photocatalysts. ¹⁵, ²¹, ²² These results indicate that photocatalytic activity of a semiconductor-metal nanocomposite is

sensitive to the shapes of metal cocatalyst particles.

In this paper we have investigated, for the first time, the {110} facets facet-dependent performance of a $Pt_3Sn$ excavated nanocube cocatalyst for solar hydrogen production. This cocatalyst loaded CdS ($Pt_3Sn$/CdS) results in a significantly higher activity for visible light hydrogen production. Both theoretical computations and experimental characterizations have indicated that excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes exposed to high-energy {110} crystal facets are more favorable for hydrogen evolution reactions, leading to excellent photocatalytic performance when loaded onto CdS. It is noted that this cocatalyst can also be loaded onto other main photocatalysts for hydrogen production. This work provides not only an insight into the effect of surface structures of a metal cocatalyst on photocatalytic activity, but also a novel approach to the synthesis of highly active photocatalysts for water splitting/reducing.

### 2. Experimental
The materials used in this research can be found in the Supporting Information.

#### 2.1. Hydrothermal synthesis of $Pt_3Sn$ alloy nanoparticles
Excavated nanocubic $Pt_3Sn$ alloy with {110} facet external surfaces was synthesized according to the method reported by Chen et al. $^{23}$ In a typical synthesis process, 12.8 mg of $Pt(acac)_2$ (0.032 mM), 1.8 mg of $SnCl_2$•$2H_2O$ (0.008 mM) and 300 mg of PVP were dispersed in 12 mL of DMF via ultrasonication. The resulting solution was then transferred to a 25 mL Teflon-lined autoclave for hydrothermal treatment at 473 K for 6 hours. After the hydrothermal treatment the obtained sample was cleaned with a mixture of acetone/ethanol under ultrasonication. Finally, the resulting powder was separated using a centrifuge operated at 8000 rpm for 10 min. To investigate the PVP concentration effect four additional reference $Pt_3Sn$ alloy nanoparticle samples were prepared, with PVP additions of 400 mg, 200 mg, 100 mg and 0 mg. For comparison, cubic Pt nanoparticles were also prepared under identical conditions without addition of Sn metal. $^{23,24}$

#### 2.2. Preparation of $Pt_3Sn$/CdS and Pt/CdS photocatalysts
The obtained metal nanoparticles were then loaded onto CdS to form $Pt_3Sn$/CdS and Pt/CdS photocatalysts using a method introduced previously.$^{25}$ The complete removal of PVP from $Pt_3Sn$/CdS and Pt/CdS was accomplished using the $NaBH_4$ and TBA treatments previously

developed by our group. $^{22}$ The XRD pattern shows that the crystal structure of the Pt₃Sn/CdS composite does not change before or after the PVP removal (Figure S1 of the Supporting Information).

### 2.3. Photocatalytic hydrogen production
A typical visible-light photocatalytic hydrogen production reaction was performed as follows:
5.0 mg 0.5 wt.% Pt₃Sn/CdS was suspended in 10 mL 1.25 M aqueous (NH₄)₂SO₃ solution (pH = 8.4). $^{15,21,25}$ The suspension was then transferred to a reactor cell with a 2.5 cm x 2.5 cm glass window. $^{26}$ Prior to light irradiation the solution was purged with high purity nitrogen to remove dissolved oxygen. A 300 W Xe lamp (PLS-SXE300/300UV, Perfectlight Co.) equipped with an optical UV cut-off filter ($\lambda$ > 420 nm) was employed as a visible light source. Hydrogen evolution was measured using an online gas chromatograph (GC) (Techcomp Limited Co., GC7890II, thermal conductivity detector) with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). The quantum efficiency (QE) at 420 nm for hydrogen production was calculated based on the following equation: $^{2,10}$

$$

For QE measurements, we used a single wavelength (420 nm) LED monochromatic lamp (CEL-LED 100) as a light source for hydrogen production. The number of incident photons was determined via a fiber spectrometer (Ocean Optic USB 2000⁺ system). For comparison, two reference photocatalysts, including a commercial CdS and a 0.5 wt.% nanocubes Pt/CdS photocatalyst, were used as baselines for the QE evaluation of 0.5 wt.% excavated nanocubes Pt₃Sn/CdS. The photocatalytic stability of prepared 0.5 wt. % Pt₃Sn/CdS photocatalyst was confirmed by a cyclic experiment.

### 2.4. Density function theory (DFT) calculations
All calculations were performed using periodic plane-wave density function theory (DFT) methods as implemented in the Vienna ab-initio simulation package (VASP). $^{27,28}$ GGA-PBE functional $^{29}$ and the projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential $^{30,31}$ were employed with [Pt]-5d⁹6s¹ and [Sn]-4d¹⁰5s²5p² treated as valence electrons. The plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 400 eV. The six-layer $p$(2×2) slabs of Pt(100), Pt(110), Pt₃Sn(100) and Pt₃Sn(110) with 14 Å of

vacuum region in z-direction were used to accommodate an adsorbed hydrogen atom (H*), corresponding to 0.25 ML of coverage. The adsorbate and the top four-layer metal atoms were allowed to relax while the bottom two layers were held fixed at their optimized bulk positions—that is, Pt: 3.976 Å, Pt₃Sn: 4.063 Å. The Brillouin zone in reciprocal space was sampled by the Monkhorst-Pack scheme³² with 4×4×1 and 3×4×1 k-points grids for (100) and (110) facets, respectively. The conjugate gradient algorithm was used in ionic optimization, and the convergence threshold was set to 10⁻⁴ eV in electronic relaxation and 0.02eV/Å in Hellmann-Feynman force on each atom. 1ˢᵗ order Methfessel-Paxton smearing of $k_BT = 0.1$ eV was employed to speed up the convergence. All reported energies were extrapolated to $k_BT = 0$ eV.

The free energy changes for H* adsorption on Pt (100), Pt₃Sn (100) and Pt (110), Pt₃Sn (110) facets were used to evaluate the activity of photocatalytic HER, calculated according to the following equation:

$$\Delta \mathrm{G_{H*}} = \Delta \mathrm{E_{H*}} + \Delta \mathrm{ZPE} - \mathrm{T} \Delta \mathrm{S} - \Delta \mathrm{G_{pH}}$$

where $\Delta \mathrm{E_{H*}}$ is the adsorption energy of an H* and can be calculated as:

$$\Delta \mathrm{E_{H^*}} = \mathrm{E_{slab+H^*}} - \mathrm{E_{slab}} - \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{E_{H_2}}$$

where $\mathrm{E_{slab+H^*}}$, $\mathrm{E_{slab}}$ and $\mathrm{E_{H_2}}$ are the total energies of H atom adsorbed on slab, clean slab, and gas-phase H₂, respectively.

$\Delta \mathrm{ZPE}$ is zero-point energy (ZPE) correction based on the calculated vibrational frequencies, $\mathrm{T} \Delta \mathrm{S}$ is the entropy contribution to the reaction at T = 298 K. $\Delta \mathrm{G_{pH}}$ represents the correction of the free energy due to the variations in H⁺ concentration, defined as $\mathrm{G_{pH}} = -kT \ln[\mathrm{H^+}] = kT \ln 10 \times \mathrm{pH}$. (More computational details can be found in Computational Detail of the Supporting Information.)

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Synthesis and characterizations of structurally tunable Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes

Excavated nanocubic Pt₃Sn alloy with {110} facet external surfaces was synthesized according to the method previously reported. ²³ X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were used to determine whether Pt and Sn are alloyed or if they are isolated individual phases.³³ As shown in

Fig. 1, the obtained $Pt_3Sn$ nanoparticles can be well indexed to form an fcc crystal structure, which is consistent with those of $Pt_3Sn$ alloy (PDF #35-1360). Moreover, all diffraction peaks of $Pt_3Sn$ nanoparticles are shifted towards lower diffraction angles compared with XRD of Pt nanocubes. This is because Sn atomic radius (225 pm) is larger than that of Pt (175 pm).$^{23}$ When smaller Pt atoms are replaced by larger Sn atoms the lattice constant of the $Pt_3Sn$ bimetallic alloy particles will be greater than that of Pt crystals, leading to the diffraction peaks shifting to lower angles. The simulated XRD spectra using the optimized Pt and $Pt_3Sn$ unit cell agree well with measured XRD spectra (Fig. 1 and 8B, Table S6). The prepared $Pt_3Sn$ alloy XRD spectrum shifting to smaller $2\theta$ of $Pt_3Sn$ relative to Pt indicates the lattice expansion of Pt unit cells due to the formation of a $Pt_3Sn$ bulk alloy rather than a surface alloy.

TEM (Fig. 2(a)) shows the uniform TEM images of the excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes, and the inset displays an individual excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocube. Fig. 2(b) depicts the corresponding histogram of particle edge dimensions. The average edge dimension of the excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes is approximately 25 nm. The prepared $Pt_3Sn$ alloy excavated nanocube is an extreme case of a concave cube, which has been reported with exposed {hk0} high index facets.$^{23, 34}$ As shown in Fig. 2(e) and (f), the two opposite concave surfaces of the nanocrystals in the TEM image along the [100] belt axis are perpendicular to each other. The $45^\circ$ concavity defined by the dihedral angle between the concave surface and the (100) plane of an ideal cube indicates that the exposed surface is {110} crystal planes. The {110}-facet excavation structure of the prepared excavated nanocubes was further confirmed by TEM images in other directions. As shown in Fig. 2 (c) to (d), reviewing from the [110] belt axis, the angle between the inner contour of the projection concave nanocrystal and its outer contour is $35^\circ$, which agrees well with the contour of the corresponding (Scheme S1).$^{23}$ These results confirm that the exposed facets of the prepared sample have a {110} concave crystal structure.

Fig. 3(a) is a TEM image of several excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanoparticles. The energy dispersive X-ray spectral element mapping (Fig. 3(b) and (c)) shows the uniform distribution of Pt and Sn elements in $Pt_3Sn$ nanoparticles, suggesting that the nanoparticles are bimetallic $Pt_3Sn$ alloy particles. The corresponding energy dispersive X-ray spectrum (EDS) shown in Fig. 3(d) confirms the co-existence of Pt and Sn atoms in the $Pt_3Sn$ nanoparticles. Based on the EDS (Fig. 3(d)) and

ICP-AES data (Table S3), the concentration of Sn in the Pt₃Sn particles is approximately 22.9 atom%, which is close to the atomic ratio of 3 : 1 for Pt and Sn. Moreover, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses show that Pt and Sn are mainly in their metallic states (Figure S13). Figure S13 also indicates that the binding energy of Sn (3d core level) in Pt₃Sn alloy particles is shifted toward a higher energy state in comparison with that of pure Sn. This result indicates that there is a charge transfer from Sn atom to Pt atom because of the alloying effect between Pt and Sn.³³ In summary, the prepared Pt₃Sn nanoparticles are excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes exposed to high-energy {110} crystal facets.

For comparison, cubic Pt₃Sn and cubic Pt nanoparticles exposed to {100} facets were also synthesized via the identical method used for the synthesis of excavated Pt₃Sn alloy particles. It has been reported that PVP addition plays an important role in the synthesis of an excavated structure.²³ The main function of PVP is to significantly change the growth kinetics of different crystal facets for Pt₃Sn nanoparticles. The amine and carbonyl functional groups in PVP molecules can be selectively adsorbed onto specific surfaces of metal/alloy crystals, thereby blocking crystal growth in those directions.²³ Figure S2 shows the effect of PVP addition on the shapes of Pt₃Sn nanoparticles. It is noted that perfect excavated morphology of Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes cannot be formed when the PVP addition is less than 300 mg (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2). However, cubic Pt₃Sn nanoparticles can be obtained in the absence of PVP. This result is consistent with previous work.²³ The formation of cubic Pt₃Sn alloy was confirmed by the XRD patterns (Fig. 1). In this research, cubic Pt nanoparticles were synthesized via the same method except that no Sn metal was involved. Figure S3 shows the TEM image and the histograms of the prepared Pt nanocube size distributions. The average size of the prepared Pt nanocubes is about 3.2 nm. Figure S4 shows the HRTEM image of nanocubes Pt₃Sn and Pt. The insets demonstrate that the exposed facets of nanocubes Pt₃Sn and Pt are both {100}.

### 3.2. Photocatalytic activities of 0.5 wt. % excavated nanocubes Pt₃Sn/CdS photocatalyst

Prepared Pt₃Sn nanoparticles were loaded onto the surface of CdS by mixing Pt₃Sn nanoparticles and commercial CdS for 2 hours at room temperature. The loading amount of Pt₃Sn alloy on CdS was about 0.5 wt. % according to ICP-AES measurements. The photocatalytic activity of the prepared Pt₃Sn/CdS was evaluated for hydrogen evolution under visible-light

irradiation using ammonium sulfite $((NH_4)_2SO_3)$ as a sacrificial reagent. The reason that we study the hydrogen production from aqueous ammonium sulfite solution is because it is one important step for a solar ammonium-sulfite $(NH_3$-S) thermochemical water splitting cycle. $^{35-36}$ The experimental results show that the photocatalytic performance of CdS is strongly affected by $Pt_3Sn$ load. The optimum $Pt_3Sn$ cocatalysts loading on CdS is 0.5 wt.% with the highest photocatalytic $H_2$ production (Figure S6). The photocatalytic performance of $Pt_3Sn$ loaded CdS is also strongly affected by PVP contamination on the surface of $Pt_3Sn$ particles. As shown in Figure S10, the photocatalytic activity $Pt_3Sn$ nanoparticles loaded CdS can be increased about 5 times after PVP removal.

Fig. 4(a) illustrates the kinetics of visible-light-induced photocatalytic hydrogen over a series of photocatalysts. Under identical conditions bare commercial CdS and a 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS photocatalyst were also included as baselines for the evaluation of 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$/CdS photocatalysts. Fig. 4(b) shows that the hydrogen production efficiencies of these photocatalysts follow Sequence (1):

0.5 wt.% excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS > 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS > 0.5 wt.% Pt
nanocubes/CdS > pure CdS (1)

Table S1 in the Supporting Information summarizes the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution rates and the quantum efficiencies for all the catalysts investigated in this research. Among these catalysts, 0.5 wt.% excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS has shown the highest hydrogen production rate of $52\ \mu mol\ h^{-1}$, which is much greater than those of 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS $(26\ \mu mol\ h^{-1})$, 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS $(15\ \mu mol\ h^{-1})$ or pure CdS photocatalyst $(9.8\ \mu mol\ h^{-1})$. The quantum efficiency of hydrogen production over the 0.5 wt.% excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS is as high as 86% at 420 nm, a significant increase in comparison with published results (Table S4).

It is noteworthy that the rate of hydrogen production over the 0.5 wt.% excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS photocatalyst is 2 times greater than that of {100} facets enclosed 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS and 3.5 times greater than that of {100} external facets 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS. Because 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$ alloy with Pt to Sn atomic ratio of 3.4 contains 84.84 wt.% of Pt, the hydrogen production rates per gram of Pt can be calculated as 24,500, 12,200 and 6,000 $\mu mol\ h^{-1}\ g^{-1}$ of Pt for 0.5 wt.% excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS, 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS

and 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS, respectively. These results indicate that when 22.7 atom% of more expensive Pt is replaced with less expensive Sn, the 0.5 wt.% excavated Pt₃Sn alloy nanoparticle loaded CdS photocatalyst can lead to a 4.09 times hydrogen production rate increase per gram of Pt in comparison with that of 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS photocatalyst. It should be pointed out that the specific surface area of the excavated structure of Pt₃Sn nanoparticles is lower than that of cubic Pt (Table S2). From a photocatalytic reaction viewpoint, these results further indicate that an excavated structure of Pt₃Sn nanoparticles possesses more active sites than that of cubic Pt. The higher energy {110} crystal plane, on the other hand, can be more favorable than {100} for photocatalytic hydrogen evolution due to optimized proton adsorption Gibbs free energy.

Figure S5 shows the TEM images of 0.5 wt. % excavated cubic Pt₃Sn nanoparticles on CdS photocatalyst (Pt₃Sn/CdS) before and after photocatalytic hydrogen production. The TEM image (Figure S5(a)) for the fresh sample shows numerous Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes dispersed onto the surfaces of CdS particles. The HRTEM images of 0.5 wt. % Pt₃Sn/CdS photocatalyst powder are the excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes on CdS particles with two different lattice fringes (Figure S7). The morphologies for a fresh catalyst and the sample after photocatalytic hydrogen production are shown in Figure S5 (a) and (b) and Figure S7. This observation indicates that Pt₃Sn nanoparticle catalyst is a stable alloy cocatalyst because the morphology of cocatalyst particles remains unchanged after a photocatalytic reaction.

The cyclic catalyst lifespan test of the 0.5 wt.% excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS for hydrogen production was performed under visible-light irradiation. Fig. 4(c) shows that total 455 μmol H₂ was produced during a 10-hour reaction period over 5 mg photocatalyst. Turnover numbers (TONs), defined as the molar ratio of the total hydrogen atoms evolved (0.91 mmol) per mole of CdS photocatalyst (0.0346 mmol) and per mole of Pt₃Sn cocatalyst ($3.55 × 10^{-5}$ mmol) are 26.3 and 25633 respectively. The higher TONs for both CdS and Pt₃Sn cocatalyst indicate that the produced hydrogen resulted from the photocatalytic reduction of water rather than from the photooxidation of either the Pt₃Sn cocatalyst or the CdS main photocatalyst.²⁵ No significant hydrogen production rate decrease or catalyst structural changes occurred after the 10ᵗʰ cyclic catalyst lifespan test (Figure S11). These results suggest that the 0.5 wt. % excavated Pt₃Sn

nanocubes/CdS is a stable catalyst for photocatalytic hydrogen production.

### 3.3. Electrochemical and photochemical behaviors of $\mathbf{Pt_3Sn}$ nanoparticles loaded CdS photocatalysts

Fig. 5(a) depicts the transient photocurrents for three photocatalysts. Under identical conditions, the photocurrents follow the same Sequence (1). This result confirms that the interfacial electron transfer is more efficient over 0.5 wt.% excavated $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS photocatalyst than over 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS or CdS photocatalysts. Fig. 5(b) and Figure S12 show the photoluminescence (PL) and UV-Vis spectra, at room temperature, of pure CdS, 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS, 0.5 wt.% $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS and 0.5 wt.% excavated $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS composites, respectively. These results indicate that high-energy {110} facets in the prepared $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ cocatalyst promote a higher rate of electron transfer from CdS to excavated $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanoparticles, reducing the recombination rate of electrons and holes generated in CdS particles, thereby improving the photocatalytic efficiency of hydrogen production.

Figure S9(a) depicts the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves of four photocatalysts carried out in a $1.25\ \text{M}\ (\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_3$ solution under irradiation of a 300 W Xe light with a cut-off filter ($\lambda > 420$ nm). The onset overpotentials (vs. SCE) for 0.5 wt.% excavated $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS, 0.5 wt.% $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS, 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS and bare CdS were -0.30 V, -0.50 V ,-0.52 V and -0.60 V, respectively. At a current density of $10\ \text{mA/cm}^2$, the corresponding overpotentials (vs. SCE) were -0.71 V, -0.75 V, -0.76 V and -0.90 V for these four photocatalysts, respectively. As observed, the onset overpotential of 0.5 wt. % excavated $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS is lower than either 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS or 0.5 wt.% $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS. This result suggests that 0.5 wt.% excavated $\text{Pt}_3\text{Sn}$ nanocubes/CdS has the highest photoelectrocatalytic activity for hydrogen production among these four catalysts.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to determine the intrinsic charge transfer abilities between the metal and alloy cocatalysts and CdS. Figure S9 (b) shows the EIS Nyquist plots of the three photocatalysts using a $1.25\ \text{M}\ (\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_3$ aqueous solution as an electrolyte. The typical semicircle appearing in the high frequency region of the EIS Nyquist plot represents the charge transfer resistance of the photocatalyst. In general, a smaller arc radius in an EIS Nyquist plot implies a higher efficiency of charge transfer in the reduction of the

recombination rate of electron-hole pairs generated in CdS semiconductor. Clearly, the arc radius of the Nyquist plot for 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS is the smallest among the Nyquist plots for the four catalyst samples. This again indicates that the 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS catalyst has the highest charge transfer capacity among these four catalysts. By comparison, the charge transfer efficiency of these catalysts follows the same order of 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS $>$ 0.5 wt.% $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS $>$ 0.5 wt. % Pt nanocubes/CdS $>$ bare CdS as shown in Sequence (1). Based on the above LSV and EIS analytic results we can conclude that 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS possesses excellent photocatalytic efficiency for hydrogen production in terms of low electrical resistance and fast rate of charge transfer.

### 4. Discussion

Although photocatalytic activity varies significantly between bare CdS, 0.5 wt.% Pt nanocubes/CdS and 0.5 wt.% excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS, there is no significant difference in the specific surface areas for these three catalysts. As shown in Table S2, the BET specific surface area of 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS is not higher, but lower than that of 0.5 wt. % Pt nanocubes/CdS. However, the hydrogen production rate over 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS is 3.5 times greater than that of 0.5 wt. % Pt nanocubes/CdS. These results imply that the surface area of a photocatalyst is not a determining factor for the increased photocatalytic activity of the 0.5 wt. % excavated $Pt_3Sn$ nanocubes/CdS.

In contrast with surface area, the proton reduction step occurring on the surface of cocatalyst nanoparticles plays a pivotal role during photocatalytic water splitting for hydrogen production. Under visible-light irradiation, the main photocatalyst (CdS) is photo-excited to produce electron-hole pairs. The photo-generated electrons in the CdS conduction band migrate from the bodies of CdS particles to the surface and then cross over metal/CdS interfaces to cocatalyst particles. Protons ($H^+$), previously adsorbed onto the surfaces of metal particles, receive electrons and are then reduced to produce hydrogen.$^{10}$ Our research finds that under certain reaction conditions the activity of proton catalytic reduction over a noble metal cocatalyst is a rate-determining step in the efficiency of the photocatalytic hydrogen production of a photocatalyst. Based on this observation we can conclude that the activity of a photocatalyst is

governed by the electrochemical activity of its cocatalyst.²²

In this research, we also found that the prepared excavated Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes are also a highly active electrocatalyst for electrochemical hydrogen evolution. Electrochemical properties of the prepared excavated Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes were evaluated using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the excavated cubic Pt₃Sn alloy is active for hydrogen generation. For a current density of 5 mA/cm², excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes require -60 mV (vs. RHE). In contrast, with the same current density of 5 mA/cm², Pt nanocubes and Pt₃Sn nanocubes catalysts require about -140 mV and -105 mV (vs. RHE) overpotential, respectively. Fig. 6(b) shows the corresponding Tafel plots of excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes, Pt₃Sn nanocubes and the Pt nanocubes. The Tafel plot of the excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes alloy is much smaller than that of the nanocubic Pt particles and Pt₃Sn nanocubes. The relatively lower overpotential and smaller Tafel slope suggest that the excavated Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes are more active for electrocatalytic hydrogen generation than either Pt nanocubes or Pt₃Sn nanocubes. The ESI semi-circular radius of the excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes is smaller than those of the Pt nanocubes sample and Pt₃Sn nanocubes (Fig. 6(c)) as well. All these results indicate that the excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes have a lower electron transfer resistance and are therefore more favorable for catalytic hydrogen production.

The improved electrocatalytic performance of excavated cubic Pt₃Sn alloy nanoparticles for hydrogen generation, as compared to that of Pt nanocubes and Pt₃Sn nanocubes, can be attributed to the increased electrochemically active surface area (ECSA). It is noted that ECSA of a metal catalyst is linearly proportional to its electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl).³⁷ Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used for the Cdl measurements for Pt₃Sn catalyst.³⁸ As shown in Fig. 7, the capacitances of excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes, Pt₃Sn nanocubes and cubic Pt can be calculated as 4.29, 3.39 and 2.83 mF cm⁻², respectively. The ECSA of the excavated Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes is greater than that of Pt nanocubes and Pt₃Sn nanocubes.

To gain deeper understanding of the highly electrocatalytic activity of the excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes we performed density function theory (DFT) calculations for hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on Pt (100), Pt (110), Pt₃Sn (100) and Pt₃Sn (110) crystal facets. Various proton adsorption sites are depicted in Fig. 8(A). Fig. 8(B) shows the DFT simulated XRD spectra for the

optimized Pt and Pt₃Sn unit cells. In comparing with experimental XRD (Fig. 1) we found that the simulated XRD (Fig. 8(B) agrees well with those experimental XRD spectra (Table S6). Particularly, the XRD of Pt₃Sn shifts to smaller 2θ relative to the XRD of Pt particles. This result further indicates, from the atomic level, the lattice expansion of Pt unit cells due to the incorporation of Sn atoms into Pt lattices. Moreover, Pt₃Sn alloy is formed throughout the bulk of the material rather than forming on the surface only. For the HER activity of an electrocatalyst under either an acidic or basic condition, proton adsorption free energy change, $\Delta G_{\text{H}*}$, can be used to predict the HER thermodynamic activity of an electrocatalyst. It has been reported that the optimal absolute $\Delta G_{\text{H}*}$ value for an HER should be close to 0.00 eV.³⁹, ⁴⁰ As shown in Fig. 8(C) and Table S5, the calculated $\Delta G_{\text{H}*}$ decreases in the sequence of:

-0.15 eV (Pt₃Sn (110)) > -0.46 eV (Pt (110)) > -0.48 eV (Pt₃Sn (100)) > -0.54 eV (Pt (100)).

The absolute $\Delta G_{\text{H}*}$ value for Pt₃Sn (110) is 0.15 eV, which is the closest value to 0.0 eV in comparison with those of other crystal planes for Pt₃Sn and Pt. This result indicates that the excavated Pt₃Sn alloy nanocube catalyst with {110} exposure facets is thermodynamically most favorable (the lowest thermodynamic energy barrier) for HER compared to Pt₃Sn (100), Pt (110) and Pt (100) external facet catalysts.

Based on the above characterizations and DFT calculations we can conclude that excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes exposed to high-energy {110} crystal facets are more favorable for proton reduction, leading to higher photocatalytic hydrogen production efficiency for the excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS photocatalyst. As expected, the prepared 0.5 wt.% excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS photocatalyst is highly active and stable for solar hydrogen production. This result verifies the effects of the shape and exposure of crystal planes of cocatalyst particles on the photocatalytic activities of a photocatalyst.

### 5. Conclusions

In this research, an excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes loaded CdS (excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS) photocatalyst was successfully synthesized and used for photocatalytic hydrogen production from an aqueous $({\text{NH}_4})_2\text{SO}_3$ solution. Experimental results show that the surface crystal planes of the prepared Pt₃Sn nanoparticles are excavated {110} high-energy faceted Pt₃Sn alloy nanocubes. We found that the shapes, as well as exposed crystal planes of cocatalyst nanoparticles, play important

roles in both electrocatalytic and photocatalytic water reduction for hydrogen production. The photocatalytic activity of the 0.5 wt.% excavated Pt₃Sn nanocubes/CdS has demonstrated much greater efficiency during visible light photocatalytic hydrogen production than that of Pt₃Sn nanocubes or Pt nanocubes loaded CdS under identical reaction conditions. The DFT calculations indicate that the high-energy {110} exposed crystal facets of the Pt₃Sn cocatalyst are the main factors responsible for the greater performance of the 0.5 wt. % excavated Pt₃Sn nanotubes/CdS photocatalyst.
