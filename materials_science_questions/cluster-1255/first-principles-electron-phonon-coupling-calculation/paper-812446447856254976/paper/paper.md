# Effects of density of states on superconductivity

P.M. Hui and David M.T. Kuo

Department of Physics, National Central University, Chung-Li, Taiwan 32054, ROC

Received 28 June 1991; revised manuscript received 19 September 1991; accepted for publication 20 September 1991
Communicated by A.A. Maradudin

The effects of the electronic density of states (DOS) on superconductivity are studied within the BCS theory using a one-electron anisotropic tight-binding model for the energy band. By tuning the ratio of the interlayer to intralayer couplings, the model interpolates the two-dimensional and three-dimensional cases. The transition temperature $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ and the isotope effect exponent $\alpha$ are studied as a function of the position of the Fermi energy for various degrees of anisotropy. The transition temperature is highest for uncoupled layers with the Fermi energy near to the logarithmic singularity in the 2D DOS, while the corresponding value of $\alpha$ shows a minimum. As the interlayer coupling is increased, the singularity in the DOS is rounded-off. The enhancement in $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ is suppressed and $\alpha$ approaches the BCS value of 0.5.

The effects of the electronic density of states (DOS) near the Fermi energy on superconductivity have attracted much attention recently [1-4]. According to the BCS theory of superconductivity (for a review, see e.g. ref. [5]), the important electrons are those with energies within a range of the order of $\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ near the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm{F}}$. Here $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ is a cutoff frequency which is taken to be the Debye frequency for phonon-mediated superconductivity. In the original BSC theory, the density of states in an energy range $\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ near the Fermi energy is taken to be a constant $N(0)$; the resulting expression for the transition temperature becomes $T_{\mathrm{c}} \sim \hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}} \exp [-1 /$ $N(0) V]$, where $V$ is the electron-phonon coupling strength. In most three-dimensional (3D) cases, the DOS near $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ can reasonably be assumed to be a constant. In two-dimensional (2D) systems, however, it has been pointed out [1,2] that a constant DOS may not be a good assumption. The reason is that there is a logarithmic singularity (Van Hove singularity) in the DOS. For $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ close to the singularity, the DOS varies rapidly within an energy of order $\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}$. Tsuei et al. [2,3] have used a slave boson technique to take into account some effects of electron correlations and showed that the Fermi level is pinned near the singularity even for electron (hole) concentrations away from half-filling. This Van Hove singularity leads to an enhancement in $T_{\mathrm{c}}$. The experimentally observed dependence of $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ and the isotope effect exponent $\alpha$ on doping in high-temperature superconductors (HTSC) are in reasonable agreement with the BSC theory if the Van Hove singularity in the DOS is taken into account.

Real materials, such as HTSC, are three-dimensional, although their physical properties may be anisotropic. In HTSC, for example, the different coherence lengths (for a review, see e.g. ref. [6]) along the $c$ axis and the $a-b$ plane clearly indicate the anisotropic nature of the system. The $\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{O}$ planes are believed to be essential for superconductivity. However, interlayer coupling is crucial for stabilizing the system so that long-range ordering may be possible. This is analogous to the case of polymers in which interchain couplings are essential in order to stabilize the system. In this paper, we study the crossover from 2D to 3D systems within the BCS theory. For simplicity, we consider an anisotropic tight-binding band which interpolates the 2D and 3D cases. The transition temperature and isotope effect are studied as a function of the position of the Fermi energy for various degrees of anisotropy. We found that the enhancement in $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ and lowering of the isotope effect exponent $\alpha$ are suppressed relative to the strictly 2D case by gradually increasing the interlayer coupling.

We consider a tight-binding energy band for the electron of the form

$$
E(\boldsymbol{k})=\epsilon_{0}-2 t\left(\cos k_{x} a+\cos k_{y} a\right)-2 t^{\prime} \cos k_{z} a, \quad(1)
$$

where $\epsilon_{0}$ is the on-site energy, $t$ and $t^{\prime}$ are the intralayer and interlayer couplings, respectively. For simplicity, we consider a simple cubic structure with lattice constant $a$. Our results can readily be generalized to the case of different lattice constants in various directions. Define the parameter $\gamma$ as the ratio of the interlayer to intralayer couplings, i.e.

$$
\gamma=t^{\prime} / t. \quad(2)
$$

The parameter $\gamma$ thus represents the degree of anisotropy of the system. If $\gamma=1$, eq. (1) reduces to the case of a 3D tight-binding band with a single value for the nearest neighbor hopping integral. If $\gamma=0$, the layers are uncoupled and eq. (1) essentially gives a 2D tight-binding band with only nearest neighbor coupling. Thus, by adjusting the value of $\gamma$, eq. (1) interpolates the 2D and 3D cases.

The density of states of the band structure given by eq. (1) can be obtained through the standard procedure of calculating the imaginary part of the Green function, $\operatorname{Im}[G(z)]$ (see e.g. ref. [7]). Fig. 1 shows $\operatorname{Im}[G(z)]$, which is proportional to the DOS, at various values of the ratio $t^{\prime} / t$. For $\gamma=0$, i.e. for uncoupled layers, the DOS has the form

$$
N_{2 \mathrm{D}}(E) \sim \frac{1}{t} \ln \left(\frac{t}{\left|E-\epsilon_{0}\right|}\right) \quad(3)
$$

with a logarithmic divergence at $\epsilon_{0}$, which is the center of the band. The logarithmic singularity is gradually rounded off as interlayer coupling is introduced $(\gamma \neq 0)$. A plateau is gradually developed near $\epsilon_{0}$ as $\gamma$ increases. The energy range over which $N(E)$ is nearly constant gets larger as $\gamma$ increases towards unity. For $\gamma=1$, one gets the typical 3D DOS of a tight-binding band.

The transition temperature can be obtained within the BCS theory by solving the equation [5]

$$
\frac{2}{V}=\int_{E_{\mathrm{F}}-\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}}^{E_{\mathrm{F}}+\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}} \tanh \left(\frac{E-E_{\mathrm{F}}}{2 k_{\mathrm{B}} T_{\mathrm{c}}}\right) N(E) \frac{\mathrm{d} E}{E-E_{\mathrm{F}}}, \quad(4)
$$

where $V$ is the electron-phonon coupling strength. For given values of $V, N(E)$ and $\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}$, eq. (4) can be solved as a function of the position of the Fermi level. For nearly half-filled bands, $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ is in the vicinity of the center of the band $\epsilon_{0}$. For $\gamma \approx 1, N(E)$ is approximately constant near the center of the band. However, for $\gamma \ll 1$, the DOS varies rapidly near $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ for nearly half-filled band and $N(E)$ cannot be taken out of the integral in eq. (4). We denote the deviation of $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ from $\epsilon_{0}$ by $\delta$, i.e. $\delta=E_{\mathrm{F}}-\epsilon_{0}$. For given values of $\gamma$, eq. (4) can be solved numerically for $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ as a function of $\delta$. Results are shown in fig. 2 for a par-

As the interlayer coupling decreases, $T_{\rm c}$ increases and gradually develops a peak at $\delta=0$, i.e. when $E_{\rm F}=\epsilon_{0}$ at which the DOS is highest. With the chosen set of parameters, the highest value of $T_{\rm c}$ is $\sim60$ K for $\gamma=0$.

For many materials with phonon-mediated superconductivity, $T_{\rm c}\sim M^{-\alpha}$, where $M$ is the mass of the ions. With the BCS theory with a nearly constant $N(E)$ near $E_{\rm F}$, $\alpha=0.5$. In general, the exponent $\alpha$ can be obtained by
$$
\alpha=\frac{1}{2} \frac{\mathrm{d} \ln \left(T_{\mathrm{c}}\right)}{\mathrm{d} \ln \left(\hbar \omega_{\mathrm{c}}\right)}. \tag{5}
$$

Results of $\alpha$ calculated numerically through eq. (5) for the same set of chosen parameters are shown in fig. 3. For $\gamma=1$, $\alpha=0.5$, as in the standard BCS theory. As $\gamma$ decreases, $\alpha$ develops a minimum at $\delta=0$, i.e. when $E_{\rm F}=\epsilon_{0}$. Thus, the highest $T_{\rm c}$ system has the lowest value of $\alpha$. For the case of uncoupled layers, i.e. $\gamma=0$, $\alpha=0.327$ for our chosen set of parameters. The behavior of $\alpha$ as a function of $\delta$ is also interesting. Besides the minimum at $\delta=0$, there is a maximum value of $\alpha$ occurring at a certain value of $\delta\neq0$. Fig. 4 shows the dependences of $T_{\rm c}$ and $\alpha$ as a function of $\gamma$ for the case of $\delta=0$, i.e. for the case of half-filling. As the logarithmic singularity is rounded off by the interlayer coupling, $T_{\rm c}$ decreases towards the BCS value and $\alpha$ increases towards the BCS value of 0.5. The $\delta$ variation of $\alpha$ found here is smaller than that in ref. [2] because of the slightly different choice of parameters and the particular choice of representing the whole band structure by a tight-binding model.

The occurrence of a maximum in $T_{\rm c}$ and a minimum in $\alpha$ as a function of the Fermi energy are observed in HTSC [2,8]. These features may be associated with the Van Hove singularity in the 2D DOS. Our results show that the introduction of interlayer coupling suppresses the enhancement in $T_{\rm c}$. In HTSC, the interlayer coupling, though it exists, is weak compared to intralayer coupling, and deviations from standard BCS results are expected.

For the case of HTSC, the band structure (1) can be interpreted as a one-band model obtained by tight-binding the Wannier functions associated with each unit cell (see e.g. ref. [9]). The oxygen content thus controls the position of the Fermi level and is related to our parameter $\delta$, maybe in a complicated way. Our calculation is based on a band structure in the one-electron picture. No electron correlations have been taken into account. Within a one-band model, electron-electron correlations lead to a renormalization of the band width within a mean field treatment of the correlation effects. The calculations here are based on the BCS theory. In general, the Eliashberg theory should be used. However, due to the lack of sufficient microscopic information, such a calculation is quite difficult to implement. Hence, our results can be regarded as a qualitative indication of what would be expected from a more complete treatment within the Eliashberg theory.

In summary, based on the BCS theory, we studied the effects of interlayer coupling within a one-elec- tron tight-binding band. The crossover from 2D to 3D is studied by tuning the interlayer coupling within an anisotropic tight-binding model. Such coupling should be important in stabilizing the system, al- though each layer may be the key to superconduc- tivity. A weak coupling slightly suppresses the effects on $T_c$ and $\alpha$ relative to the strictly 2D case.
