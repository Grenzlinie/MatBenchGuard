# ENTHALPIES OF PHASE TRANSFORMATIONS IN THE ALKALI FULLERIDE RbC₆₀

L. Gránásy¹, T. Kemény¹, G. Oszlányi¹, G. Bortel¹, G. Faigel¹, M. Tegze¹, S. Pekker¹, L. Forró³ and A. Jánossy²

¹Research Institute for Solid State Physics, H-1525 Budapest, POB 49 Hungary
²Institute of Physics, Technical University of Budapest, H-1521 Budapest, Hungary
³Departament de Physique, Ecole Polytechnique Federale de Lausanne 1015-Lausanne, Switzerland

(Accepted 24 October 1995 by A. Zawadowski)

Phase transformations in the alkali fulleride RbC₆₀ between the polymeric, dimeric and metastable fcc phases are investigated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and x-ray diffraction in the ~215 to 500 K range. The transformation enthalpies of the polymer→monomer and dimer→monomer transitions (25.8 and 10.5 kJ/mol, respectively) are less than expected for covalent interfullerene bonding which suggests substantial contributions from Madelung and deformation energies. Stability of the polymer phase depends on the temperature and duration of polymerization. In samples quenched from 500 to 273 K a transformation from an fcc phase with hindered rotation of the C₆₀ monomers to another fcc phase in which C₆₀⁻ ions are freely rotating is observed at 290 K. The relative Gibbs free energies of the stable and metastable phases are estimated from the measured transformation temperatures and enthalpies. The polymer is the most stable phase at low temperatures.

Keywords: A. fullerenes, D. phase transitions, D. thermodynamic properties

## 1. Introduction

The alkali fulleride RbC₆₀ has a number of stable and metastable crystalline modifications. Above 370 K a face centered cubic (fcc) rocksalt structure is formed with freely rotating C₆₀⁻ ions.¹·² An orthorhombic phase³ which contains covalently bonded charged polymer chains⁴⁻⁶ is stable below 370 K. Metastable phases of monomer C₆₀⁻ ions or (C₆₀⁻)₂ dimers are formed by quenching the sample from high temperature to below 300 K.⁷⁻¹⁰ Various phase transformations between these phases were reported. The dimer transforms into an fcc phase¹⁰ at about 270 K and into the polymer in the 253 to 300 K range.⁷⁻¹⁰,¹² The stability range of these phases and the sequence of phase transitions in this system are uncertain. In a previous work¹³ we briefly reported on a differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) study of phase transformations in RbC₆₀. In this paper we present a more detailed investigation of the thermal properties and estimate the Gibbs free energy of the dimer, polymer and fcc phases from the measured thermodynamic data. The results are correlated with structures determined by x-ray diffraction.

## 2. Experimental

Polycrystalline powder samples were prepared by solid state reaction from stoichiometric amounts of Rb metal and high purity (99.9 %) C₆₀ powder at 650 K. The phase transformations were investigated by a Perkin-Elmer DSC-2 calorimeter. Parallel beam x-ray powder diffraction spectra were recorded at the X3B1 beamline of the National Synchrotron Light Source at Brookhaven National Laboratory. The powder samples were phase pure in the sense that only the monomer rocksalt RbC₆₀ phase was observed by x-ray diffraction at 470 K.

### 3. Results and Discussion

#### A. Polymer-Monomer Transition

The DSC thermogram recorded during a 20 K/min heating of a sample polymerized for several weeks at room temperature is shown in Fig. 1. A broad endothermic peak is observed at the transformation of the polymer into the monomer fcc state. Decomposition of the polymer begins at about 340 K and is peaked at 405 K. The enthalpy of transformation is $Q_P$=25.8 kJ/mol. In the polymer the orientation of the $C_{60}^{-}$ ions is fixed while in the monomer phase the ions rotate freely. We estimate the rotational contribution as $Q_{rot}\approx{3/2}RT$ (5.3 kJ/mol at 425 K) where $R$ is the universal gas constant. $Q$=$Q_P$-$Q_{rot}$=20.5 kJ/mol is the bonding energy including Madelung and deformation contributions but without the rotational contribution. Q is the energy relative to the van der Waals bonded monomeric state. The interfullerene bonds in the polymer form by [2+2] cycloaddition. $^{4}$ For $RbC_{60}$ the heat of formation is considerably lower than for other cycloadduct bonds (e.g. 52 kJ/mol for cyclobutane). Also, the weaker bonds lower the decomposition temperature to ~400 K in contrast to ~800 K for cyclobutane. In the polymer, deformations of the covalent interfullerene bonds reduce the enthalpy of formation. X-ray diffraction shows an elongation of the intrafullerene C-C bonds $^{5}$ which take part in the cycloaddition and evidently there are bond angle deformations also. Such effects may reduce the formation energies significantly, e.g. a reduction of ~110 kJ/mol was found for the ethylene→cyclobutane reaction.

Our value of $Q_P$=25.8 kJ/mol is considerably higher than that reported by Zhu et al. $^{1}$ The polymer phase evolves slowly at ambient temperature and we find that $Q_P$ depends on the way the polymer is formed. It is significantly smaller if instead of a few weeks the sample is kept only a few hours at ambient temperature. Above room temperature but below 350 K the polymerization is faster $^{12}$ and a stable compound is obtained in reasonable time.

To study the dependence of the depolymerization peak on the temperature of polymerization, $T_{pol}$, a series of thermograms were taken on samples polymerized for 1 hour at different temperatures between 290 K and 400 K. The sample was first rapidly cooled from 500 K to $T_{pol}$ where it was kept for 1 hour. The thermograms were recorded from 200 K, which temperature was reached from $T_{pol}$ by cooling at the maximum rate achievable in the calorimeter (about 1 K/s). Samples heat treated between 290 K and 350 K were fully polymerized in the sense that no trace of the transformations characteristic of the bulk monomer or dimer phases were detected. The polymer state formed at $T_{pol}$=350 K is stable, while that formed at 290 K relaxes slowly even after an hour.

At higher temperatures ($T_{pol}$>350 K) the polymerization was not completed in 1 hour and some monomer phase remained. These monomers transform into the dimer phase during the rapid cooling to 200 K that precedes the recording of the thermograms. ### B. Decomposition of the Dimer Phase
Previous x-ray studies showed that in samples sufficiently rapidly cooled to avoid polymerization the orthorhombic dimer is the majority phase at 160 K.⁹,¹⁰ In the present work the dimer state was obtained by cooling the sample from 500 K to 200 K at an average cooling rate of about 1 K/s. The DSC thermogram recorded during a subsequent heating at 20 K/min is denoted by a continuous line in Fig. 3. We detected four endothermic ($T_{peak}$=260, 275, 305 and 405 K) and one exothermic ($T_{peak}$=360 K) peaks.

We cannot identify the weak endothermic peaks at 260 and 275 K with any of the known transitions. These peaks disappear if the sample is cycled between 215 and 273 K before the thermogram is taken (see dotted line in Fig. 3). Simultaneously with the disappearance of the low-T peaks the area under the peak at 305 K increases by about the same amount. We believe that the small endothermic peaks originate from inhomogeneities of the dimer phase that are removed during temperature cycling.

![](./images/813063009747337217_4.jpg)

Fig. 3. DSC thermograms recorded during heating the dimer phase. (——: Ini-tial dimer state is reached by rapid cooling from 500 to 200 K; - - - : Initial dimer state reached by rapid cooling to 200 K followed by cycling between 215 and 275 K; D - dimer.)

We interpret the endothermic peak at 305 K as the decomposition of dimers into a transient phase which according to Ref. 13 has an fcc structure. The existence of a transient state between the dimer and polymer is evident from the thermograms shown in Fig. 3. The decomposition of the dimer is completed at about 320 K where the polymerization just begins. The transient monomer fcc phase has also been observed in recent infrared measurements.¹¹ By x-ray diffraction a direct dimer→polymer transformation was observed¹⁰, however, this is a rather slow method and the transient could have been missed. The endothermic peak at ~405 K corresponds to depolymerization. (The area of the depolymerization peak is significantly less than that for the fully polymerized state shown in Fig. 1, as on the time scale of the DSC experiment the polymerization was incomplete.) The sequence of phase transformations during heating is: dimer→(transient) fcc→polymer→fcc. The repeated appearance of the fcc structure implies that the interfullerene bonding in the dimer and the polymer are different. In other words the dimers of the metastable phase below 290 K have a different molecular structure than the dimer units of the polymer. At 100 K the dimers are unstable against decomposition into monomers while the monomeric phase is unstable against polymerization.

The enthalpy of the dimer→fcc transition was determined by integrating the thermograms between 235 K (dimer) and 425 K (freely rotating fcc). We find $Q_{D}$≈10.5 kJ/mol independently of temperature cycling (i.e. for both thermograms shown in Fig. 3). Since the initial state is expected to contain some fcc phase as well, this value should be taken as a lower limit. After correcting for the rotational contribution, the enthalpy of formation for the dimer bonds is 5.2 kJ/mol. In the polymer there are twice as many interfullerene bonds per mole as in the dimer but Q is about four times larger. Therefore, the formation energy per polymer bond (measured relative to the van der Waals interacting $C_{60}$ monomers of the fcc phase) is about twice the energy for a dimer bond. This result supports our expectation that the interfullerene bonding in the dimer

differs from that in the polymer.

### C. Metastable fcc Phases

In samples rapidly cooled from 500 K to 273 K the majority phase is an fcc structure (henceforth fcc1³) of monomers. After quenching the sample may also contain up to 30 % dimers.¹⁵ On cooling from 273 K an exothermic peak (2.9 kJ/mol) is observed at ~235 K which we assign to the fcc1→dimer transition (Fig. 4.a). This value is smaller than $Q_D$-$Q_{rot}$=5.2 kJ/mol obtained for the cycled ("homogenized") sample. This may indicate that a significant fraction of the material transformed into the dimer phase during the initial quench and/or the dimerization was incomplete on the time scale of the cooling experiment. On heating the sample from the fcc1 phase (i.e. from 273 K), an endothermic transformation appears at $T_{peak}$=305 K in addition to the exothermic polymerization at about 360 K and the depolymerization at 405 K (Fig. 4.b). We assign this peak to a transformation of the fcc1 phase which precedes polymerization, because unlike the decomposition of the dimers (which also peaks at 305 K (Fig. 3)), this endothermic peak is reversible with a small hysteresis (Fig. 5.a). To obtain the cooling curve, the sample was kept for only a short time above 290 K to minimize polymerization. The enthalpy of transformation is 4.8 kJ/mol, close to that expected for the onset of free rotation ($Q_{rot}$=³/₂RT=4.0 kJ/mol at 320 K) and is significantly less than that for dedimerization (Fig. 5.b).

X-ray diffraction (Fig. 6) demonstrates the presence of two distinct fcc phases (fcc1: a=14.02 Å and fcc: a=14.06 Å) at 300 K. A series of spectra taken at 300 K (after rapid cooling from 500 K to 273 K) shows a fast disappearance of the fcc1 phase and the appearance of the fcc phase with a slightly larger lattice constant. This suggests that a change in the rotational state of the $C_{60}{^-}$ ions occurs which is similar to the transition of pure $C_{60}$ at 260 K. The rotation of the $C_{60}{^-}$ ions of the fcc1 phase is hindered, and the transition shown by the thermogram at 305 K is to a freely rotating state. This is supported by the observation that the fcc1 phase is more stable against polymerization than the fcc which transforms rapidly into the polymer. ### D. Phase Diagram
The complicated phase sequences obtained during heating the dimer and fccl phases may be understood from the relative Gibbs free energies of the phases involved. The relative Gibbs free energy of two phases can be estimated from their equilibrium temperature $T_{\text{eq}}$, heat of transformation $Q$ and specific heat difference $\Delta C_{\text{p}}$ as $\Delta G{=}\Delta H{-}T\Delta S$, where $\Delta H{=}Q{-}\int_{T}^{T_{\text{eq}}}\Delta C_{\text{p}}\,\text{d}T$ and $\Delta S{=}Q/T_{\text{eq}}{-}\int_{T}^{T_{\text{eq}}}[\Delta C_{\text{p}}/T]\,\text{d}T$. In the calculation we used the following data: dimer→fcc transition: $T_{\text{eq}}{=}280$ K, $Q{=}10.5$ kJ/mol; fccl→fcc transition: $T_{\text{eq}}{=}290$ K, $Q{=}4.8$ kJ/mol; polymer→fcc transition: $T_{\text{eq}}{=}370$ K, $Q{=}25.8$ kJ/mol. The equilibrium temperature for the dimer→fcc and fccl→fcc transitions were chosen as the onset temperature of the thermal effects (Fig. 5b). Since the polymer state strongly depends on the thermal history an equilibrium temperature of general validity cannot be defined. Therefore, we performed the calculation for a polymer which is in equilibrium with the fcc phase at 370 K. We assumed that the specific heat of all phases are the same except for the rotational contribution ($C_{\text{rot}}{=}^{3}/_{2}R$ for the fcc phase and 0 for the dimer, polymer and the fccl phases). The results are shown in Fig. 7. The stable phases are the fcc for $T{>}370$ K and the polymer below 370 K. The dimer is unstable with respect to the polymer. Slight corrections of the estimated Gibbs free energies may be necessary when accurate experimental data for the specific heats become available. Further work is needed to test the fine details of the calculated Gibbs free energy diagram. An interesting prediction is the appearance of the fccl transient phase between ~270 and 290 K during the decomposition of the dimer phase in heating (see thick solid line in Fig. 7). This may seem to contradict our experimental finding that the dimer transforms directly to the freely rotating fcc phase. Note, however, that the route shown in the Gibbs free energy diagram is only expected to be valid at low heating rates. On the time scale of the present experiments (performed at a heating rate as high as 20 K/min) the dimer→fccl and the fccl→fcc transitions are probably too close to be resolved.

Recently we became aware of a similar study by Kosaka et al. $^{16}$ in which they report a phase sequence for the decomposition of the dimer on the basis of DTA measurements performed at a considerably lower heating rate (3 K/min). It is rather similar to the one shown in Fig. 7: dimer→orientationally ordered→fcc→polymer→fcc. The only difference is that they propose an orientationally ordered (possibly simple cubic) transient phase instead of the fccl with hindered rotation. Our x-ray measurements do not exclude this possibility. High-$q$ x-ray diffraction experiments are required to clarify the structure of this transient phase.

### 4. Conclusions
The sequence and heat of phase transformations occurring in $\text{RbC}_{60}$ samples prepared by isothermal heat treatments and rapid cooling were determined. It was demonstrated that the polymer state depends on the duration and temperature of polymerization. Samples quenched into the dimer or metastable fccl phases display a repeated appearance of the fcc phase on heating: dimer→fcc→polymer→ fcc and fccl→fcc→polymer→fcc, respectively. The metastable fccl phase with hindered rotation of the $C_{60}$ monomers transforms to another metastable fcc state consisting of freely rotating $C_{60}^{-}$ ions at ~290 K. The enthalpy for the polymer→fcc and dimer→fcc transformations differ by a factor of two implying that the interfullerene bonding in the dimer and polymer are different. The respective values are consistent with a covalent interfullerene bonding accompanied with a substantial Madelung/deformation energy. The relative Gibbs free energies of the fcc, fccl,

dimer and polymer phases were estimated.
