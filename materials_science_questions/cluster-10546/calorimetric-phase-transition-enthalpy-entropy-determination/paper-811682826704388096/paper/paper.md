# The Thermodynamic Rules Governing Polymorphism
of Beryllium Oxide

Ya. O. Shablovskii

Gomel State Technical University, Gomel, Belarus
e-mail: shablov@gstu.gomel.by
Received September 28, 2009

**Abstract**—A thermodynamic analysis of qualitative and quantitative rules governing polymorphism of BeO was performed. Equations for the temperature dependences of its polarization and isobaric heat capacity in the low-temperature neighborhood of the wurtzite polymorph–rutile polymorph transition point were suggested. Equations describing the curvature of the line of this transition and mutual correlation of the temperature dependences of the thermodynamic characteristics of beryllium oxide were obtained.

DOI: 10.1134/S0036024410120022

Beryllium oxide BeO is distinguished among other thermally stable and chemically inert substances by its high heat conductivity close to the heat conductivity of metals [1, 2]. In addition, the attention of researchers is attracted by wide possibilities of structural modification of beryllium oxide solid solutions [3] and the possibility of its nanostructuring [4].

Like all crystalline substances, beryllium oxide possesses polymorphism. Under usual conditions, the low-temperature polymorph (LTP) exists. It is known in mineralogy as bromellite and has a hexagonal polar structure of the wurtzite type (space symmetry $P6_3mc$). It was predicted [5] that, at pressure $p > 40$ GPa, beryllium oxide should have a polymorph with higher symmetry of the structure. Later, model concepts [5] were repeatedly revised, and each subsequent estimate of the baric boundary of the stability of the wurtzite polymorph of BeO corresponded to higher and higher pressures [6].

Efforts to find a hyperbaric structural polymorph of BeO drew the attention of researchers from the observation that, at atmospheric pressure, beryllium oxide experiences the transition to the high-temperature polymorph (HTP) at $T_\lambda = 2102 \pm 5$ K. The structure of the high-temperature polymorph has tetragonal spatial symmetry $P4_2/mnm$ (the rutile structure). This polymorphic transition was observed as long ago as the middle of the past century [7], but it was not studied either theoretically or experimentally as yet. At the same time, in its crystal chemical nature, polymorphism of BeO has no analogues among the known compounds with the wurtzite structure and is therefore exceptionally interesting. The purpose of this work was to thermodynamically analyze the rules that govern polymorphism of beryllium oxide.

The LTP $\rightarrow$ HTP transition will be considered as the $g \rightarrow g^*$ transition, where $g$ is the symmetry group of the LTP and $g^*$ is the symmetry group of the HTP. The polymorphic transition is unambiguously determined by a finite set $f$ of crystal structure symmetry elements lost (or acquired) in this transition,

$$
f = g^*\setminus g. \tag{1}
$$

A set of tensor invariants $j_m$ ($m = 1, 2, ...$) corresponds to set $f$. These invariants describe anisotropic properties inherent in the LTP but not characteristic of the HTP because these properties are forbidden by symmetry of the HTP,

$$
j_m \in (F\setminus F^*), \tag{2}
$$

where $F$ and $F^*$ are sets of invariants of groups $g$ and $g^*$. The invariant of the lowest rank in set (2), the main invariant $\mathfrak{I}$, characterizes the main effect caused by structure symmetry lowering and therefore determines the crystal chemical nature of the corresponding polymorphic transition.

The physicochemical property of a polymorphic substance characterized by the $\mathfrak{I}$ value only appears in the low-symmetry polymorph; for the high-symmetry polymorph of the same substance, $\mathfrak{I} \equiv 0$. This allows a polymorphic transition to be treated as a phase transition (PT) assuming that $\mathfrak{I} \equiv 0$ for one structural phase (high-symmetry polymorph) and $\mathfrak{I} > 0$ for the other phase (low-symmetry polymorph). As a rule, a poly-

morph with a more symmetrical structure exists at a higher temperature $T$. We then have

$$
\left.\mathfrak{J}\right|_{T<T_{\lambda}}>0 ;\left.\quad \mathfrak{J}\right|_{T \geq T_{\lambda}} \equiv 0.
$$

The $\mathfrak{I}$ value increases as the temperature of the crystal decreases from the polymorphic transition point $T=T_{\lambda}$. We can select some reference value

$$
\overline{\mathfrak{J}}=\left.\mathfrak{J}\right|_{T \ll T_{\lambda}},
$$

introduce the auxiliary parameter $q=\mathfrak{I} / \overline{\mathfrak{I}}$, and expand the thermodynamic potential $\varphi$ of the polymorphic crystal in the low-temperature neighborhood of the $T=T_{\lambda}$ point in powers of this small parameter,

$$
\varphi=[\varphi]+\tilde{A} q+\frac{a q^{2}}{2}+\frac{\tilde{B} q^{3}}{3}+\frac{b q^{4}}{4}+\frac{\tilde{H} q^{5}}{5}+\frac{h q^{6}}{6}+\ldots, \quad(3)
$$

where $[\varphi]=\left.\varphi\right|_{q=0}$.

Any anisotropic structure symmetry change is considered in theoretical crystal chemistry in view of the general Curie principle [8]: dissymmetry that appears in the system should be present in the reasons that cause this dissymmetry. At the same time, in practice, polymorphic transitions are usually induced by isotropic actions, heating (cooling) and hydrostatic compression. The contradiction between symmetry lowering of an anisotropic crystal structure during its polymorphic transformation and isotropy of external actions causing this transformation is removed because crystals are separated into domains in the transition to the LTP. Separate domain structure symmetry is lower than HTP structure symmetry, but the arrangement of domains in an LTP crystal obeys symmetry elements lost in the polymorphic transition. As a consequence, crystal symmetry as a whole does not change. The domenization phenomenon is well known, but it is believed that it is inherent in ferroelectrics and magnets. In reality, by virtue of the Curie principle, domenization is characteristic of all the polymorphic crystals. As applied to the problem under consideration, this means that expansion (3) should be invariant with respect to the replacement $q \rightarrow-q$ and can therefore only contain even powers of $q$. If we ignore omitted terms in expansion (3), then, taking into account the aforesaid, an approximate equation for the thermodynamic potential in the low-temperature neighborhood of the polymorphic transition point has the form

$$
\varphi \approx[\varphi]+\frac{a q^{2}}{2}+\frac{b q^{4}}{4}+\frac{h q^{6}}{6}.
$$

In view of the thermodynamic formalism that we are developing, the existence of a stable polymorph is equivalent to the presence of a minimum of the $\varphi(q)$ profile at some $q=q^{\prime}$ value. For the high-symmetry polymorph, $q^{\prime}=0$ (that is, $\varphi(q)$ has a single central minimum), and, in the region of the stability of the low-symmetry polymorph, the $\varphi(q)$ profile, which is an even function of $q$, has two symmetrical side minima. Three possible $\varphi(q)$ profiles are shown in Fig. 1, where curve 1 is the thermodynamic potential profile of a high-symmetry polymorph and curves 2 and 3 are thermodynamic potential profiles of a low-symmetry polymorph. Accordingly, the high-symmetry polymorph $\leftrightarrow$ low-symmetry polymorph transition can follow three paths.

(1) Sequential transformation curve $1 \leftrightarrow$ curve $2 \leftrightarrow$ curve 3 (precritical FT of the first order).

(2) A change in the ratio between the depths of $\varphi(q)$ minima: in both structural phases, the $\varphi(q)$ profiles are qualitatively the same (curve 2), and a deeper minimum corresponds to the stable polymorph (noncritical phase transition of the first order).

(3) A change in convexity of $\varphi(q)$ as a result of the curve $1 \leftrightarrow$ curve 3 transformation (critical FT, or FT of the third order, which is the same).

Variant 3 is a degenerate case of variant 1 [9].

According to thermodynamic stability requirements, $h$ should be considered a positive constant in (4). Because of thermodynamic potential linearity with respect to temperature [10], multipliers $a$ and $b$ are given by

$$
a=a_{T}\left(T-T_{\lambda}^{+}\right)
$$

for variant **1**, where $a_T$ and $b$ are positive constants, and
$$b = b_T(T - T_\lambda^-)\tag{6}$$
for variant **2**, where $a$ and $b_T$ are positive constants.
Here, $T_\lambda^+$ and $T_\lambda^-$ are the temperatures of the precritical and noncritical FT of the first order, respectively; it is assumed that pressure $p$ is given.

Both variants **1** and **2** correspond to FTs of the first
order. To distinguish between them, it should be borne
in mind that different crystalline polymorphs of the
same substance are formed by the same structural
units. Therefore, the mutual relation between poly-
morph structures cannot be arbitrary. The continuity
of crystalline polymorphs of a substance in its struc-
tural transformations is determined by the existence
of a common crystallographic supergroup $\mathcal{G}$ of sym-
metry groups of the structures of all the polymorphs,
that is,
$$g \subset \mathcal{G},\quad g^* \subseteq \mathcal{G}.\tag{7}$$

Two cases are possible,
$$g \subseteq g^*\tag{8}$$
or
$$g \cap g^*,\quad g \not\subset g^*.\tag{9}$$

In case (8), the subgroup $\to$ group transition corre-
sponds to the LTP $\to$ HTP transformation. The rigor-
ous character of relation (8) provides unambiguity and
two-sided reproducibility of the $g \to g^*$ transition on
the phase diagram of a substance. At the same time, in
case (9), the transition between adjacent subgroups
corresponds to the LTP $\to$ HTP transformation. Rela-
tion (9) is not rigorous, and the thermodynamic path
of the $g \to g^*$ transition is therefore not unambiguous.
It follows that, if (8) is satisfied, the $g \to g^*$ transition
is enantiotropic, and, if we have (9), the transition can
be both enantiotropic and monotropic. The latter
means that variant (8) corresponds to a precritical
phase transition of the first order (or its degenerate
case, critical phase transition), whereas variant (9)
corresponds to a noncritical PT.

For the polymorphic transformation of beryllium
oxide,
$$g=3m,\quad g^*=4/mmm.$$

It follows that, for this crystal,
$$\mathcal{G}=m\overline{3}m.\tag{10}$$

Conclusion (10) of the presence of a cubic BeO
polymorph, which is a praphase of its wurtzite and
rutile polymorphs, is in agreement with the available
experimental data [11, 12] on the presence of BeO
polymorphic transition from the wurtzite polymorph
(B4) to a cubic polymorph with a halite structure (B1).
These observations can be an answer to the question
raised two decades ago [5] about the hyperbaric high-
symmetry beryllium oxide polymorph. In spite of this,
the authors of the last theoretical works [13–15]
ignored the results obtained in [11, 12]. The proba-
ble reason for this was likely the strongly smeared
rather than jump, as repeatedly predicted, character
of the observed B4–B1 transition: the wurtzite and
halite phases coexisted at pressures 137 GPa $< p <$
175 GPa. At the same time, the smeared character of
the observed transition is evidence that the halite
phase of beryllium oxide is, with respect to the
wurtzite phase, just the structural praphase rather
than the “usual” adjacent polymorph and corre-
sponds to the crystallographic supergroup of the
other BeO structural polymorphs.

In view of the thermodynamic analysis performed
above, the following conclusions can be drawn from
(7) and (10). Beryllium oxide LTP and HTP are the
results of trigonal and tetragonal, respectively, dis-
tortions of its cubic praphase. The LTP–HTP tran-
sition is a noncritical first-order PT, and the main
invariant $\Im$ in this transition is the polarization $P$ of
the crystal lattice. In addition, a precritical or criti-
cal transition of BeO from the tetragonal to cubic
polymorph is predicted at 2150 K $< T <$ 2800 K. This
means that the $T_\lambda^+$ temperature in (5) corresponds to
this predicted polymorphic transformation of beryl-
lium oxide, and the $T_\lambda^-$ temperature in (6) corre-
sponds to the observed LTP–HTP polymorphic
transformation point.

It follows from (4) and (6) that the temperature
dependences of polarization $P$ and isobaric heat
capacity $C_p$ of beryllium oxide in the low-temperature
neighborhood of the LTP–HTP transition point are
approximately described by the equations
$$P \approx \overline{P}\left(B_h(T_\lambda^--T)+\left(B_h^2(T_\lambda^--T)^2-A_h\right)^{1/2}\right)^{1/2},\tag{11}$$

$$\begin{gathered}
\frac{C_p}{T} \approx \frac{[C_p]}{T}+hB_h^2\left(B_h(T_\lambda^--T)+\left(B_h^2(T_\lambda^--T)^2-A_h\right)^{1/2}\right)^{1/2} \\
+hB_h^3(T_\lambda^--T)\left(1-\frac{B_h}{\left(B_h^2(T_\lambda^--T)^2-A_h\right)^{1/2}}\right),
\end{gathered}\tag{12}$$

where $\overline{P}=\left.P\right|_{T \ll T_\lambda^-}$, $B_h=\frac{b_T}{2h}$, and $A_h=\frac{a}{h}$.

In (12), square brackets denote the additive regular
component of heat capacity “insensitive” to the poly-
morphic transition (compare (3) and (4)). This term is
the heat capacity of the structural praphase, the cubic
BeO polymorph. We do not consider this regular com-
ponent, because the thermodynamic characteristics of

To obtain such an equation, we must perform a thermodynamic analysis of the "rutile polymorph–cubic polymorph" phase transition, which should be made independently.

Let us discuss the behavior of the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion $\alpha$ and volume compressibility of beryllium oxide. Its molar entropy $S$ and molar volume $V$ are functions of the form $S = S(\theta)$ and $V = V(\theta)$, where $\theta = T - T_{\lambda}^{-}$. Bearing this mind, we can write $\alpha = V_{0}^{-1}(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ and $\beta = -V_{0}^{-1}(\partial V/\partial p)_T$, where $V_0$ is the molar volume under standard conditions (for beryllium oxide, $V_0 = 8.25 \times 10^{-3}$ m³ [17]). Using the known equation $(\partial V/\partial T)_p = -(\partial S/\partial p)_T$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha &= -\frac{1}{V_0}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial \theta}\right)_p\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial p}\right)_T = -\frac{1}{V_0}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_p\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial p}\right)_T, \\
\beta &= -\frac{1}{V_0}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial \theta}\right)_p\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial p}\right)_T = -\frac{1}{V_0}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial p}\right)_T.
\end{aligned} \tag{13}
$$

Here, beryllium oxide LTP and HTP are treated as thermodynamically stable phases. The polymorphic transition temperature $T_{\lambda}^{-}$ therefore depends on pressure only, and

$$
\left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial p}\right)_T = -\frac{d T_{\lambda}^{-}}{dp}. \tag{14}
$$

Taking (14) into account, Eqs. (13) can be written as

$$
\alpha = \frac{\gamma^{-} C_p}{V_0 T}, \tag{15}
$$

$$
\beta = \alpha \gamma^{-}, \tag{16}
$$

where $\gamma^{-} = \frac{d T_{\lambda}^{-}}{dp}$. Let us write (16) in the form

$$
\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial p}\right)_T = \gamma^{-}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial p}\right)_T
$$

and integrate it in pressure. We obtain an additional linear equation

$$
V = V_0 + S \gamma^{-}. \tag{17}
$$

The $\gamma^{-}$ value depends on pressure only, and it follows from (15)–(17) that, at given $p = \text{const}$, the temperature dependences of the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion, bulk compressibility, reduced heat capacity $C_p/T$, and the temperature dependences of molar volume and molar entropy of beryllium oxide

coincide to within a constant factor. The value of this factor is determined by the slope of the $T_{\lambda}^{-}(p)$ line at a given $p =$ const value. Let us determine the equation for the $T_{\lambda}^{-}(p)$ line.

The structure of BeO weakly changes as the pressure varies [7, 18]. We can therefore arbitrarily select a reference point $T_{\lambda}^{-}(p)$ on the $(p^{\circ}, T_{\lambda}^{\circ})$ line and write for the vicinity of this point the equation that follows from the Clausius–Clapeyron equation in a linear approximation,
$$
=T_{\lambda}^{\circ}\left[\{V\}_{\lambda}^{\circ}+\left.\frac{d\{V\}_{\lambda}}{d p}\right|^{\circ}\left(p-p^{\circ}\right)\right] /\left[\Theta^{\circ}+\left.\frac{d \Theta}{d p}\right|^{\circ}\left(p-p^{\circ}\right)\right].^{(18)}
$$

Here, $\Theta$ is the heat of the transition, braces $\{\}_{\lambda}$ denote the jump of the corresponding value in the LTP–HTP transition, and the superscript “°” relates values to the reference point. The integration of (18) yields
$$
T_{\lambda}^{-}(p)=T_{\lambda}^{\circ}\left[1+\Gamma\left(p-p^{\circ}\right)\right]+A \ln \left|1+B\left(p-p^{\circ}\right)\right|, \quad(19)
$$
where
$$
\Gamma=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma^{\prime \prime}}, \quad A=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime \prime}\{V\}_{\lambda}^{\circ}-\Gamma^{\prime} \Theta^{\circ}}{\left(\Gamma^{\prime \prime}\right)^{2}}, \quad B=\frac{T_{\lambda}^{\circ} \Gamma^{\prime \prime}}{\Theta^{\circ}},
$$
$$
\Gamma^{\prime}=\frac{V_{0}\left(\{\beta\}^{\circ}+\{\alpha\}^{\circ} \gamma^{\circ}\right)}{T_{\lambda}^{\circ}}, \quad \Gamma^{\prime \prime}=V_{0}\{\alpha\}^{\circ}+\gamma^{\circ} \frac{\left\{C_{p}\right\}^{\circ}}{T_{\lambda}^{\circ}}.
$$

The available literature data on the temperature dependences of isobaric heat capacity and the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion of beryllium oxide allow Eq. (15) to be checked. The experimental $\alpha$ and $C_{p}/T$ values [19–21] corresponding to the same temperature ($40\ \text{K} \leq T \leq 720\ \text{K}$) were the coordinates of the points plotted on the $C_{p}/T$; $\alpha$ plane (Fig. 2). The $\alpha(C_{p}/T)$ dependence obtained this way was rectilinear, as predicted by (15). This construction allowed us to determine the initial slope of the LTP–HTP transition line; at atmospheric pressure, $\gamma^{-}=7.48\ \text{K/MPa}$. This in turn was used in the equation
$$
\beta=\frac{(\gamma^{-})^{2}}{V_{0}} \frac{C_{p}}{T}, \tag{20}
$$
that follows from (15) and (16), which allowed us to calculate the temperature dependence of the volume compressibility of beryllium oxide using the experimental $C_{p}(T)$ dependence [19, 20].

To summarize, we performed a thermodynamic analysis of the qualitative and quantitative rules that governed beryllium oxide polymorphism. Most attention was focused on its “wurtzite polymorph–rutile polymorph” transition. The following results were obtained:

The existence of the hyperbaric cubic beryllium oxide polymorph follows directly from the group theory relation between the structures of its wurtzite and rutile polymorphs (Eqs. (7), (10)). The temperature dependences of the polarization and isobaric heat capacity of beryllium oxide in the low-temperature neighborhood of the “wurtzite polymorph–rutile polymorph” transition point are described by approximate equations (11) and (12). The mutual correlation of the temperature dependences of the coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion, volume compressibility, and reduced heat capacity $C_{p}/T$ and also the temperature dependences of the molar volume and molar entropy of beryllium oxide is described by Eqs. (15)–(17) and (20). The curvature of the “wurtzite polymorph–rutile polymorph” transition line is determined by Eq. (19).
