# Molar heat capacity and thermodynamic properties of p-quaterphenyl $^{a}$

KAZUYA SAITO, TOORU ATAKE, $^{b}$ and
HIDEAKI CHIHARA

Chemical Thermodynamics Laboratory and Department of Chemistry,
Faculty of Science, Osaka University, Toyonaka, Osaka 560, Japan

( Received 14 September 1984; in revised form 26 November 1984)

The molar heat capacity of p-quaterphenyl was measured by adiabatic calorimetry between 3 and 300 K. The phase transition associated with a molecular-conformation change is observed as a very broad anomaly with a maximum at $(233.0 \pm 0.5)$ K. The molar enthalpy and entropy of transition are $(414 \pm 20)\ \text{J·mol}^{-1}$ and $(1.82 \pm 0.10)\ \text{J·K}^{-1}·\text{mol}^{-1}$, respectively. A comparison is made with the properties of biphenyl and p-terphenyl.

## 1. Introduction

p-Quaterphenyl $(1,1':4',1'':4'',1'''-quaterphenyl)$ is a member of the so-called p-polyphenyl family of compounds. The first several members have internal flexibility regarding the twisting degrees of freedom of the phenyl rings and their conformations depend strongly on the environment. While they are twisted in the gaseous and liquid states, $^{(1-10)}$ they crystallize in the structure of $P2_{1}/a$ with statistically planar conformation $^{(11-20)}$ at room temperature.

Since Hochstrasser et al. $^{(21)}$ reported on the existence of a structural phase transition in biphenyl crystal, a number of studies of p-polyphenyls have been made by use of neutron, $^{(22-30)}$ optical, $^{(31-38)}$ and other techniques, $^{(39-55)}$ and a similar structural change has also been found in p-terphenyl $^{(56)}$ and in p-quaterphenyl. $^{(57)}$ Thus, it has been commonly accepted that the transition is associated with the resumption of a twisted molecular conformation below the transition temperature. For biphenyl crystal, the transition occurs at 40.4 K, and is of a displacive type associated with a soft mode. The structure of the low-temperature phase is incommensurate in the directions of both $a$ and $b$ axes. $^{(23,24)}$ An incommensurate commensurate transition (so-called lock-in transition) in the direction of the $a$ axis occurs at $16.8\ \text{K}.^{(42)}$ However, the crystal remains incommensurate along the $b$ axis at lower temperatures, no lock-in transition being observed so far in the $b$ direction.

On the other hand, p-terphenyl crystal undergoes a transition at 193.5 K without a soft mode. Instead, the transition is of an order-disorder type;⁽⁵⁶⁾ the Ising model appears to be applicable to the heat-capacity anomaly.⁽⁵⁸⁾

Only a few reports⁽²⁰,³⁷,⁴⁶,⁵⁷⁾ have been published on p-quaterphenyl. At room temperature the crystal belongs to $P2_1/a$ and the molecule has a statistically planar conformation,⁽²⁰⁾ but the symmetry of the crystal is lowered to $P\overline{1}$ at a low temperature where the adjacent phenyl rings in the molecule are twisted alternately.⁽⁵⁷⁾ Bolton and Prasad⁽³⁷⁾ reported from their Raman study that there was a transition at about 230 K and that it was continuous. Toudic *et al.*⁽⁴⁶⁾ found by n.m.r. the behavior of critical slowing down, which is very similar to the case of p-terphenyl.⁽⁴⁴⁻⁴⁶⁾

Although many interesting features of the phase transitions in p-polyphenyls have been discovered, few calorimetric studies have been reported so far.⁽³⁹,⁴⁰,⁵⁵⁾ Since accurate thermodynamic properties give indispensable clues to the nature of phase transitions we began a series of measurements on p-polyphenyls, *i.e.* biphenyl⁽⁴¹⁻⁴³⁾ and p-terphenyl⁽⁴³,⁵⁸⁾ and their deuterated analogues,⁽⁴²,⁴³⁾ and p-quaterphenyl. This paper provides a full description of precise measurements of equilibrium thermodynamic properties of p-quaterphenyl. A comparison with the properties of biphenyl⁽⁴¹⁻⁴³⁾ and p-terphenyl⁽⁴³,⁵⁸⁾ is also given.

### 2. Experimental

Commercially available p-quaterphenyl (nominal purity, 99.9 moles per cent, Tokyo Kasei Kogyo Co., Ltd.) was purified further by fractional sublimation in vacuum at about 470 K.

The powdered specimen, mass 9.9818 g (0.032577 mol), was loaded into the gold-plated copper calorimeter vessel. The calorimeter was evacuated and sealed after the addition of a small amount of helium gas for heat exchange (8 kPa at room temperature); the contribution of the helium to the total heat capacity was negligibly small. The sample contributed about 50 per cent to the total heat capacity including that of the calorimeter vessel below 20 K. As the temperature increased, the contribution of the sample decreased to about 30 per cent at 100 K. Above 100 K, however, it increased again up to about 40 per cent at room temperature.

The apparatus and operation of the adiabatic calorimeter will be described elsewhere.⁽⁵⁸⁾ The operational criterion for thermodynamic equilibrium is discretionary in any measurement of adiabatic calorimetry. The criterion used in our laboratory is that the temperature of the calorimeter be stationary to within $1 \times 10^{-4}$ K during 4 min. The normal length of time needed to reach such a drift or fluctuation rate depends on the working temperature as will be described in the following section. A platinum resistance thermometer (model 8164, Leeds and Northrup Co.) and a germanium resistance thermometer (model CR-1000, CryoCal Inc.) were used. Their temperature scales are based on the IPTS-68, helium gas thermometry, and the $1958\ ^4$He scale.⁽⁵⁹,⁶⁰⁾

### 3. Results and discussion

#### HEAT CAPACITIES AND THERMODYNAMIC FUNCTIONS

The measured molar heat capacities are shown in figure 1 and tabulated in table 1 in chronological order. The temperature increment due to each energy input may be deduced from the adjacent mean temperatures; it was small enough to permit the curvature correction to be ignored in comparison with the experimental precision. After each energy input, thermal equilibrium within the calorimeter vessel was attained in about 2 min below 15 K, 10 min at about 50 K, 20 min at about 150 K, and 10 min above 200 K. Concerning thermal equilibration, no anomalous behavior was observed in the transition region (180 to 270 K). Some thermodynamic functions at round temperatures were calculated from the primary results and are given in table 2, in which the small contributions below 4 K were evaluated by smooth extrapolation to $T \rightarrow 0$.

## PHASE TRANSITION AT 233 K

As seen in figure 1, the transition is observed as a broad anomaly with a heat-capacity maximum at $(233.0\pm 0.5)$ K. The general shape of the anomaly was the same when smaller temperature intervals (about 1 K) were used for the heat-capacity measurements. The results of such measurements are not included in table 1 or in figure 1 because the scatter of the measured points was slightly larger than for those displayed in figure 1. The shape and magnitude of the anomaly due to the phase transition was obtained by subtracting the normal portion of the heat capacity by drawing a smooth interpolating curve between the lower- and the higher-temperature sides as shown by the solid line in figure 1. The excess heat capacities thus determined are shown in figure 2, and extend over a very wide temperature range of about 90 K. The shape of the anomaly shows that the phase transition is of higher order. The enthalpy and the entropy of the transition are tabulated in table 3 together with those for biphenyl $^{(41)}$ and $p$-terphenyl $^{(58)}$ for the sake of comparison.

HEAT CAPACITY OF $p$-QUATERPHENYL

It is interesting to compare the three lowest members of the $p$-polyphenyls with regard to behavior at the phase transitions. In biphenyl, the transition temperature is the lowest of the three and the entropy of the transition as well as the shape of the heat-capacity anomaly are consistent with a model of soft-mode transition. $^{(61)}$

$p$-Quaterphenyl also shows an anomaly of similar shape which extends over a wide temperature range comparable to the case of biphenyl (about 40 per cent of the center temperature of the transition, 90 to 233 K). However, the entropy of transition is larger by a factor of 14 than that of biphenyl although it is smaller than the $R \ln 2$ expected if the mechanism of the transition were of a simple order-disorder type arising from the twisting of the molecule about the central C-C bond.

$p$-Terphenyl is different from the other two in regard to the shape of the heat-capacity anomaly, i.e. it is more like an Ising spin transition. The entropy of the transition is, on the other hand, close to that in $p$-quaterphenyl. A critical phenomenon is reported for this transition. $^{(29,44-46)}$

Why the transition temperatures are in the order of increasing molecular size may be understood if we take into account the increasing number of interacting atom pairs between adjacent molecules and within a molecule as the molecular size becomes larger. It is therefore the overall combination of those intermolecular and intramolecular interactions that determines the force constant for the twisting motion.

The twisting of phenyl rings relative to one another in a molecule is a result of a subtle balancing of various forces. Inside a molecule, the repulsion between hydrogen atoms at the ortho positions favors the twisted conformation but delocalization of $\pi$-electrons is against it. There must, therefore, be an optimum twisting angle which would give an overall potential minimum in either direction. Usually, such a double-minimum potential tends to cause an order-disorder type of transition if there is a mechanism by which the probability of a molecule to assume one of the two minima is strongly dependent on the orientation of the neighboring molecules, i.e. a kind of stochastic effect. However, if dynamical coupling between neighboring molecules is such that there is a dispersion in the optical branch of the vibrational spectrum dependent on temperature, a soft-mode transition would result. There will of course be intermediate cases in which the stochastic and dynamical effects are present to different degrees.

Which effect, the stochastic or the dynamical, prevails in a particular substance probably depends on the distribution of vibrational modes. For biphenyl the dynamical effect seems to play a more important role while $p$-terphenyl seems to lie at the other extreme where the stochastic mechanism should apply.

The situation may have a certain relation to the intramolecular twisting modes. As figure 3 shows, there are two such modes in $p$-terphenyl and three in $p$-quaterphenyl if phenyl rings are treated as rigid and the molecular symmetry as $\mathrm{D}_{2 \mathrm{h}}$. Biphenyl has only one such mode. It has been demonstrated that the twisting force-constant in biphenyl is small and it was also shown by a lattice-dynamics calculation that the small force constant can explain the low-temperature heat capacities (see the following section). If one compares the shape of the anomalous portion of the heat capacity in $p$-quaterphenyl with that in biphenyl, one can infer

![](./images/812345510437322753_3.jpg)

FIGURE 3. Possible normal modes of twisting motion, assuming phenyl rings to be rigid and molecular symmetry to be of planar conformation $(D_{2h})$.

that there must be a twisting mode or modes in the $p$-quaterphenyl molecule that have small force constants. It is thus likely that the $A_u$ mode is one such candidate because it has only one node, oscillates in the same way as the biphenyl molecule, and has a small force constant. In contrast to this, the two modes of $p$-terphenyl must oscillate in a steeper potential well because of a larger number of interacting ortho-hydrogen pairs, giving a larger force constant.

It is difficult to make quantitative calculations at this time because of lack of detailed knowledge of the crystal structures. However, it is possible qualitatively to understand the behavior of the three substances by using the composite model outlined here.

CROSSOVER OF HEAT CAPACITIES AT LOW TEMPERATURES

In the course of the series of studies on $p$-polyphenyls, we found that the heat capacity of biphenyl becomes larger than that of $p$-terphenyl as it is cooled, *i.e.* there is a crossover in the low-temperature heat capacities. In figure 4, a comparison is shown in terms of the corresponding Debye temperatures assuming 6 degrees of freedom per molecule. The crossover between biphenyl and $p$-quaterphenyl is observed at about 6 K. According to the rigid molecular model of the Debye theory, the heavier molecule should show a larger heat capacity in the low-temperature region, where only low-lying lattice modes are excited.

The crossover between biphenyl and p-terphenyl at about 12 K was rationalized
by a simple model calculation of the lattice-vibration spectrum. $^{(43)}$ The calculation
was able to reveal that the abnormally large heat capacities of biphenyl at low
temperatures are due to the small value of the force constant for the twisting
motion. The intramolecular modes are coupled with the lattice modes, which results
in a high density of states of the biphenyl crystal at low frequencies. The crossover
between biphenyl and p-quaterphenyl may also be rationalized by a similar
calculation. However, the relation of the incommensurability to this phenomenon is
not obvious. Studies on this point are in progress in our laboratory.

However, the two curves approach each other as the temperature decreases, and
they may cross at even lower temperatures.
