The diffusion pathways of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions within the frameworks of the high conductive solid electrolytes $\text{LiTi}_{2}(\text{PO}_{4})_{3}$ and the Al-doped one $\text{Li}_{1.3}\text{Ti}_{1.7}\text{Al}_{0.3}\text{P}_{3}\text{O}_{12}$ are determined by molecular mechanics and molecular dynamics methods. From a potential energy profile for the lithium migration in $\text{LiTi}_{2}(\text{PO}_{4})_{3}$, the barrier height of lithium migration is estimated as 28.95 kJ/mol (0.30 eV) which is in excellent agreement with experimental data. The main influence on the activation energy of lithium diffusion is caused by changes in electrostatic interactions. Long molecular dynamics simulations for $\text{Li}_{1.3}\text{Ti}_{1.7}\text{Al}_{0.3}\text{P}_{3}\text{O}_{12}$ also confirm a solid state diffusion process via rare and sporadic hopping of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions from their energy minimum site to a neighboring energy minimum site.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Lithium titanium phosphate $\text{LiTi}_{2}(\text{PO}_{4})_{3}$ (LTP) is one of the promising $\text{Li}^{+}$ conductive solid electrolytes and has been extensively studied because of its potential use in all-solid-state lithium batteries. $^{1-11}$ Although LTP shows relatively high chemical stability against ambient atmosphere, the ionic conductivity of polycrystalline pure LTP has been reported to be relatively low $(10^{-7}\,\text{S}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ at $25\,^{\circ}\text{C})$ for practical use. $^{7,8}$ Several researchers have tried to enhance the conductivity of LTP by partial replacement of $\text{Ti}^{4+}$ ions with trivalent ions such as $\text{In}^{3+}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{Sc}^{3+}$, $^{1-8,10}$ or by the addition of lithium salts such as $\text{Li}_{3}\text{PO}_{4}$ or $\text{LiNO}_{3}$. $^{7,9,10}$ Aono *et al*. $^{7-9}$ have concluded that the main reason for the conductivity enhancement in both cases seems to be attributable to the increase in the sintered pellet density. One of the approaches for further understanding of the enhancement mechanism is a theoretical simulation for clarifying the conduction mechanism of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions in crystalline pure LTP.

So far the diffusion pathway of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions in LTP has not been well investigated in detail from both experimental and theoretical points of view, partly because of its complicated crystallographic structure. The anionic framework of LTP shown in Fig. 1 is composed of a three-dimensional (3D) network of $\text{PO}_{4}$ tetrahedra sharing corners with $\text{TiO}_{6}$ octahedra and with lithium ions located in interstitial sites. In pure LTP all Li(1) sites are occupied, whereas the Li(2) and Li(3) positions are empty.

This is a similar structure to NASICON $(\text{Na}^{+}$ super ionic conductors $\text{Na}_{1+x}\text{Zr}_{2}\text{Si}_{x}\text{P}_{3-x}\text{O}_{12}$, $0{<}x{<}3).^{12-14}$ In NASICON, the path along Na(1)–Na(2)–Na(1) sites via so-called ‘‘bottlenecks’’ $^{15}$ was unambiguously extracted as the most favorable diffusion pathway for $\text{Na}^{+}$ migration. $^{16}$ $\text{PO}_{4}$ tetrahedra and $\text{TiO}_{6}$ octahedra determine the effective width of the bottlenecks, which influence largely the hopping rate of $\text{Na}^{+}$ ions. By analogy with NASICON, $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions are speculated to migrate along Li(1)–Li(2)–Li(1) sites via similar bottlenecks in the LTP structure (see Fig. 1). In cation-doped LTP systems such as $\text{Li}_{1+x}\text{Ti}_{2-x}\text{Al}_{x}(\text{PO}_{4})_{3}$, conductivity enhancement could be attributed to an increase in both concentration and hopping rate of mobile ions; further an increase in population of Li(2) site occupation would open the bottleneck width, causing a higher $\text{Li}^{+}$ diffusion. An experimental distinction between these two effects (concentration and hopping rate of mobile ions) is usually rather difficult.

In the present work, we have performed molecular mechanics (MM) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for the $\text{Li}^{+}$ migration in LTP and Al-doped LTP in order to clarify the conduction pathways in crystalline LTP, as well as to estimate the effective bottleneck size and the hopping rates of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions in pure LTP.

## II. SIMULATION METHOD

For all simulations the Universal Force Field (UFF)¹⁷ available in the CERIUS² program package¹⁸ is modified to be useful for inorganic intercalation compounds. The force field is represented by the following sum of potential energy terms:

$$
E_{\text{tot}}=E_{\text{bonds}}+E_{\text{angles}}+E_{\text{torsions}}+E_{\text{vdW}}+E_{\text{Coulomb}}. \tag{1}
$$

The host framework of LTP and Al-doped LTP is modeled by calculating all P–O, Ti–O, and Al–O interactions and the corresponding O–X–O and X–O–P angle terms explicitly (X=Ti,Al), whereas all $\text{Li}^{+}$–framework and $\text{Li}^{+}$–$\text{Li}^{+}$ interactions are simulated by van der Waals and Coulomb forces only. Previous simulations on layered $\text{Li}_{x}\text{CoO}_{2}$ oxides¹⁹ have shown that the ‘‘natural bond lengths’’ $r_{ij}$ determined with the default parameters of the UFF are usually too short for their use in inorganic framework and layer compounds. In order to preserve the general character and the transferability of the modified UFF, the parameter set for the calculation of ‘‘natural bond lengths’’ $r_{ij}$ is fitted to produce interatomic distances which are equivalent to those gotten with the effective ionic radii of Shannon’s systematic study of halides and chalcogenides.²⁰ For the ‘‘natural bond angles’’ $\theta_{0}$ ideal coordination is assumed for the $\text{PO}_{4}$ tetrahedra and the $\text{TiO}_{6}$ octahedra, whereas a zeolitic like P–O–Ti bond angle of 145.5° is used as a default for the corner connection of these polyhedra. The adjusted parameter set for all MM and MD calculations is summarized in Table I.

Periodic boundary conditions were enforced and for the long range Coulomb forces as well as for the dispersive part of the van der Waals interactions the Ewald summation procedure was used.²¹,²² Partial charges were obtained with the charge equilibrium method²³ recommended for the use in conjunction with the UFF. The great advantage of this method is that charge neutrality can be reached for every simulation box and the charges are adjusted to match different electrostatic environments for a given atom/ion.

In summary, because of the general character of the UFF, the results are of good quality and transferability when considering energy differences of similar configurations, but for other physical properties there might be more excellent results by using specialized force fields. On the other hand, the physical clarity of the UFF derived from general atomic properties like hybridization and connectivity would be lost then.

## III. ENERGY MINIMIZATION USING THE UFF

For all MM energy minimization a quasi-Newton method using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno formula²⁴,²⁵ shows the best results for the chosen LTP compounds. In this method the inverse hessian is updated between cycles of minimization based upon the gradient and position from the current and previous cycles. During one minimization cycle a maximal displacement step in the region of 0.001–0.01 Å was preferred. The experimental unit cell parameters are constrained during the energy minimization process and only the positions of the atoms within the unit cell are optimized with energy minimization techniques. Using this procedure, not only bond distances, but also bond angles are in excellent agreement with experimental data in NASICON like compounds. A full energy minimization is possible, but a deviation of experimental and calculated unit cell parameters in the region of 3%–6% was considered to be too large for the later determination of activation energies of lithium diffusion. This fact is a shortcoming of the UFF, but does not interfere with the aims presented in this paper.

Since the calculation of second derivatives is computationally very expensive, the conjugate gradient method²⁴ is chosen for all preliminary energy minimization steps in MD calculations where the simulation boxes contain more than 1700 atoms.

After intensive testing the following optimization procedure turned out to be appropriate in conjunction with the charge equilibrium method; In the first step partial charges are appended to all atoms in the simulation box, then a first energy minimization cycle is performed until convergence is achieved. ## IV. EFFECTIVE CHANNEL DIAMETER IN $LiTi_{2}(PO_{4})_{3}$

For an estimation of the effective channel size in $LiTi_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$, so called Connolly surfaces were calculated using the corresponding CERIUS $^{2}$ module. Connolly surfaces are a first helpful tool for discovering empty channels or holes in an extended solid state structure. The method is based on van der Waals interactions only, where in principle a test probe sphere of a certain size is chosen and all empty volume occupiable by this test probe is calculated and represented by Connolly surfaces. In Fig. 2 the Connolly surfaces for the $[Ti_{2}P_{3}O_{12}]^{-}$ framework of LTP are shown where a test probe of $0.61$ Å is used. For comparison, the ionic radii of $Li^{+}$ with coordination number IV is $0.59$ Å.

Clearly a connected three-dimensional channel structure can be recognized and large holes are observed at the crystallographic Li(1) sites. Therefore it is not surprising that only these Li(1) sites are occupied in $LiTi_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$. All Li(1) sites are connected by more narrow tubes which have a bulge at the midpoint of the tubes. At these sites the Li(2) are located which will be occupied if the lithium content increases in $Li_{1+x}Ti_{2-x}M_{x}P_{3}O_{12}$ $(0<x<2)$. At the most narrow places of the channels represented in Fig. 2 the Li(3) sites are located. If the test probe size is larger than $0.61$ Å, the channels will be cut at these narrow sites. Therefore, without any distortion of the LTP lattice framework only $Li^{+}$ ions can migrate through the channels. For example, the tubes in LTP are too narrow for an easy migration of an $Na^{+}$ ion whose radius is $0.99$ Å for CN IV. $^{20}$ For comparison, the Connolly surfaces of the similar NASICON-type compound $LiZr_{2}P_{3}O_{12},^{25-32}$ were also calculated. As a result, the channels in $LiZr_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ are of similar shape, but distinctly larger than those of LTP. The largest test probe which can pass the tubes at the Li(3) sites without any distortion of the framework has a radius of $0.82$ Å. At first sight, the $Li^{+}$ ions which just fit into the LTP framework seem to migrate more easily in the $LiZr_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ framework. However, if the channels are larger than the migrating species, trapping of the $Li^{+}$ ions at certain sites because of $X-O^{-}Li^{+}$ chemical interactions will become more likely. Such trapping could be one explanation of the distinctly higher activation energy of $LiZr_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ in comparison to LTP.

The accessible volume represents the system of connected tubes where the $Li^{+}$ ions can migrate without perturbation via the bottleneck Li(1)–Li(3)–Li(2)–Li(3)–Li(1). In contrast to the accessible volume, the occupiable volume also includes holes that are isolated from the remaining channel system.

$Na^{+}$ ions are already too large for insertion in LTP or $LiZr_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ without expansion of the lattice. Moreover the negligible difference between the occupiable and the accessible volume in LTP is a first indication that the framework of LTP is very well fitted for the insertion of mobile $Li^{+}$ ions. The nature of lithium diffusion in LTP is examined in more detail in Sec. V.

## V. ACTIVATION ENERGY OF LITHIUM MIGRATION IN $LiTi_{2}(PO_{4})_{3}$

The results of Sec. IV confirmed that the conduction pathway in LTP is similar to the original NASICON compound $Na_{1+x}Zr_{2-x}Si_{x}P_{3}O_{12}$. Let us next look at the concrete pathway and the barrier height of lithium migration that can be determined with MM energy minimization. The general procedure is described in Sec. III of this work. Further one unit cell using periodic boundary conditions proved to be best suitable as simulation box. The unit cell contains 6 f.u. of $LiTi_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ or a total number of 108 atoms. Test investigations using MM energy minimization techniques indicated that the crystallographic Li(1) sites are global minima on the lithium migration pathway, whereas the Li(2) sites represent the energetic transition state of lithium migration. No local energy minima are detected with MM simulations. Therefore the transition state configuration $Li^{(2)}Ti_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ of Fig. 3(b) had to be determined in an indirect way.

In order to locate the saddle point on the potential energy surface, one $Li^{+}$ is shifted from the Li(1) ground state to a neighboring interstitial site Li(2) derived from crystallographic data of similar NASICON compounds. $^{12-16}$ The exact position of the Li(2) transition state is found from this starting site by trial and error. After fixing this $Li^{+}$ ion as well as all Ti atoms of the unit cell, the remaining lattice is allowed to relax leading to the transition state configuration $Li^{(2)}Ti_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$. Without constraining the Ti atoms, the whole lattice around the fixed Li(2) ion is shifted during an energy minimization in such a way that in principle the ground state configuration $Li^{(1)}Ti_{2}P_{3}O_{12}$ is obtained. Even so, in our experience this limitation of a full relaxation of the surrounding lattice will only insignificantly falsify the energy of the transition state. Moreover from a physical point of view, it is not expected that the titanium atoms that are the heaviest atoms of the framework will distinctly change their positions during lithium migration. After removal of the Li and Ti constraints and subsequent energy minimization, the $Li^{+}$ ion "migrates" from the optimized Li(2) position to the Li(1) position via an interstitial Li(3) site.

The potential energy difference between $Li^{+}$ in an Li(1) site [Fig. 3(a)] and $Li^{+}$ in an Li(2) site [Fig. 3(b)] is calculated as 28.95 kJ/mol or 0.30 eV. Interestingly, this result is equivalent to the experimental value of the barrier height of lithium diffusion measured by means of a complex impedance method. $^{9,34}$ Aono et al. determined the activation energy of electrical conduction for the bulk of LTP as 0.30 eV. From an inspection of Table III and Fig. 4 it can be derived that the barrier height of lithium migration is mainly influenced by the change of the Coulomb energy. This result is in contrast to previous investigations on the layer compound $\text{Li}_{x}\text{CoO}_{2}$, $^{19}$ where repulsive van der Waals interactions are most important for the height of the activation energy of lithium diffusion. In the LTP framework the dominating Coulomb energy is continually decreasing without any local minimum during migration of the Li ion from the transition state Li(2) to the ground state Li(1). However, the total potential energy is slightly influenced by the change of van der Waals interactions. During $\text{Li}^{+}$ migration a maximum of the van der Waals energy is passed [see Fig. 4(c)] where the $\text{Li}^{+}$ ion is just located at the most narrow sites of the channels of Fig. 2. From a crystallographic point of view, these Li(3) positions are equivalent to the Na(3) sites detected by x-ray crystallography in sodium rich NASICON compounds. $^{12-14}$ The maximum in the van der Waals energy leads to a point of inflection in the total energy $E_{\text{tot}}$ at the corresponding minimization step. A slight variation in the bond energy term $E_{\text{bonds}}$ and the bond angle energy term $E_{\text{angles}}$ is observed during energy minimization in Fig. 5, but this change nearly does not affect the total energy $E_{\text{tot}}$ and the activation energy of lithium migration, respectively.

This feature is in agreement with the determination of the effective channel diameter mentioned above. The channels are already wide enough for an easy $\text{Li}^{+}$ diffusion and only an insignificant distortion of the framework is needed during $\text{Li}^{+}$ migration. Hence the strain on the framework caused by the $\text{Li}^{+}$ ion at an interstitial site is energetically negligible in comparison to the change of the electrostatic interactions (compare Figs. 4 and 5). Of course, the activation energy of ion migration and the location of bottlenecks are also very sensitive to the alkali ion content, the size of the migrating ion, and the channel size of the framework. Therefore the results and statements gotten for LTP are only transferable to other NASICON compounds in a qualitative way. The general diffusion path of NASICON is confirmed, $^{12-14}$ but the course of the potential energy might be very different.

Let us next consider the importance of $\text{Li}^{+}-\text{Li}^{+}$ interactions within the channels referring to the barrier height of lithium migration. In a simple hypothetical model all $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions except one were removed from the unit cell and MM energy minimization was performed by analogy with the procedure described above. Whereas the general trends of the potential energy and its corresponding valence and nonbonding terms are more or less the same, the barrier height of lithium diffusion decreases to 18.62 kJ/mol or 0.19 eV. A detailed inspection shows that neighboring $\text{Li}^{+}(1)-\text{Li}^{+}(2)$ repulsion forces contribute significantly to the increase in the activation energy of lithium ion diffusion in LTP.

Another important question is how doping influences lithium diffusion. The doped LTP compound $\text{Li}_{1.3}\text{Ti}_{1.7}\text{Al}_{0.3}\text{P}_{3}\text{O}_{12}$ is especially noted to have exceptional high conductivities comparable to those of $\text{Li}_{3}\text{N}.^{7-9,33,34}$ Unfortunately, in LTP compounds the effects caused by Ti/Al disordering or occupation of interstitial Li sites cannot be simulated in a realistic way as long as small simulation boxes like one unit cell are used. If the lithium content in LTP or doped LTP is higher than 1 f.u., image effects caused by $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions leaving the box on one side and simultaneously entering the box as their image on the other side, will influence the validity of these models distinctly. ## VI. LITHIUM DIFFUSION IN Li₁.₃Ti₁.₇Al₀.₃(PO₄)₃

Let us first look at the computational details of the MD simulations presented in this work. The MD simulation box of the doped LTP compound $Li_{1.3}Ti_{1.7}Al_{0.3}P_{3}O_{12}$ shown in Fig. 6 contains 16 unit cells $(4×4×1)$ leading to a total number of 1757 atoms. First, all Li(1) sites are occupied similar to undoped LTP. A random generator simulates the disordering of the surplus Li atoms over all interstitial Li(2) sites as well as the substitution of 15% of all Ti atoms for Al atoms. With a stoichiometry of $Li_{1.3}Ti_{1.7}Al_{0.3}P_{3}O_{12}$, 15% of all available Li(2) sites are also occupied. In the same way as for pure LTP, partial charges are assigned with the charge equilibration method. This step is computationally most expensive and therefore the limiting factor for the size of the simulation box. Before the actual MD simulation, an energy minimization of the simulation box using the conjugate gradient method is performed. Such an energy minimization is useful in order to relax very high energetic strain that may lead to an unreal behavior in MD simulations.

After a set of initial velocities is added to a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the lattice is equilibrated in a microcanonical ensemble for 5 ps (5000 time steps of 0.001 ps each) to the target temperature of 300 K by applying velocity scaling. Then the system is further allowed to relax at 300 K for 230 ps (230 000 steps) without velocity scaling. The main reason for the choice of a microcanonical (constant NVE) ensemble is the fact that the size of the simulation box is fixed during the simulation that allows a better comparison with the MM calculations described above. MD simulations using an NPT ensemble show more or less the same results as the simulation in an NVE ensemble, but by analogy with an energy minimization without any cell constraints, the deviation of experimental and calculated unit cell parameters are in the region of 3%–6%. Figure 6 represents a ‘‘snapshot’’ of the simulation box after the complete MD run (235 ps).

A detailed inspection of the complete trajectory file shows that most of the $Li^{+}$ ions only vibrate about their equilibrium position. Only a small fraction of all $Li^{+}$ ions really migrated to a neighboring position or further. Two of the detected examples are marked with an arrow in Fig. 6. These observations in our MD simulation reproduce the expected ion diffusion mechanism in solid state compounds very well.⁵,³⁶ From a computational point of view, it is surprising that the hopping mechanism can be directly observed in MD simulations of solids using the UFF. In a previous work on the layer compound $LiCoO_{2}$, a comparable size of the MD simulation box and observation time were too small to observe such a lithium hopping at 300 K.¹⁹ This discrepancy is presumably due to the fact that image effects caused by lithium ions at the border of comparably large MD boxes are less disturbing in such a flexible 3D framework like LTP than in a more rigid 2D layer compound.

The supposed migrating process for $Li^{+}$ ions, in which most of them vibrate at their equilibrium positions and few of them hop to another site successively, can be observed in the mean square displacement (MSD), as shown in Fig. 7. In our model the MSD (Å²/atom) of a trapped $Li^{+}$ ion is about 10–15 times higher than the average MSD of a framework $O^{2-}$ ion indicating a high vibration or in other words high Debye–Waller type thermal motion of the $Li^{+}$ ions.

In summary, a long MD simulation reproduces a realistic picture of the lithium diffusion even in doped and disordered LPT compounds. As mentioned above, a direct observation of hopping events is not self-evident for MD simulations in ionic conducting solids. Therefore, these first results appear to be very promising for a future determination of average activation energies of lithium migration even in $Li_{1.3}Ti_{1.7}Al_{0.3}P_{3}O_{12}$. As soon as actual diffusion constants can be measured for a statistical reliable number of hopping (and not only vibrating) $Li^{+}$ ions, the activation energy of lithium migration could be determined from several long MD simulations performed at various temperatures.³⁷ One solution might be a distinct enlargement of the simulation

box which is beyond the possibilities of our present computer size and memory.

Even so, a few qualitative indications are already possible with a very simplified model. In various conceivable configurations two Ti atoms are substituted for two Al atoms in the simulation box of Fig. 3, but all $Li^{+}$ ions remain at their Li(1) sites. Moreover, the cell parameters are adapted to the slightly changed experimental unit cell parameters of doped LTP in comparison to pure LTP. Potential energy calculations similar to the procedure described above showed no change in the activation energy of lithium migration with the exception of two cases. If octahedron 1 of Fig. 3 is occupied with aluminum at its center, the activation energy will decrease slightly about to 0.02 eV, with alumina atoms in octahedra 1 and 2, the activation energy will decrease to about 0.03 eV in comparison to pure LTP. Because of the less shielded negative charge of the neighboring oxygen ions when replacing $Ti^{4+}$ for $Al^{3+}$, the potential energy of $Li^{+}$ ions at the transition state Li(2) is slightly lowered due to stronger $Li^{+}-O^{2-}$ electrostatic attraction forces. All in all, a substitution of Ti for Al within the framework does not cause a drastic change in the activation energy of lithium migration. However, general predictions of how doping influences the activation energy of lithium migration in LTP are very difficult using only one simulation model. Since the framework is able to adapt the local environment of the migrating $Li^{+}$ ion by lowering unfavorable repulsive electrostatic interactions, the long range Coulomb part mainly contributing to the barrier height of lithium migration must be recalculated for each changing lithium content and slightly changing geometry.

## VII. CONCLUSIONS
The extraordinary high conductivities of LTP and doped LTP compounds is controversially discussed in the literature. $^{38}$ The results of electrical conductivity measurements using a complex impedance method point out that grain boundary resistance drastically influences the actual conductivity in LTP and doped LTP compounds. $^{6-9,34}$ In LTP the activation energy of electrical conduction is measured as 0.48 eV for the grain boundary, but only 0.30 eV for the bulk. The latter result is in exact agreement with the activation energy of $Li^{+}$ migration determined by MM simulations presented in this work. Our simulations on a simple model, which has been optimized in order to represent a realistic reproduction of the bulk of pure LTP, support the theory that stoichiometric LTP actually has a high $Li^{+}$ ion conductivity, but the mobility of the $Li^{+}$ ions is disturbed by grain boundaries.

Other author's postulate that lithium oxides lost during sintering at high temperatures could be responsible for a distinct conductivity reduction in pure LTP. $^{38-40}$ Our MM simulations for a single $Li^{+}$ ion in one unit cell of LTP show the following trend: if the LTP framework is preserved after lithium loss without any defects, the activation energy will decrease to about 0.1 eV. Of course, this is not a clear refutation of the theory, since structural changes in the LTP framework cannot be excluded after lithium oxides are lost.

Besides, our results on doped LTP indicate that the substitution of Ti for Al within the framework will only slightly influence the activation energy of lithium migration if the changing lithium content is neglected.
