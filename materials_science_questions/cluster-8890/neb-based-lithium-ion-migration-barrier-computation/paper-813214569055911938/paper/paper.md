# Lithium Chalcogenidotetrelates: LiChT—Synthesis and Characterization of New $Li^{+}$ Ion Conducting Li/Sn/Se Compounds

ABSTRACT: Five new lithium chalcogenidotetrelates, so-called "LiChT" phases, with the elemental combination $Li/Sn/Se$, $Li_4[SnSe_4]$ (1), $_{\infty}\{Li_2[SnSe_3]\}$ (2), and the respective solvates $Li_4[SnSe_4]\cdot13H_2O$ (3), $Li_4[Sn_2Se_6]\cdot14H_2O$ (4), and $Li_4[SnSe_4]\cdot16MeOH$ (5) were generated in single-crystalline form. We present and discuss syntheses, crystal structures, spectroscopic and thermal behavior, as well as $Li^{+}$ ion conducting properties of the phases that represent uncommon $Li^{+}$ ion conducting materials with a maximum conductivity found for $1$ ($\sigma_{20^{\circ}C}=2\times10^{-5}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$, $\sigma_{100^{\circ}C}=9\times10^{-4}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$). The latter was elucidated via impedance spectroscopy and further studied by electronic structure calculations, revealing vacancy migration as the dominant $Li^{+}$ transport mechanism. Thus, studies on a selenido-LISICON family were found to be a very interesting starting point for an extension of the LISICON-related solid state lithium ion conductors (SSLIC).

KEYWORDS: inorganic $Li^{+}$ conductors, binary anions, crystal structures, ion conductivity, $Li^{+}$ migration simulations

## INTRODUCTION

Lithium ion conducting materials, especially solid state lithium ion conductors, are under strong investigation with regard to their possible main application in rechargeable lithium ion batteries. Examples of solid state lithium ion conductors (SSLIC) are the LISICON system of the general chemical composition $Li_{2+2x}Zn_{1-x}GeO_4$, with $\sigma=1.3\times10^{-1}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $300\ ^{\circ}C$ but only $\sigma=1\times10^{-7}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at room temperature $^{1,2}$ or the so-called thio-LISICON family. $^{3}$ The latter is the sulfur relative but shows better conductivities upon substitution of phosphorus instead of zinc for the tetrel atom, thus leading to the general formula $Li_{4-x}Ge_{1-x}P_xS_4.^{4}$ The highest conductivity was reported for $Li_{10}GeP_2S_{12}$ ($\sigma=1.2\times10^{-2}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $27\ ^{\circ}C$). $^{5}$

Even though the effect of silicon as the tetrel has been investigated almost simultaneously with the germanium compounds, the exchange of the germanium atom with tin has been investigated only recently. $^{6,7}$ In the case of the silicon homologues, $Li_{13}SiP_3S_{16}$ exhibited the best conductivity ($\sigma=6.44\times10^{-4}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $27\ ^{\circ}C$), $^{6}$ whereas for the tin compounds, only the unsubstituted, ternary phase $Li_4SnS_4$ has been explored to date, however, with very promising conductivities of $\sigma=3\times10^{-3}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $100\ ^{\circ}C$ and $\sigma=7\times10^{-5}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $20\ ^{\circ}C.^{7}$

Beside experiments that direct toward quaternary thiostan-nate-LISICON systems upon addition of phosphorus or further replacements, we are currently testing the continuation of a homologue replacement in the chalcogen group, thus generating selenium or tellurium analogs.

Among the diverse chalcogenide tetrelates that have been widely investigated in the last decades, only few lithium salts have been reported beside the phases mentioned, such as $Li_4Sn_2E_6\cdot xen$ ($E=S$, Se, Te; $x=4$, 8; en = ethane-1,2-diamine). $^{8}$ In these salts, $Li^{+}$ cations are usually coordinated by solvent molecules such as ethylendiamine (en), which hamper effective ion transport.

Herein, we report on solvent free lithium selenidostannates containing monomeric or polymeric anions, Li₄[SnSe₄] (1) and ₁^∞{Li₂[SnSe₃]} (2), as well as water or methanol solvates Li₄[SnSe₄]·13H₂O (3), Li₄[Sn₂Se₆]·14H₂O (4), and Li₄[SnSe₄]·16MeOH (5), which result from the purification of the crude solvent-free products by extraction in protic solvents and (partial) evaporation of the liquid. We discuss syntheses, crystal structures, and Li⁺ ion conductivities both from experimental measurements and density functional theory (DFT) simulations.

■ EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Syntheses. General. All reaction steps were performed with strong exclusion of air and external moisture (Ar atmosphere at a high-vacuum, double-manifold Schlenk line, or N₂ atmosphere in a glovebox). Methanol was dried and freshly distilled prior to use; water was degassed by applying dynamic vacuum (10⁻³ Torr) for several hours.

Synthesis of Li₄[SnSe₄] (1) and ₁^∞{Li₂[SnSe₃]}(2): A stochiometric mixture of 0.799 g/0.503 g (8.6 mmol/5.4 mmol) of Li₂Se, 0.510 g/0.643 g (4.3 mmol/5.4 mmol) of Sn, and 0.679 g/0.855 g (8.6 mmol/10.8 mmol) of Se was fused by using a gas burner in a silica-ampule and then heated for 30 min. For single-crystalline products, the reaction was performed in a furnace with a definite temperature program: (1) heating to a temperature of 650 °C with a heating rate of 70 °C/h, (2) keeping for 12 h at 650 °C, (3) cooling to room temperature with a rate of 4 °C/h. Total yields (single-crystalline + polycrystalline) besides unreacted starting material and polyselenide byproducts: 1, 1.292 g (65%); 2, 1.437 g (72%).

Synthesis of Li₄[SnSe₄]·13H₂O (3), Li₄[Sn₂Se₆]·14H₂O (4), and Li₄[SnSe₄]·16MeOH (5): Compounds 1 or 2 obtained from the described reactions were finely pulverized. Two grams of the powder was solved in 20 mL of H₂O or 30 mL of methanol, respectively, and stirred for several hours. The solution was filtered to remove starting materials and insoluble residues, and a clear yellow solution was obtained. Crystals of Li₄[SnSe₄]·13H₂O (3), Li₄[Sn₂Se₆]·14H₂O (4), or Li₄[SnSe₄]·16MeOH (5) were obtained in yields of 3, 2.877 g (73%); 4, 1.877 g (70%); and 5, 1.967 g (47%) by very slow evaporation of the solvent under dynamic vacuum for several hours.

X-ray Diffraction. Single crystal data were collected on diffractometers using the Stoe imaging plate detector systems IPDS I (5) or IPDS-IIT (1−4), using Mo Kα radiation with graphite monochromatization (λ = 0.71073 Å) at T = 100 K. Structure solution and refinement was performed using Shelxs-97 and Shelxl-97 software.⁹ Table 1 summarizes crystallographic, structure solution, and refinement details. Powder diffraction data were collected on a Philips X'Pert powder diffractometer MPD Pro, using graphite-monochromized Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.7903 Å). Rietveld profile fits of the experimental data of 3¹⁷ to the single-crystal X-ray diffraction data were performed to confirm the purity of the bulk material by using X'Pert Plus and X'Pert HighScore Plus software.¹⁰⁻¹³

Spectroscopy and Thermal Analyses. NMR spectra were measured from solutions of the compounds in H2O/D2O on a Bruker DRX 400 MHz or a Bruker DRX 500 MHz. The given shifts in ppm correspond to SeMe2 (⁷⁷Se) or SnMe₄ (¹¹⁹Sn). IR (infrared) spectra were recorded on a Bruker IFS 88 as suspensions of the compounds in nujol oil. Raman spectra were measured on a Labram HR 800 from Jobin Yvon with He/Ne laser excitation at 632.817 nm. TGA (thermogravimetric analysis) and DSC (differential scanning calorimetry) measurements were performed between 60 and 700 °C on an STA 409 CD from Netzsch.

Ion Mobility Simulations. For the characterization of ion- and vacancy transport in compounds 1 and 2, we employ density

functional theory (DFT).¹⁵ We expand the wave functions on a real- space grid as implemented in the GPAW code.¹⁶,¹⁷ Additionally, we check selected energy barriers using plane-waves as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package.¹⁸ In both cases, we use the projector-augmented-wave method¹⁹,²⁰ in the local density approx- imation.²¹ Barriers obtained using both methods agree within 0.05 eV.

All calculations are spin-paired, but we also checked some configurations against spin-polarized calculations to ensure the absence of spin-related effects. We used supercells with 1 × 2 × 2 unit cells corresponding to 16 formula units per supercell. A Monkhorst-Pack k- point sampling²² of 2³, resulting in 4 irreducible k-points, was used in all cases. Transition barriers were calculated with the nudged elastic band method,²³,²⁴ for which a spring constant of 5 eV/Å² was used. Also here, we verified the results by checking selected barriers using different spring constants of 0.1 eV/Å² and 10 eV/Å². The defect-free structures obtained by the crystal structure analysis were first relaxed, after which the defect states with single Li vacancies or single interstitial Li ions used in the ion mobility simulations were relaxed keeping the cell shape and volume constant. Eight or nine NEB images in addition to the end-points were used for each transition path. Frenkel defects were generated by moving the corresponding Li⁺ ions from their regular lattice sites to adjacent interstitial sites and by relaxing the resulting structures keeping the cells fixed. All systems were calculated in their charge neutral state. For a comparison with charged supercells and calculations with the PBE functional, see the Supporting Information.

Impedance Spectroscopy. The Li⁺ ion conductivity was measured on polycrystalline phases 3ᵈʳʸ and 4ᵈʳʸ that were generated to examine pure phases upon the dehydration of 3 and 4, respectively (see the Experimental Section). Whereas 4ᵈʳʸ is not identical with the solvent-free phase 2, 3ᵈʳʸ indeed produces the same powder XRD pattern as 1 (see the Supporting Information). The samples were pressed into pellets with a diameter of 3 mm by applying a pressure of approximately 1 GPa. Platinum was coated on both faces of the pellet using a BAL-TEC SCD005 sputter coater. For AC impedance measurements, the pellets were mounted into an airtight two-electrode sample cell. AC impedance data were collected using a Novocontrol Alpha-AK impedance analyzer over a frequency range of 1 MHz to 0.01 Hz, with an applied root-mean-square AC voltage of 100 mV. The measurements were carried out at temperatures between −100 and 100 °C, the sample temperature being controlled by the Novocontrol Quatro Cryosystem. The maximum temperature variation tolerated during the impedance measurements was ±0.1 K.

### RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Syntheses. As illustrated in Scheme 1, the lithium chalcogenidotetrelates Li₄SnSe₄ (1) and ¹∞{Li₂[SnSe₃]} (2) were prepared by high temperature reactions of lithiumselenide, tin, and selenium in appropriate stoichiometries. To purify the phases, which are always obtained with some solid byproducts, an extraction/evaporation procedure was added. This way, Li₄[SnSe₄]·13H₂O (3), Li₄[Sn₂Se₆]·14H₂O (4), and Li₄[SnSe₄]·16MeOH (5) were prepared by extraction of 1 or 2 using water or methanol, with ensuing purification by filtration and evaporation of the solvent. Additionally, the crystal water of 3 and 4 was removed by heating up to 320 °C under application of dynamic vacuum during 3 h, producing compounds 3ᵈʳʸ and 4ᵈʳʸ. Whereas 3ᵈʳʸ is identical with 1, as confirmed by means of powder XRD (see the Supporting Information), this is not the case for 4ᵈʳʸ. The latter differs from the parent compound 2 due to the break-up of the one- dimensional strands upon solvation to give compound 4. Nevertheless, both Li/Sn/Se compositions are identical to 3 and 4 as proven by EDX measurements. The presence of [SnSe₄]⁴⁻ (3ᵈʳʸ) or [Sn₂Se₆]⁴⁻ anions (4ᵈʳʸ) was confirmed by NMR spectroscopy (see the Supporting Information).

The solvent-free phases are highly hygroscopic, especially the “dried” ones, and all compounds are sensitive to humid air, decomposing into tin oxide and elemental selenium within several hours. However, in dry air, the solvent-free phases do not show decomposition over several days; thus, we consider the Sn−O bond formation being the initial step.

Crystal Structures. Compound 1 (Figure 1a,c,d) crystal- lizes in the orthorhombic space group Pnma (No. 62) with four formula units per unit cell. The structure consists of isolated, tetrahedral [SnSe₄]⁴⁻ anions with the selenium atoms coordinated by four or five lithium ions. The Li⁺ ions, in turn, are coordinated octahedrally (Li1) or tetrahedrally (Li2, Li3) by selenium atoms. Interatomic distances and angles (Table 2) accord with those observed in other salts of the [SnSe₄]⁴⁻ anion, such as K₄SnSe₄.²⁵ Similar to the related LISICON and thio-LISICON parent systems Li₄GeO₄²⁶ and Li₄GeS₄,²⁷ compound 1 exhibits a substructure “Li₃SnSe₄”, which forms a diamond-related network, whereas a quarter of the Li⁺ ions occupy one of the four octahedral sites per formula unit within this network.

As reported recently, the “Li₃TS₄” fragment of the crystal structures of Li₄GeS₄ and its Sn homologue, Li₄SnS₄,⁷ adopt the γ-Li₃PO₄ topology, thus containing four-ring arrangements unlike the diamond topology. The fourth Li⁺ ion occupies one octahedral site in the case of Li₄GeS₄, and is statistically distributed over two octahedral sites in the case of Li₄SnS₄. However, the network observed in 1 has been unknown so far; thus, its relationship with/deviations from the diamond topology are different from those observed for the quoted lighter homologues. While the γ-Li₃PO₄ topology represents a 3-nodal 4,4,4-c net with the stoichiometry (4-c)2(4-c)(4-c) and the point symbol {4.6⁵}{2{4².6⁴}{6⁵.8}, the network topology of 1 consists of a 2-nodal 4,4-c net with the stoichiometry (4-c)(4- c) and the point symbol {4.6⁵}{6⁶}. Along with this structural difference, the Li⁺ atomic positions in 1 do obviously show no disorder; it is expected that an extension of the Li₄SnS₄ topology into an isostructural network with larger Sn−Se bonds would have allowed for an even more distinct disorder, with the consequence of better ion mobility/conductivity on the one hand but also with lower crystal stability on the other hand. This might be the thermodynamic reason for the adoption of an alternative network topology, and the missing disorder correlates with the observation of a somewhat lower Li⁺ conductivity of 1 as compared with Li₄SnS₄.

**Calculation of the Ion Mobility of 1 and 2.** Ab initio calculations within the DFT framework were carried out in order to determine the diffusion mechanisms for $Li^{+}$ ions in compounds 1 and 2. Both ion and vacancy diffusion were examined. Apart from the calculation of minimum energy paths and the corresponding energy barriers using the nudged elastic band method, $^{23,24}$ the formation energies for the generation of Frenkel defects, that is, the movement of a $Li^{+}$ ion from its original position to one of the interstitial octahedral sites within the diamond lattices, were examined. The contributions due to thermal activation of Frenkel defects were taken into account explicitly for the calculation of the overall diffusion activation energies.

Compound 1: $Li^{+}$ ions at tetrahedrally and octahedrally coordinated sites in the defect-free structure can form Frenkel defects by moving to one of the initially unoccupied interstitial octahedral sites. Interestingly, the formation energies of Frenkel defects differ by less than 0.1 eV for tetrahedrally and octahedrally coordinated $Li^{+}$ ions, being slightly higher for tetrahedral ones. The reason for the similar Frenkel defect energies are the almost degenerate binding energies of the $Li^{+}$ ions in the two initially adopted coordination environments. More precisely, removing a tetrahedrally coordinated $Li^{+}$ ion from the structure (to vacuum), requires an energy that is by 0.13 eV lower than for the removal of an octahedrally coordinated $Li^{+}$ ion. However, the formation energy of a Frenkel defect strongly depends on the initially unoccupied octahedral interstitial site that hosts the $Li^{+}$ ion after defect

Second, we focused on the diffusion of $Li^+$ ions, which can only take place via neighboring octahedral interstitials. Furthermore, for thermodynamic reasons, diffusion paths of $Li^+$ ions must involve type 2 interstitials due to their significantly higher $Li^+$ binding energy compared to the type 3 and 4 interstitials. However, type 3 and 4 interstitials may serve as intermediate states for hopping between type 2 interstitials. For this scenario, the lowest energy barrier is 0.75 eV with a type 3 interstitial serving as intermediate state. Direct hopping between adjacent type 2 interstitials is also possible. However, the corresponding energy barrier of 0.89 eV is even higher than for the previous situation. Note that type 1 interstitials are almost completely occupied except for a small number of thermally activated vacancies such that they do not play a role for ion diffusion but potentially for vacancy diffusion, which is examined in the following.

For compound 1 there are three different scenarios for elementary vacancy diffusion steps:
1.  Vacancy diffusion between adjacent octahedral sites of type 1.
2.  Vacancy diffusion between adjacent tetrahedrally coordinated $Li^+$ ion sites.
3.  Vacancy diffusion between adjacent octahedral sites of type 1 and tetrahedrally coordinated $Li^+$ ion sites.

In case 1, we find energy barriers of 0.58 and 1.01 eV depending on whether the diffusion path is along the crystallographic $a$ or $c$ direction, respectively. In case 2, energy barriers as low as 0.17 eV and, in case 3, as low as 0.21 eV are found. The continuous diffusion paths with the smallest overall energy barrier height (0.30 eV) involve both tetrahedrally coordinated and octahedral type 1 vacancies. These diffusion paths are strictly one-dimensional (see Figure 4a). However, hopping of vacancies between adjacent diffusion channels is possible via tetrahedrally coordinated sites. For the latter process, a barrier height of 0.33 eV has to be overcome such that in total three-dimensional diffusion of vacancies with a low energy barrier is possible in compound 1 (Figure 4b).

All the presented diffusion mechanisms require that vacancies or interstitials are present in the structure. At sufficiently low temperatures, the defect concentration does not depend on temperature but on the number of extrinsic vacancies per volume.²⁸ In this situation, the activation energy is determined solely by the lowest energy barrier $E_B$ of the possible diffusion path. At sufficiently high temperatures, thermally activated, intrinsic Frenkel defects prevail. The associated defect concentration scales as $\exp(-E_F/2kT)$, where $E_F$ is the energy needed to create the Frenkel defect.²⁸ For an existing defect, the rate of migration for an energy barrier $E_B$ scales as $\exp(-E_B/kT)$. In sum, the activation energy determining the Arrhenius behavior of ion conductivity is given by $E_A = E_B + {^1/_2}E_F$.

Including the minimum Frenkel defect formation energy of 0.53 eV yields a total activation energy of 0.60 eV for the three-dimensional vacancy diffusion. For ion diffusion, the migration barrier of 0.75 eV and the Frenkel defect formation energy result in a total activation energy of 1.02 eV. Thus, we conclude that due to the considerably lower activation energy, transport of $Li^+$ ions in compound 1 is dominated by vacancy diffusion.

Conductivity Measurements. Impedance spectroscopic
measurements were performed on the pure phases $3^{\text {dry }}(=\mathbf{1})$
and $4^{\text {dry }}$ to gain insight into the ion mobility and conduction
properties of the novel $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$containing phases. Measurements on
the as-prepared parent compounds 1 and 2 were not possible
due to the coexistence of solid byproducts that needed to be
removed by the described extraction/evaporation process (see
the Experimental Section) prior to impedance spectroscopic
investigations. We thus compared the two products of the
drying process applied to 3 , containing $\left[\mathrm{SnSe}_{4}\right]^{4-}$ anions, and 4 ,
containing $\left[\mathrm{Sn}_{2} \mathrm{Se}_{6}\right]^{4-}$ anions, namely $3^{\text {dry }}(=\mathbf{1})$ and $4^{\text {dry }}$, to
experimentally explore the influence of different anionic
structures on the $\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ion conductivity.

The conductivity of $Li_4SnSe_4$ amounts to $2 \times 10^{-5}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $20\ ^{\circ}C$ and to $9 \times 10^{-4}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$ at $100\ ^{\circ}C$. At lower temperatures, compound $3^{dry}$ performs gradually worse than the sulfide analog due to its higher activation energy ($E_A = 0.45$ eV versus $E_A = 0.41$ eV). This observation is put down on the structural peculiarities outlined, hence the expected necessity to form Frenkel defect sites in the Li/Sn/Se system.

Although the calculated $E_A$ of 0.60 eV is a slight overestimate, the experimental and theoretical values are in good agreement within the error of the method used. We note that using a restricted supercell size and not charging the defects due to computational limitations, the ion mobility simulations tend to overestimate the vacancy diffusion barriers (see the Supporting Information). Thus, our results serve to model the $Li^+$ ion migration observed in the LiChT phase $3^{dry}(=1)$, suggesting diffusion of intrinsic vacancies as the main transport mechanism.

Experimentally, we found almost the same activation energy in compound $4^{dry}$ ($E_A = 0.46$ eV) as in $3^{dry}$. However, the conductivity values of $4^{dry}$ are significantly lower (see Figure 6), as can be seen from the lower value of the pre-exponential factor $A$ ($4^{dry}$: $\log A = 3.8$; $3^{dry}$: $\log A = 5.6$). At $20\ ^{\circ}C$ the conductivity of $4^{dry}$ amounts to $2 \times 10^{-7}\ S\cdot cm^{-1}$, which is by about 2 orders of magnitude lower than in compound $3^{dry}$. Note that, in the case of $4^{dry}$, a direct comparison to the DFT calculations is hampered by the fact the purification of compound 2 came along with a structural change of the Sn/ Se anion. Vice versa, calculation of a solvent-free compound with dimeric anions is not possible, since it was not possible to collect single crystal data of $4^{dry}$.

In summary, because of its remarkably high conductivity, $3^{dry}$ was found to be a very interesting starting point for extending the LISICON-related systems by studies on a selenido- LISICON family. Based on the output of the DFT calculations, which indicate vacancy diffusion as the basic transport mechanism, the systematic development of new selenide- LISICON phases may include aliovalent substitution by group15 elements. Of course, one has to keep in mind that small variations in the structure, which are not easily predictable, may play a crucial role for the properties of a phase and can easily overrule general trends; however, using selenium as the chalcogen proves to be a promising approach in extending the field of SSLIC.

## CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK
We synthesized five new lithium chalgogenidotetrelate, LiChT, phases and characterized the compounds with respect to their crystal structure, ion mobility, ion conductivity, and thermal behavior. Our studies showed that the investigated selenidos- tannate compounds are potentially interesting new ion conductors and thus promising candidates for the extension of the field of SSLIC research. DFT calculations served to elucidate vacancy diffusion as the main transport mechanism, that direct to further modifications of the involved elements, also extending the phases to quaternary ones.
