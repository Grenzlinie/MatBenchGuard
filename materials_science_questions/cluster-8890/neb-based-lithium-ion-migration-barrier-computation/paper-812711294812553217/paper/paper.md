# Hierarchical Composite of Rose-Like VS₂@S/N-Doped Carbon with Expanded (001) Planes for Superior Li-Ion Storage

Wenjuan Yang, Ningjing Luo, Cheng Zheng, Shuping Huang,* and Mingdeng Wei*

In the present work, a hierarchical composite of rose-like VS₂@S/N-doped carbon (VS₂@SNC) with expanded (001) planes is successfully fabricated through a facile synthetic route. Notably, the d-spacing of (001) planes is expanded to 0.92 nm, which is proved to dramatically reduce the energy barrier for Li⁺ diffusion in the composite of VS₂@SNC by density functional theory calculation. On the other hand, the S/N-doped carbon in the composite greatly promotes the electrical conductivity and enhances the structural stability. In addition, the hierarchical structure of VS₂@SNC facilitates rapid electrolyte diffusion and increases the contact area between the electrode and electrolyte simultaneously. Benefiting from the merits mentioned above, the VS₂@SNC electrode exhibits excellent electrochemical properties, such as a large reversible capacity of 971.6 mA h g⁻¹ at 0.2 A g⁻¹, an extremely high rate capability of 772.1 mA h g⁻¹ at 10 A g⁻¹, and a remarkable cycling stability up to 600 cycles at 8 A g⁻¹ with a capacity of 684.5 mA h g⁻¹, making it a promising candidate as an anode material for lithium-ion batteries.

## 1. Introduction

Nowadays, the worldwide energy shortage and serious environmental pollution are forcing humankind to change the status quo, and it is of utmost urgency to search for alternative clean and sustainable energies. Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), as a renewable energy power device, show the most promising prospects in a variety of electronic equipment due to their high energy densities, low self-discharge, and environmental friendliness.[¹] Nevertheless, the commercial graphite as an anode is unable to satisfy the booming development of these devices, considering its relative low capacity (372 mA h g⁻¹), unsatisfied rate capability and limited life span.[²] Therefore, unremitting efforts need to be made for developing new anode materials with high specific capacity, outstanding rate capability, and excellent cycle stability.

The emergence of graphene has attracted tremendous attention in 2D layered transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), such as SnS₂,[³] MoSe₂,[⁴] and MoS₂.[⁵] The unit layer of TMDs resembles a sandwich structure that a transition metal slab is inserted between the two chalcogenide slabs, and the unit layers are linked together by weak van der Waals interaction.[⁶] Such a unique structure enables facile insertion and extraction of Li-ion between the interlayers. Moreover, the theoretical capacity of TMDs is higher than that of graphite due to the contribution of both intercalation and conversion reaction.[⁷] Despite the advantages of TMDs mentioned above, their further development is plagued by intrinsic low electrical conductivity.[⁸] As a member of layered TMDs, VS₂ not only inherits the advantages of TMDs, but also possesses metallic conductivity,[⁹] which endows it with immense amounts of active sites and fast charge transfer simultaneously. Up to now, diverse VS₂-based materials have been synthesized and extensively investigated in various rechargeable systems, such as LIBs,[¹⁰] sodium-ion batteries (SIBs),[¹¹] aqueous Zn-ion batteries,[¹²] and hybrid-ion batteries.[¹³] However, the interlayer spacing of VS₂, as a critical index, dramatically influences the electrochemical properties, as open-enough channels can lower the energy barrier and expose more active sites for the intercalation of guest ions.[¹⁴] More recently, a theoretical study[¹⁵] revealed that VS₂ monolayers could provide fast mobility for guest ions and be able to absorb three layers of Li, which contributes to a large theoretical capacity as high as 1397 mA h g⁻¹. In addition, the volume variation during repetitive Li⁺ intercalation/deintercalation process leads to structural collapse and short cycle life.[¹⁶] In view of this, many countermeasures were proposed and the mainstream method was to construct composite with carbonaceous materials, especially heteroatom-doped (such as N and S) carbonaceous materials to tune the physical and chemical properties of the materials.[¹⁷] It has been reported that the nitrogen doped in carbon can promote the reactivity and electronic conductivity of the electrode by introducing extrinsic defects.[¹⁸] While the introduction of sulfur into the carbon matrix can even provide additional active sites for Li⁺ since sulfur is electrochemical

Herein, a facile one-pot solvothermal route has been adopted for synthesizing a composite of rose-like VS₂@S/N-doped carbon (VS₂@SNC), in which the *d*-spacing of (001) planes was expanded to 0.92 nm. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation was carried out to investigate the possible pathways for Li⁺ diffusion, and verify the improved electrochemical kinetics in interlayer-expanded VS₂@SNC. Meanwhile, the expanded interlayer can also expose adequate active sites for Li⁺ intercalation to improve the material utilization. The void space among the "petals" in the hierarchical rose-like VS₂@SNC can mitigate the volume variation upon cycling and favor electrolyte infiltration. In addition, the heteroatom-doped carbon can enhance the electronic conductivity and provide more active sites for Li⁺ storage. Consequently, the composite exhibits an excellent rate capability and a superior cycling stability.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was further performed to characterize the chemical bonds in m-$VS_2$ and $VS_2@$SNC. As illustrated in Figure 1c, both of them show two bands at 978 and $1629\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, which arise from $v(\text{V=S})$ and $v(\text{C=O})$, respectively. $^{[23]}$ For the composite of $VS_2@$SNC, the band appeared at $3460\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ originates from adsorbed water molecules. However, the spectrum for m-$VS_2$ is much more complicated, in which a series of weak bands in the region of $1200$–$1700\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ are found, and they belong to the representative stretching vibrational modes of C–N heterocycles, $^{[24]}$ further proving the existence of melam in m-$VS_2$. Additionally, the band at $3323\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ is assignable to the functional group of $-\text{NH}$.

The thermal analysis of m-$VS_2$ was carried out by using the thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry analysis (TG-DSC) in nitrogen atmosphere, and the detected temperature ranged from 50 to $600\ ^\circ\text{C}$. As the TG-DSC curves presented in Figure 1d, the weight loss before $150\ ^\circ\text{C}$ corresponds to the volatilization of absorbed water and ammonia molecules, which originate from the air and decomposition of TAA, respectively. Additionally, two exothermal peaks appear at 285 and $420\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and the weight of the sample decreases in two steps. The two exothermic reaction peaks are ascribed to the decomposition of melam accompanying with carbonization reaction. $^{[25]}$ TG analysis was also performed to quantify the content of $VS_2$ in the composite of $VS_2@$SNC. The TG curves for p-$VS_2$ and $VS_2@$SNC are displayed in Figure 1e, in which the weight loss of both from 50 to $150\ ^\circ\text{C}$ are caused by the volatilization of absorbed water molecules, and a weight loss of $22.09\%$ for the former at $150$–$600\ ^\circ\text{C}$ corresponds to the oxidation of $VS_2$ to $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ in the air, whereas the latter exhibits a lager weight loss of $30.19\%$ due to the additional carbon content. As displayed in Figure 2a, spin-orbit coupling contributes to a doublet in the V 2p peak ($2\text{p}_{1/2}$–$2\text{p}_{3/2}$), which located at 524.4 and 517.1 eV, confirming the $\text{V}^{4+}$ oxidation state. $^{[27]}$ There are multiple peaks in the high-resolution S 2p spectrum (Figure 2b), where the two peaks located at 161.5 and 162.6 eV can be attributed to S $2\text{p}_{3/2}$ and S $2\text{p}_{1/2}$ of $\text{S}^{2-}$,$^{[28]}$ while another two peaks at 163.7 and 164.9 eV associate with C–S $2\text{p}_{3/2}$ and $2\text{p}_{1/2}$, respectively, $^{[29]}$ and the peak at higher binding energy (168.9 eV) belongs to sulfate species, indicating the surface of $VS_2@$SNC is oxidized on account of exposing to air. $^{[30]}$ The core-level XPS spectrum of C 1s (Figure 2c) is deconvoluted into four components centered at 284.8, 285.3, 286.5, and 288.6 eV, which can be attributed to C–C $\text{sp}^2$, C–C $\text{sp}^3$/C–S, C–N, and C=O, respectively. $^{[31]}$ The appeared peaks for C–N and C–S attest the successful incorporation of S and N dopants into the carbon structures. As displayed in Figure 2d, the core-level XPS spectrum for N 1s is composed of three components, namely pyridinic N (398.8 eV), pyrrolic N (399.6 eV), and graphitic N (400.4 eV), further proving N moieties are doped in the carbon matrix. $^{[17b]}$

Figure 4a shows the characteristic Raman spectra of 1T-VS₂ for VS₂@SNC and p-VS₂, $^{[33]}$ and the schematic illustration of two Raman-active modes (E₁g and A₁g) is presented in Figure 4b. The E₁g vibrational mode is associated with the in-plane displacements of sulfur atoms, while the A₁g vibrational mode represents the out-of-plane symmetric displacements of sulfur atoms along the $c$-axis. $^{[34]}$ For the composite of VS₂@SNC, the band of E₁g shifts to lower wave number compared with that of p-VS₂, which results from the weakened van der Waals force with expanded interlayer spacing. The Li⁺ storage behavior for the composite of VS₂@SNC was investigated by Li-ion half-cells. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves for the first three cycles were performed in the potential range of 0.01–3.0 V at a scan rate of 0.2 mV s⁻¹. As shown in Figure 5a, there are three cathodic peaks in the first discharge process. The first one at 1.70 V corresponds to the intercalation of Li⁺ into the interlayers of VS₂ to form LiₓVS₂, $^{[10a,37]}$ which gradually shifts to lower voltage with weakened intensity because of the consumption of active materials in the subsequent cathodic scans. $^{[38]}$ The followed inconspicuous peak at 0.78 V can be ascribed to the reduction of LiₓVS₂ to Li₂S and metallic V by a conversion reaction, which is similar to that happened in SIBs. $^{[11]}$ The last broad cathodic peak at 0.38 V relates to the formation of solid electrolyte interface (SEI) film. In the subsequent anodic scan, the split peaks located at 1.70 and 1.88 V associate with the multistep delithiation process. $^{[11a]}$ In addition, the next two closely linked peaks at 2.25 and 2.36 V are identified as oxidation of Li₂S to S, and the split phenomenon is universal in Li–S batteries. $^{[39]}$ During the cathodic process in the second cycle, the CV curve is different from that of the initial cycle, a new peak at 2.06 V is observed due to the reduction of S to Li₂S. Figure 5b shows the galvanostatic charge/discharge profiles of the battery for the initial three cycles, and the voltage plateaus match well with the potential of redox peaks in the CV curves.

Fascinated by the remarkable rate capability of the VS₂@SNC anode, the in-depth kinetic analyses were carried out based on Dunn and co-workers research.[⁴¹] It is apparent that the CV curves still maintain a similar shape but the peaks grow wider when the scan rates increase from 0.1 to 5 mV s⁻¹ (Figure 6a). The degree of the capacitive effect can be investigated according to Equation (2),[⁴¹] where $a$ and $b$ are adjustable parameters

$$i = av^{b} \tag{2}$$

$$i(v) = k_{1}v + k_{2}v^{1/2} \tag{3}$$

The value of $b$ is determined by the slope of log ($i$) versus log ($v$). As is well known that the $b$ value of 0.5 refers to a diffusion-controlled behavior, whereas that of 1.0 reflects a pseudocapacitive behavior. Herein, the $b$ values of peak 1, peak 2, peak 3, and peak 4 are calculated to be 0.787, 0.779, 0.710, and 0.787, respectively (Figure 6b), demonstrating the electrochemical process is influenced by both diffusion-controlled and pseudocapacitive behavior when VS₂@SNC is applied as an anode for LIBs. Furthermore, the pseudocapacitive contributions at various sweep rates are quantified through Equation (3),[⁴²] where $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ are constant values. By calculation, the capacitive contribution accounts for 56.6% of the total capacity at a scan rate of 1 mV s⁻¹ (Figure 6c). As displayed in Figure 6d, the ratio of the pseudocapacitive contribution gradually increases with the increasing of scan rate and finally reaches a maximum value of 88.7% at 5 mV s⁻¹.

To further explore the enhanced rate capability of the VS₂@ SNC anode, the electrochemical impedance analyses were implemented after 10 cycles at 0.1 A g⁻¹ (Figure S6, Supporting Information). For the electrode of p-VS₂, there are two depressed semicircles in the high-medium frequency, which can be assigned to the impedance of electrode/electrolyte interface ($R_{f}$) and charge transfer impedance ($R_{ct}$), respectively, while there is only an overlapped semicircle of $R_{f}$ and $R_{ct}$ for the electrode of VS₂@SNC. Moreover, both of them exhibit an inclined line in the low frequency, which associates with the solid-state ion diffusion in the electrode. According to the parameters of the fitted circuit (Table S1, Supporting Information), the $R_{ct}$ for VS₂@SNC (6.0 Ω) is roughly one seventh of that for p-VS₂ (44.0 Ω), demonstrating the significantly enhanced charge transfer efficiency for the electrode of VS₂@SNC.

Based on the XRD pattern of the prepared material in experiment, the trigonal (T) VS₂ with Pm1 space group was used to model monolayers and supercell structure of VS₂. The lattice parameters of the conventional cell are $a = b = 3.221$ Å, $c = 5.755$ Å, $\alpha = \beta = 90^\circ$, and $\gamma = 120^\circ$.[10b,12] After optimized by general gradient approximation (GGA) functional with DFT-D2 method, the interlayer spacing of $3 \times 3 \times 2$ supercell is 5.736 Å (Figure S7a, Supporting Information), which is close to the experimental one.

Different interlayer spacing of VS₂ will greatly affect Li⁺ intercalation mechanism, diffusion paths, and corresponding barriers. To study the effects of interlayer spacing, the VS₂ supercell with an interlayer spacing of 5.736 Å and a monolayer of VS₂ (Figure S7b, Supporting Information) are used. In this work, a monolayer is used to model the VS₂ with interlayer spacing larger than 9.227 Å because the adjacent layer nearly has no effect on Li, and the charge density difference plot depicted in Figure S8 in the Supporting Information presents this situation clearly. The blue charge density represents an electron deficient region and the yellow one means the opposite result; the blue region around Li and the yellow region around S show strong Coulomb interaction between Li and S, and the electrons transfer from Li to VS₂. The overlarge interlayer spacing (9.227 Å) for VS₂ changes the bonding situation and coordination numbers of Li, making it approximately absorbed on a VS₂ monolayer, and this similar phenomenon also happened in MoSe₂ system.[14b]

There are two possible interstitial sites for Li insertion in VS₂ supercell. As illustrated in Figure S9 in the Supporting Information, depending on the different ligancy of S with Li, we label them as O or T site (octahedral or tetrahedral site). For the O site and T site in bulk VS₂, the $E_{\text{bind}}$ is $-2.06$ and $-1.36$ eV, respectively. The more negative value means the more favorable process in thermodynamics, so we can draw the conclusion that the interaction of Li with bulk VS₂ is more likely to happen on the O site first. Similar to the diffusion of Na in VS₂ supercell,[43] there exists two possible Li diffusion paths: O–T–O′ and O–O′ (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Activation energies for these migration paths can be derived by the diffusion barriers of migrating ion along those diffusion paths.[44] In the VS₂ supercell with an interlayer spacing of 5.736 Å, Li can migrate from the most stable O site to T site or another adjacent O site. The climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) and dimer calculations indicate that the Li diffusion barrier of the O–T path (0.75 eV) is very close to that of the O–O′ path (0.74 eV, Figure S11, Supporting Information), while the barrier of the T–O′ path is only 0.04 eV (Table S2, Supporting Information).

On a monolayer of VS₂, there are three possible adsorption sites unlike in the supercell (Figure S12, Supporting Information): on the top of V atom (T_V site), on the top of S atom (T_S site), and on the hollow center of S atoms (H_center site). During the structure relaxation, we found that the Li atom at the T_S site would migrate to the T_V site, which agrees with our previous study of Na adsorption on VS₂ monolayers.[11a] For the T_V site and H_center site, the $E_{\text{bind}}$ are $-1.410$ and $-1.509$ eV, respectively, indicating the H_center site is more favorable with lower binding energy than T_V site. In order to get a deep insight into the Li diffusion kinetics on a VS₂ monolayer, three potential pathways are considered: T–H, H–H′, and T–T′ paths (Figure 7), and the corresponding energy barriers are listed in Table 1. For the T–H path, the energy barrier is 0.13 eV. The T–T′ path is composed of T–H and H–T′ paths, and the barriers of which are 0.13 and 0.23 eV, respectively. The H–H′ path is composed of H–T and T–H′ paths, and the barriers of which are 0.23 and 0.13 eV, respectively. Therefore, the rate-determining activation energy of Li diffusion on a VS₂ monolayer is 0.23 eV. The activation energy for the migration of Li greatly decreases from 0.75 or 0.74 eV in VS₂ supercell with an interlayer spacing of 5.736 Å to 0.23 eV in the VS₂ monolayers, indicating the expanded interlayer spacing is beneficial for Li diffusion in VS₂.

### 4. Experimental Section
Preparation and Characterization: In general, 265 mg of vanadyl acetylacetonate (VO(acac)₂) and 300 mg of TAA were dissolved in 30 mL of 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) and vigorously stirred at room temperature for 30 min. Next, 100 mg of melamine was added to the above solution and stirred for another 30 min. Then, the as-prepared homogeneous solution was transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and maintained at 200 °C for 24 h. After that, the obtained black precipitate denoted as m-$VS_2$ was washed with ethanol by centrifugation several times. Finally, the m-$VS_2$ was calcined at 500 °C in nitrogen for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min⁻¹ to obtain $VS_2$@SNC. The $VS_2$-NH₃ was synthesized in a similar way as the p-$VS_2$ except for the addition of melamine, and as a control sample, the pure $VS_2$ (p-$VS_2$) was obtained by calcining the $VS_2$-NH₃ at 500 °C in nitrogen for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min⁻¹.

XRD measurements were implemented using Rigaku Ultima IV with Cu Kα radiation. FT-IR spectra were obtained by a BRUKER-EQUINOX-55 IR spectrophotometer. TG-DSC analyses were performed on an STA 449 F3 Jupiter instrument. The elemental analyses were characterized by XPS (ESCALAB 250). The morphologies were investigated via SEM (Hitachi 4800) and TEM (TECNAI G2 F20 instrument). Raman spectra were acquired on an HR Evolution Raman system utilizing a 532 nm laser.

Electrochemical Measurements: Electrochemical measurements were evaluated using CR2025 coin-type cells. Specifically, the $VS_2$@SNC composite was mixed with acetylene black and sodium alginate at a mass ratio of 7:2:1. Then, the mixture was ground thoroughly and the obtained slurry was pasted on copper foil circular flakes. The final electrodes were vacuum-dried at 110 °C for 12 h. Lithium slices were used as the counter and reference electrodes. The electrolyte was LiPF₆ (1 м) solution in a mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC) in a volume ratio of 1:1 with the additives of 10% (volume) fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and 1% (volume) vinylene (VC). A Celgard 2400 (America) microporous polypropylene membrane was applied as the separator. The cell was assembled in a glovebox filled with highly pure argon gas (O₂ and H₂O levels <1 ppm). Galvanostatic charge/discharge measurements were carried out on an electrochemical workstation (Land CT 2001A, Wuhan, China) within a voltage range of 0.01-3.0 V (vs Li⁺/Li) at room temperature. CV was carried out on an electrochemical workstation (CHI 600C, Shanghai, China). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on an IM6 electrochemical workstation (Zahner Elektrik, Germany). Electrochemical measurements for p-$VS_2$ were also performed under the same conditions.

Computational Details: The first-principles spin-polarized calculations were performed with DFT, which was implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP).[⁴⁵] The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional form of the GGA was used for the exchange-correlation energy.[⁴⁶] The projector augmented wave potential (PAW)[⁴⁷] was used to represent core electrons with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 500 eV. For simulating one lithium adsorption and diffusion in $VS_2$ system with different interlayer spacing, a 3 × 3 × 2 supercell with an interlayer spacing around 5.7 Å and a 3 × 3 × 1 monolayer with a vacuum layer of 20 Å were used.[¹⁵] The DFT-D2 method was used to deal with the van der Waals forces between Li and $VS_2$ in supercell or monolayers.[⁴⁸] The 3 × 3 × 2 and 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack grids of Brillouin-zone k-point sampling were used for total energy calculations for $VS_2$ supercell and monolayers, respectively. The energy criterion for self-consistency was set to less than 10⁻⁴ eV per unit cell, and the force convergence threshold of geometry optimizations was 0.02 eV Å⁻¹. The binding energy $E_{bind}$ was calculated to evaluate the structural stability of different bond sites for Li in supercell and monolayer through Equation (4):[¹¹ᵃ]

$$E_{\text{bind}}=E_{\text{Li}_x\text{VS}_2}-E_{\text{VS}_2}-xE_{\text{Li}} \tag{4}$$

where $E_{\text{Li}_x\text{VS}_2}$ is the energy of the $VS_2$ supercell or monolayers with one Li atom added, $E_{\text{VS}_2}$ is the energy of free $VS_2$ supercell or monolayers, and $E_{\text{Li}}$ is calculated by bulk Li.

To investigate the lithium diffusion path and corresponding barriers, the CI-NEB method[⁴⁹] and the dimer method[⁵⁰] were used, and the imaginary frequency was examined for all transient states. The force convergence criterion was set to less than 0.02 eV Å⁻¹.
