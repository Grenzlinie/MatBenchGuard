# Controlled Growth Lateral/Vertical Heterostructure Interface for Lithium Storage

Tao Wang, Mingsheng Li, Li Yao, Wenlong Yang,* and Yuliang Li*

Artificial heterostructures with structural advancements and customizable electronic interfaces are fundamental for achieving high-performance lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). Here, a design idea for a covalently bonded lateral/vertical black phosphorus (BP)-graphdiyne oxide (GDYO) heterostructure achieved through a facile ball-milling approach, is designed. Lateral heterogeneity is realized by the sp²-hybridized mode P-C bonds, which connect the phosphorus atoms at the edges of BP with the carbon atoms of the terminal acetylene in GDYO. The vertical connection of the heterojunction of BP and GDYO is connected by P-O-C bond. Experimental and theoretical studies demonstrate that BP-GDYO incorporates interfacial and structural engineering features, including built-in electric fields, chemical bond interactions, and maximized nanospace confinement effects. Therefore, BP-GDYO exhibits improved electrochemical kinetics and enhanced structural stability. Moreover, through ex- and in-situ studies, the lithiation mechanism of BP-GDYO, highlighting that the introduction of GDYO inhibits the shuttle/dissolution effect of phosphorus intermediates, hinders volume expansion, provides more reactive sites, and ultimately promotes reversible lithium storage, is clarified. The BP-GDYO anode exhibits lithium storage performance with high-rate capacity and long-cycle stability (602.6 mAh g⁻¹ after 1 000 cycles at 2.0 A g⁻¹). The proposed interfacial and structural engineering is universal and represents a conceptual advance in building high-performance LIBs electrode.

## 1. Introduction

The heterostructures effectively regulate the confinement and transport of charge carriers in the nano-interfacial regions, showing new electronic properties and performance, that are ultimately responsible for a wide range of exceptional physical and chemical capabilities, especially in modern electrochemical energy storage systems. The controllability of the interface structure can lead to superior performance and fast response behavior of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs).[¹] Layerd BP is a promising material for high-rate, high-specific capacity, and long-lifetime lithium storage with several appealing features. First, it possesses a high theoretical capacity of 2596 mAh g⁻¹ as a result of its three-electron alloying reaction with Li⁺ (Li₃P). Moreover, BP has a relatively high electrical conductivity of ≈80 S m⁻¹ and a low diffusion barrier for Li⁺ of 0.08 eV in the zigzag direction.[²] However, the reconstruction of edge atoms on BP impedes the migration kinetics of Li⁺ across the surface.[³] Even, due to electrode materials are susceptible to pulverization, agglomeration, and detachment from the current collectors due to a significant increase in volume (≈300%) and the shuttle/dissolution effect of phosphorus intermediates during electrochemical reactions.[⁴] As a result, BP electrodes show irreversible alloying reactions and with a low ability to sustain high charge/discharge rates.

In recent years, the rise of two-dimensional (2D) materials has played a positive role in solving the bottleneck problems of batteries. Integration of 2D materials into heterostructures allows the advantages of each building block and shows us functionality of 1+1 is greater than 2.[¹] Researchers have focused on constructing heterostructures on a microscale level to address the limitations of BP.[⁵] These heterostructures consist of BP layers and other components stacked vertically or stitched together in-plane.[⁵] Despite advances in LIBs using BP-based lateral heterostructures, electrode pulverization and agglomeration remain unsolved during lengthy cycling due to the uninhibited shuttle effect of phosphorus intermediates and volume expansion.[⁶] Therefore, in order to stabilize the electrodes during cycling, it is often necessary to add a third or more components,

Graphdiyne (GDY) is an exceptional carbon nanomaterial with 18-C cavities measuring 0.54 nm, high activity triple bonds, excellent electrical conductivity of $2.5 × 10^{-4} S m^{-1}$, and remarkable chemical stability. $^{[9]}$ Furthermore, GDY provides numerous storage sites and diffusion channels during $Li^{+}$ insertion/extraction procedures. $^{[10]}$ Therefore, it is a promising candidate for a 2D building block. Graphdiyne oxide (GDYO) is produced through mild oxidation of GDY followed by ultrasonic exfoliation while retaining its electronic, physical, and chemical properties. $^{[11]}$ Many characteristics of GDYO and BP are highly matched and fused, which is conducive to the formation of high-performance interface structures. (i) The highly chemically reactive terminal triple bonds and oxygen-containing functional groups of GDYO might enable it to form more stable interfacial contacts with BP. $^{[4a,12]}$ (ii) By creating in-plane P-O-C bonds, the oxygen-containing functional groups at edges/surfaces of GDYO might alleviate the structural stress caused by discrepancies in the spatial symmetry of P and C atoms at the interface. $^{[11]}$ Likewise, out-of-plane P-O-C bonds could establish a vertical contact between BP and GDYO while preserving the triple bonds in the GDYO plane. (iii) GDY or GDYO-based heterostructures can promote the formation of atomic interface electric fields and accelerate the transfer of ions and electrons and may limit the shuttle/dissolution of phosphorus intermediates. $^{[13]}$ These advantages indicate that the combination of GDYO and BP can form dual lateral/vertical heterostructure, ensuring fast reaction kinetics and maintaining structural stability during charge and discharge.

Herein, we present a dual lateral/vertical BP-GDYO heterostructure with covalently bonded interfaces to investigate the structure design and interfacial engineering of electrodes. During the ball-milling process, the phosphorus atoms at the edges of BP and carbon atoms of the terminal acetylene in GDYO are connected through $sp^{2}$ hybridization mode to form a lateral contact. Meanwhile, BP and GDYO are connected by P-O-C bonds to form a vertical heterogeneity, maximizing the line-to-line/face-to-face contact between the two building blocks (Figure 1a). The unique structure configurations were verified through X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements and density functional theory (DFT) simulations. Multiple electrochemical experiments and theoretical analyses were employed to reveal the synergistic effects between interfacial and structural engineering, which improve ion and electron transfer and enhance structural

stability. The lithiation mechanism of the BP-GDYO anode was elucidated using ex situ X-ray diffraction (XRD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and in situ Raman, demonstrating that GDYO provides more lithium storage sites, diffusion channels, and a confinement effect on BP and its electrochemical products ($\text{Li}_x\text{P}_y$). This makes the BP-GDYO heterostructure a promising material for LIB anode with high reversible capacity, exceptional stability, and high rate performance. These results emphasize the importance of designing heterostructure architectures with multiscale interfacial and structural engineering to develop high-performance electrodes for LIBs.

## 2. Results and Discussions

The fabrication of the BP-GDYO heterostructure relies on the use of highly crystalline GDYO nanosheets, which were synthesized by a mild oxidation process of bulk GDY in a solution containing $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, followed by ultrasonic exfoliation.$^{[11]}$ Typical scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and TEM images, as shown in Figure 1b,c, respectively, reveal that the GDYO exhibits a 2D lamellar morphology. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis was conducted to examine the crystal structure of GDYO nanosheets. Figure 1c, inset and Figure 1d display the planar views of the ordered arrangement of layers and atoms for GDYO nanosheets. The magnified region and corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns show an interlayer spacing of 0.37 nm (Figure 1c, inset; Figure S1a, Supporting Information) and a lattice spacing of 0.45 nm (Figure 1d, inset; Figure S1b, Supporting Information), which agrees well with the ABC-stacking GDY models.$^{[14]}$ Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Figure 1e) reveals the 2D morphology of GDYO with an average thickness of 2.8 nm. GDYO retains the skeletal structure of GDY, as evidenced by its high crystallinity, serving as a basis for forming covalently bonded heterostructures with BP through lattice matching. Bulk BP has the characteristic tightly arranged layer structures, measuring 28.7 nm in thickness (Figures S2a,d, and S3a, Supporting Information). In the HRTEM of bulk BP (Figure S1d, Supporting Information, top inset), the lattice spacings of 0.53 nm and 0.21 nm correspond to the (020) and (002) planes, respectively.$^{[15]}$ The SAED pattern, as shown in the bottom inset, also demonstrates the high crystalline quality of BP. The SEM and TEM images of ball-milled GDYO (GDYO-BM) and BP (BP-BM) are displayed in Figures S2b,e,c,f (Supporting Information) respectively. After the ball-milling process, GDYO maintained its thinness (2.5 nm, Figure S3b, Supporting Information), while the bulk BP underwent cutting and exfoliation to form irregular nanosheets with a thickness of 12.9 nm (Figure S3c, Supporting Information).

The solid-state mechanochemical approach has been widely used to link building blocks to create lateral/vertical heterostructures as an alternative to solution-phase chemical synthesis.$^{[16]}$ Figure 1a illustrates dry ball milling to fabricate the BP-GDYO heterostructure (30% GDYO by weight). This method was chosen for its ecological sustainability and lack of organic solvents, making it environmentally friendly. GDYO and BP underwent significant plastic deformation during the ball milling procedure due to the high temperature and shear force. The formation of the BP-GDYO heterostructure was ultimately facilitated by the covalent interaction involving the broken BP and GDYO. The morphology of the BP-GDYO heterostructure was analyzed by SEM and TEM. Figure 1f,g shows the tight integration of the basal planes of BP and GDYO, which is further supported by the corresponding elemental mapping images (Figure 1g; Figure S4, Supporting Information). The overlapping regions of P and C elements in the BP-GDYO heterostructure can be attributed to the out-of-plane contact. The HRTEM image (Figure 1h) taken at the interface of the BP-GDYO heterostructure shows two domains with contrasting crystalline characteristics. The right-hand crystal domain shows lattice fringes corresponding to the (002) lattice plane of BP with a d-spacing of 0.21 nm.$^{[15]}$ The left crystal domain of the heterostructure exhibits lattice fringes characterized by a d-spacing of 0.45 nm, corresponding to the (110) lattice plane of the GDYO crystal.$^{[14b]}$ Furthermore, Figure 1i displays the HRTEM image, which reveals interlayer spacings of 0.37 nm and 0.53 nm for GDYO and BP, respectively, suggesting an alignment of three GDYO layers with every two BP layers. The interface exhibits noticeable distortion in the GDYO structure due to the mismatched number of layers. Nonetheless, due to the weak van der Waals interactions between the GDYO layers caused by the oxygen-containing functional groups, the strain induced by the dislocations close to the grain boundary can be mitigated while the GDYO configuration away from the interface remains largely intact.$^{[17]}$ AFM images also show in-plane contact in the BP-GDYO heterostructure (Figures 1j; Figure S5, Supporting Information). Additionally, the thickness of BP-GDYO is 16.7 nm, which is greater than that of ball-milled BP (12.9 nm). This difference in thickness may be attributed to the out-of-plane contact between BP and GDYO.$^{[18]}$

The XRD results confirm the absence of impurity phases in the BP-GDYO heterostructures. Figure S6a,b (Supporting Information) shows the XRD patterns of BP-GDYO heterostructures, which reveal multiple lattice planes of both BP and GDYO, including the (002) plane of GDYO and the (020) and (040) planes of BP (PDF#73-1358). This indicates that the orthorhombic structure of BP remained stable even after the ball-milling process.$^{[19]}$ To determine how GDYO and BP connected, covalent bonding or physical interaction, we prepared bulk GDYO without ultrasonic exfoliation and a physical combination of BP and GDYO (BP+GDYO) for comparison. A series of spectroscopic analyses were conducted to clarify this question. We initially conducted a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) analysis (Figure S6c, Supporting Information). Comparing the FTIR spectrum of bulk GDYO before and after ultrasonic exfoliation, we noticed that the peak at $2195.6\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ remains unaltered, while a new peak at $2100.3\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ appears, demonstrating the production of terminal acetylene in GDYO nanosheets during the sonication process.$^{[20]}$ The FTIR spectrum of BP-GDYO also displays a vibrational peak of acetylenic bonds at $2195.6\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, indicating the presence of GDYO in the BP-GDYO heterostructure. Besides, two new vibrational peaks at $990.7\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ and $1400.0\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, which are absent in the spectrum of BP+GDYO, are probably derived from the P-O-C and P-C bonds, respectively.$^{[21]}$ Significantly, the disappearance of the terminal alkynes peak in the spectrum of BP-GDYO, but its persistence in the spectrum of BP+GDYO provides evidence of the involvement of the terminal acetylene in forming P-C bonds. Electric fields within the BP-GDYO heterostructures could lower the internal resistance and improve the ion diffusionrate, thus enhancing lithium storage performance. $^{[13]}$ 

For a more detailed study of the atomic level bonding structure of BP-GDYO at the interfaces, we performed XAS investigations and DFT calculations. Figure S9 (Supporting Information) com- pares the P K-edge spectra between the BP-GDYO heterostruc- ture and bulk BP. The photon energy of peak A (P 1s $\to$ 3p) in BP GDYO (2145.2 eV) is lower than that of BP (2145.8 eV). This can be explained by the electron transfer from GDYO to BP, which is consistent with the C K-edge spectra (Figure 2f). $^{[4 a]}$ Besides, P-O-C bonding can be attributed to the newly observed peak B(P 1s $\to$ O 2 p) at $2154.6 eV.^{[29]}$ We also obtained P L-edge spec tra of the BP-GDYO heterostructure and bulk BP, which provide more spectral features than the K-edge spectra (Figure 2e). BothBP-GDYO and bulk BP exhibit three distinct peaks: A (P $2 p \to$ 3s-like $a_{1})$ , B (P $2 p \to$ 3p-like $t_{2}^{*})$ , and C (P $2 p \to$ 3d). $^{[30]}$ In con trast to bulk BP, the BP-GDYO heterostructure exhibits a promi- nent peak D at 137.9 eV. The energy of this new peak is lower than that of the P-N bond (138.9 eV), suggesting a reduced po- larization of the phosphorus bonds. $^{[31]}$ Considering the different electronegativities of C (2.55), N (3.04), and O (3.44), it is justifi- able to attribute peak D to the C atoms covalently bonded to the P atoms. $^{[4 a, 31,32]}$ Similarly, peak D in the C K-edge spectra is attributed to C-P bonds. $^{[4 a]}$ Four prominent peaks are observed in the BP-GDYO heterostructure spectrum: A (285.9 eV, C sp $^{2} 1 s$  $\to \pi^{*}$ , out-of-plane), A' (286.2 eV, C sp $1 s \to \pi^{*}$ , out-of-plane), B(289.3 eV, C-O), and C (292.5 eV, C $1 s \to \sigma^{*}$ , in-plane). $^{[33]}$ These peaks indicate that the GDYO lattice maintains its ordered planar configuration after the ball milling process. If P atoms form a per- pendicular $\pi$ bond with GDYO, the $\pi^{*}$ absorption feature (peaks A and A') would exhibit broadening or even splitting. $^{[4 a, 33 b]}$ This indicates the out-of-plane linkage between BP and GDYO via P- O-C bonds based on the oxygen-containing functional groups in GDYO. Notably, the $\pi^{*}$ absorption feature exhibited a slight de crease in relative intensity compared to GDYO, implying that the bond between the P atom and the C atom changes the hybridiza- tion of sp carbons to $sp^{2} \cdot^{[26]}$ By integrating FTIR and XPS analy sis, we hypothesize that the mechanism for establishing the in- plane covalent bond between BP and GDYO is based on the con- version of sp-hybridized carbon atoms in the terminal alkynes of GDYO to $sp^{2}$ hybridization. The measured shift of ≈0.3 eV toward the lower photon energy of peak C indicates strain and distortion in the carbon-carbon bonds within the GDYO plane, which agrees with the HRTEM images. $^{[33 a]}$ These findings sug gest that the BP and GDYO are laterally and vertically connected by $\sigma$ bonds.

DFT simulations were performed to gain deeper insights into the geometry of lateral BP-GDYO heterostructure and the bond- ing mechanism. Based on the above analysis and considering the two potential configurations of the GDYO's edges (Figure S10a-d, Supporting Information), we systematically constructed four lateral BP-GDYO heterostructure models: The terminal alkynes of GDYO form bonds with P atoms directly (Figure S10g, Supporting Information, models i and iii), and terminal alkynes undergo a sp-to-sp $^{2}$ carbon atom hybridization transition, wherein they are converted into four-membered carbon rings that incorporate with P atoms (Figure S10g, Supporting Information, models ii and iv). Also taken into account is the oxidative cleavage of diacetylenicl bonds, which generates oxygen- containing functional groups that establish P-O-C bonds with the P atoms. After optimization, the relative positions of the P and C atoms in model ii and iv interfaces exhibit minimal changes, accompanied by lower formation energy $(E_{form })$ values of 0.62 eV for model ii and 0.85 eV for model iv. However, the interfaces in the structures where the terminal alkynes are directly bondedto the P atoms display significant distortion, with higher $E_{form }$ values of 0.96 eV for model i and 0.94 eV for model iii (Figure 2g; Table S2, Supporting Information). The discrepancy could be attributed to the distinct spatial symmetry of the P and C atoms at the interfaces. Interestingly, different levels of cyclization of the GDYO's terminal alkyne are observed in models i and iii. Par- ticularly when a four-membered carbon ring structure is present(in model i), the atomic arrangement at the interface is better maintained. These results are consistent with our proposed hy- pothesis based on FTIR, XPS, and XAS analysis. Different charge density maps were utilized to illustrate the bonding mechanism between the P and C atoms. Using model ii as an example, charge accumulates at the central region of the P and C atoms at the interface (Figure 2h,I; Figure S11, Supporting Information),


demonstrating that the P atoms from the BP establish covalent connections with the C atoms from the GDYO. Next, we inves- tigated the hybridization mechanism of the P-C covalent bonds using theoretical analysis. In pure BP, each P atom at the edges is bonded to two neighboring P atoms, indicating that the P atom possesses one unpaired electron. Similarly, the ball milling pro- cess produces sp² C atoms at the edges of GDYO, which also have one unpaired electron in addition to the initially occupied π elec- tron in the $2p_{z}$ orbital. When the P atom is close to the C atom, the lone electron of the P atom would form a covalent bond with an electron from the C atom. Nonetheless, it is still unclear whether the sp²-hybridized orbital electron or the $2p_{z}$ orbital electron of the C atom is linked to the P atom. To resolve this matter, we conducted calculations on the projected density of states (PDOS) on the p orbitals of the P and C atoms, as shown in Figure 2j. The $p_{y}$, $p_{z}$, and $p_{x}$ orbitals of atom P1 overlap with the equiv. orbitals in atoms P2 ($3p_{y}$), P3 ($3p_{z}$), and C1 ($2p_{x}$), respectively. Addition- ally, the $2p_{z}$ orbital of the C1 atom overlaps with C2 and C3. These results suggest that the P1-P2 and P1-P3 bonds retain the original σ bonds, while the $p_{z}$ orbital of the C1 atom continues to form a π conjugation in GDYO.¹³⁴¹ The sp²-hybridized orbital is the main contributor of the C1 atom in the P-C covalent bonds, while the $2p_{z}$ orbital has a minimal effect. This suggests that the P and C atoms form a new σ bond through sp² hybridization, consistent with the XAS data. Covalent bonding between P and C atoms may prevent the reconstruction of P atoms at the boundary, allowing for unobstructed edges and facilitating the rapid entrance of $Li^{+}$. The closer contact between P and C atoms at the edges of the BP-GDYO heterostructure is attributed to the shorter P-C bond lengths (L3, Figure 2i) than the P-P bonds (L1 and L2). Put differently, P atoms at the edges preferentially form covalent bonds with C atoms in GDYO during the ball milling.¹³⁴¹

Based on the analysis, we proposed reasonable structure configurations for the BP-GDYO heterostructure and its for- mation mechanism. The bulk BP is exfoliated into thin- layer BP nanosheets through high-energy ball milling. This mechanochemical process breaks P-P bonds, generating reactive entities such as radicals and ions at the edges. Ball milling acti- vates the terminal alkynes of GDYO, generating sp² hybridized C atoms in a four-membered carbon ring structure (Figure 2k).¹³⁵¹ The C atoms are connected to the activated edge of BP through covalent P-C bonds, achieving in-plane contact. The out-of-plane contact between BP and GDYO is based on the P-O-C bonds con- struction, which involves oxygen-containing functional groups in GDYO. This results in the formation of a dual lateral/vertical BP- GDYO heterostructure. Mixing bulk BP and GDYO without ad- ditives through high-energy ball milling can create covalent link- ages between them. This procedure is both convenient and envi- ronmentally sustainable.

Figure S12a (Supporting Information) illustrates the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas and pore size distributions. The surface area of BP-GDYO is $11.7\ \text{m}^2\ \text{g}^{-1}$, which surpasses that of GDYO ($3.9\ \text{m}^2\ \text{g}^{-1}$) and bulk BP ($4.8\ \text{m}^2\ \text{g}^{-1}$). The increased surface area of BP-GDYO facilitates $Li^{+}$ transport and acts as a cushion for the volume expansion during charging/discharging processes.¹³⁶¹ Most pore sizes in BP-GDYO fall within the 2.0 to 6.0 nm range, demonstrating a mesoporous structure. The mesoporous structure of BP-GDYO enables rapid $Li^{+}$ transport and electrolyte infiltration. Contact angle tests were conducted on BP-GDYO using an organic electrolyte, revealing a contact angle of $0^{\circ}$ for BP-GDYO, $60.3^{\circ}$ for bulk BP, and $33.4^{\circ}$ for GDYO (Figure S12b, Supporting Information). The elec- trolyte absorption capabilities of BP-GDYO were investigated. According to Figure S12c (Supporting Information), BP-GDYO demonstrated an electrolyte uptake of $79.2\%$ after 5 h, while GDYO and BP had electrolyte uptakes of $60.3\%$ and $51.8\%$, respectively. As the anode absorbs enough electrolytes, the dis- tance for ion transfer decreases, improving the diffusion of $Li^{+}$ to the active material and resulting in enhanced electrochemical performance of the BP-GDYO heterostructure.

The performance and mechanism of lithium storage of the BP-GDYO heterostructure were investigated using coin cells (CR2032-type) with metallic lithium foil as the counter electrode (Figure 3a). The BP-GDYO heterostructure with a weight ratio of 30% GDYO was chosen as the optimal material for lithium stor- age anode material (Figure S13, Supporting Information).

Figure 3b displays the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of bulk BP, GDYO, and BP-GDYO heterostructure for the first cycle. The scan rate was $0.1\ \text{mV}\ \text{s}^{-1}$, and the voltage window ranged from 0.01 and 3.0 V. The cathodic scan of the BP-GDYO anode revealed peaks at 1.10, 0.78, and 0.56 V, corresponding to the three-step reactions involved in the lithiation process of BP.¹²ᵃ,³⁷¹ The for- mation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) and the reduction of residual oxygen-containing functional groups in GDYO may have contributed to the peak at 1.87 V, which vanished in sub- sequent scans.¹³ᵃ¹ The peak at 0.01 V can be ascribed to the in- sertion of lithium ions into GDYO. During the anodic scan, the peak observed at 0.26 V is attributed to the lithium extraction from GDYO.¹³⁸¹ Peaks ranging from 0.8 to 1.3 V suggest a re- versible delithiation process of the BP phase. The bulk BP anode exhibits a substantial peak current during the lithiation process. Yet, during the delithiation process, the peak current is consid- erably reduced compared to that of the BP-GDYO anode, indi- cating an irreversible alloying reaction. After the initial cycle, the GDYO and BP-GDYO anodes exhibited notable reversibility and excellent cyclability in their CV curves (Figure S14, Supporting Information). Ex situ XRD patterns were performed to investi- gate the structural and phase transformation of the BP-GDYO anode for lithium storage. The fresh electrode pattern exhibited the characteristic diffraction peaks of crystalline BP (Figure 3c). As the cell voltage decreased to 1.1 V, new peaks were observed at $17.0^{\circ}$, $30.4^{\circ}$, and $31.1^{\circ}$, attributed to $Li_{3}P_{7}$ (PDF#77-2425). Upon discharge to 0.75 V, the intensities of the $Li_{3}P_{7}$ peaks decreased, and additional diffraction peaks appeared at $18.0^{\circ}$ and $36.5^{\circ}$, in- dicating a conversion process from $Li_{3}P_{7}$ to LiP (PDF#83-1575). Distinct peaks corresponding to $Li_{3}P$ (at $2\theta$ of $23.5^{\circ}$, $24.1^{\circ}$, and $26.8^{\circ}$, PDF#74-1160) appeared at a voltage of 0.55 V, accompanied by a faint signal of LiP. The intensity of $Li_{3}P$ peaks increased at 0.01 V, indicating the continuous formation of the $Li_{3}P$ phase. As the charging process continued, the $Li_{3}P$ phase disappeared, and the LiP phase reappeared at 1.0 V, indicating a reversible alloy- ing reaction in BP-GDYO anode. Specifically, the $Li_xP_y$ solution transitioned to an almost colorless state in the presence of GDYO. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy confirmed that GDYO has a higher adsorption capability for $Li_xP_y$ than GO (Figure 3g). This high adsorption capacity of GDYO for $Li_xP_y$ may be attributed to large conjugated groups (-C≡C-C≡C-) that exhibit electrostatic interaction with the $Li_xP_y$ through the lithium-ion.⁽⁴⁰⁾ Using BP-GO as the anode material resulted in the discharged electrolyte displaying an additional peak within the 520-489 cm⁻¹ range on the FTIR spectrum (Figure S15a, Supporting Information).⁽⁴¹⁾ This peak can be attributed to the polyphosphate, which suggests that the polyphosphate can dissolve in the ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate electrolyte. The absence of this peak observed following discharge in the case where BP-GDYO was employed as the anode material indicates that GDYO can inhibit the shuttle/dissolution of $Li_xP_y$. DFT calculations were performed on

the binding energies of $Li_3P_7$, LiP, and $Li_3P$ phases with GDY to provide additional evidence that the acetylenic bond restricts the dissolution/shuttle effect of $Li_xP_y$.[37b,41a,42] Graphene (GR) was used as a control sample. Figure S15c-q (Supporting Information) displays the optimized structures and calculation results. The binding energies of $Li_3P_7$, LiP, and $Li_3P$ at GDY sites are higher than those at GR sites. This demonstrates that the large conjugated system of GDY, which includes acetylenic bonds and benzene rings, has a stronger confinement effect on the soluble electrochemical products of $Li_xP_y$ phases than GR. This helps to maintain the structural integrity of BP during lithium storage. The lithium storage mechanism of the BP-GDY anode was further evaluated using in situ Raman spectroscopy (Figure 3h). The lithiation process caused slight redshifts in the D and G bands of BP-GDY due to the chemical bond transformation of GDYO. Furthermore, the alkyne and G band peak intensities decreased. Upon charging, the peak intensities returned to their initial state, indicating that GDYO can provide reactive sites.[38] Above all, the BP-GDY heterostructure exhibits a dual mechanism of lithium storage (Figure 3i). First, the unique carbon network of GDYO, which consists of $sp^2$ and sp hybridized carbon atoms, as well as uniformly distributed 18-C cavities, provides sites for the absorption of Li ions ($LiC_3$). Second, reversible alloying reactions of BP phase: $BP \leftrightarrow Li_3P_7 (1.10\ V) \leftrightarrow LiP (0.78\ V) \leftrightarrow Li_3P (0.56\ V) \leftrightarrow BP$.

The lithium storage mechanism can also be determined through galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) curves (Figure S16, Supporting Information). Figure 3j and Figure S17 (Supporting Information) display the GCD curves for the first cycle of BP, GDYO, BP+GDYO, and BP-GDY heterostructures. The BP+GDYO electrode demonstrated a discharge capacity of 676.0 mAh $g^{-1}$, with an ICE of 61.6% due to the volume expansion resulting in loss of electrical contact with GDYO.[4b] The covalently bonded BP-GDY electrode has an initial discharge specific capacity of 2498.5 mAh $g^{-1}$. Its reversible charge specific capacity is 1842.6 mAh $g^{-1}$, and the ICE (73.8%) is much higher than that of the BP electrode (5.6%). Furthermore, the hysteresis ($\Delta Ep$) of the discharge and charge plateaus for BP+GDYO and BP-GDY decreased from 0.41 to 0.29 V. This decrease proves that the well-designed covalently bonded lateral/vertical BP-GDY heterostructure could enhance Coulombic efficiency (CE) resulting from improved connectivity between materials during volume change in charging/discharging processes.[4b] To emphasize the role of GDYO, we prepared a BP-graphene oxide heterostructure (BP-GO) with a weight ratio of 30% GO. BP-GO showed a decrease in initial charge/discharge specific capacity (614.3 mAh $g^{-1}$/1004.6 mAh $g^{-1}$), a reduction in ICE of 61.2%, and an increase in $\Delta Ep$ of 0.42 V. These observations may be attributed to the insufficient electrical contact between BP and GR, as well as the ineffective confinement effect of GR on $Li_xP_y$ phases. To analyze the rate performance of the BP-GDY anode, we investigated the correlation between cycle numbers and specific capacities at different current densities. Figure 3k and Figure S18a (Supporting Information) show that a high capacity of 1550 mAh $g^{-1}$ is obtained at a current density of 0.1 A $g^{-1}$. Capacities decrease as current densities increase: 1276 mAh $g^{-1}$ at 0.2 A $g^{-1}$, 955.9 mAh $g^{-1}$ at 0.5 A $g^{-1}$, and 795.2 mAh $g^{-1}$ at 1.0 A $g^{-1}$, respectively. At 5.0 A $g^{-1}$, a significantly high capacity of 428.4 mAh $g^{-1}$ was obtained. To demonstrate the advantages of using BP-GDY anode, we evaluated the long-term cycling performance of several as-prepared electrode samples. The results in Figure S18c,d (Supporting Information) demonstrate that the specific capacity of the BP-GDY anode remains at 961.0 mAh $g^{-1}$ after 100 cycles at a current density of 0.1 A $g^{-1}$, which is significantly higher than that of BP (89.2 mAh $g^{-1}$), GDYO (407.5 mAh $g^{-1}$), BP+GDYO (364.8 mAh $g^{-1}$), and BP-GO (528.0 mAh $g^{-1}$). In addition, the CEs of the BP-GDY electrode remained ≈100% from 2 to 100 cycles. Figure 3j and Figure S18e (Supporting Information) illustrate the CEs and cycling stability of various anodes, including BP, GDYO, BP+GDYO, BP-GO, and BP-GDY. These anodes were cycled at a high current density of 2.0 A $g^{-1}$, followed by an activation procedure with one cycle at a current density of 0.1 A $g^{-1}$. The BP-GDY anodes exhibited remarkable cycling stability, maintaining a high specific capacity of 602.6 mAh $g^{-1}$ after 1000 cycles, much higher compared to BP (50.4 mAh $g^{-1}$), GDYO (148.4 mAh $g^{-1}$), BP+GDYO (19.0 mAh $g^{-1}$), and BP-GO (175.1 mAh $g^{-1}$). Meanwhile, the CE of the BP-GDY anodes remained consistently close to 100% throughout the entire cycle process. The BP-GDY electrode exhibited a decrease in specific capacity during the first 120 cycles, followed by an increase to 609.2 mAh $g^{-1}$ after 650 cycles, and ultimately stabilized in subsequent cycles. Irreversible adverse reactions may have caused the initial decline in discharge capacity.[43] The progressive increase in capacity is likely due to the activation process of the BP and the defects of GDYO that are formed during the charge/discharge process.[10a,44] These results suggest that the chemical-bridged BP-GDY heterostructure enhances the stability and $Li^+$ diffusion behavior of the electrode. In comparison to previously documented BP- and GDY-based anode materials for LIBs, the BP-GDY heterostructure exhibits superior specific capacity, high ICE, and a longer lifespan (Table S3, Supporting Information).

Based on the findings present, the BP-GDYO anode demon-
strates superior electron and $Li^+$ transport rates, as well as a
more stable structure compared to BP and GDYO. As a result,
it exhibits better lithium storage performance. The exceptional
lithium storage performance of the BP-GDYO heterostructure
can be attributed to the dual lateral/vertical structural advan-
tages when analyzed from the "synthesis-structure-performance"
standpoint. (Figure 5). GDYO maintains the structural integrity
of GDY, which features 18-C cavities that provide abundant stor-
age sites and pathways for $Li^+$. The C = O groups in GDYO
can effectively coordinate with $Li^+$. Additionally, the interfacial

More importantly, the covalently bonded lateral/vertical structure is paramount. (i) The in-plane chemical bonds between BP and GDYO restrict edge reconstruction of the BP phase, enabling rapid $Li^+$ entry through open edges. The electron transfer across the in-plane or out-of-plane interfaces of the heterostructure is also promoted. In addition, the copious presence of P-C bonds can act as reunion centers for re-establishing P-P bonds.$^{[12]}$ (ii) The GDYO exhibits exceptional flexibility, allowing for proper coupling with the BP phase, which enhances overall conductivity. In particular, GDYO forms a vertical connection with BP through out-of-plane P-O-C bonds based on oxygen-containing functional groups. This ensures that the alkyne bonds in the plane of GDYO are not disrupted, allowing for the reversible transformation of alkyne-alkene complexes. As a result, the lithium-ion storage sites and migration rate in GDYO are maintained.$^{[38,54]}$ (iii) Such a dual lateral/vertical heterostructure maximizes contact between binary building blocks, prevents electrode pulverization, agglomeration, and exfoliation by effectively suppressing the dissolution/shuttle effect of $Li_xP_y$ phases, inhibiting volume expansion, and further alleviating structural stress during charging and discharging process. Overall, each building block is tailored to fulfill specific functions and multiscale interfacial and structural synergies, resulting in exceptional lithium storage performance.

GDYO acts as a regulation of the volume expansion of BP during the cycle, provides more active sites, and restrains the shuttle effect of phosphorus intermediates to promote alloying and conversion reversibility. The results also demonstrate that the covalent bond formed by BP and GDYO limits edge reconstruction in BP, ensures rapid access to $Li^+$ for open edges, and provides an ideal electron transport channel to generate a built-in electric field for rapid reaction kinetics. BP-GDYO combines interface and structural engineering features, such as built-in electric fields, chemical bond interactions, and maximum nanospatial confinement effects to achieve the desired functional synergies. Various synergies promote the transformative properties of the BP-GDYO heterostructure and its efficient application in LIB. The BP-GDYO anode as prepared demonstrates ultra-high rate performance and long period stability (602.6 mAh g$^{-1}$ after 1000 cycles at 2.0 A g$^{-1}$).
