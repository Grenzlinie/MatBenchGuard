# Multidimensional $B_4N$ as novel anode materials for lithium ion batteries

Jiyuan Guo,* Binwei Tian, Huabing Shu, Ying Wang, and Jun Dai

View Article Online
DOI: 10.1039/D0CP02668A

Based on first-principles calculations and *ab initio* molecular dynamics simulations, multidimensional $B_4N$ are investigated as the anode materials for lithium ion batteries. Present results identify that the monolayer $B_4N$ can reach remarkably high specific capacity of 1874.27 mAhg⁻¹ and possesses low diffusion barrier (0.29 eV). Testing of bilayer $B_4N$ and bulk $B_4N$ reveals that the materials exhibit irreversible structural phase transformation. They are turned from layered structure to more stable cavity-channel structure due to the adsorption of Li atoms. The volume expansions of their saturated lithiation cavity-channel structures are about 12%, which is close to that of graphite (10%). Moreover, it is found that the energy barriers of the bilayer and bulk $B_4N$ are less than 0.5 eV in the cavity-channel. The saturated adsorption of bulk $B_4N$ yields a specific capacity of 468.57 mAhg⁻¹, being greater than the capacity of commercial graphite (372 mAhg⁻¹). More importantly, all the lithiation structures in the monolayer, bilayer, and bulk $B_4N$ are verified to be thermodynamically stable at the temperature of 350 K. These findings may encourage further experimental investigation on designing multidimensional B4N materials as novel candidate anode materials for lithium ion batteries.

## 1 Introduction

Rechargeable secondary lithium (Li) ion batteries (LIBs) have met with tremendous successes in terms of commercialization and they are extensively utilized in the field of electric vehicles, portable electronic equipment and grid energy storage systems.¹⁻⁴ A typical LIB is consisted of four main components: intercalated Li salts as cathode, graphite as the most commercial anode, complex of Li ion with organic compound as electrolyte, and polyethylene layers support for the separator.⁵⁻⁶ However, graphite as anode material is blocked for the LIBs application due to its low charge capacity of 372 mAhg⁻¹.¹⁻⁴ Therefore, choosing high charge capacity anode materials for energy storage devices is the focus of currently researchers.

To make a visible leap forward along the above direction, rational designing and choosing optimally performing anode materials can achieve many things at one stroke. Specially, the two-dimensional (2D) materials have recently proven to be a catalyzing factor due to its large surface-to-volume ratio, outstanding electronic, mechanical and thermal properties. For example, doped graphene had been experimentally shown a high reversible capacity together with high-rate capability and long-term cyclability.⁷⁻⁹ MXenes were regarded as good anodes for metal-ion batteries due to low diffusion barriers and relatively high charge capacity.¹⁰⁻¹³ Newly fabricated graphynes were tested with excellent energy storage capacities as well as fast charge/discharge rates.¹⁴⁻¹⁶ So far, most of the reported 2D materials for anodes were investigated via the freestanding 2D sheet. This does not take into account the problem of volume expansion, which seriously restricts silicon as ion battery anode material.¹⁷⁻¹⁹ In fact, 2D materials are often multilayer structures, even close to the bulk form in actual synthesis processes. And the volume expansion problem is circumvented usually for the investigation of 2D freestanding materials. Therefore, the practical adsorption behaviors of Li ion are likely to be not as simple as the action with the 2D freestanding materials. Despite those reported advances, adsorption behaviors of Li ion with the multilayers of the 2D material, the interaction mechanism between the Li ion and the bulk form, the accompanying volume expansion effects, electronic properties and structural characteristics of the

composite structures still remain to be further studied.

Herein, motivated by the aforementioned experimental and theoretical processes, we investigate a newly auxetic $B_4N$ material from the monolayer, bilayer to bulk as novel anode materials for LIBs using first-principles calculations. The $B_4N$ monolayer had been recently exposed with high in-plane Young's modulus, ultrahigh tensile strains (19% and 18% along x and y directions, respectively), nonmagnetic metal property, excellent thermal and dynamic stabilities as well. $^{20}$ $B_4N$ is originated from the nitrogen-embedded edge of the hexagonal ring of $\chi_3$-borophene and the mass ratio of B atom can reach to 75%. Most of the reported borophenes had been found as excellent anode materials. $^{21-23}$ Based on these identified fascinating properties, we first optimized the structures of the monolayer $B_4N$ and compared the results with previous reports. Then, the adsorption behaviors of Li with the monolayer, bilayer and bulk $B_4N$ were calculated, respectively. Thirdly, the electric properties of Li-$B_4N$ structures were investigated by analyzing the electron density of states (DOS) and difference charge densities. Fourthly, the Li diffusion barriers in representative directions of the $B_4N$ structures were calculated and compared. Finally, the volume expansion effects and thermal stabilities were clarified and specific capacities of different dimensional $B_4N$ as the anode material in LIBs were obtained.

## 2 Computational details

The structure relaxation and electronic property calculation were based on the spin-polarized density of functional theory (DFT) in the popular and efficient open source SIESTA code. $^{24-25}$ And the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) $^{26}$ with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional $^{27}$ was used for the exchange-correlation calculation in the present work. The pseudopotentials of potential energy in ion cores were obtained from Abinit's Fritz-Haber-Institute (FHI) pseudo database. $^{28}$ The optimized double-$\zeta$ basis sets were used for description of the valence electron wave function. $^{29-30}$ The van der Waals interaction was taken into account using the Grimme's D2 method to corrections. $^{31}$ A vacuum distance of 30 Å along the z direction was used to avoid the interaction between adjacent layers for the monolayer

and bilayer structures. The Monkhorst-Pack (MP) mesh cutoff was set 280 Ry in all the calculations. The energy threshold precision was set as $10^{-5}$ eV and the criterion for force convergence was set less than 0.02 eV/Å. A $4 \times 1$ supercell of monolayer B₄N was employed to model the adsorption and diffusion of Li, and the Brillouin Zone integration was performed using $5 \times 5 \times 1$ k-point grid. The smearing of the electronic occupations was done, which proposed by Methfessel and Paxton (MP).³² A canonical ensemble (NVT) with the Nosé thermostat was adopted for the ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) calculations. Based on the maximum working temperature of LIB usually was 60 °C, here, the AIMD simulations were carried out at 350 K to check the stability of Li decorated B₄N. The time step was set to 1.0 fs and this simulation went up to 5 ps.

Diffusion pathway and energy barriers for transport of Li atom on the B₄N surface are computed by the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method.³³ This method is mainly to optimize the adsorption atoms on the substrate surface and get the energy barriers when the Li atoms diffused from the most stable adsorption site to the adjacent most stable adsorption site. And it can ensure the preferential diffused path. The parameters used for the CI-NEB simulations are the same as in the geometry optimization. Seven images including the initial and the final positions are used for the CI-NEB calculations.

The cohesive energy of monolayer B₄N is defined as:¹²

$$
E_{coh}=(8E_{B}+2E_{N}-E_{B_{4}N})/10 \tag{1}
$$

Where $E_{B}, E_{N}$ and $E_{B_{4}N}$ are the total energy of an isolated B atom, an isolated N atom, and a primitive cell of the monolayer B₄N, respectively. The adsorption energy of Li on the B₄N substrate is calculated by:¹¹⁻¹²

$$
E_{\text{ad}}=(E_{M-B_{4}N}-E_{B_{4}N}-nE_{M})/n \quad (M=\text{Li}) \tag{2}
$$

Where $E_{M-B_{4}N}$ and $E_{B_{4}N}$ are the total energies of optimized B₄N substrate after and before adsorption, respectively. $E_{M}$ is the per atom energy in the bulk Li metal crystal and $n$ represent the number of Li atoms. The specific capacity of the material in mAhg⁻¹ can be obtained from:³⁴⁻³⁵

$$
\text{Capacity} = \frac{n_M F}{3.6(n_B m_B + n_N m_N)} \tag{3}
$$

Where $n_M, n_B$ and $n_N$ are the number of Li, B and N atoms in the system. $m_B$ and $m_N$ are the atomic masses of the B and N atoms. F is the Faraday's constant (96486.7 C/mol). When the volume and entropy effects are negligible during the lithiation process, the open circuit voltage (OCV) profile of the LIB could be determined by:¹²,¹⁶

$$
OCV \approx -\frac{E_{Mx_2-B_4N} - E_{Mx_1-B_4N} - (x_2 - x_1)E_M}{(x_2 - x_1)e} \tag{4}
$$

Here, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the number of adsorbed Li atoms. $E_{Mx-B_4N}$ is the total energy of the adsorbed system with $x$ number of Li atoms. $E_M$ is the per atom energy in the bulk Li metal crystal and $e$ is the elementary charge. The electronic charge gain and depletion were estimated by the Hirshfeld charge analysis approach.³⁶⁻³⁸ The systematic difference of charge densities due to the adsorption of an adatom can be described as:³⁴⁻³⁸

$$
\Delta \rho = \rho_{\text{total}} - \rho_{\text{B}_4\text{N}} - \rho_{\text{M}} \tag{5}
$$

Where $\rho_{\text{total}}, \rho_{\text{B}_4\text{N}}$ and $\rho_{\text{M}}$ represent the charge densities of adsorbed substrate, substrate and the adatom, respectively. The temperature-dependent molecular transition rate(D) was investigated by using the Arrhenius equation:²³,³⁹

$$
D \sim \exp(\frac{-E_d}{k_B T}) \tag{6}
$$

Where $E_d, k_B$ and $T$ are the diffusion energy barrier, Boltzmann's constant and temperature, respectively. The binding energy of interlayer is calculated as⁴⁰

$$
E_{\text{be}} = E_n - nE_s \tag{7}
$$

Where $E_n, E_s$ and $n$ are the energy of n layer B₄N, monolayer B₄N and the layer number, respectively.

## 3 Results and discussion

### 3.1 Geometrical structures of monolayer $B_4N$

The optimized structure of a $4 \times 1$ supercell of monolayer $B_4N$ is shown as in Fig. 1a. The monolayer $B_4N$ shows a planar auxetic material with re-entrant structure. The structure exposes $C_{mmm}$ symmetry (space group No. 65) with orthogonal lattice. And the optimized structural parameters are a = 2.98 Å, b = 10.74 Å, and the bond length of B-N is 1.33 Å. There are two types of B-B bond, whose bonds length are 1.67 Å and 1.81 Å, respectively. Fig. 1b shows the band structure of monolayer $B_4N$, in which the band lines travel across its Fermi level, indicating it is a metal. These calculation results are matched well with previous report, $^{20}$ which can be seen in the Table S1. Additionally, cohesive energy is a widely accepted parameter to evaluate the feasibility for experimental synthesis of the 2D material. The calculated cohesive energy of 7.56 eV for monolayer $B_4N$ is smaller than that of grapether (7.70 eV) and graphene (7.91 eV), $^{41}$ but larger than that of the experimentally synthesized 2D $Ti_2C$ (7.37 eV), $^{12,42}$ black phosphorene (3.27 eV) $^{43}$ and $P_2C_3$ (4.60 eV), $^{44}$ indicating that $B_4N$ monolayer contains robust B-N and B-B bonds. The electron localization function (ELF) of $B_4N$ monolayer is also calculated, as shown in Fig. S1a. The high electron localization between the nearest neighboring B atoms also suggests strong bond strengths of the B-B bonds. Also, the $B_4N$ monolayer has been verified to possess a dynamic stability by its phonon spectrum without negative frequency. $^{20}$

### 3.2 Li interaction with monolayer $\mathbf{B_4N}$

To extend our focus on the potential applications as anode material, we first examine the preferred adsorption site for a single Li atom on the monolayer $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ supercell surface. According to the symmetry of the structure, eight adsorption sites are chosen for the initial adsorption simulations. As shown in Fig. 1a, adsorption sites are marked as $\text{H}_1/\text{H}_2$ (hollow site), $\text{B}_1/\text{B}_2/\text{B}_3$ (bridge site), $\text{T}_{\text{B}1}/\text{T}_{\text{B}2}$ and $\text{T}_\text{N}$ (top site), respectively. To be a promising anode material for Li ion batteries, a relatively strong adsorption energy with Li atoms should be obtained. By optimizing the adsorption structures, it is found that all the initial sites have been optimized to the $\text{H}_1$ site (the biggest vacancy site). The optimized distance from Li to the flat surface is $1.30$ Å and to the nearest N atom is $2.00$ Å, and the corresponding adsorption energy ($\text{E}_{\text{ad}}$) is -1.37 eV. As calculated by the same formula (2), we note that the Li adsorption energy on the monolayer $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ surface is slightly smaller than that on the reported anode materials of $\text{MoC}_2$ (-1.73 eV),$^{12}$ $\text{NiC}_3$ (-1.57 eV),$^{42}$ $\beta_{12}$-borophene (-1.76 eV) and $\beta_1^\text{s}$-borophene (-1.62 eV).$^{21-23}$ But it is larger than those of other 2D materials, such as b-SiS (-0.51 eV),$^{45}$ penta-graphene (-0.30 eV),$^{46}$ and BP (-0.20 eV).$^{47}$ It is very close to that value on the $\chi_3$-borophene surface (-1.43eV),$^{21}$ this should be ascribed to the highly similar structure and high percentage of B atoms. The negative $\text{E}_{\text{ad}}$ indicates the metallic atom dispersive distribution that enhances stability against the formation of metallic dendrites. Likewise, the larger negative $\text{E}_{\text{ad}}$ indicates stronger interactions between Li and $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ surface and also expects to achieve high theoretical specific capacity. Interestingly, Li atom on other tried positions is converged to the neighboring $\text{H}_1$ site easily. The $\text{H}_1$ site is a strong adsorption center. For further understand the adsorption properties of Li on $\text{H}_1$ site, charge density difference is calculated and the results are presented in Fig. 2. Clearly, electron accumulation can be observed with notable electron localization around the nearby B and N atoms in monolayer $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ substrate and distinct depletion around Li atoms. Accordingly, Hirshfeld charge analysis shows 0.31 e flowing from Li to the substrate. Unlike Bader charge analysis,$^{48}$ Hirshfeld charge analysis applied molecular electron density with fictitious promolecule rather than electronic typology to define atomic domains. This method usually tends to be small in obtaining the amount of

charge transfer. For example, according to the reported calculations of Hirshfeld charge analysis, the transferred charge from one metal atom to substrate is usually less than 0.5 e. $^{36-38}$ Based on these analysis, our transferred charge might be the electronic origin of the larger negative $E_{ad}$ and could support that the strong interaction between Li and $B_4N$ monolayer.

To find the maximum loading for theoretical specific capacity calculation of the $B_4N$ monolayer, the Li atom is added step by step on the $4 \times 1$ supercell in possible adsorption sites. The configuration with the lowest energy is adopted, then, adding Li atoms continuously. Interestingly, added Li atoms are all optimized to the $H_1$ sites with an increase of Li atoms. For one layer adsorption configuration, shown in Fig. 3, all Li atoms are adsorbed at the $H_1$ sites. There are eight Li atoms that are arranged on one side. From top view, the adsorbed substrate remains as good as it was originally. However, the side view of along x direction shows that some small distortion to the substrate. Mainly, the triangular meshes that formed only by B atoms have moved out of the plane. As shown in Fig. 3b, the thickness of the wrinkle is about 0.56 Å after Li monolayer has been regulated on the monolayer $B_4N$ substrate, which is bigger than the buckling thickness of pristine silicene (0.41 Å) and smaller than that of pristine borophene (0.91 Å).$^{49}$

The distance between the uniformly distributed Li layer and the substrate is 1.25 Å while the nearest Li-Li distance in this configuration is 2.99 Å that is the minimum distance in Li metal. The average adsorption energy per Li for the eight atoms is -1.16 eV, which indicates that Li atoms can be adsorbed stably without clustering. Adding more Li atoms would lead to a second layer of Li to be arose. As shown in Fig. S2 and Fig. S3, deposition of the ninth Li atom would result in a seed for the second layer. After the complete second Li layer is formed, there are sixteen Li atoms on the substrate and the distance between the second Li layer and the substrate is 2.72 Å corresponding to the average $E_{ad}$ is -0.74 eV. Although the structure of monolayer $B_4N$ keeps well and the $E_{ad}$ still could resists agglomeration, this is not expected due to the multilayer of Li is unlikely to form in a bilayer, multilayer or bulk $B_4N$ system. Otherwise, it will lead to very serious volume expansion effects which should pay special attention to avoid in the application processes. Fig. S4 is the charge density difference for eight Li atoms adsorbed on monolayer $B_4N$ substrate. A net gain of charges around the B atoms which close to the Li atoms due to wrinkle effects. The electronegativity of B is larger than that of Li. Hirshfeld charge analysis shows that there is about 0.20 e per Li transfer to the substrate, indicating there are still considerable interactions between them.

For practical applications, specific capacity is one great performance of the anode materials. The adsorptions of one, two or three layers of Li atoms on both sides of the freestanding monolayer were reported elsewhere. $^{11-13,34}$ By this way, the remarkable specific capacity can be obtained naturally for the freestanding monolayer. Actually, it can be considered that the calculation model of multilayer adatoms for the two-sides provides an estimate of the maximum total capacity of the material. Fig. S5 shows the adsorption energy decreases as the number of Li atoms increases in $B_{32}N_8Li_x$. The decreased adsorption energy is attributed to the repulsive interaction between Li atoms, which leads to more difficult for the Li adsorption. When the number of Li atoms is up to 16, corresponding to two layers of Li atoms on the substrate for one side, the adsorption energy is still negative. Therefore, for the freestanding monolayer $B_4N$, according to the above results and formula (3), the specific capacity can be estimated to reach to

$1874.27\ \text{mAhg}^{-1}$ after two layers of Li atoms were soundly adsorbed on each side and the corresponding optimized adsorption structure can be seen in Fig. S6. When only one layer Li atoms was adsorbed on the surface, as shown in Fig. 3, this would lead to a specific capacity of $468.57\ \text{mAhg}^{-1}$. But this value is still bigger than that of the commercialized graphite $(372\ \text{mAhg}^{-1})$ $^{50}$ and $\text{TiO}_2(200\ \text{mAhg}^{-1}),^{51}$ and most of the previously monolayer materials such as phosphorene $(433\ \text{mAhg}^{-1})$, germanene $(369\ \text{mAhg}^{-1})$ and stanene $(226\ \text{mAhg}^{-1}),^{52-53}$ respectively. According to formula (4), the open circuit voltage (OCV) values with the number of Li atoms are calculated and the results are shown in Fig. S5b. OCV values as a function of specific capacity is also presented in Fig. 4a for the double-sided adsorption of 2D $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ monolayer. It is worthy to note that the voltage curve may further drop due to formation of solid electrolyte interface after first cycle in real applications. The low values of OCV mean suitability as an anode materials. Our voltage figure of monolayer $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ for increasing Li atoms is similar to that of $\text{Mo}_2\text{B}_2$ monolayer,$^{11}$ 2D N-Graphdiyne,$^{16}$ and other 2D materials.$^{35}$ To ensure whether the monolayer $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ may not be caused structural damage or cracking during the rechargeable battery application, the AIMD simulation including two layers of Li on each side for 5 ps at temperature 350 K was conducted. The result was shown in Fig. S7, which confirmed that the integral adsorbed configuration was well maintained. It also should be noted that the pristine monolayer $\text{B}_4\text{N}$ had been reported to be stable up to 500 K by the previous investigation.$^{20}$

### 3.3 Li mobility on the monolayer B₄N
Another basic characteristics to evaluate the good performance of the anode material for LIBs is the rate capability, which almost depends on the mobility of Li atoms. This means that the barriers for the Li atoms moving from one stable site to another adjacent stable sites on the surface need to be small or comparable to that of surface on the graphite (about 0.40 eV). The barrier is smaller, the charge/discharge capability is better. The CI-NEB method was used to search the minimum energy pathways, and then the diffusion energy barriers were measured. Fig. 4b illustrates two possible pathways (Path-1 and Path-2) between the diffusion energy barriers and the corresponding neighboring stable sites on the monolayer B₄N surface. Path-1 is set from one H₁ site to another H₁ site by passing over a nitrogen atom on the surface. The energy barrier for Li diffusion along this way is 0.29 eV. Path-2 starts from one H₁ site to another H₁ site by passing over the boron triangle area possesses the relative high energy barrier with value of 0.69 eV for Li diffusion. Usually, the Path-1 is the preferred pathway for Li diffusion on the monolayer B₄N surface due to the lower energy barriers which are smaller than that on graphite. Moreover, the suitable diffusion energy barrier of Li is comparable to that of monolayer BP (0.36 eV),⁴⁷

and monolayer $C_2N$ (0.23 eV),⁵⁴ respectively. Particularly, the lower diffusion barriers of Li on monolayer $B_4N$ is much smaller than that on their similar structure analogues, $\chi_3$-borophene (0.6 eV),⁵¹ which suggests a much faster LIBs charge/discharge speed. According to the equation (6), at 300 K, the transition rate of Li on $B_4N$ surface is estimated to be $1.6×10^5$ times faster than that on $\chi_3$-borophene and 70 times faster than that on graphite. Furthermore, diffusion property of Li transferring through monolayer $B_4N$ was also explored. In Fig. 4b, as shown along the Path-3, the transfer barrier is 1.05 eV. While the transfer barriers for pristine h-BN and graphene were 6.75 eV and 7.92 eV, respectively.⁵⁵ These indicates that Li can diffuse through monolayer $B_4N$ with relatively higher permeability.

### 3.4 Bilayer of $B_4N$, volume expansion and the new adsorption structure

Bilayer structure could provide a model to predict the upper limit of the possible volume expansion as the two layers can move freely when the Li atoms are intercalated. To obtain the minimum energy bilayer stacking, the upper layer is moved relative to the down layer with step by step. For the bilayer $B_4N$, there are four possible stacking configurations, including AA, $AB_1$, $AB_2$, and $AB_3$, as shown in Fig. S8. All bilayer structures are kept planar well after optimization. On the basis of formula (7), the interlayer binding energies of AA, $AB_1$, $AB_2$, and $AB_3$ are -4.99 eV, -5.76 eV, -6.66 eV, and -6.24 eV at the corresponding interlayer distances of 3.13 Å, 3.08 Å, 2.99 Å, and 3.05 Å, respectively. It is found that the $AB_2$ stacking is the most stable stacking. The interlayer spacing of bilayer $B_4N$ is bigger than the equilibrium interlayer distance between blue phosphorene and borophene (2.75 Å). The blue phosphorene and borophene heterostructure was reported as a promising anode materials for LIBs.⁵⁶ However, the interlayer spacing of bilayer $B_4N$ is smaller than that of the most stable AB stacking graphite, which was reported as 3.35 Å.⁵⁷

To observe the volume expansion, Li atoms are intercalated between the two layers of $AB_2$ stacking. Fig. 5 shows the optimized expanding structures after accommodating eight Li atoms which just form single layer on the bilayer $B_4N$ surface. From the different views, the structure of $AB_2$ stacking seems

good and slightly layer shifting is observed along y negative direction when compares it to the original AB₂ stacking as shown in Fig. S8c. Interestingly, from the side view of Fig. 5b, along x direction, cavity-channels are formed by moving some B atoms out the original plane and bond each other to form fences that are noted by yellow in Fig. 5. The connected bond length is about 1.74 Å, being smaller than that of original bond length (1.81 Å) in the monolayer B₄N plane. The thickness of the buckled layer is about 0.99 Å, which is slightly bigger than that of pristine borophene (0.91 Å).⁴⁹ Electron localization function of lithiation of bilayer B₄N is presented in Fig. S1b, in which the densities of electron distribution among the connected bonds indicate strong strength of bonds. We will investigate the new structural characteristics of the bilayer in the next. In the optimized expanding structures, Li atoms still lie on the H₁ sites regularly but near the down layer and slightly far from the up layer due to the repulsive interaction between the Li atoms and nitrogen atoms in the up layer. The interlayer distance between the two layers expands to about 3.35 Å. According to the adsorption on the monolayer B₄N, eight Li atoms are the maximum loading between the bilayer B₄N and then this expansion could be taken as the upper limit to the possible volume expansion of the material. Compare to the pristine bilayer system, a 12% expansion is found, which is similar to that of graphite (10%)⁵⁸ and biphenylene (11%)¹⁸ and better than that of silicene (25%)⁵⁹ and SiC₃ (28%).¹⁹

![](./images/812578050280521728_7.jpg)

Fig. 5 Lithiation structures of bilayer B₄N: (a) Top view and (b) side view. B atoms moved out the original plane and bond each other to form fences that are noted by yellow spheres.

Bilayer structure with cavity-channels, as found in the Fig. 5, could be considered as the result of structural phase transition, which occurs in the adsorption processes of Li. Taking the layered $AB_2$ stacking as the initial structure, we carry out the structural optimization with adsorbed different number of Li atoms to explore the initial structural phase transition. Only one Li atom is added for each structural optimization, which allows us to monitor the change in structure with gradual increases of lithiation. When the number of adsorbed Li atoms reaches to 4, the upper and lower B atoms begin to connect each other. If we continue to increase the number of adsorbed Li atoms, more B atoms are connected between the upper and lower layers, and structural phase transition can be seen. The reason could be ascribe to the strong bonding nature between B and B atoms. On the other hand, B is an electron deficient system and Li is an outer multi-electron system. The multi-electrons of Li can enhance the interaction between upper and lower B atoms, and then induce the structural phase transition. The typical transformed structures can be found in Fig. S9 and Fig. S10.

Surprisingly, we find that this structure is irreversible. We remove all the Li atoms in Fig. 5, and then carry out the structural optimized calculation again. In Fig. S11, the result shows that cavity-channel structure remains completely and the interlayer distance turns to $3.05$ $\text{\AA}$ which is very close to the interlayer distance of the layered $AB_2$ stacking and the cavity-channel structure is more stable than the optimized $AB_2$ layered configuration by 1.41 eV. Interestingly, we do the calculation for the Li atoms are removed from the adsorbed $B_4N$ monolayer. The optimized result shows that the wrinkled monolayer (Fig. 3) convert back to the planer structure. This further proves the triggering effects of Li atoms in the adsorption processes for the structure phase transition in Fig.5. Fig. 6a is the density of state (DOS) of the optimized cavity-channel structure. It still preserves the metallic feature well, which guarantees high electronic conductivity during the following lithiation processes. Based on the optimized cavity-channel bilayer structure (Fig. S11), the intercalation of eight Li atoms (Fig. 5) provides an average adsorption energy of -2.45 eV per Li which ensures this loading is stable and indicates that Li atoms could spontaneously insert into the cavity-channel. The increased adsorption energy may ascribe to the

layer-layer interaction that had been reported elsewhere.¹⁶,¹⁹ Sequentially, we investigate the diffusion properties of Li in the cavity-channel. Two possible diffusion paths and the corresponding energy barriers are shown in Fig. 6b, Path-1 and Path-2 are set as same as the paths on the monolayer B₄N surface. The calculated results indicate that migration energy barrier is 1.31 eV along Path-2, which is much higher than that of Path-2 on the monolayer B₄N surface due to the formed fences of B-B bond. While the diffusion energy barrier of Li along Path-1 is 0.42 eV which slightly larger than that on the graphite. In addition, to study whether the cavity-channel bilayer structure is stable or not with its maximum loading, AIMD calculation was performed at 350 K. Fig. S12 shows the evolved structure after 5 ps. The different views of the figure show that the structure does not change notable with time and that cavity-channel bilayer structure remains stable.

### 3.5 Bulk B₄N and the volume expansion
It may be more general in real applications that the stacking effect of the 2D sheets with binding energy often leads to form layered bulk morphology. Here, we continue to increase the bilayer B₄N to bulk B₄N to further understand expansion and their stability during Li intercalation. AB₂ stacking was used and performed geometry optimized calculations allowing all three direction lattice parameters to vary. The

optimized bulk $B_4N$ is shown in Fig. S13. It can be seen that bulk $B_4N$ has obvious flat layers stack from the side view. The optimized c lattice constant is $5.98$ $\mathring{A}$ which means that the interlayer distance in bulk is approximately equal to that of the pristine bilayer $(2.99$ $\mathring{A})$. To ascertain how the bulk configuration evolution on Li intercalation, the tested maximum loading for bulk $B_4N$ was used. A full loading of eight Li atoms in every layer, sixteen atoms in the corresponding supercell altogether, was introduced. Lattice parameters were still set to allow variation for the configuration optimization. Results were shown in Fig. 7, from the top view, the $AB_2$ stacking keeps good and all the Li atoms are optimized directly above the $H_1$ sites on the $B_4N$ layers. Here c lattice constant is still $5.98$ $\mathring{A}$. It is the same as that of the pristine bulk $B_4N$. However, the cavity-channel is formed too, just like the lithiation of bilayer $B_4N$. From the side views of Fig. 7, the connected bond length of B-B is about $1.84$ $\mathring{A}$ which increases 6% than that of the lithiation bilayer $B_4N$ $(1.74$ $\mathring{A})$. And the thickness of the buckled layer turns to $1.18$ $\mathring{A}$ which increases 19% than that of lithiation bilayer $B_4N$ $(0.99$ $\mathring{A})$. It can be concluded that the expansion effects of Li intercalation in the 3D $B_4N$ are released due to the more curved cavity-channel formation. The strong bonding nature of the lithiation bulk $B_4N$ system is also explicated by electron localization function, which has been shown in Fig. S1c. It is also found that the phase transition mechanism of the bulk structure is similar to that of the bilayer structure. Therefore, the same meaning pictures are no longer put in.

![](./images/812578050280521728_9.jpg)

Fig. 7 (a) Top view and (b) side view of the bulk $B_4N$ configurations with the maximum Li atoms intercalation. B atoms move out the original plane and bond each other to form fences that are noted by yellow spheres.

Worthy to note the structural phase transition caused by lithiation of pristine and layered bulk $B_4N$. Sixteen Li atoms were removed from the cavity-channel structure and the variational cell was set for the geometry optimization. Fig. S14 shows that the cavity-channel remains in the bulk structure. The optimized cavity-channel configuration has c lattice constant of $5.35$ Å which is smaller than that of the pristine layered bulk $B_4N$ and the bulk cavity-channel structure is also more stable than the pristine layered bulk $B_4N$ by $10.04$ eV. These indicate that the layered bulk $B_4N$ would prefer to form as one bulk cavity-channel structure when there encounter the influence of lithiation. Using the bulk cavity-channel structure as the original reference, it could obtain an expansion of $12\%$ after adsorbing sixteen Li atoms in the bulk supercell. This expansion is very close to that of the graphite. For the loading of sixteen Li atoms, an average adsorption energy is $-1.76$ eV which provides the loading could indeed stable and ensures Li atoms could naturally enter the cavity-channel. Furthermore, the diffusion of single Li in the cavity-channel of bulk $B_4N$ is estimated. Considering the barrier of Path-2 in the bilayer $B_4N$ is much higher, only Path-1 is investigated here. As shown in Fig. 8, the calculated energy barrier is $0.33$ eV which is smaller than that on the graphite ($0.40$ eV). To pay further attention on the thermal stability of the fully loading bulk $B_4N$, the structure of Fig. 7 was performed AIMD calculation at $350$ K for $5$ ps. Fig. S15 shows that the structure only slightly changed. Also, according to the formula (3), the cavity-channel of bulk $B_4N$ could support the specific capacity of $468.57$ mAhg⁻¹. Thus, the bulk $B_4N$ is still a promising anode material for Li ion battery.

On the other hand, lithiation bilayer $B_4N$ and bulk $B_4N$ do not form the van der Waals layered structures. Their layers are connected by B-B bonds giving rise to $sp^3$ hybridization. To explore the Li atoms movement, trajectories of Li atoms at 300 K and 400 K are carried out by AIMD calculations. For the $B_4N$ monolayer, single Li atom movement on it for 5000 fs are shown in Fig. S16a. It can be seen that Li atom mainly stays on the $H_1$ site. This further proves that the $H_1$ is a stable position. Single Li atom movement in the bilayer $B_4N$ for 5000 fs are presented in Fig. S16b . The Li atom can move across the connected B-B bonds in the bilayer $B_4N$, which was identified in the above CI-NEB calculations. Trajectories of Li atoms in the bulk $B_4N$ at 300 K and 400 K for 5000ps are similar to that of Li atom movement in the bilayer $B_4N$. These calculations also prove that the monolayer, bilayer and bulk $B_4N$ are stable at 300 K and 400 K.

## 4 Conclusions
In summary, the adsorption structure, diffusion properties, volume expansion, thermal stability and Li-ion storage of monolayer $B_4N$, bilayer $B_4N$ and bulk $B_4N$ have been studied on the basis of first-principles calculations, respectively. Distinct hollow sites ($H_1$) provide large space for free movement and strong

adsorption ability for Li ions, causing monolayer, bilayer, and bulk $B_4N$ to have relatively high theoretical specific capacity and good rate capability. More intriguingly, in the processes of Li intercalation, bilayer $B_4N$ and bulk $B_4N$ changed from layered structure to irreversible cavity-channel structure. The structural phase transition leads to $sp^3$ hybridization and shows the volume expansions small for saturated lithiation bilayer $B_4N$ and bulk $B_4N$. AIMD simulations clearly indicate good thermal stabilities of lithiation monolayer, bilayer, and bulk $B_4N$ at 350 K. Overall, from monolayer to bulk $B_4N$ are novel anode materials for LIBs. Our findings will guide the new impetus for additional experimental and theoretical investigation.
