# TiO₂ Nanocrystal-Framed Li₂TiSiO₅ Platelets for
Low-Voltage Lithium Battery Anode

Di He, Boya Wang, Tianhao Wu, Heng Su, Xu Zhang, Yang Ren, Gui-Liang Xu,
Zhiwei Liu, Jinshu Wang, Khalil Amine, and Haijun Yu*

Titanium-based anode materials are attracting considerable attention for
use in high-performance lithium-ion batteries, but the compromised energy
density caused by high voltage plateaus and unsatisfactory capacities
severely retards their practical applications. Herein, a molten-salt synthesis of
Li₂TiSiO₅ crystalline platelets and a subsequent selective facet modification by
in situ growth of TiO₂ nanocrystal frames are facilely achieved. The discharge
voltage plateau at around 0.5 V renders the Li₂TiSiO₅ anode safe compared
with graphite and confers a high energy density compared with zero-strain
Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ anode. With the optimized size, structure, and content of modified
TiO₂ nanocrystals associated with the exposed (001) plane of Li₂TiSiO₅, the
Li₂TiSiO₅-based anodes can deliver a capacity of above 300 mAh g⁻¹, enhanced
rate performance, and a capacity retention of 66% after 10 000 cycles. In
situ X-ray diffraction and ex situ transmission electron microscopy have
demonstrated the structural stability of the anodes upon charge/discharge.
Further theoretical calculation reveals 3D migration paths of Li⁺ ions in
Li₂TiSiO₅. The selective modification of in situ grown TiO₂ nanocrystals on
certain facets of crystallites opens a new door for the development of electrode
materials possessing superior electrochemical properties.

## 1. Introduction

Energy storage devices are key compo-
nents in electric vehicles and sustainable
energy facilities.[1] Among them, lithium-
ion batteries (LIBs) are the most prom-
ising type, owing to their high energy
density, long life span, and broad appli-
cation.[2,3] As a classic anode material in
LIBs, graphite shares more than 80% of
the global anode market.[4] However, the
discharge plateau of graphite in LIBs is
so low as to approach the plating potential
of lithium (around 0.1 V vs Li⁺/Li), pos-
sibly resulting in safety risks.[5,6] Although
many other anode materials have been
developed, they still suffer from various
drawbacks and lack the needed perfor-
mance balance.[7-12] It is thus important to
design new structures for anode materials
with excellent overall performance for
practical applications.

Titanium-based oxides have been
widely explored as high-performance
anodes for LIBs. However, their energy densities are usually
unsatisfactory, owing to their imperfect capacities or working
potentials. The commercial Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ anode has a relatively
low capacity (Figure 1a), although it offers superior safety and
reversibility.[13-15] The TiO₂ polymorphs anatase and TiO₂-B
possess a higher theoretical capacity of 335 mAh g⁻¹, how-
ever, such a high capacity is very difficult to realize without
critical particle size control.[16,17] As to the working potentials,
most titanium-based oxides (TiO₂, TiNb₂O₇, LiTiₙO₂ₙ₊₁, etc.)
operate close to 1.5 V on average (Figure 1a), which is too high
for high-energy-density anode materials.[18-20] Although cer-
tain titanium-based oxides (e.g., Li₂MTi₃O₈ (M = Mg, Zn) and
MLi₂Ti₆O₁₄ (M = Sr, Ba, 2Na)) exhibit lower discharge poten-
tials, their capacities are inadequate.[21,22] Fortunately, Li₂TiSiO₅
(LTSO) has a reversible capacity of above 240 mAh g⁻¹ with a
reasonable average potential of around 0.5 V,[23,24] making it a
more balanced anode material than graphite and Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ for
practical usage (Figure 1b). LTSO belongs to the space group
P4/nmm, with a tetragonal layered structure resembling the
framework of α-VPO₅ (inset of Figure 1c),[25] which has been
only sporadically used in LIBs or capacitors.[26,27] The optimiza-
tion of LTSO by appropriate structural modification can further
improve its electrochemical performance,[28] a topic requiring
extensive studies.

The design and adjustment of anode-material nanostructures provide opportunities to tailor their properties on demand. For example, the exposure of specific facets of Li-based nanocrystals can be employed to tune the reaction kinetics through the controlled transfer of Li⁺ ions along certain directions.[29,30] Through the introduction of a second structure, such as TiO₂, the Li⁺-ion transfer rate and specific capacity can be greatly enhanced in Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ owing to the existence of grain boundaries.[31,32] Therefore, the design of local structures plays an increasingly significant role in high-performance electrode materials, contributing to the new material design paradigm of architecting crystal domains as functional units.[33,34] The combination of these strategies would facilitate the design of superior electrode materials to create LIBs with unique performance.

Herein, a stable crystalline-platelet LTSO material is developed with (001) plane-preferred exposure through a facile molten-salt route. Furthermore, a "photo frame"-like selective coating of the LTSO platelets with in situ growth of high-capacity TiO₂ nanocrystals was realized by a two-step treatment including hydrothermal and calcination processes. Through the introduction and adjustment of TiO₂ nanocrystals, the resultant materials show significantly enhanced capacities, rate performance, and cyclability. It is believed that TiO₂ nanocrystals could accelerate the Li⁺ ions transfer, fully accommodate one Li⁺ ion per formula, and increase the total capacity through the contribution of capacitive effect. In situ/ex situ analysis of the structural evolution of representative material demonstrates the negligible crystal structure change during electrochemical cycling, suggesting its ultrastability. The Li⁺ ions transfer paths have been calculated by density functional theory (DFT), and the results imply the important role of the platelet design and the selectively exposed facets of the materials.

## 2. Morphology Design and Nanocrystal Adjustment

To efficiently control the crystallinity and oriented growth, the LTSO anode material was synthesized by a facile and scalable molten-salt method (see the Experimental Section). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) investigation of the synthesized LTSO reveals its platelet morphology, with typical side lengths above 4 μm and thickness below 500 nm (Figure S1, Supporting Information). This characteristic morphology of LTSO implies preferred growth along certain directions and exposure of certain facets favored by the molten-salt synthesis. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the LTSO (Figure 1c) indicates a pure structure with no impure phase, in which SiO₄ tetrahedrons are surrounded by TiO₅ pyramids to form a layer along the (001) direction, while Li⁺ ions occupy the distorted octahedral cavities to interlink neighboring [TiSiO₅]²⁺ layers (inset in Figure 1c). The particularly strong (001) peak of LTSO demonstrates its oriented growth, indicating that the crystalline facets of LTSO can be well-controlled by the molten-salt synthesis.

The crystalline structure of the LTSO platelet was examined by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). As shown in Figure 2a, the TEM image captured from the panel of a typical LTSO platelet illustrates a perfect single-crystal structure, with the yellow-marked lattice distances measured to be 0.46 and 0.45 nm, in line with the interplanar distances of the (110) and (1$\overline{1}$0) planes, respectively. The corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Figure 2b) can be indexed into the [001] zone axis, suggesting the (001) crystallographic plane of the platelet panel. The atomic structure of LTSO was further studied by high angle annular darkfield (HAADF) imaging. As shown in Figure 2c, Ti (blue balls) and Si (orange balls) atoms can be well distinguished owing to their different diameters, and alternately locate along the [100] and [010] directions, in good agreement with the structural model in Figure 1c.

Although many approaches have been proposed to modify electrode materials for enhanced performance, the selective modification of certain orientations or crystal facets is still a great challenge. This delicate manipulation could provide anisotropic control over these materials' physical/chemical properties. Toward this goal, a two-step process (hydrothermal reaction and calcination) for in situ modification of TiO₂ nanocrystals, assisted by carbon coating, was used to modify the LTSO platelets. Meanwhile, glucose was used as a carbon source to be polymerized into an outer layer, which was further converted into a conductive carbon coating layer after calcination.$^{[35]}$ We have examined the influence of both the hydrothermal time (t) and calcination temperature (T) on the modification, and the resultant $TiO_{2}$-modified LTSO ($TiO_{2}@LTSO$, or T@L) is referred to as T@Lt(T) for simplicity. Accordingly, the modified LTSO platelet in Figure 2d was first hydrothermally reacted for 5 h and then calcined at $700\ ^{\circ}C$, and thus termed as T@L5(700). T@L5(700) possesses a newly formed 250-nm-thick nanoframe surrounding the platelet, as shown in the schematic diagram in Figure 2d, while the central panel remains as crystalline LTSO, as shown in the inset SAED image. The HRTEM image of the nanoframe in Figure 2e indicates that it is constructed of $TiO_{2}$ nanocrystals with ultrathin outer carbon layers. The content of carbon layers account for no more than 3 wt% of the total, as indicated by the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) result (Figure S2, Supporting Information). It was found that calcination temperature greatly affects the architecture of nanocrystals, yielding integrated multiple nanocrystals $\approx 7$ nm in size at $600\ ^{\circ}C$ (T@L5(600)) and isolated crystals larger than 100 nm in size at $800\ ^{\circ}C$ (T@L5(800)) (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The results demonstrate that the facet-selective modification of LTSO by nanocrystal architecture and the coating with conductive carbon layers can be synchronously achieved and well controlled in a facile two-step process.

To elucidate the chemical structure of the nanocrystals, the elemental distribution of a typical T@L5(700) platelet (Figure 2f) was analyzed by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (Figure 2g-j). It is clearly observed that the contents of elemental Ti and C are higher in the nanoframe than in the inner panel, suggesting the formation of Ti-rich nanocrystals as well as carbon coating layers. In contrast, the Si content is lower in the nanoframe compared with the inner panel, suggesting a deficiency of Si in the nanocrystals. This trend

of elemental distributions is also revealed by the element content-distance plot in Figure 2k, acquired along the blue arrow in Figure 2f. Further identification of these nanocrystals by HRTEM combined with SAED demonstrates the coexistence of two crystalline structures, i.e., anatase (TiO₂-A) (Figure 2l) and TiO₂-B (Figure 2m). Since only glucose and H₂O were added during the hydrothermal process, it can be concluded that TiO₂ nanocrystals were grown in situ from LTSO plate- lets during the two-step treatments. The elemental mappings (Figure 2g-j) suggest that Li⁺ ions as well as Si species may be released from the LTSO edges in the hydrothermal process. In the hydrothermal process, the glucose aqueous underwent complicated chemical reactions and produced a series of com- plex compounds.[³⁵] Considering the glucose decomposition in hot-compressed water, the proposed formation process of TiO₂ nanocrystal may be caused by the corrosion of generated organic acids.[³⁶⁻⁴¹]

We further tuned the fractions of LTSO, TiO₂-A, and TiO₂-B structures by altering the reaction conditions, and a series of T@L materials (T@L3(800), T@L5(600), T@L5(700), T@L5(800), and T@L7(800)) were obtained. The Raman spectra of pure LTSO and the five T@Ls are shown in Figure 3a and Figure S4 (Supporting Information). In contrast to the spec- trum of pure LTSO, with no TiO₂ peaks, T@Ls have character- istic Raman peaks of TiO₂-A and TiO₂-B, in agreement with the TEM results. Specifically, the peaks at 127.0 and 151.5 cm⁻¹ are assigned to the B₉ mode of TiO₂-B and the E₍₁₎ mode of TiO₂-A, respectively, definitely confirming the coexistence of TiO₂-A and TiO₂-B nanocrystals. The blueshifts of the two peaks from the frequencies of the bulk materials (i.e., 121 cm⁻¹ for TiO₂-B and 144 cm⁻¹ for TiO₂-A) can be attributed to the reduced size of the nanocrystals.[⁴²,⁴³] The crystal structures of the T@Ls are revealed by the XRD patterns in Figure 3b. In addition to the dominant LTSO peaks, a small peak at around 25° can be found for all samples, albeit with different intensities; this peak is indexed into the TiO₂ crystal structure. To quantify the com- ponent contents, Rietveld refinement was performed on these materials (Figure 3c for T@L5(700) and Figure S5, Supporting Information, for others), and the quantified percentages of LTSO, TiO₂-A and TiO₂-B are given in Figure 3d. The total frac- tion of TiO₂ structure can be altered over a large range from 3.09% to 15.63%, and the fractions of TiO₂-A and TiO₂-B struc- tures are also variable.

## 3. Electrochemical Performance of T@Ls
The electrochemical performances of the pristine and TiO₂ nanocrystal-modified LTSO anode materials were tested in LIBs. The anodic/cathodic peak pairs at 0.5 V/0.2 V in all cycles suggest the reversible redox reactions of LTSO. The absence of a reversible peak between 0.8 and 2.0 V is in line with the low discharge voltage of LTSO (Figure S6b, Supporting Information). In particular, T@L5(700) and T@L5(800) are able to deliver capacities above $325\ \text{mAh}\ \text{g}^{-1}$, which are very appealing for anode materials. Although $TiO_2$ has a higher capacity than LTSO, its higher working potential may reduce the energy density of T@Ls. The energy density of the representative T@L5(700) is thus compared with LTSO as well as LTO in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). It shows that the energy density of T@L5(700) is significantly higher than that of LTSO and LTO, confirming the advantages of the $TiO_2$ modification. The results demonstrate that prolonged hydrothermal time $t$ or increased calcination temperature $T$ promotes the formation of $TiO_2$, but an excessively large $t$ or $T$ may significantly destroy the LTSO platelets and increase the $TiO_2$ crystal size. As a result, medium values of both $t$ and $T$ are necessary for the optimization of rate performance, as demonstrated by the T@L5(700) anode. Figure 4d shows that the cycle performance of T@L5(700) is significantly better than that of LTSO. T@L5(700) can deliver a high and consistent capacity of around $300\ \text{mAh}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ at $100\ \text{mA}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ for 100 cycles, while LTSO only exhibits a capacity below $200\ \text{mAh}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ with a poor consistency. To further investigate the effect of $TiO_2$ nanocrystal and carbon coating on the electrochemical behavior, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were recorded for T@L5(700) and LTSO as shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information). The smaller semicircle of T@L5(700) compared to pure LTSO indicating faster charge transfer, attributing to the better conductivity of carbon coating and nanosized $TiO_2$ nanocrystals. Moreover, T@L5(700) can undergo highly reversible reactions at high current densities. As seen from Figure 4e, T@L5(700) shows a capacity of $196\ \text{mAh}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ at $500\ \text{mA}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ for 500 cycles, with a capacity retention of 99.4%. To investigate the structure stability, the energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mappings and charge-discharge curves after long cycles were shown in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). After repeated cycles, oxygen-containing thick SEI was formed on particle surface in Figure S10b,c (Supporting Information). In the frame area of T@L5(700), the difference of elemental distribution of Ti and Si in Figure S10d,e (Supporting Information) agrees with the pristine particles indicating the existence of $TiO_2$ nanocrystals and the structural stability. Even at $2000\ \text{mA}\ \text{g}^{-1}$, T@L5(700) shows an initial capacity above $120\ \text{mAh}\ \text{g}^{-1}$, with a capacity retention of 66% after 10 000 cycles, indicating its excellent electrochemical stability. Furthermore, full cell battery with lithium-rich layered oxides as cathode was assembled as shown in Figure S11 (Supporting Information), exhibiting high stability and its potential applications. Overall, the modification of LTSO by $TiO_2$ nanocrystals can not only contribute additional energy density, but also significantly improve the rate performance and cyclability.

## 4. Reaction Mechanism of T@Ls
The electrochemical stability of electrode materials is crucial for the life spans of batteries, which are largely determined by the degree of structural deformation (contraction/expansion) for intercalated materials. To evaluate the structural deformation of T@Ls, in situ XRD was used to investigate the structural transformation of T@L5(700) upon lithiation/delithiation. A 2D contour plot of the XRD patterns during discharge/charge is shown in Figure 5a, the main peaks attributed to the (001), (101), (200), and (201) planes of LTSO can be clearly observed for T@L5(700). An overall view of these peaks during the two discharge-charge cycles shows the reversible change in peak intensity but the continuity of the initial structure. Two typical peaks belonging to the (001) and (101) planes (in the white dashed boxes) were magnified for further analysis. For the discharging stages, the two peaks, especially the one for the (001) plane, shift toward a higher degree, indicating a decrease in lattice distance. At the end of discharging, the intensity of all peaks dramatically decreases owing to the insertion of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions. In the charging stages, the peaks shift back to the lower degree, suggesting the reversibility of the LTSO structure. The deduced lattice parameters in the charge-discharge processes are displayed in Figure 5b; the largest variations along the $a$ axis and $c$ axis are only 0.25% and 0.08%, respectively, while the total volume change is no more than 0.37%, demonstrating the negligible volume change of LTSO.

The possible $\text{Li}^{+}$ insertion sites and corresponding transfer paths in LTSO were further investigated by DFT calculations (Figure 5c). In addition to the original $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions locations in LTSO (4e site), there are another two possible sites that can accommodate $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions including the 2b and 4d sites. As shown in Figure 5c, the migration of $\text{Li}^{+}$ ions follows a 3D channel via $2\text{b}\rightarrow4\text{d}\rightarrow2\text{b}$ sites. The corresponding energy barrier as a function of transfer distance along $2\text{b}\rightarrow4\text{d}\rightarrow2\text{b}$ is plotted in Figure 5d, showing a small value of around 0.36 eV, which favors the $\text{Li}^{+}$ transfer at room temperature. ## 5. Conclusion
In summary, a $Li_2TiSiO_5$ anode material with a crystalline platelet configuration and (001) plane-preferred panel was synthesized by a facile molten-salt method. The LTSO platelets were further selectively coated with in situ grown $TiO_2$ nanocrystals as modified "frames." The size, structure, and content of $TiO_2$ nanocrystals can be effectively tailored by the treatment conditions, which are found to be important in determining the electrochemical performance. A high capacity of above 300 mAh g⁻¹, promising rate performance, and high cycle stability of 10 000 cycles were thus obtained for the optimized anode material. ## 6. Experimental Section
Synthesis of Pure LTSO: Titanium butoxide (TBT, Alfa Aesar, 99%) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, Alfa Aesar, 98%) in a stoichiometric ratio were dissolved in ethanol (>99%) and thoroughly stirred for 10 min in air, during which time 0.1 m stoichiometric LiOH (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) solution (with H₂O as the solvent) was added dropwise. The resultant milky suspension was further stirred for 24 h, and the solvent was then removed by heating at 70 °C in an oven (in air) to yield a dry precursor powder. Then the precursor and salts (NaCl:LiCl = 58.44:42.39 mol%) were ground for 30 min in a molar ratio of 1:10. The mixture was then transferred to an alumina crucible and heated at 700 °C in a muffle furnace for 4 h in air. Finally, the synthesized LTSO platelets were purified by washing the salts away with deionized (DI) water repeatedly until no Cl⁻ ions were detectable by AgNO₃ titration.

Synthesis of TiO₂@Li₂TiSiO₅ (T@Ls): The as-prepared LTSO was added to a 0.05 m glucose (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, 99%, H₂O as the solvent) aqueous solution and dispersed by bath sonication for 30 min. The suspension was then transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and kept at 180 °C for a variable time (t) to allow the hydrothermal reaction to proceed. After reaction, the cooled suspension was centrifuged and the precipitate was washed with water and then ethanol to yield a brown powder. The powder was further calcined for 2 h at different temperatures (T) under Ar/H₂ (5% H₂) protection. A series of T@Ls were synthesized as listed in Table S3 (Supporting Information), which are termed as T@Lt(T) for simplicity.

Characterizations: The XRD patterns were recorded on an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) equipped with a Cu target (Kα, $\lambda = 1.5405$ Å). Rietveld refinements of the XRD results were carried out using Topas software. The SEM images were acquired using a S-8020U system (Hitachi, Japan). The carbon content of T@L5(700) was examined by a thermogravimetric analyzer (DTG-60H, Shimadzu, Japan) with a ramp rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ in air. Raman spectra were obtained on a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw, UK) with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. The TEM investigation was carried out using a Titan ETEM system (Thermo Fisher, USA) equipped with a Cs aberration corrector at 300 kV.

Electrochemical Measurements: To prepare a working electrode, an as-prepared active material (70 wt%), consisting of acetylene black (20 wt%) and polytetrafluoroethylene binder (10 wt%) were mixed well to produce a slurry, which was then rolled and cut into uniform disks ($\varphi = 6$ mm). The disks were then pressed onto Cu grids to serve as the electrodes, in which the loading mass of active material was around 2 mg cm⁻². The electrolyte was a 1 m LiPF₆ solution in a mixed ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate (1:1 volume ratio) solvent. The electrodes were then dried at 90 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven. The electrode, separator (Celgard 2500, USA), and lithium film ($\varphi = 10$ cm) were then assembled in a CR2032 coin cell inside an argon glovebox, and around 50 μL of electrolyte was added.

The in situ battery for XRD detection was assembled in the glovebox; the preparation method for the electrode was the same as described previously, but in a diameter of 12 mm, the loading mass of active material was about 4 mg. A homemade coin cell was used as the battery case and the current density was set at 50 mA g⁻¹.

The full cell was assembled in a coin cell (CR2032) using Li-rich layered oxide (LLOs) as the cathode with a N/P ratio of 1.08. The cutoff voltage ranges from 4.3 to 1.3 V for the first cycle under a current density of 0.1C (1C = 200 mA g⁻¹) and 4.1–1.3 V for the rest of cycles at 1C. The anode was precycled in half cell for two cycles to improve the coulombic efficiency.

The electrochemical performance of cells was evaluated on a Neware testing system, with the operating voltages between 0.1 and 3.0 V. The CV curves of the materials were obtained on a Solartron Metrology system with a scan rate of 0.1 mV s⁻¹. The electrode materials for ex situ TEM investigation were obtained by disassembling cells operated at specific stages inside the glovebox and washed three times with dimethyl carbonate.

Theoretical Calculation: All calculations were performed using DFT, which was implemented in the Vienna ab initio package with pseudopotentials established by the projector-augmented wave and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof methods. The plane wave expansion of the eigenfunctions was calculated with a cutoff energy of 450 eV, whereas the Brillouin zone was set at the Γ-point for a 1a × 1b × 2c supercell. All

structures were relaxed until the self-consistent force was less than $10^{-2}$ eV $\AA^{-1}$ and the energy between two consecutive steps was less than $10^{-5}$ eV. All transition states were computed via the climbing image nudged by the elastic-band method for energy barriers.
