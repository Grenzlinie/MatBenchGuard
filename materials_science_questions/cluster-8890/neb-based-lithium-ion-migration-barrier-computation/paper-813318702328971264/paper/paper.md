# A High-Coincidence Twin Boundary in Lithium Battery Material $\boldsymbol{LiCoO_2}$

Craig A. J. Fisher¹,*, Rong Huang¹,², Taro Hitosugi³, Hiroki Moriwake¹, Akihide Kuwabara¹, Yumi H. Ikuhara¹, Hideki Oki⁴, and Yuichi Ikuhara¹,⁵

⁵Institute of Engineering Innovation, The University of Tokyo, Bunkyo, Tokyo, 113-8656, Japan

A high-coincidence twin boundary observed by scanning transmission electron microscopy in a thin film of $\text{LiCoO}_2$ and characterized using atomistic simulation techniques is reported. The boundary can be described in coincidence site lattice theory as a near-$\Sigma 2$ $(1\bar{1}04)/[4\bar{4}0\bar{1}]$ $\theta=180^\circ$ twist boundary. Using a two-body potential model the grain boundary excess energy was calculated to be very low $(0.09\ \text{Jm}^{-2})$, indicating high stability. Together with the surface energy of a $(1\bar{1}04)$ terminated crystal, this gives a work of cohesion of $1.75\ \text{Jm}^{-2}$, also consistent with a strongly bound interface. Although the layered structure is not drastically perturbed within the vicinity of the symmetrical grain boundary, small changes in the $d$-spacing within 4 to 5 planes $(\sim 0.6\ \text{nm})$ of the interface are associated with large changes in the Li ion migration energies.

**Keywords:** Lithium Cobaltite, Lithium Ion Battery, Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy, Atomistic Simulation.

The capacity, stability and performance of lithium ion batteries depend critically on the microstructures of the component materials.¹ Increasingly the most commonly used cathode material, $\text{LiCoO}_2$, is prepared as nanoparticles, as this shortens lithium diffusion distances and increases contact area between the cathode and liquid electrolyte, resulting in improved charge/discharge rates.¹⁻⁴ To avoid the risk associated with the flammable liquid electrolyte, all-solid-state batteries are also being developed. In this case, the cathode is typically prepared as a thin polycrystalline film.⁵,⁶

In nanomaterials, the interface regions, particularly surfaces and grain boundaries, can have a substantial impact on the material's properties. Here we report the atomic level structure and characterization of a high-coincidence boundary in a thin film of $\text{LiCoO}_2$, based on a combination of scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and atomistic simulation techniques.

An epitaxial thin film of $\text{LiCoO}_2$ approximately 20 nm in thickness was prepared on the (0001) surface of a single-crystal sapphire $(\alpha\text{-Al}_2\text{O}_3)$ substrate by pulsed laser deposition at room temperature and oxygen partial pressure of $10^{-3}$ Torr. An Li-rich target was used to compensate for Li loss during synthesis. The film was then annealed at $600\ ^\circ\text{C}$ in air for 1 hr. A cross-sectional sample for STEM observation was prepared by standard methods. The sample was analyzed using a Cs-corrected STEM (JEM-2100F, JEOL, Co., Tokyo, Japan) operated at 200 kV with a high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) detector. Further details regarding the synthesis⁷ and STEM characterization⁸ methods are available elsewhere.

Of several grain boundaries observed, the twin boundary shown in Figure 1 was the most symmetrical and well-defined, and was found in various places throughout the film. From the electron diffraction and atomic configuration in the STEM image, the zone axis was identified as $[\bar{1}\bar{1}20]/[1\bar{1}20]$, and the interface plane as $(1\bar{1}04)/(\bar{1}104)$. The basal planes of the two crystals meet at an angle of $109.5^\circ$.

Two-body potential parameters taken from Hart and Bates $^{10}$ were used for the simulations. These reproduce the $R\bar{3}m$ unit cell with $c/a=4.99$ reported by Holzapfel et al. $^{11}$ extremely well ($<0.05\%$ error in the hexagonal lattice parameters). All calculations were carried out using the efficient energy minimization techniques embodied in the GULP code. $^{12}$

A structural model of the twin boundary containing 576 atoms was constructed by bisecting a perfect crystal and rotating one half by $180^{\circ}$ about an axis perpendicular to the $(1\bar{1}0\bar{4})$ plane (namely, $[4\bar{4}0\bar{1}]$). This produces a coincident site lattice (CSL) in which the CSL unit cell has almost twice the volume of the original unit cell, corresponding to a near-$\Sigma2$ configuration (Fig. 2). This lowest of all possible $\Sigma$ values (rhombohedral/hexagonal systems permit both odd and even values $^{13}$) is possible because of the particular $c/a$ ratio, and indicates high-coincidence between the two lattices; specifically, one in two lattice points coincide.

Since the STEM image only provides 2D information about the position of the Co atoms, a search for the lowest energy configuration was carried out by systematically translating one crystal relative to the other. This was done in increments of about $0.5$ Å in two orthogonal directions parallel to the interface. In this way a total of over 200 initial configurations were treated and the interface energy for each calculated after minimization. In each case, the simulation box dimensions were held fixed parallel to the $(1\bar{1}0\bar{4})$ interface (to mimic the constraining effect of the bulk in a real crystal), but allowed to vary normal to it, with 3D periodic boundary conditions applied.

The lowest energy structure was confirmed to be that for the symmetrical boundary (no translation) with an interface energy of $0.09$ Jm$^{-2}$; the grain boundary structure after relaxation is shown in Figure 3. Using the same potential model, the energy of the free $(1\bar{1}0\bar{4})$ surface was calculated to be $0.92$ Jm$^{-2}$, giving a work of cohesion for the grain boundary of $1.75$ Jm$^{-2}$. The high cohesion energy is consistent with a strongly bound interface, as expected for such a high-coincidence boundary.

The activation energy for Li migration corresponds to the saddle-point in the energy surface between two Li sites, and was calculated using the Mott-Littleton method¹⁴ by systematically placing an Li ion at positions between vacant Li sites. The structure was then allowed to relax while one of the migrating Li ion's coordinates was held fixed to prevent it sliding back into one of the energy minima at the vacant lattice sites. The highest point along this migration trajectory was taken to be the activation energy for Li ion migration.

Previous simulations using density functional theory (DFT) revealed that in the dilute limit (100% Li content), a Li ion travels in a straight path between neighboring (octahedral) Li sites within 2D layers parallel to the (0001) basal plane.¹⁵ We refer to this mechanism as the octahedral-octahedral (O-O) mechanism. Once two vacancies are present on neighboring sites, however, a Li ion adjacent to both vacancies is able to migrate via an intermediate tetrahedral position with a much lower activation barrier; we refer to this as the octahedral- tetrahedral-octahedral (O-T-O) mechanism. Similar calculations for migration in the bulk using our empirical model produce migration energies of 0.74 eV and 0.23 eV for the O-O and O-T-O mechanisms, respectively, in excellent agreement with the DFT calculations,¹⁵ and consistent with the low activation energy measured for partially delithiated $Li_{1-x}CoO_2$.¹⁶

Migration energies for both O-O and O-T-O mechanisms were calculated at increasing distances from the twin boundary plane. The results indicate that, regardless of the mechanism or pathway, the migration barriers are, on the whole, larger in the vicinity of the grain boundary than in the bulk. The smaller $d$-spacings appear to be correlated with higher activation energies, most probably because of the stronger repulsive forces between atoms in close proximity compared with the bulk, making it harder for Li ions to squeeze through. Given the relatively minor disturbance to the crystal structure near this high-coincidence boundary, we postu- late that more general or random grain boundaries will present even higher Li migration barriers and poorer conductivities on account of the greater disruption to the energy surface through which lithium ions must pass. On the other hand, grain boundaries with larger $d$-spacings than the bulk may make it easier for Li ions to be transported at the boundary. Such effects warrant further investigation as they will directly impact the power densities achievable in Li ion batteries. An optimum parti- cle size that minimizes interface resistance while keeping

Li diffusion lengths to a minimum would be the ideal sit- uation; both experimental and theoretical techniques will be needed to achieve this as part of the continuing effort to develop improved $LiCoO_2$-based cathodes.
