## ABSTRACT

Using first principles calculations, we have investigated the electronic structure of $\text{Pb(ZrTiNb)O}_3$ (PZTN), a system with a low leakage current and high reliability in thin films. We proposed that in PZTN, the oxygen vacancy is suppressed due to the addition of a Nb atom at the $B$ site and that this change prevents a bandgap narrowing which would enhance the leakage current. The oxygen vacancy in the perovskite structure reduces the bandgap because it lowers the $d$ orbital energy of the nearest-neighbor transition metal through the Madelung potential; this bandgap narrowing induces the leakage current in conventional $\text{Pb(ZrTi)O}_3$ (PZT) systems with the Shottky type Pb-O deficit. In contrast, the PZTN systems, which also have the Pb deficit but lack the oxygen vacancy, can maintain the bandgap, and attain a low leakage current.

## INTRODUCTION

Perovskite-type oxides, represented by the chemical composition $AB\text{O}_3$, are widely used as piezoelectric and ferroelectric thin-film devices. In manufacturing oxide thin-films, the most important factor for reliability is the suppression of leakage current. Recently, Kijima *et al* [1] introduced a new material which achieves a very low leakage current, a factor of $10^{-4}$ lower than those of conventional $\text{Pb(ZrTi)O}_3$ (PZT) systems, and designated these systems as $\text{Pb(ZrTiNb)O}_3$ (PZTN) systems. In their work, they substitute 20% to 25% Nb for the $B$ site atom and use a few mol percent of Si in the sintering process. Their leakage mode is of the Shottky type and the Fowler-Nordheim (F-N) tunneling mode is well suppressed.[1] Since this new material also appears highly reliable in fatigue and imprint tests, Kijima *et. al.* suggest that the low leakage current in their PZTN systems is related to this high reliability. They also suppose that the effect of doped Si is to lower the annealing temperature and thus to enable the substitution of 20% to 25% Nb for the $B$ site atom.

The idea of Nb substitution at $B$ site for thin film has already been realized by Sadashivan *et. al.* to compensate the oxygen vacancy[2]. Sadashivan succeeded in substituting 4% Nb for the $B$ site atom. In their paper they qualitatively discussed the relationship between the carrier emission mode and the leakage current at electrode interface.

In this paper we will investigate the electronic structure of PZTN and compare it with that of a conventional PZT by substituting Nb for the $B$-site atom and by subtracting from the $A$ site Pb and/or oxygen. Considering these electronic structures, we will discuss the mechanism of the low leakage current in PZTN systems. Finally, we will examine the energy stability of the PZTN through the defect-formation energy.

### METHODS

To obtain the electronic structure of the oxides, we adopt the density functional theory (DFT) [3] within the local density approximation (LDA). We solve one-electron Kohn-Sham equations self-consistently using the full-potential-linear-augmented-plane-wave (FLAPW) method [4] in a scalar-relativistic fashion. Self-consistency is achieved by the linear tetrahedron method. The Pb(5d,6s,6p), Ti(3s,3p,3d), Zr(4s,4p,4d), Nb(4s,4p,4d), and O(2s,2p) states serve to augment the valence states. We assume a cubic structure with (2x2x2) $ABO_3$ units in a supercell in which the internal atom positions are fixed at neutral positions corresponding to those found in paraelectric states. The lattice constants are optimized so as to minimize the total energy; our energy cut off is 204eV. We also assume that the oxygen vacancy is located at the site nearest to both the Pb deficit site and the Zr site because this placement of atoms gives the lowest total energy. Because of its lower doping ratio in Kijima's experiment compared to the amount of Nb doping, Si is neglected. Our assumption that the doped Nb localizes at the $B$ site is based on the result of a previous Raman experiment on the PZTN system.[1] Further, in the following discussion, we obtain the energy bandgap (Eg) using the DFT. Though the DFT always underestimates the Eg, the relative behavior of the calculated bandgap is deemed to be reliable.[5]

### RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

#### Electronic structure

Figure 1 shows the density of states (DOS) of the PZT and PZTN. The assumed $B$-site atom positions for PZT are as follows: 4 out of 6 Ti atoms line up in an arrangement of straight lines that resemble a checkerboard when viewed from above, and the remaining 2 Ti atoms and the Zr locate at each diagonal site in the (2x2x2) supercell. In the assumed $B$-site atom positions for PZTN, the Ti arranges itself in a straight line that resemble a checkerboard when viewed from above, and the Zr and Nb locate at each diagonal site in the (2x2x2) super cell.

Figure 1(a) is the DOS of the $Pb(Zr_{0.25}Ti_{0.75})O_3$ which has no atom deficit. The top of the valence band consists of oxygen $2p$ orbitals and the bottom of the conduction band is made up of Ti $3d$ orbitals. The center of mass of the Zr $4d$ states is found at a position about 5eV higher than that of the Ti $3d$ because, in atom states, the energy level of the Zr $4d$ is higher than that of the Ti $3d$ .[6] This is the reason why, in PZT, the conduction band bottom consists of Ti $3d$ orbitals. Figure 1(b) depicts the case in which Nb replaced 25% of the Ti (a 1/4 substitution at the $B$ site in a supercell). The position of the Fermi level indicates that electrons are doped at the bottom of the conduction band, and area of the DOS at the Fermi level contains the same number of excess electrons as those brought into the system by a Nb atom compared to a Ti. This situation corresponds to an ion model in which Nb and Ti can be expressed as a 5+ and 4+ ion, respectively, in this oxide system. Figure 1(c) shows the DOS of PZT which has a Pb deficit of 12.5% (a 1/8 deficit at the $A$ site in a super cell). The holes are doped at the top of the valence band. The number of holes is the same as that of the lost valence electrons accompanying the Pb deficit. This corresponds to an ion model in which Pb acts as a 2+ ion in this oxide system.

Figure. 1(d) is the DOS of PZTN in which we substituted 25% Ti for Nb and, at the same time, created a 12.5% deficit at the Pb site. In this case, the doped electron and hole cancel out each other, and the Fermi surface disappears, i.e., an insulator state revives. The bottom of the conduction band is comprised of both Ti and Nb $d$ orbitals. Comparing these results with those

in Fig 1(a) and (d), we can recognize that the electronic structures of PZT and PZTN are almost the same. Thus, the operation including the Nb substitution and the Pb subtraction for the PZTN induces rigid-band-like changes in the PZT. Notably, in the PZTN case, the system clearly maintained a band gap relative to that of the PZT. The stability of PZTN, which has a Pb deficit without an oxygen vacancy, will be discussed in the next section.

In contrast, Fig.1(e) shows the DOS of PZT with a Pb deficit of 12.5% accompanying a nearest-neighbor oxygen deficit. This is just the Shottky deficit. Here, remarkable impurity states appear at the bottom of the conduction band; however, the material remains an insulator. Such impurity states narrow the bandgap drastically compared to the cases shown in Figures 1(a) and (d). These states consist of $d$ states of transition metal atoms which are the nearest neighbor to the oxygen vacancy, in this case, Zr $4d$.

We plot the relationship between the amount of the Pb deficit and the $\delta$Eg (bandgap difference) in Fig. 2. In the PZT with the Pb-O deficit (Fig.1(e) case), the Eg is narrowed by nearly 1.2eV compared to the original PZT; however, in the PZTN (Fig.1(d) case), the Eg is narrowed by only 0.2eV, even it loses same amount of Pb. The bandgap narrowing of the PZT comes from the oxygen vacancy, since that oxygen vacancy lowers the $d$ orbital energy of the nearest-neighbor transition metal through the Madelung potential.

A wide bandgap is the necessary condition for a good insulator; however, the band offset between the electrode and the insulator determines the leakage current.[7] The reason why the PZTN has a low leakage current is that it avoids the bandgap narrowing resulting from an oxygen vacancy. Thus, it has a large enough band offset to maintain the Shottky leakage mode with a low leakage current. In contrast, the stoichiometry of the conventional PZT is off because the high Pb vapor pressure induces a Pb-O Shottky deficit. Such a deficit is especially likely to occur at the interface with the electrodes. In the conventional PZT, the oxygen vacancy narrows the band gap as seen in Figure 2, and reduces the band offset at the electrode interfaces. In this situation, the leakage mode is no longer the Shottky one, but an F-N tunneling producing the large leakage current.

### Defect formation energy

Next, we shall discuss the energy stability of the PZTN using the defect formation energy ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}$) as compared with the PZT ($\mathrm{Pb(Zr_{0.5}Ti_{0.5})O_3}$). Figures 3(a) and (b) show an $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}$ diagram regarding the Pb and/or oxygen deficit for the PZT and PZTN, respectively. To obtain the $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}$ of the Pb deficit, we used the optimized total energy of metal fcc-Pb states, and for that of the Pb-O pair deficit, we used the optimized total energy of PbO in the B10 structural form (P4/nmm). Again, in our calculation, we optimized the lattice constants of a superlattice; however, the internal atom positions are fixed at the neutral ones as an approximation. The assumed $B$-site atom positions for the PZT are as follows: the Ti and Zr atoms both line up in an arrangement of straight lines that resemble a checkerboard when viewed from above. Those of the PZTN are the same as in the previous sections.

In Fig. 3(a), the most stable state is that of the PZT with no deficit (a-I). In the ground state calculation, it is thought to be very natural that the stoichiometric state has a lower energy than the defect states. However, under the condition of Pb evaporation due to high vapor pressure, we should use the a-II state as a starting point. The arrow indicates that, with oxygen loss, the a-II state easily changes to the a-III state. Under a condition such that Pb easily evaporates, the a-III state, in which the Pb-O pair deficit occurs, is the most stable state in this diagram, and it forms solely a Shottky deficit in the PZT.

Figure 3(b) suggests that the b-II is the most stable state in this diagram. We suppose that this b-II corresponds to the state of PZTN reported by Kijima. The b-I state in which there is no deficit is the metallic state and it has a 4.41 eV/supercell higher energy than the b-II. Subtracting Pb from the b-I state, we obtain the b-II as the most stable state. Figure 3(b) shows that the subtraction of Pb or oxygen from the b-II increases the energy of the system to levels like those of the b-III, b-IV and b-V states. Thus, from this calculation, we can conclude that, in the PZTN, the oxygen vacancy is suppressed due to the addition of a Nb atom at the $B$ site even as a Pb atom is lost. Evidently, we can avoid an oxygen vacancy by doping with a Nb atom; however, we cannot avoid Pb evaporation in the thin-film fabrication process.

From the experimental view point, to introduce Nb at the $B$-site we need to suppress the reaction routes in Figure 3 that form Nb oxides and other pyrochlore structures. We propose that, in Kijima's experiment, adding Si may act to suppress these reaction paths by allowing a lower fabrication temperature.

In the discussion of this energy diagram, it should be noted that the entropy contribution coming from defects can be considered negligible because it is sufficiently small compared to the $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}$. To determine the $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}$ more exactly, we have to examine various defect positions by relaxation of internal atom positions in a much larger supercell.

## CONCLUSIONS

We have investigated the electronic structure of PZTN using first-principles calculations. We proposed that in PZTN the oxygen vacancy is suppressed due to the addition of a Nb atom at the $B$ site and that this change prevents the bandgap narrowing which enhances the leakage current. The oxygen vacancy in the perovskite structure reduces the bandgap because it lowers the $d$ orbital energy of the nearest-neighbor transition metal through the Madelung potential; this bandgap narrowing induces the leakage current in conventional PZT systems with the Shottky type Pb-O deficit. In contrast, the PZTN systems, which also have a Pb deficit but lack an oxygen vacancy, can maintain the bandgap, and achieve a low leakage current.
