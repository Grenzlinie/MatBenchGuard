# The Influence of Defects on Mo-Doped TiO₂ by First- Principles Studies

Xiaohui Yu, Tingjun Hou, Xuhui Sun,* and Youyong Li*[a]

TiO₂ doped with transition metals shows improved photocatalytic efficiency. Herein the electronic and optical properties of Mo-doped TiO₂ with defects are investigated by DFT calculations. For both rutile and anatase phases of TiO₂, the bandgap decreases continuously with increasing Mo doping level. The 4d electrons of Mo introduce localized states into the forbidden band of TiO₂, and this shifts the absorption edge into the visible-light region and enhances the photocatalytic activity. Since defects are universally distributed in TiO₂ or doped TiO₂, the effect of oxygen deficiency due to oxygen vacancies or interstitial Mo atoms is systemically studied. Oxygen vacancies associated with the Mo dopant atoms or interstitial Mo will reduce the spin polarization and magnetic moment of Mo-doped TiO₂. Moreover, oxygen deficiency has a negative impact on the improved photocatalytic activity of Mo-doped TiO₂. The current results indicate that substitutional Mo, interstitial Mo, and oxygen vacancy have different impacts on the electronic/optical properties of TiO₂ and are suited to different applications.

## 1. Introduction

Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) is one of the most efficient photocatalysts among the semiconductors due to its high catalytic activity, long lifetime of photogenerated carriers, high chemical stability, and nontoxicity.[1–3] However, the photocatalytic activity of TiO₂ is seriously limited by its intrinsic bandgap (3.0 eV for rutile phase[4] and 3.2 eV for anatase phase),[5] which allows only a small portion of the solar spectrum in the UV region (<380 nm) to be absorbed. Effective utilization of visible light is therefore very important for improving the photocatalytic performance of TiO₂.

To address the UV limitation, great efforts have been made to modify the electronic structure of TiO₂ by doping it with various transition metal[6–10] and nonmetal atoms[11–18] to shift its absorption edge toward the visible region. Theoretical investigations have also been carried out to study the energy band structures of TiO₂ phases including rutile[19–25] and anatase.[26–30] Rutile and anatase, which are the two stable phases of TiO₂, exhibit a direct and indirect band structure, respectively.

Defects may play an important role in applications of TiO₂ by altering the electronic and optical properties. It is well known that oxygen defects will lead to an unpaired electron in the primitive cell, and in the absence of additional defects will turn the material into a conductor. Mo has been used to dope TiO₂ for photocatalytic application, and increases the photocatalytic activity of TiO₂ in the visible region.[31–39] The ionic radius of Mo⁴⁺ is similar to that of Ti⁴⁺, and the four ionization potentials of Mo and Ti are close to each other. Thus, TiO₂ should be easily doped with Mo⁴⁺, and at a certain doping level the material will exhibit a semiconductor-to-metal transition. However, until now, little theoretical effort has been made to study the electronic and optical properties of Mo-doped TiO₂ with oxygen deficiency.

Here we study Mo-doped TiO₂ with oxygen deficiency by using DFT calculations to investigate the influence of Mo doping and defects on the energetic, electronic-structure, and optical properties of both the rutile and anatase phases of TiO₂. The results suggest that it is important to introduce Mo at appropriate lattice sites, and thereby achieve significant redshift in energy gap. The phenomenon of spin polarization associated with doping and oxygen deficiency are also investigated herein.

## Calculation Methods and Models

Computational Details: Calculations were performed by using the plane-wave pseudopotential method as implemented in the CASTEP code.[40] The exchange and correlation interactions were modeled by using the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) of Wu and Cohen.[41] The electron wave function was expanded in plane waves with a cutoff energy of 380 eV. This value was chosen after a full convergence study from 100 to 600 eV. The electronic energy of the rutile and anatase unit cell models shown in Figure 2a falls by less than 1 kJ mol⁻¹ between a cutoff of 380 and 600 eV. For the smallest rutile and anatase supercells, a 7×7×3 Monkhorst–Pack grid[42] was used for irreducible Brillouin zone sampling. For the larger supercells, a 3×3×3 grid was used. Density functional theory is known to usually underestimate bandgaps by up to 50% for oxide semiconductors.⁽⁴³⁾ The DFT + U method⁽⁴⁴⁾ introduces an effective Hubbard $U$ value to improve the estimation of bandgaps by accounting for the d electrons of transition metals. We find that the relationship between bandgap and the Hubbard $U$ potential is dependent on the crystal phase (Figure 1). The bandgap is closest to the experimental value $U$ ≈ 8 eV. This is a large value and implies that there is some physical process that cannot be explained by adding a fixed potential to the titanium d electrons. In addition, accurate experimental measurements of the bandgaps of Mo-doped TiO₂ are not available. Here we are interested in the effects introduced by Mo doping and defects. Thus, we used the DFT method instead of DFT + U for our calculations, which is consistent with our previous DFT study for doped ZnO.⁽⁴⁵⁾ In addition, we performed PBE0 single-point energy calculations and obtained similar conclusions to the GGA results (see the Supporting Information for details.)

Model Structures: The standard-size rutile and anatase supercells (Ti₁₆O₃₂) were used in all calculations, in order to minimize the errors due to interaction of defects. We also investigated a larger supercell (Ti₃₂O₆₄), but it takes a long time to relax the geometry and only reduced the interaction between defects by $12\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. Thus, we used the Ti₁₆O₃₂ supercell in all calculations for parallel comparison.

The defect structures investigated here are shown in Figure 2b. The defect structures can be categorized into three types: Mo substitution of Ti, oxygen defects, and interstitial Mo defects. Mo substitution of Ti sites involves either direct substitution of a Ti atom by an Mo atom (rutile model 1 and anatase model 1) or double substitution of two Ti atoms (rutile model 2 and anatase models 2–3). Oxygen defect structures include an oxygen vacancy between the double substitutions of two Ti atoms (rutile model 3 and anatase models 4–5). Interstitial Mo defects can be treated as transition states between adjacent sites in rutile and anatase TiO₂ (rutile model 4 and anatase models 6–7). Oxygen and interstitial Mo defects can be referred to as oxygen deficiency.

First, the formation energy for substitutionally Mo-doped rutile supercells is more negative than that for the pure rutile phase, which is consistent with the fact that the oxygen affinity of Mo is higher than that of Ti. The measured standard energy of formation for $\text{MoO}_2$ is $-3.4419$ eV/atom and $-3.1358$ eV/atom for rutile $\text{TiO}_2$ (the experimental value is $3.2\pm0.27$ eV/atom). Therefore, the formation energy of doped supercells is more negative than that of pure rutile $\text{TiO}_2$. This corresponds to the fact that the Mo-doped rutile supercell is more stable than rutile $\text{TiO}_2$. However, with oxygen deficiency, the formation energy of the Mo-doped rutile supercell increases, which is also dependent on the supercell size and associated defect (oxygen vacancy or interstitial Mo atom). On the other hand, according to the data summarized in Table 1, the formation energy of Mo-doped anatase supercell is less negative than that of the pure anatase phase. This is in contrast to rutile, in that the substitutionally Mo doped anatase state is less thermodynamically favored than the pure phase, and suggests lower stability of the doped anatase phase. The essential difference is that anatase phase is metastable in nature, and the distortion is greater in anatase than in rutile. Similar to the case of rutile, the formation energy of such defect structural configurations increases with increasing oxygen deficiency, too, and this suggests an unfavorable role for oxygen vacancies or interstitial Mo atoms.

### 2.2.2. Anatase Phase
The DOS and PDOS for Mo-doped anatase TiO₂ are shown in Figure 6. Unlike rutile, pure anatase has an indirect gap with the top of the valence band at M and the bottom of the conduction band at G, and the calculated bandgap is 2.12 eV. This is in agreement with previous investigations.²⁶,²⁷,²⁹,³⁰ The bottom of the conduction band is dictated by the Ti 3d electron states and the top of the valence band is dominated by the oxygen 2p electron states (Figure 6a). Oxygen defects were also considered in anatase phase; they lead to unpaired electrons in the crystal structure, which turn the material into a conductor. This effect was observed by many experimental studies.⁴⁷

Without oxygen defects, spin polarization analysis was carried out for Mo-doped anatase. For the doped system, the intermediate bands are located close to and mixed with the bottom of the conduction band in the spin-polarized condition, and the Fermi level shifts into the conduction band, showing typical n-type metallic behavior. The up and down spins are evidently differentiated and induce intermediate bands (Figures 6b–d). This suggests that the intermediate bands are dictated by the up spins of Mo 4d states. Similar to Mo-doped rutile, substitutional Mo causes effective reduction of the bandgap. Increasing Mo doping level makes the bandgaps continuously decrease. Additionally, the considerable spin polarization due to Mo in anatase originates from the Mo–O bonds as well, leading to potential spintronics applications. As mentioned above, spin polarization is asso- ciated with Mo-O bonds, especially the nearest Mo-O bond for substitutional Mo. Such strong bonding is not associated with the interstitial Mo atom.

### 2.3. Optical Properties
We calculated the optical properties on the basis of the dielec- tric function. The imaginary part $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ is calculated directly from the full many-electron wave function, and the real part $\varepsilon_{1}(\omega)$ is then determined via a Kramers-Kronig transform. The other optical spectra can be gained from $\varepsilon_{1}(\omega)$ and $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$, for example, absorption coefficient $I(\omega)$. The optical properties are determined by the bandgap, and so underestimation by DFT will lead to significant errors. These errors can be removed by correcting the gaps. Here we focus on the impact introduced by doping and defects and did not make corrections for the bandgaps. Our results show that optical properties corre- sponding to different directions are similar. Here we mainly consider the components of the optical properties correspond- ing to the polarization vectors perpendicular to the c axis. Figure 8 shows the absorption coefficient spectrum of the Mo- doped TiO₂ systems with different configurations. For both rutile and anatase, the absorption regions are quite wide and the main absorption part is still located in the UV region. Com- pared with pure TiO₂, Mo doping leads to an enhanced absorp- tion coefficient across the visible range, which is due to the transition of the Mo 4d impurity band into the conduction band. Hence, Mo-doped rutile is more important than anatase in photocatalytic applications.

## Conclusions
We have performed first-principles calculations to investigate the electronic and optical properties of Mo-doped TiO₂ with defects. For both rutile and anatase phases, substitutional Mo doping is effective in reducing the bandgap of TiO₂ and the redshift is significantly enhanced through increasing Mo concentration. Moreover, in contrast with rutile, Mo-doped anatase phase is less thermodynamically stable than the pure phase, since anatase phase is metastable, and doped rutile phase is more important for photocatalysis applications. Intermediate energy bands are introduced into the forbidden bands, due to the spin states of the Mo 4d electrons, which dominate the effects on bandgap reduction. Spin polarization occurs for substitutional Mo doping at Ti sites for both rutile and anatase phases, and it originates from the Mo–O bonds. However, little spin polarization is associated with interstitial Mo atoms. Doping TiO₂ with Mo at Ti sites may be useful for spintronics applications. Oxygen vacancies associated with the substitutional Mo atoms will reduce the magnetic moment, and also decrease the intensity of the absorption coefficient of Mo-doped systems in the visible range. Localized levels are associated with defects, including oxygen vacancies and interstitial Mo atoms. These defects are not beneficial for the photocatalytic activity of TiO₂. Thus for photocatalytic applications, oxygen deficiency should be avoided. Thermal treatment in an oxygen environment will be helpful for photocatalytic applications of TiO₂.
