Effective electron tunneling mass $m_{tunnel}$ is extracted from trap-assisted leakage in Sr-rich strontium titanate and rutile titanium oxide films in metal–insulator–metal (MIM) capacitors and compared with theoretical values obtained from first principles calculations of the imaginary band structure. Optimum orientations of films and stoichiometry impacting $m_{tunnel}$ are also discussed. Because future vertical DRAM integration schemes also stipulate maximum thickness of the MIM capacitor, $m_{tunnel}$ is shown to be a critical parameter influencing intrinsic leakage and potentially limiting further scaling. © 2013 American Vacuum Society. [http://dx.doi.org/10.1116/1.4767125]

## I. INTRODUCTION

In the near future, the semiconductor industry will require back-end metal–insulator–metal (MIM) DRAM capacitors with the specific capacitance corresponding to the equivalent SiO₂ thickness (EOT) in the 0.3–0.4 nm range, requiring adequately high permittivity $\varepsilon$ dielectrics.¹² These capacitors will also have to conform to stringent leakage requirements of $10^{-7}$ A cm⁻² at $\sim$1 V (determined as the operating voltage plus guard band). Moreover, the dielectrics will need to be grown using highly conformal deposition techniques, such as atomic layer deposition. Finally, the choice of electrode material should be economical and semiconductor processing friendly.

While the leakage is at present typically determined by conducting defects,³ for prudent material choices for future generations of MIM capacitors it is essential to look ahead to understand the *ultimate scaling limits*. Specifically, the leakage will be limited by the intrinsic direct-tunneling mechanism, which in turn depends on (1) dielectric thickness $t_{ox}$, (2) metal/dielectric barrier $\Phi_{b}$, and (3) effective tunneling mass $m_{tunnel}$.⁴

In this paper, we focus on the latter parameter for dielectric film candidates titanium oxide (TiO) and strontium titanate (STO) currently under development. We first use the nonideal property of our existing films, namely the trap-assisted leakage, to extract $m_{tunnel}$. This is done by using the trap depth $\Phi_{trap}$ obtained from the temperature-dependent high temperature regime (Sec. III)⁵ to extract $m_{tunnel}$ from the temperature-independent low temperature regime, which generally depends on the product of $m_{tunnel}$ and $\Phi_{trap}$ (Secs. IV and V). We then perform first principles calculations of the imaginary band (IB) structure of both materials, extract the theoretical $m_{tunnel}$, and compare with the experimental values (Sec. VI). Finally, we investigate the interplay of $m_{tunnel}$ with the other abovementioned parameters $t_{ox}$ and $\Phi_{b}$ needed to meet the ultimate requirements of future DRAM MIM capacitors (Sec. VII). Specifically, because the future DRAM integration schemes stipulate *maximum* $t_{ox}$, scaling may not be limited by the ability to achieve sufficiently high dielectric permittivity $\varepsilon$, but sufficiently high $m_{tunnel}$.

## II. EXPERIMENT

TiO and STO films were deposited on 5 nm Ru/10 nm TiN/Si(100) 300 mm diameter wafers. Prior to atomic layer deposition, some of the Ru substrates were oxidized in an O₂ flow for 1 min. TiO and STO films were deposited at 250 °C from metal precursors Ti(OCH₃)₄ and Sr(ᵗBu₃Cp)₂ and, respectively, O₃ and H₂O as oxidants. The as-deposited STO dielectric stack consisted of 1 nm (H₂O based TiO₂)/9 nm Sr-rich STO (62% Sr). The as-deposited films were in amorphous state and required a 600 °C postdeposition anneal to crystallize them. During the crystallization anneal, the TiO film crystallized into the rutile phase, while the Sr-rich STO intermixed with the TiO₂ underlayer resulting in a Ti richer STO film with a composition of $\sim$54% Sr and perovskite structure.⁶ The resulting orientation was (110) for both materials and the dielectric film physical thicknesses were $\sim$12 and $\sim$9 nm and relative dielectric permittivities $\varepsilon \sim 80$ and $\varepsilon \sim 90$ for the rutile TiO (r-TiO) and STO, respectively.

Similar films have been previously characterized by inter- nal photoemission (IPE) and photoconductivity (PC) meas- urements. IPE measurements have determined the metal Fermi level to be pinned at midgap for both STO and $r$-TiO, i.e., $\Phi_{b} \sim 1.5\,\text{eV}$, independently of the electrode metal used. $^{3}$ PC measurements yielded band gap values $E_{g}$ for both mate- rials and in thick STO films have shown a defect level tail extending $0.8$-$1.0\,\text{eV}$ below the conduction band minimum (CBM, cf. Fig. 1). $^{5}$ These parameters are summarized in Table I.

Electrical characterization was performed in a liquid nitrogen-cooled cryostat from $80\,\text{K}$ up. Each capacitor was measured only once, from $0$ to either positive or negative gate (i.e., top electrode) biases $V_{G}$. Only $V_{G}>0\,\text{V}$ leakage, i.e., bottom electrode injection, is discussed here. Current density $J_{G}$ was checked to be area-independent, i.e., perime- ter leakage was ruled out.

### III. LEAKAGE: HIGH TEMPERATURE REGIME

We have previously shown that at temperatures $T>\sim 150\,\text{K}$ the leakage current in our STO films is strongly temperature activated, with the typical activation energies for STO were extracted in the $0.9$-$1.0\,\text{eV}$ range. $^{3,5}$ This value excellently matches the PC signal tail observed below band gap of STO (discussed above), strongly suggesting that the leakage in our nonoptimized films is controlled by *bulk* defects and is thus very well described by the Poole–Frenkel mechanism (cf. Fig. 1) in the form

$$
J_{G} \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\Phi_{trap}}{k_{B}T}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\beta_{PF}}{k_{B}T}E_{ox}^{1/2}\right)E_{ox},
\tag{1}
$$

where $k_{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $E_{ox}$ is the electric field in the dielectric, calculated as $V_{G}/t_{ox}$. Some fixed charge in the dielectric was deduced from C–V curves (not shown here), but was disregarded in further analysis. Lastly,

where $q$ is the elementary charge, and $\varepsilon_{0}$ and $\varepsilon_{d}$ are, respec- tively, the vacuum and the dynamic (optical) relative dielec- tric constants. The $T$-dependent STO $J_{G}$–$V_{G}$ curves fitted with Eq. (1) are shown in Fig. 2.

Although the $T$-dependent $J_{G}$–$V_{G}$ could be also fitted with an analytically similar formula corresponding to the Schottky emission, this mechanism has been ruled out for the following reasons: (1) The extracted current leakage acti- vation energies do not correspond to the $\sim 1.5\,\text{eV}$ barrier observed by IPE, expected for this electrode-limited Schottky mechanism; and (2) the values of $\varepsilon_{d}$ extracted using the Schottky mechanism are very low, typically below $2$, and thus inconsistent with the high $\varepsilon$ values of these films.

In $r$-TiO, a similar considerations have resulted in $\Phi_{trap} \sim 1.2$-$1.4\,\text{eV}$ below the CBM (Table I).

### IV. LEAKAGE: LOW TEMPERATURE REGIME

Below $\sim 150\,\text{K}$ the leakage has been found to be independ- ent of $T$ (Fig. 3), consistent with a *tunneling* mechanism.

![](./images/813266449018650625_5.jpg)

FIG. 3. (Color online) Low-$T$ leakage (symbols) in STO films with (a) RuOx and (b) Ru bottom electrodes. The leakage at higher $V_G$ can be well described by $T$-independent TAT mechanism [Eq. Here, we make the critical assumption that the bulk defects are also responsible for the low-$T$ leakage, and the leakage can thus be described by the trap-assisted tunneling (TAT) mechanism (cf. Fig. 1) in the form

$$
J_{G} \propto E_{o x}^{-1} \exp \left(-\gamma E_{o x}^{-1}\right), \tag{3}
$$

where

$$
\gamma=\frac{4 \sqrt{2 m_{t u n n e l} \Phi_{t r a p}^{3}}}{3 \hbar q}. \tag{4}
$$

In Eq. (4), $\hbar$ is Planck's constant $h/2\pi$ and $m_{tunnel}$ is the effective tunneling mass of the electron in the dielectric. $^{7}$ Note that the TAT equation implicitly assumes parabolic, i.e., $E=\hbar^{2} \kappa^{2}/2m_{tunnel}$ dispersion relation between electron energy $E$ and imaginary part of the wavenumber $\kappa.^{8}$ The fits to the STO low-$T$ $J_G$-$V_G$ using Eq. (3) are shown in Fig. 3. Similar fitting has been done for the $r$-TiO below 220 K (not shown).

### V. EXTRACTION OF EFFECTIVE TUNNELING MASS

With the knowledge of trap depth $\Phi_{trap}$, Eqs. (3) and (4) readily allow extracting the effective tunneling mass as

$$
m_{tunnel}=\frac{9 \hbar^{2} \gamma^{2}}{32 q \Phi_{trap}^{3}}. \tag{5}
$$

Because of the cubic dependence on $\Phi_{trap}$, an uncertainty in $\Phi_{trap}$ value is magnified. The ranges of $m_{tunnel}$ are given in Table I in terms of the free electron mass $m_e$. Interestingly, the effective tunneling mass is found to be significantly higher in the Sr-rich STO than that in $r$-TiO.

### VI. CALCULATION OF EFFECTIVE TUNNELING MASS

The solution of the Schrodinger equation in the band gap of a material yields complex wavenumbers, with the imaginary component $\kappa$ describing the evanescent wave. Consequently, in order to corroborate the experimentally extracted values of $m_{tunnel}$, linked with $\kappa$ as shown below, calculations of complex band structure$^{9}$ have been performed for both materials. The complex bands were computed for the bulk of the material with Quantum ESPRESSO ab initio package$^{10}$ at USPP/PBE level of DFT and an energy cutoff of 540 eV. As the DFT bandgap is typically largely underestimated, there are uncertainties in the position of conduction band and branch point of the imaginary bands. Nevertheless, it has been shown that scaling of the gap to the experimental values in Table I describes correctly the system.$^{11}$ An example of the IBs in the band gap of stoichometric STO is shown in Fig. $4.^{13}$ To conform to the TAT assumption, $m_{tunnel}$ is extracted from the IB using the parabolic approximation at the respective energy below the CBM, i.e.,

$$
m_{tunnel}=\frac{\hbar^{2} \kappa(E)^{2}}{2\left(E_{C B M}-E\right)}. \tag{6}
$$

The resulting values are tabulated in Tables II and III for $r$-TiO and STO, respectively. The experimentally extracted values are transcribed from Table I for easier comparison.

For rutile $TiO_2$(110), the calculated $m_{tunnel}$ matches well the experimental value (cf. Table II). Interestingly, other orientations are expected to yield higher, i.e., more favorable $m_{tunnel}$, suggesting the importance of texture control (cf. the leakage of $r$-TiO(101)$^{14–16}$).

The calculated $m_{tunnel}$ values for all stoichiometric $SrTiO_3$ are similar to those of $TiO_2$, i.e., much lower than the values extracted experimentally (cf. Table III). This is ascribed to the band structure being controlled by the Ti sublattice 3d-orbitals. However, the structures with excess Sr, i.e., those corresponding more closely to our films, yield calculated $m_{tunnel}$ values matching the extracted ones. We speculate that excess Sr results in increased separation (dilution) of the Ti sublattice, due to both the increase of the lattice constant$^{17}$ and local substitution of Sr for Ti site. The latter mechanism is feasible in a Sr-rich STO film—with the order of Sr and Ti rows in such a film changing, a Sr atom can, e.g., take place at a Ti-O-Ti column.$^{18}$

### VII. ULTIMATE LEAKAGE

The leakage in optimized MIM structures will be ultimately controlled by the intrinsic, i.e., trap-free, tunneling. It is instructional to discuss the limits of this intrinsic leakage and the significance of $m_{tunnel}$ and the interplay with other parameters. The trap-free leakage is calculated using a tunneling current model that accounts for carrier statistics in electrodes and calculates numerically the tunneling probability through dielectric stacks, by taking into account main material parameters (dielectric constants, barriers/band offsets, and effective thicknesses). For simplicity, the calculation assumes parabolic dispersion relation with energy-independent $m_{tunnel}$. All parameters affecting the intrinsic leakage, i.e., the effective tunneling mass $m_{tunnel}$, the electrode/dielectric barrier $\Phi_b$, and dielectric physical thickness $t_{ox}$, are then varied to cover the entire parameter space.

One “slice” of the parameter space is illustrated in Fig. 5, while the overall result is summarized in Fig. 6. From Fig. 5 it is apparent that with higher $m_{tunnel}$, a thinner dielectric film is sufficient to achieve the specified leakage. This in turn projects to smaller required $\varepsilon$, calculated as $t_{ox}\times 3.9/$ required EOT (Fig. 6 top axis). We note that because the future DRAM integration schemes also stipulate maximum $t_{ox}$ (e.g., 6 nm in Fig. 6), scaling may not be limited by the ability to achieve sufficiently high $\varepsilon$, but sufficiently high $m_{tunnel}$. For example, with $\Phi_b=1.5\,\text{eV}$ observed in our films, the specified leakage can be reached with a modest $\varepsilon$ but a value of $m_{tunnel}{\sim 0.4m_e}$ is needed to accommodate integration requirements.

### VIII. CONCLUSIONS

We have used the trap-assisted leakage in Sr-rich STO and $r$-TiO films to extract $m_{tunnel}$ and compared it with theoretical values obtained from first principles calculations of the imaginary band structure. Optimum orientations of films and stoichiometry influencing $m_{tunnel}$ were also discussed. We have shown that because the future vertical DRAM integration schemes may also stipulate maximum thickness of the MIM capacitor, $m_{tunnel}$ is a crucial parameter to achieve specified leakage.
