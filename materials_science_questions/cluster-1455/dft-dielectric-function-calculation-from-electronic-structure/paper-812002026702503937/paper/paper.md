# PHYSICS OF SEMICONDUCTORS AND DIELECTRICS

## BAND STRUCTURE AND CHEMICAL BOND IN ALKALI METAL CARBONATES

I. A. Fedorov, Yu. N. Zhuravlev, and D. V. Korabel'nikov
UDC 538.915

The band structure, state density, optical functions, and distribution of valence and difference density in alkali-metal carbonates are calculated within the local electron-density functional theory using the method of pseudopotential in the basis of numerical pseudoorbitals. When passing from a lithium cation to a potassium one, the character of hybridization between the crystal sublattices changes to result in an increase in the valence-band width, a decrease in the forbidden-band width, a complication of the structure of state-density spectrum, and a shift of the maxima of optical functions to the low-energy range. It is found that the electron overflow between the $\sigma$- and $\pi$-orbitals of crystallographically nonequivalent oxygen atoms can occur in different ways, hence their interaction force with the surrounding atoms is different. The role of cations in stabilization of anion chains resulting from the electron-cloud overlapping in lithium and sodium carbonates is shown.

## INTRODUCTION

Metal carbonates are widely used as structural and engineering materials. Hence alkali-metal carbonates, in particular, lithium and potassium carbonates, are promising for optics and energy-accumulating units. However, the fundamental physical-chemical characteristics of these materials are still poorly understood. This is due to the fact that the electronic properties and chemical bond in oxianion crystals are usually described in terms of behavior of the corresponding anion [1]. Therefore, even a simple comparison between the carbonates of alkali and alkaline-earth metals shows significant differences between their crystal structures and physical-chemical properties. This is indicative of the fact that the cation subsystem substantially affects the energy and spatial distribution of the valence electrons. Earlier studies into magnesium and calcium carbonates allowed the main regularities of the chemical-bond formation in magnesium and calcium carbonates to be found [2, 3]. In this work, the electronic structure of alkali metal carbonates is considered and the role of cations in the formation of band structure and chemical bonding in these compounds is discussed.

The band structure is calculated within the framework of the density functional theory using the method of pseudopotential in the basis of numerical $sp^3d^5$-pseudoorbitals expanded in terms of ~ 5000 plane waves. The state density $N(E)$ is calculated using the interpolation of energy bands by the symmetrized Fourier series. A sublattice method is used to analyze chemical bonding [2]. This method consists in calculating the difference density calculated by subtracting all the valence electron sublattice densities from the valence crystal density. Thus, the difference density determines the direction of charge overflow caused by the sublattice interaction.

Lithium and sodium carbonates belong to a monoclinic system with the C2/c and C2/m space symmetry groups, respectively. These crystals contain different numbers of sublattices, each unit cell containing two formula units. One lithium-, one carbon-, and two nonequivalent oxygen (with C-O1 = 1.267 Å and C-O2 = 1.289 Å) sublattices can be distinguished in $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ [4]. In $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, there are three sodium sublattices and two oxygen ones with C-O1 = 1.257 Å and C-O2 = 1.276 Å. In $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$, a metal atom is surrounded by four oxygen atoms at a minimal bond length of 1.899 Å. The Na1 and Na2 atoms have an approximately octahedral oxygen surroundings with minimal bond length Na1-O1 = 2.358 Å

## BAND STRUCTURE AND DENSITY OF STATE

Figure 1 shows the Li₂CO₃ and K₂CO₃ band spectra and state densities $N(E)$ for M₂CO₃ (M: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs). The last filled state is taken as zero energy, and the points of the Brillouin zone have the following coordinates in units of reciprocal lattice vectors: $A(1/2,1/2,0)$, $\Gamma(0,0,0)$, $X(1/2,1/2,1/2)$, $L(1/2,0,0)$, $B(0,1/2,1/2)$, $W(1/2,1/2,0)$, $R(0,0,1/2)$, $P(0,1/2,1/2)$, $E(1/2,1/2,1/2)$, $S(1/2,0,1/2)$, and $U(1/2,0,0)$.

The valence bands of Li₂CO₃ and Na₂CO₃ contain 24 bands that form five bundles separated by the forbidden energy gaps. An individual band labelled by the numbers from I to V corresponds to each density of state bundle. The main-

point positions of the $N(E)$ peaks correlate with the energies of the $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ ion molecular orbitals calculated by quantum-mechanical methods, with the photoelectron $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ spectrum measured in [7], and with the $\text{MgCO}_3$ spectrum calculated in [2].

Two bands I are 0.22 eV and 0.1 eV wide in $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ and $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, respectively. These bands are formed by the oxygen $s$-states, with the contribution from O1 (per one atom) being dominant. A bundle consisting of four bands II in $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ (0.72 eV) is also wider in $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ than in $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ (0.33 eV). The contributions from the oxygen-atom $s$-states are nearly similar, whereas hybridization is much stronger for a couple of equivalent atoms (O2 for $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ and O1 for $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$).

In the middle of the valence range, there are bundles III and IV formed by the hybridized $s$- and $p$-states of oxygen and carbon atoms. The strongest hybridization is observed for states of carbon and oxygen atoms with the shortest spacing. Since the numbers of these atoms differ, the corresponding bands also differ in width.

The upper valence band V in $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ is 4.0 eV wide. It is formed by the bundles with close energies containing six or four bands. $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ has a different band grouping, which manifests itself in a different structure of the $N(E)$ spectrum. The crystal orbitals (CO) corresponding to the states from this energy spectrum range are almost completely formed by the oxygen $p$-orbitals of different orientation. The crystal orbitals of the uppermost valence band formed by the carbon $p$-orbitals directed perpendicular to the anion plane are stronger hybridized. The valence band top and the bottom of the lower unfilled band correspond to the same point $\Gamma$. The forbidden-band width is 4.4 eV in $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ and 2.6 eV in $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$.

In the case of $\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3$, there are three $N(E)$ bands in the lower part of the valence range. The bands corresponding to ranges I and II are formed by the $s$-states of O2 and O3 atoms, whereas bands III are formed mainly by the $s$-states of the O1 atoms. The contribution from the O2 atoms to bands IV is higher than that from the O3 and carbon atoms. The maxima of partial electron density calculated from the band V states correspond to the O1 atoms and the bonding line C–O1. The upper valence band IV consists of five individual subbands formed by the $p$-states of oxygen atoms (as a rule, of each individual atom), with the crystal orbital in the form of the O1-atom $\pi$-orbital corresponding to the uppermost subband. Such a structure of the $\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3$ valence band is atypical of ionic-molecular crystals and is due to weak interaction between the sublattices.

When going to rubidium and cesium carbonates, the band structure is qualitatively unaffected. However, the bands form groups in such a way that the number of bands in the $N(E)$ spectrum of the upper valence range decreases for $\text{Rb}_2\text{CO}_3$, while increasing for $\text{Cs}_2\text{CO}_3$. Thus, the change in the carbonate-ion geometry due to a crystal field results not only in splitting but also in significant energy shifting of the degenerate molecular states. The strong cation dependence of band spectrum is characteristic of alkali metals alone, it is not observed in alkaline-earth metals. A change in the charge state of an anion as a whole and those of atoms constituting the anion can be the reason for the above behavior.

# ELECTRON DENSITY DISTRIBUTION AND CHEMICAL BONDING

To determine the mechanisms of chemical bonding in alkali-metal carbonates, we consider a electron-density distribution. Figure 2 shows the crystal $\rho(\boldsymbol{r})$ and difference $\Delta\rho(\boldsymbol{r})$ density maps in the anion planes of lithium and sodium carbonates. Dotted lines correspond to the negative values of $\Delta\rho(\boldsymbol{r})$, solid lines – to the positive ones, and heavy lines – to the null contour. A uniform scale is used and the numerical values are measured in $e\cdot\mathring{\text{A}}^{-3}$, where $e$ is the electron charge. The density distribution profiles along the corresponding bonding lines are given in the insets.

The valence density distribution is indicative of the well-defined chain structure formed in the crystals due to the overlap of electron clouds of the neighboring anions. In $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$, the chains are directed along the $b$-axis, whereas in $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, they are perpendicular to it. This electron density distribution in alkali-metal carbonates differs fundamentally from that in $\text{MgCO}_3$, where this distribution is of a layered character [2]. In $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, the O1 atoms are combined by common lines of high $\rho(\text{r})$.

In both compounds, the anion-atom sublattice densities also have chain structures in the indicated directions. The valence density distribution in the metal sublattices is quite uniform, with weak minima at the atom sites and the maxima in the interatomic regions. For the $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ lattice, this maximum corresponds to the intersection point of lines with the shortest spacing $\text{Li–Li}=2.700\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ and $\text{O2–O2}=2.907\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ for two adjacent anions. Thus, the lithium sublattice links the anion chains

together using its valence electrons. A more complicated situation occurs in $Na_2CO_3$. The Na3 sublattice plays a role similar to that in the case of lithium, that is, it links the chains using O1 atoms of adjacent anions. The density maximum corresponds to the O2-O2 line of the adjacent anions for the Na1 sublattice, and it is also involved in the chain linking. Finally, the Na2 sublattice distributes the majority of its charge in the region between the adjacent anions inside the chain.

The foregoing special features of sublattice hybridization are shown in the difference density. So, in $Li_2CO_3$, the charge flows out from the region as an O1-atom $\sigma$-orbital and O2-atom $\pi$-orbitals perpendicular to the anion plain. The charge transfer into the binding C-O2 region and behind the oxygen atoms occurs symmetrically to this line. Similar distribution $\Delta\rho(r)$ was also observed for $MgCO_3$ [2] with the only difference that in our case, one of the paired maxima has common level lines with the maximum at the bond. A different character of electron overflow results in the fact that the nonequivalent oxygen atoms have different charge states and different forces of chemical bonding with carbon in the crystal.

In $Na_2CO_3$, the charge flows out from the $\pi$-region of O2 atoms and $\sigma$-region of O1 atoms, and vice versa, the charge transfer occurring into the $\sigma$- region of O2 atoms and $\pi$- region of O1 atoms. In contrast to lithium carbonate, the charge overflow is observed in the vicinity of carbon nucleus. The charge flows out from the $\pi$-region perpendicular to the anion plane, whereas the charge transfer occurring into the binding C-O1 regions with the maximum shifted to the nucleus.

## OPTICAL FUNCTIONS

The above differences in the energy spectrum structure and electron density distribution are bound to manifest themselves in the physical properties including the optical ones. Figure 3 shows the spectral dependence of the imaginary component of the complex permittivity $\varepsilon_{2}(E)$ for lithium, sodium, and potassium carbonates and crystal $LiKCO_{3}$ whose structure is similar to that of $K_{2}CO_{3}$ [4]. The permittivity and optical functions are calculated by the method of special points using an interpolation scheme of the symmetrized Fourier series. In interpretation of the $\varepsilon_{2}(E)$ spectra, it should be taken into account that the density functional method gives understated values of the band-gap width, therefore, the corresponding energy shift of the spectra in Fig. 3 should be made.

Two bands of much the same intensities with the maxima at 9.9 and 13.9 eV are clearly seen in the $\varepsilon_{2}(E)$ spectrum of lithium carbonate. On the contrary, a low-energy maximum at 5.2 eV followed by a dip to the band at 9 and 10.5 eV is observed for $Na_{2}CO_{3}$. This structure of optical spectrum is typical of the majority of oxianion crystals [8]. The dependence $\varepsilon_{2}(E)$ for $K_{2}CO_{3}$ has a similar structure, but in this case, the only intense peak is observed at 5.7 eV.

Solid solutions $(Li_{x}K_{1-x})_{2}CO_{3}$ are of special interest for producing new optical materials. It follows from Fig. 3 that the bands with the maxima at 7.2, 8.6, and 9.8 eV followed by a decrease in the intensity down to zero are observed in the dependence $\varepsilon_{2}(E)$ for this compound. This spectrum structure qualitatively corresponds to those for initial solid-solution components. However, the quantitative characteristics of the spectra differ.

The structure of $\varepsilon_{2}(E)$ spectra may be accounted for by the energy-band structure and nature. It is seen from Fig. 1 that the lower empty bands of lithium and sodium carbonates are the dispersion ones and no special features are observed for the state density in this energy range. In contrast, the lowest empty bands are separated in $K_{2}CO_{3}$. Thus, a single band with the maximum at 3.4 eV corresponds to these bands in the dependence $N(E)$ (Fig. 1). In addition, the distribution of hypothetical electron density of the states from this band has an anion-localized character. With allowance for the nature of upper valence bands, the low-energy electron excitations are expected to be of an intra- and intermolecular character. Since the oxygen atoms are in various charge states, these excitations cause charge transfer, which is likely to result in the photoemission activity of these compounds.

Thus, the regularities in the energy structure of electronic spectrum found for the range of low valence and empty states are indicative of different optical and X-ray photoemission properties of metal carbonates. These results should be experimentally validated.
