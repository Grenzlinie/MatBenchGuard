## Abstract
We have derived analytic expressions of the curvature contribution to the dipole moment as well as to the static polarizability and hyperpolarizabilities of push-pull $\pi$-conjugated systems. These expressions were obtained from harmonic zero-point energy using the analytical evaluation of electrical properties method and the valence-bond charge-transfer model. We have found that the curvature contribution can be expressed in terms of the electronic hyperpolarizabilities. On the other hand, the curvature contribution can be as important as—or even more important than—electronic contribution.

## 1. Introduction
The static electrical properties such as dipole moment, $\mu$, polarizability, $\alpha$, first hyperpolarizability, $\beta$, and second hyperpolarizability, $\gamma$, of polyatomic molecules have different contributions, namely, electronic (el), vibrational (vib), curvature (cur) and rotational (rot) $[1,2]^1$. Such electrical properties give the response of a molecule under the effect of a static electric field. The electronic component describes the distortion of the electronic environment by the electric field. On the other hand, the vibrational contribution corresponds to the nuclear geometry deformation under the effect of the field, whereas the curvature contribution comes from the field dependence of the vibrational energy. There is yet the rotational component, but it is much less important than others; therefore, it will not be considered here.

$^1$ We closely follow the procedure presented in [1]. However, what in our paper has been referred to as 'vibrational' and 'curvature' contributions, in [1] is defined as 'nuclear relaxation' and 'vibrational' contribution (see [13] for the correspondence between different terminologies and theoretical approaches).

![](./images/812123323830894592_1.jpg)

Figure 1. Valence-bond (VB) and charge-transfer (CT) configurations for push-pull systems. The common feature of these molecules is the presence of an electron donor group (D) coupled, through a $\pi$-conjugated bridge, to an electron acceptor group (A).

In contrast to the extensive literature concerning the electronic problem, studies of the nuclear (vibrational and/or curvature) contribution are relatively recent. Theoretical calculations of nuclear contributions considering different levels of approximation have been preformed on the molecules pyridine [1], HF, CO, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{H_2CO}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$ [2], $\mathrm{CH_{4-n}F_n}$ [3], $\pi$-conjugated organic molecules [4-6], polyacetylene, polyyne, polydiacetylene, polybutatriene, polycumulene, polysilane, polymethineimine, polypyrrole [7], etc. In general, the results reveal that the nuclear contributions must be included in order to obtain accurate values of the static electrical properties.

In this work, we have used the general expressions of the analytical evaluation of electrical properties (AEEP) method [1] to compute the diagonal components of the nuclear contribution to the electrical properties of push-pull systems. The AEEP method allows us to obtain the nuclear contributions from the coefficients of the power series expansion of the molecular potential energy with respect to normal coordinates, field strength or both. All these coefficients are evaluated at the equilibrium geometry for the zero-field case. Moreover, in the AEEP method, it is straightforward to observe that when the molecular vibrations are assumed to be harmonic and the electronic properties to be linear in the normal coordinates, the so-called double harmonic approximation (DHA) [8], the vibrational contribution is entirely due to harmonic coefficients, while the curvature contribution must be zero. However, it is important to underline that the expressions of the AEEP method are restricted to static quantities whereas the general perturbation theory expressions [9-14] are able to deal with both static and dynamic (in the infinite optical frequency approximation) properties. The connection between these two formalisms has been presented in [13]. It shows that the AEEP method is obtained in the limit of static fields from the perturbation theory approach.

One important point to consider for the calculation of the nuclear contributions to the hyperpolarizabilities is the effect of the electrical and mechanical anharmonicities associated with the potential energy surface. Some studies have been performed taking into consideration the anharmonicities on nuclear contributions [1-5, 16-19]. From these studies, large anharmonic corrections to NLO responses have been recognized on conjugated molecules [1,4,5,19] and polymers [16,18]. However, the importance of this contribution for medium-sized and large molecules is unknown owing to extensive computational requirements for its determination.

The single-mode VB-CT model [20] has been used in the literature in order to express analytically the dependence from a few structural parameters of various molecular properties of push-pull molecules (see figure 1). Among these properties are the electronic, vibrational and curvature contributions [4,5,19,20,22-27]. In particular, Zerbi et al [28] found from the VB-CT model that the relative variation of the static vibrational first hyperpolarizability $(\beta_{zzz}^{\text{vib}})$ to the static electronic first hyperpolarizability $(\beta_{zzz}^{\text{el}})$ for push-pull $\pi$-conjugated compounds is close to unity. However, the validity of the VB-CT model has been criticized [29] on general grounds as well as quantitative calculations. In order to evaluate the applicability

of the VB–CT treatment for vibrational, as well as electronic, hyperpolarizabilities several parameter-independent relations between these properties were derived by Bishop, Champagne and Kirtman from the VB–CT model [30]. In that work they used ab-initio calculations to evaluate the electronic and vibrational polarizabilities and first hyperpolarizabilities of ten push–pull systems. Except for a couple of compounds, these relations were found to deviate noticeably from the predictions of the VB–CT model, i.e. $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{vib}} \approx \beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$. In fact, they found cases where $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{vib}}$ is much larger than $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$. Therefore, they conclude that the assumptions and approximations involved in the VB–CT model are not adequate for establishing the quantitative relationship between $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{vib}}$ and $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$. As examples of push–pull $\pi$-conjugated organic molecules for which the ratio $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{vib}}/\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$ is large, we can cite the $\alpha$, $\omega$-nitro,amino all-trans polyenes [6] and merocyanines [31]. Additionally, contrary to what had been suggested by Cho [32], recently was found that the magnitude of the ratio $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{vib}}/\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{e}}$ is not reduced by including electron correlation [33]. As a consequence, it is now clear that the quantitative predictions of the VB–CT model are of limited applicability. In our case, we should stress that, even though the VB–CT model is certainly too crude to provide quantitative results for push–pull molecules, it is sufficiently simple to describe the general trend of the static electrical properties of push–pull systems.

Recently [19], we have carried out an estimation of the curvature contribution for the diagonal components of $\mu$, $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$, denoted as $\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{cur}}$, $\alpha_{zz}^{\mathrm{cur}}$, $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\gamma_{zzzz}^{\mathrm{cur}}$, respectively, of push–pull conjugated systems using the AEEP method [1] and the VB–CT model [20]. The results obtained show an important correlation between (i) $\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ and the electronic first hyperpolarizability, $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$, (ii) $\alpha_{zz}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ with the electronic second hyperpolarizability, $\gamma_{zzzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$, (iii) $\beta_{zzz}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ with the electronic third hyperpolarizability, $\delta_{zzzzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$, and, finally, (iv) $\gamma_{zzzz}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ with the electronic fourth hyperpolarizability, $\chi_{zzzzzz}^{\mathrm{el}}$. From these relationships, we have also found that the curvature contribution is not always negligible and should be taken into account to calculate static hyperpolarizabilities.

The aim of the present work is to provide the analytic relationship between the curvature and electronic contributions. The analytic formulae presented here can be used for predicting the trend of NLO properties of push–pull systems in terms of the equilibrium bond length alternation (BLA) coordinate associated with the conjugated bridge. However, an important issue to be considered in further studies is the structure of the hyperpolarizability hypersurfaces in terms of specific geometric distortions (bonds length or/and bond angles) in the molecule [34–36]. In particular, Maroulis [35] studied the effect of specific molecular parameters on the second hyperpolarizability of trans-butadiene. As a result of his work, explicit formulae for the dependence of all components of $\gamma$ on the $\mathrm{C=C}$ and $\mathrm{C-C}$ bond lengths around the experimental value of these parameters near the Hartree–Fock limit were obtained. Overall, small changes of the $\mathrm{C=C}$ bond length result in more than appreciable changes of $\gamma$. The effect is less pronounced when the $\mathrm{C-C}$ bond length is varied. It can be seen that in the change of the mean hyperpolarizability the two effects are opposed, with the $\mathrm{C=C}$ one dominating.

In the following section, we shall outline the AEEP method for push–pull molecules and the closed-form equations between the curvature and electronic contributions. Section 3 is dedicated to present the results obtained. Finally, this paper concludes with a summary of the major results and suggests directions for future research.

## 2. Curvature contribution to the static electrical properties of push–pull molecules form the AEEP method

From the AEEP method [1], the curvature contribution is obtained as derivatives of the vibrational energy with respect to the applied field strength. Additionally, the vibrational

energy is given by the harmonic zero-point energy (ZPE) (to take into account excited vibrational states see [37]). For linear molecular systems with a single vibrational mode, the diagonal component (along the z-axis) of the curvature contribution to $\mu$, $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are given by the following:

$$
\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=-\left.\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\partial \omega}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}=-\left.\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0},\qquad(1)
$$

$$
\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=-\left.\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \omega}{\partial \varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}=-\left.\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial^{2} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2 K}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)^{2}\right\}\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0},\qquad(2)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{cur}} & =-\left.\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{3} \omega}{\partial \varepsilon^{3}}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0} \\
& =-\left.\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial^{3} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{3}}\right)-\frac{3}{2 K}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)+\frac{3}{4 K^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)^{3}\right\}\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}
\end{aligned}\qquad(3)
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=-\left.\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{4} \omega}{\partial \varepsilon^{4}}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}= & -\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial^{4} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{4}}\right)-\frac{2}{K}\left(\frac{\partial^{3} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{3}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)-\frac{3}{2 K}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}\right. \\
& \left.+\frac{9}{2 K^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)^{2}-\frac{15}{8 K^{3}}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)^{4}\right\}\left.\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}.
\end{aligned}\qquad(4)
$$

As can be observed, the curvature contribution is a function of the field-dependent force constant, $K$, and their derivatives with respect to the applied electric field strength. In these expressions, the quantities $\bar{\mu}$ and $\omega=2 \pi \nu$ are the reduced mass and harmonic vibrational frequency associated with $K$, respectively. For molecular systems with a single vibrational mode, $K$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
K=K\left(q_{\mathrm{eq}}(\varepsilon), \varepsilon\right)= & 2 a_{20}+6 a_{30} q_{\mathrm{eq}}(\varepsilon)+12 a_{40} q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}(\varepsilon)+20 a_{50} q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}(\varepsilon)+30 a_{60} q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{4}(\varepsilon) \\
& +\left(2 a_{21}+6 a_{31} q_{\mathrm{eq}}(\varepsilon)+12 a_{41} q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}(\varepsilon)+20 a_{51} q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}(\varepsilon)\right) \varepsilon+\left(2 a_{22}+6 a_{32} q_{\mathrm{eq}}(\varepsilon)\right. \\
& \left.+12 a_{42} q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}(\varepsilon)\right) \varepsilon^{2}+\left(2 a_{23}+6 a_{33} q_{\mathrm{eq}}(\varepsilon)\right) \varepsilon^{3}+2 a_{24} \varepsilon^{4}+\cdots,
\end{aligned}\qquad(5)
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_{\mathrm{eq}}(\varepsilon)=- & q_{1} \varepsilon-\left(\frac{3 a_{30}}{2 a_{20}} q_{1}^{2}-\frac{a_{21}}{a_{20}} q_{1}+q_{2}\right) \varepsilon^{2}-\left\{-\left[\frac{2 a_{40}}{a_{20}}-\frac{9}{2}\left(\frac{a_{30}}{a_{20}}\right)^{2}\right] q_{1}^{3}\right. \\
& \left.-\left(\frac{9 a_{30} a_{21}}{2 a_{20}^{2}}-\frac{3 a_{31}}{2 a_{20}}\right) q_{1}^{2}-\left[\frac{a_{22}}{a_{20}}-\left(\frac{a_{21}}{a_{20}}\right)^{2}\right] q_{1}-\left(\frac{a_{21}}{a_{20}}-\frac{3 a_{30}}{a_{20}} q_{1}\right) q_{2}+q_{3}\right\} \varepsilon^{3}
\end{aligned}\qquad(6)
$$

is the field-dependent equilibrium BLA coordinate [20] with $q_{1}=a_{11} / 2 a_{20}, q_{2}=a_{12} / 2 a_{20}$ and $q_{3}=a_{13} / 2 a_{20}$. In the above expressions, the quantity

$$
a_{n m}=\frac{1}{n! m!}\left(\frac{\partial^{(n+m)} U(q, \varepsilon)}{\partial q^{n} \partial \varepsilon^{m}}\right)_{q_{\mathrm{eq}(\varepsilon=0)}}\qquad(7)
$$

is the coefficient of the power series expansion for the field-dependent potential energy, $U(q, \varepsilon)$, evaluated at $q_{\text {eq }}(\varepsilon=0)$. In the present work, the coefficients up to $n+m \leqslant 6$

are considered in the expansion of the potential energy to obtain exact expressions of $\beta_{zzz}^{\text{cur}}$
and $\gamma_{zzzz}^{\text{cur}}$.

In the above equations for $K$ besides the electrical harmonic coefficients $(a_{20}, a_{11}, a_{12}$
and $a_{13})$, $K$ includes the mechanical anharmonicity $(a_{30}, a_{40}, a_{50}$ and $a_{60}$ terms), and electrical
anharmonicity to the dipole moment $(a_{21}, a_{31}, a_{41}$ and $a_{51}$ terms), polarizability $(a_{22}, a_{32}$ and
$a_{42}$ terms), first hyperpolarizability $(a_{23}$ and $a_{33}$ terms) and second hyperpolarizability $(a_{24}$
term). It is of interest to point out that $a_{11} = -(\partial \mu_{z}^{\text{e}}/\partial q)$, $a_{12} = -(1/2)(\partial \alpha_{zz}^{\text{e}}/\partial q)_{q_{\text{eq}},\varepsilon=0}$
and $a_{13} = -(1/6)(\partial \beta_{zzz}^{\text{e}}/\partial q)_{q_{\text{eq}},\varepsilon=0}$ can be calculated directly from the IR, Raman and hyper-
Raman spectra [23].

The VB-CT model allows us to calculate the coefficients $a_{nm}$ from the following
expression of the field-dependent potential energy of the ground state of push-pull
systems:

$$
U(q, \varepsilon)=\frac{V_{\mathrm{o}}-\mu_{\mathrm{CT}} \varepsilon+(k / 2)\left[\left(q-q_{\mathrm{VB}}^{\mathrm{o}}\right)^{2}+\left(q-q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{\mathrm{o}}\right)^{2}\right]-\sqrt{\left(V_{\mathrm{o}}-\mu_{\mathrm{CT}} \varepsilon+k \delta q\right)^{2}+4 t^{2}}}{2}.
\tag{8}
$$

In the above expression, $q$ is the BLA coordinate, $V_{\mathrm{o}}$ is the electronic energy gap
between the charge-transfer (CT) and valence bond (VB) states (see figure 1) evaluated
at the corresponding equilibrium BLA coordinates $q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{\mathrm{o}}$ and $q_{\mathrm{VB}}^{\mathrm{o}}$ (with $q_{\mathrm{VB}}^{\mathrm{o}}=-q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{\mathrm{o}}$ and
$\delta=q_{\mathrm{VB}}^{\mathrm{o}}-q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{\mathrm{o}}=-2 q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{\mathrm{o}}$), $\mu_{C T}$ is the dipole moment of the CT state, $t$ is the charge transfer
integral ($t$ is positive) and $k$ is the force constant appropriate for the polyene linkers.

From equation (8), it is straightforward to verify that the zero-field equilibrium BLA
coordinate is given by

$$
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=q_{\mathrm{VB}}^{\mathrm{o}}-\delta f,
\tag{9}
$$

where $f$ is the fraction (weighting factor) of the CT state in the electronic ground state
$\Psi_{\mathrm{gr}}=(1-f)^{1 / 2} \Psi_{\mathrm{VB}}+f^{1 / 2} \Psi_{\mathrm{CT}}:$

$$
f=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{V}{\left(V^{2}+4 t^{2}\right)^{1 / 2}}\right],
\tag{10}
$$

with $V=V_{\mathrm{o}}+k \delta q_{\mathrm{eq}}$. As $f$ increases from 0 to 1, the VB-CT model leads to a structural
evolution in which the polyene double bonds (1.33 Å) in the VB state (see figure 1) change to
polyene single bonds (1.45 Å) in the CT state and vice versa. As a result, $q_{\mathrm{eq}}$ in equation (17)
changes from $q_{\mathrm{VB}}^{\mathrm{o}}=-0.12$ Å to $q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{\mathrm{o}}=0.12$ Å as $f$ goes from 0 to 1. Therefore, each bond
decreases and increases by 0.12 Å.

From equation (8), and after some algebraic manipulation, we can rewrite equations (1)–
(5) as

$$
\left.K\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}=k\left[1-\frac{2 k t^{2} \delta^{2}}{\left(V^{2}+4 t^{2}\right)^{3 / 2}}\right],
\tag{11}
$$

$$
\left.\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}=-C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right) \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}},
\tag{12}
$$

$$
\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{2} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}=-C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right)^{2}\left[\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\frac{B\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}{3 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}\right],
\tag{13}
$$

$$
\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{3} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{3}}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0}=-C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right)^{3}\left[\delta_{z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\frac{4 B \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}} \gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}{3 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}+\frac{4 B^{2}\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{3}}{12\left(\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}\right]
\tag{14}
$$


and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{4} K}{\partial \varepsilon^{4}}\right)\right|_{q_{\mathrm{eq}}, \varepsilon=0} & =-C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right)^{4}\left[\chi_{z z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\frac{4 B\left(\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}{3 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}\right. \\
& \left.+\frac{20 B^{2}\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2} \gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}{12\left(\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}+\frac{2 B \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}} \delta_{z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}{\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
$$
where $\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=-2 a_{02}, \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=-6 a_{03}, \gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=-24 a_{04}, \delta_{z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=-120 a_{05}$ and $\chi_{z z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=-720 a_{06}$ are the electronic hyperpolarizabilities [1], which from the VB-CT model [20] are given by
$$
\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{2 \mu_{\mathrm{CT}}^{2} t^{2}}{\left(V^{2}-4 t^{2}\right)^{3 / 2}}
\tag{16}
$$
$$
\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{6 \mu_{\mathrm{CT}}^{3} t^{2} V}{\left(V^{2}-4 t^{2}\right)^{5 / 2}},
\tag{17}
$$
$$
\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{24 \mu_{\mathrm{CT}}^{4} t^{2}\left(V^{2}-t^{2}\right)}{\left(V^{2}-4 t^{2}\right)^{7 / 2}},
\tag{18}
$$
$$
\delta_{z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{120 \mu_{\mathrm{CT}}^{5} t^{2} V\left(V^{2}-3 t^{2}\right)}{\left(V^{2}-4 t^{2}\right)^{9 / 2}}
\tag{19}
$$
and
$$
\chi_{z z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{720 \mu_{\mathrm{CT}}^{6} t^{2}\left(V^{4}-6 V^{2} t^{2}+2 t^{4}\right)}{\left(V^{2}+4 t^{2}\right)^{11 / 2}}.
\tag{20}
$$

In expressions (12)-(15) the parameter-dependent factors $C$ and $B$ are introduced to make a transformation from the electronic to curvature contributions:
$$
C=k \delta / \mu_{\mathrm{CT}}
\tag{21}
$$
and
$$
B=\frac{6 k t^{2} \delta^{2}}{\left[\left(V^{2}+4 t^{2}\right)^{3 / 2}-2 k t^{2} \delta^{2}\right]}.
\tag{22}
$$

Finally, inserting equations (11)-(15) in equations (1)-(4), we can write $\mu_{z}^{\text {cur }}, \alpha_{z z}^{\text {cur }}, \beta_{z z z}^{\text {cur }}$ and $\gamma_{z z z z}^{\text {cur }}$ in a form that illustrates the relationship between the curvature and electronic contributions in the VB-CT and AEEP framework:
$$
\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}} C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right) \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}},
\tag{23}
$$
$$
\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}} C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right)^{2}\left[\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{B}{3 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}+\frac{C^{2}}{2 K}\right)\right],
\tag{24}
$$
$$
\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}} C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right)^{3}\left[\delta_{z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\frac{11 B C^{2} \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}} \gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}{6 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}+\frac{7 B^{2}\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{3}}{12\left(\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}\right]
\tag{25}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{cur}}=\frac{\hbar}{4(\bar{\mu} K)^{1 / 2}} C^{2}\left(1+\frac{B}{3}\right)^{4} & {\left[\chi_{z z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\frac{8 B \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}} \delta_{z z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}{3 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}+\frac{11 B\left(\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}{6 \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}\right.} \\
& \left.+\frac{122 B^{2}\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2} \gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}}{36\left(\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{2}}+\frac{37 B^{3}\left(\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{4}}{72\left(\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}\right)^{3}}\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{26}
$$

where $K$ is given by equation (11). The above equations are the main result in this work, since from theoretical/experimental values for electronic hyperpolarizabilities; the trend for the curvature contribution can be calculated for push-pull molecules. Another important point is that no simple correlation exists between the curvature contribution and its electronic counterparts, as could be anticipated from the connection found between the vibrational contribution to the polarizability, $\alpha_{z}^{\mathrm{vib}}$, first hyperpolarizability [4], $\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{vib}}$, and second hyperpolarizability [5], $\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{vib}}$, and $\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}, \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}$ and $\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}$, respectively, i.e.,

$$
\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{vib}}=\frac{2}{3} B \alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{e}},\qquad(27)
$$

$$
\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{vib}}=B\left(1+\frac{B}{3}+\frac{B^{2}}{27}\right) \beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{e}}\qquad(28)
$$

and

$$
\gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{vib}}=\frac{2 B}{3}\left\{2+R+\frac{2 B}{3}\left[C+2 R\left(1+\frac{B C}{3}\right)\right]\right\} \gamma_{z z z z}^{\mathrm{e}}.\qquad(29)
$$

In these relationships $B$ is given by equation (22) and the factors $R$ and $C$ are related to the VB-CT parameters by

$$
R=\frac{9 V^{2}}{8\left(V^{2}-t^{2}\right)}\qquad(30)
$$

and

$$
C=\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{B}{3}+\frac{B^{2}}{36}\right).\qquad(31)
$$

It is important to indicate that equations (28) and (29) [4, 5] take into account all the harmonic and anharmonic terms associated with the potential energy $U(q, \varepsilon)$ (equation (8)).

### 3. Results and discussions
Calculations have been carried out considering the following values of the VB-CT parameters: $k=33.55$ eV $\AA^{-2}, t=1.184$ eV, $q_{\mathrm{VIB}}^{o}=-0.12$ $\AA$, $q_{\mathrm{CT}}^{o}=0.12$ $\AA$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{CT}}=26$ D. These parameters correspond to push-pull molecules with D and A linked through a hexatriene $\pi$ system, such as 1,1 dicyano, 6-(di-butyl amine) hexatriene [20]. We have chosen $\mu=10^{-26}$ kg as the reduced mass associated with the zero-field equilibrium BLA coordinate, $q_{\mathrm{eq}}$.

Given $V_{\mathrm{o}}$ and an initial value for $q_{\mathrm{eq}}$, the self-consistent determination of $q_{\mathrm{eq}}$, involves the following steps: (i) evaluate the function $f$ according to equation (10), (ii) calculate a new value of $q_{\mathrm{eq}}$ by means of equation (9) and (iii) repeat set (i) and (ii), until convergence is achieved. From the iterated for $q_{\mathrm{eq}}$, we have calculated the static electrical properties (equations (16)-(20) and (23)-(29)) as a function of $V_{\mathrm{o}}$. The results of such calculations are shown in figures 2-8 as a function of the CT character $f$.

We start considering the evolution of $\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{el}}, \mu_{z}^{\mathrm{vib}}, \mu_{z}^{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\beta_{z z z}^{\mathrm{el}}$ with $f$. In figure 2 we observe that the values of $\mu_{z}^{\text {cur }}$ are always smaller than $\mu_{z}^{\text {el }}$, while that the $z$ component of the nuclear vibrational to the dipole moment, $\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{vib}}$, is null [1]. In general, our results show that the most important contribution to $\alpha_{zz}^{\text{total}}$, $\beta_{zzz}^{\text{total}}$ and $\gamma_{zzzz}^{\text{total}}$ is the vibrational. However, the curvature contribution to the first and second hyperpolarizability is not negligible and it can be similar in magnitude to the electronic contribution for *some* VB–CT model parameters. In relation to this observation, Luis *et al* [38] carried out the calculation of static zero-point vibrational averaging (ZPVA) corrections and other vibrational curvature contributions to the polarizability and hyperpolarizabilities using field-induced coordinates (that utilize only the essential vibrational information). They found that $\beta_{zzz}^{\text{vib}}$ and $\gamma_{zzzz}^{\text{vib}}$ in para-nitroaniline are greater than, or roughly the same as, the corresponding static electronic property. In contrast, the ZPVA correction (that is a term of the curvature contribution) was smaller than the vibrational and electronic contributions.

Before we close this section, it is important to indicate that the results presented here are only valid when the ZPE approximation is taken into account to calculate the curvature contribution. Additionally, because higher-order derivatives are involved in the expression of the field-dependent force constant at the equilibrium geometry (equation (5)), our results are subject to the details of the theoretical model used to describe the potential energy curve of the ground state, i.e., the VB–CT model. Therefore, we should anticipate that our results are approximate in relation to the *ab-initio* calculations. However, without relying on any extensive numerical calculations, one can get a simple physical picture on the structure-static NLO properties relationship of push–pull molecules.

## 4. Conclusions

By applying the VB-CT model and the AEEP method, we have established closed-form equations between the curvature and electronic contributions for push-pull systems, which confirm the results obtained in [19]. On the other hand, we have also shown the evolution of the electronic, vibrational and curvature contributions with the weighting factor of the CT state $(f)$. From these results, we have found that $\mu_{z}^{\mathrm{el}}$ and $\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{el}}+\alpha_{z z}^{\mathrm{vib}}$ are the major contribution to $\mu_{z}^{\text {total }}$ and $\alpha_{z z}^{\text {total }}$, respectively. On the other hand, even if the vibrational contribution is more important than the electronic and curvature contribution to $\beta_{z z z}^{\text {total }}$ and $\gamma_{z z z z}^{\text {total }}$ for some VB-CT model parameters, the curvature contribution is not negligible.

The VB-CT model is too simple to provide quantitative information on the absolute magnitudes for hyperpolarizabilities, further investigation by means of $a b$-initio methods as well as experimental measurements to a series of push-pull are needed.

Finally, the generalization of the above results to different dynamic phenomena should be important point to be considered, since the nuclear contribution for push-pull systems depends strongly upon the NLO process. Recently, Quinet et al [39] found that the ZPVA correction (curvature contribution contain the so-called term ZPVA) at the time-dependent Hartree-Fock level of approximation for second harmonic generation in para-nitroanilina may represent a large contribution to the total $\beta(-2 \omega, \omega, \omega)$ response for high frequencies. Additionally, calculations of the vibrational and electronic second hyperpolarizability of various quadrupolar $\pi$-conjugated systems performed at the $a b$-initio Hartree-Fock level of approximation shown that for unsubstituted $\pi$-conjugated compounds, the Raman term, which contributes to the dc-Kerr and degenerates four wave mixing phenomena, is of the same order of magnitude as the static electronic counterpart. On the other hand, the term related to the IR and hyper-Raman intensities is substantial and, in the case of a squaraine dye, dictates the sign of global response. This IR-hyper-Raman term, which is often negligible for unsubstituted compounds, contributes substantially to the vibrational dc-Kerr and electric field-induced second harmonic generation responses of symmetrically substituted $\pi$-conjugated systems [40].
