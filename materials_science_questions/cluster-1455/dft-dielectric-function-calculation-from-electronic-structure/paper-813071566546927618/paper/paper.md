## A B S T R A C T

Black phosphorus (BP) is a new two-dimensional (2D) material. It has received significant attention because of its great potential in the field of optoelectronics, which arises due to its low-dimensional effects. In this paper, the effect of bi-axial strain on the band structure of monolayer BP has been studied by using the first-principles method based on density functional theory (DFT). We find that the band gap range of monolayer BP can be moderately and effectively adjusted by external stress from 0 to 1.845 eV. Simultaneously, the direct band gap is maintained during the stretching process, which leads to a high electron mobility suitable for applications. Moreover, the compressive strain can result in a semiconductor-metal transition (SMT) with few-layer BP, whereas tensile strain only affects the band gap. Based on the electronic properties of monolayer BP, its optical properties are analyzed in detail, and the influence of strain-from a compressive strain of 12% to a tensile strain of 12%-on these properties is systematically discussed. It is found that the strain range and mode have a significant impact on the optical properties. In addition, the dielectric function and absorption spectra calculated along two different directions indicate that a significant optical anisotropy appears, giving a strong directionality dependence in the primitive and strained monolayer BP. It can be concluded that by applying external stress to monolayer BP, its optical properties can be readily controlled, which is beneficial for device applications. Furthermore, based on its direction-dependent response to the external stress, this behavior can also be used to detect the orientation of the deposited BP without the need for precise electron microscopy.

## 1. Introduction

The discovery of the excellent physical properties of graphene [1] laid the foundation for many new fields of research. One of the most important fundamental requirements is to deepen the study of 2D atomic-layered systems. Such materials, such as hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), silene, germylene, and other 2D compounds, have attracted wide attention [2-5]. Graphene is well known for its many excellent properties, but the absence of a forbidden band is detrimental as the lack of a band gap generates only a small current-switching effect. However, a band gap is very important for semiconductor device applications such as solar cells and field-effect transistors, so its absence limits the application of graphene in practical components [6]. Therefore, the search for materials with a high carrier mobility and proper band gap has become a hot topic.

Black phosphorus (BP) has been studied since the 1950s [7]. It is a stable allotrope of phosphorus that is widely regarded as a graphene cousin, with a layered structure similar to graphite. The biggest difference from graphene is that BP is a semiconductor with a direct band gap; bulk BP has a band gap of 0.3 eV [8] while that of monolayer BP reaches 1.59 eV [9], and this material is more stable than silicon. Monolayer BP is a new type of 2D semiconductor material with a direct band gap that is favored by researchers because of its wide range of attributes, including its adjustable direct band gap, high carrier mobility, significant in-plane photoelectron anisotropy, and phonon properties.

By using density functional theory (DFT), Qiao and Wang et al. [10,11] demonstrated that the band gap of 2D BP depends on the number of layers: the band gap decreases with an increasing number of layers. The band gap reaches a maximum value of 1.59 eV when BP is reduced to a monolayer. A direct-indirect-direct transformation of the band gap occurs when uniaxial strain is applied to 2D BP, as reported by Peng et al. [12]. Ong et al. [13] reported that the strong thermal transport anisotropy and strain modulation in monolayer BP. In a recent theoretical study by Ouyang et al. [14] it was shown that the stress responses of monolayer BP show

Tailoring the electronic properties of semiconductor nanostructures has been critical for their applications. Strain has long been used to tune the electronic properties of semiconductors [12,18]. As a practical issue, strain is almost inevitable in fabricated monolayer nanostructures, which manifests as buckling and the formation of ridges [19]. A more interesting case comes from intentionally introduced and controlled strains. Methods for introducing strain include lattice mismatch, functional wrapping, material doping, and direct mechanical application [20]. However, considering these previous studies, a full and detailed systematic analysis of the effect of strain on the band structure of BP is still missing. Therefore, it is of great practical significance to conduct a comprehensive and systematic study of the photoelectric properties of the system by using accurate calculation methods. This article will provide a full analysis of the effect of strain on the band structure and optical properties of monolayer BP.

The first-principles method has been widely applied in varied studies on the photoelectric properties of materials. In this work, all calculations were performed using the Cambridge Sequential Total Energy Package (CASTEP) code based on the primary principle of plane wave turns potential method. Given that BP is a semiconductor material with a tunable band gap, the use of this and other 2D materials in practical applications often needs to consider the interfacial contact stress problems. In this paper, the band gap is modified by applying external stress to the system, and the adjustable range and responses of monolayer BP are studied systematically. The effects of bi-axial strain on the band structure and optical properties of monolayer BP crystals are discussed in detail, including the trends of these properties with strain. It is hoped that the results will provide a useful theoretical basis for the experimental research on and application of BP crystal materials in optoelectronic devices.

## 2. Simulation details

BP is one of the most stable allotropes of phosphorus. The bulk BP material possesses a lamellar structure, similar to graphite. Fig. 1(a) shows a model of the perfect crystal structure of bulk BP, which is orthorhombic with a Cmca space group and eight P atoms in each unit cell. The experimental [21] lattice parameters are $a = 3.313$ Å, $b = 10.473$ Å, and $c = 4.374$ Å, and the crystal plane angles are $\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90^\circ$. The crystal structure of BP is different from that of graphene as it consists of folded layers of P atoms, and each P atom is connected to three others by short covalent bonds. This structure ensures that the properties of BP are more stable than those of red phosphorus and white phosphorus. The crystal structure of monolayer BP was obtained by isolating a layer from bulk BP, as shown in Fig. 1(b).

The calculations are performed by using DFT with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) implemented in CASTEP, and using Vanderbilt-type ultra-soft pseudo-potentials for the electron-ion interactions. Periodic boundary conditions are used in this calculation process. The exchange-correlation potential of Ceperley and Alder as parameterized by Perdew and Zunger (CAPZ) [22] in the local density approximation (LDA) were used, along with the GGA schemes of Perdew and Wang (PW91) [23] and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) [24]. In particular, different DFT-D approaches to treat the van der Waals (vdW) interactions were employed, notably the Ortmann, Bechstedt, and Schmidt (OBS) [25] correction to PW91, as well as the Tkatchenko and Scheffler (TS) [26] and Grimme [27] corrections to PBE. These methods have been shown to be reliable and are often used for electronic structure calculations [28–30]. Accordingly, in this work, the above-mentioned methods are employed to describe the electronic exchange-correlation potential. In the subsequent calculations of the band structure, density of states, and optical properties of this system, since the PBE exchange-correlation functional often underestimates the band gap, the more accurate hybrid Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) [31] functional was used; the norm-conserving pseudo-potential was chosen because it is more accurate than the ultra-soft pseudo-potential in optical calculations [32].

Before starting the simulation work, the convergence test and geometric optimization of the BP cell are first performed. The test results are shown in Fig. 2. The kinetic energy cutoff for the plane wave basis set was chosen to be 500 eV. For the bulk BP cell, the reciprocal space was meshed at $8 \times 10 \times 4$ using the Monkhorst–Pack method. For single layer BP thin film K-point grid, the Brillouin zone integrations are performed using a Monkhorst–Pack grid of $8 \times 10 \times 1$ for relaxation. The total energy until convergence obtained during the structure optimization was less than $10^{-6}$ eV/atom, and all of the structure models were fully relaxed until the forces were less than 0.01 eV/Å, per the convergence criterion.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Geometric structure optimization

The theoretical equilibrium crystal structure was obtained by full structural optimization using LDA, GGA, and three versions of GGA-D. The calculated structural parameters are presented in Table 1. Taking the experimental cell volume as the theoretical reference, we find the differences between the calculated and experimental measured volumes to be 11.07% with LDA, 8.77% with PW91, and 10.0% with PBE. These large errors indicate that the exchange-correlation potential does not capture the intermolecular interactions in BP accurately. However, the calculated results found with the three DFT-D methods were in good agreement with the experimental values, with the errors in the predicted equilibrium volumes being only 1.55%, 2.92%, and 0.51%, respectively, for the PW91-OBS, PBE-TS, and PBE-Grimme parametrizations. Considering the three lattice parameters, it is obvious that the maximum error is found for $b$, the parameter lying perpendicular to the BP layers along the $z$-direction. Consequently, dispersive forces play a dominant role in the interlayer interactions, and considering this type of interaction is very important to provide an accurate description. The lattice constants of $a$, $b$, and $c$, along with the unit cell volume, were calculated by the GGA-PBE-Grimme method with relative errors of 0.48%, 0.53%, 0.53%, and 0.51%, respectively. These are the minimum errors obtained of all the methods, and the system essentially keeps the original structure. These findings show that the overall results obtained by the GGA-PBE-Grimme method were in good agreement with the experimentally measured values. The model and calculation method used in this paper were reasonable and reliable, and gave highly accurate results.

### 3.3. Effect of strain on the band gap of monolayer BP

There have been many reports on the regulation of band gap energies and the transition from insulator to metallic behavior under stress [36,37]. A 2D material is easier to compress or stretch than the bulk material. For example, 2D MoS₂ thin films transition from being a semiconductor to metallic under stress [37,38]. Inspired by these reports, we have studied the electronic structure changes of monolayer BP under planar bi-axial strain (i.e., simulta- neous proportional a- and b-direction compression or stretching). In this paper, for the monolayer BP band gap, both DFT-PBE and HSE06 methods are used in the calculations. In the following, the influence of strain on the electronic structures of monolayer BP will be discussed in detail. Table 2 summarizes the electronic band gaps calculated for different strain values. The band gaps of mono- layer BP with strains from −12% to 12% are given in Fig. 2. The pos- itive and negative strains relate to the stretched and compressed lattice constants, respectively. As seen in Table 1 and Fig. 2, using the hybrid functional significantly enlarges $E_{gap}$. However, the trend of the band gap versus strain remains unchanged regardless of the method used. While the $E_{gap}$ of strainless monolayer BP is calculated as 0.862 eV, consistent with previous calculations per- formed with GGA-PBE, it becomes 1.509 eV when using HSE06. In addition, we observe that the band gap of monolayer BP is very sensitive to the applied strain.

Fig. 4 shows that the band gap monotonously decreases with increasing compressive strain and eventually falls to zero. When the band gap is reduced to zero to form a gapless semiconductor, the VBMAX and CBMIN touch at the Fermi level G-point. That is to say, a semiconductor-metal transition (SMT) can be induced in monolayer BP by applying compressive strain. The scenario is different for the compressive strain. Fig. 4 shows that the band gap increases and then decreases as the tensile strain varies from 0% to 12%. To gain insight into the effect of strain on the band structure, the band structures of strained BP monolayers are presented in Fig. 5 for comparison with the case without strain as shown in Fig. 3(c). Fig. 5 shows the energy bands corresponding to six different strains: 3%, 7%, and 10% are tensile strains, and −3%, −7%, and −10% are compressive strains. For strains in the range of −7% to 10% (Fig. 5(a-e)), VBMAX and CBMIN are always found at the high-symmetry G-point. This shows that an applied strain does not change this characteristic of the monolayer BP direct band gap semiconductor. As the tensile strain increases from 0% up to 3%, the band gap gradually increases (see Fig. 4), which can be ascribed to the decreasing bandwidth. It is well known that the bandwidth is closely related to bond length, and large bond lengths result in small bandwidths. At a tensile strain of 3%, the CBMIN lies the furthest away from the Fermi level and gives the greatest band gap. At this moment, the energy level of the conduction band starts to be divided into two groups. Interestingly, the band gap does not increase with a further increase of tensile strain, but gradually decreases as the strain increases from 4% to 12%. With a tensile strain of 10% (Fig. 5(c)), two groups of energy bands above the Fermi level are clearly separated (marked as A and B in the figure).

The main reason for this energy band separation is that the dispersion force of the band decreases when the length of the P-P bond increases under the tensile strain. The system maintains the electronic structure of the direct gap semiconductor throughout the application of tensile strain. Through the analysis of the band structures arising from different tensile strains, we find that the conduction band gradually moves away from (approaches) $E_F$ and the energy of the valence band hardly changes, leading to an increase (decrease) of the band gap. The change of band gap is due to the change of the energy at the bottom of the conduction band caused by the strain, while the energy at the top of the valence band remains constant.

Next, we discuss the effect of compressive strain on monolayer BP. Comparing Fig. 5(d) and (e), it can be found that as the compressive strain increases, the dispersion of the band increases with the decreasing P-P bond length. The energy of the conduction band moves down at the G-point and that of valence band moves up near the X-point, leading to a smaller band gap. A compressive strain of 9% is the critical strain relating to the SMT; the VBMAX and CBMIN touch at the G-point of the Fermi level. When the compressive strain further increases, the SMT occurs in monolayer BP. As shown in Fig. 5(f) for a compressive strain of 10%, the CBMIN crosses $E_F$ near the G-point and VBMAX crosses $E_F$ between the X- and G-points. Previous reports on monolayer BP under uniaxial strain indicated [12] that SMT occurs, but the process requires more strain than in the bi-axial case. In addition, the uniaxial strain also induces a direct-indirect band gap transition; however, this work on bi-axial strain does not show this kind of phenomenon.

### 3.4. Effect of strain on the optical properties of monolayer BP

As a new type of 2D material, BP has great application prospects in the field of optoelectronics. However, there are few studies on the optical properties and anisotropy of 2D BP. The relatively simple electronic structure of monolayer BP determines its stability and excellent optical properties. In the following, based on the electronic properties of the monolayer BP crystal, the influence of strain on its optical properties is discussed in detail. The interaction between photons and electrons in a solid material results in transitions between the occupied and unoccupied electronic states, which determine the macroscopic optical properties of the solid material [40]. In the linear response range, the macroscopic optical response function of a solid material is usually measured by the complex permittivity $\varepsilon(\omega)$, expressed as

$$
\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_{1}(\omega)+i \varepsilon_{2}(\omega). \tag{1}
$$

$\varepsilon(\omega)$ describes the linear response characteristics of the lattice system to electromagnetic radiation, which determines the propagation behavior of electromagnetic radiation in the medium, and $\omega$

Especially, the imaginary part of the dielectric function, $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$, is an important component of the band structure [40]. In this paper, we mainly analyze the optical properties of monolayer BP thin films to provide a theoret- ical basis for the application of BP crystals as a 2D material.

Fig. 6(c) and (d) show the dispersion curves of the imaginary part of the dielectric function along the $x$-axis $(\varepsilon_{2x}(\omega))$ and $y$-axis $(\varepsilon_{2y}(\omega))$ with different strains. In the absence of an applied strain, as can be seen from Fig. 4(c), the primitive monolayer BP shows an optical absorption starting from 0.7 eV. Under varied tensile strains, all of the structures exhibit blueshifts, and the absorption edges become 0.96 eV and 1.36 eV for strains of 3% and 10%, respectively. However, the structures under compressive strains appear redshifted. $\varepsilon_{2x}(\omega)$ and $\varepsilon_{2y}(\omega)$ of monolayer BP under different strains show different peak positions, and their corresponding energies are given in Table 3. It is advantageous to identify the interband transitions that are responsible for the peaks in $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ using the calculated band structures shown in Figs. 3(c) and 5. Owing to the constraint of the selection rule for electronic interband transitions, the dielectric function reflects the characteristics of the band structure near the Fermi level. The detailed transitions are denoted by A, B, C, and D in Fig. 5, corresponding to the peaks in the imaginary parts of the dielectric function, $\varepsilon_{2x}(\omega)$ and $\varepsilon_{2y}(\omega)$. The primitive monolayer BP shows three major peaks located at A (5.51 eV), B (2.05 eV), and C (5.94 eV). Based on the analysis of the electronic structure, peak A mainly originates from the transition between −3.1 and 2.41 eV. The transition between the occupied states at −0.25 eV and the unoccupied state at 1.80 eV may lead to peak B. The highest energy peak, denoted C, may be due to the electron transition from the occupied states at about −1.84 eV to the unoccupied state at about 4.10 eV, labeled as C in the band structure in Fig. 3(c). For a 7% strain, peaks A, B, and C of $\varepsilon_{2x}(\omega)$ and $\varepsilon_{2y}(\omega)$ are attributed to the interband transitions of $-2.02 \to$ 3.39 eV, $0 \to 3.09$ eV, and $-0.97 \to 4.17$ eV. Peak B mainly originates from the transition between −0.28 and 1.43 eV, while the origin of peak C along the $y$-direction might be the $-1.35 \to 4.83$ eV interband transition. For a −7% compressive strain, the imaginary part $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ presents four prominent peaks labeled A, B, C, and D, which are interband transitions of $-1.95 \to 3.05$ eV, $-0.59 \to 0.83$ eV, $-3.11 \to 1.85$ eV, and $-2.48 \to 4.08$ eV, respectively. It must be noted that the indirect interband transition should involve phonon scattering. Hence, the indirect transition only gives a small contribution to $\varepsilon$ ($\omega$) compared to the direct transition [44], so we neglect this in our calculations. It is also worth mentioning that a particular peak in $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ does not correspond to a single interband transition, since many transitions can be found in the band structure with energies corresponding to the same peak [45,46]. Since the imaginary part $\varepsilon_{2}$ of the dielectric function is related to the optical absorption properties of the material, it is predicted from the dielectric function spectrum that monolayer BP will have a stronger light absorption in the photon energy range of 2–12 eV. In particular, under a compressive strain of up to 10%, the peak response range expands and the initial response decreases to zero. The above phenomenon is due to the internal electronic structure changes of the system occurring with the SMT under compressive strain. As can be seen from Fig. 6(a) and (b), the imaginary part of the dielectric function along the $x$- and $y$-axes exhibits a strong anisotropy at different strains.

### 3.4.2. Reflectivity
When light is injected into a solid surface from free space, the ratio of reflected to incident light intensity is given by $R(\omega)$. The optical reflectance spectra of single layer BP with different strain values along the $x$ (armchair) and $y$ (zigzag) directions of polarized light are shown in Fig. 7. The calculated results show that the band-to-band transition of monolayer BP mainly occurs in the high-energy region after 5.0 eV, and the overall reflectance is less than 50%. The reflectivity in the infrared region and part of the visible region (<3.0 eV) is less than 20%. There are three main reflection peaks along the $x$-axis in the absence of strain, which are located at 1.61 eV, 6.58 eV, and 10.72 eV (see the black curve in Fig. 7(a)). The maximum reflectivity occurs at an incident photon energy of 10.72 eV, at which the value is 0.29. When the polarized light is along the $y$-axis (the black curve in Fig. 7(b)), the overall reflectivity is higher than that along the $x$-axis. Also, there are only two main peaks in the whole reflectivity spectrum at 7.18 eV and 10.86 eV. The reflectivity along the two directions of the polarized light in the visible region is low. This phenomenon shows that monolayer BP is almost transparent to visible light. That the reflectivity is not 100% when the photon energy is 0 eV suggests that BP has the optical characteristics of a semiconductor [47], which is consistent with the analysis of the energy band diagram results.

As can be seen from Fig. 7, as the tensile strain increases, the peak reflectivity moves toward a lower energy and the peak height decreases relative to when no strain is applied. However, under the condition of compressive strain, the peak height increases with increasing strain and the peak shifts to a higher energy. We find that the compressive strain increases the reflectivity while the tensile strain decreases it, the compressive strain ratio has more of an effect on the reflectivity than the tensile strain, and the reflectivity along both the $x$- and $y$-axes exhibit a strong anisotropy. When the incident photon energy is greater than 18 eV, the reflectivity curves of the two directions tend to zero under all strains.

### 3.4.3. Absorption coefficient

All atoms in a crystal possess a fixed vibrational frequency. When the natural frequency of the incident wave is consistent with that of the atoms in the crystal, resonant absorption and the propagation of light in the material will occur. The strength of the transmitted light is thus weakened; this phenomenon is termed the attenuation of the absorbed light. The corresponding absorption spectra calculated for different strains along different directions are plotted in Fig. 8. Comparing the curves of the absorption coefficients obtained along the x-axis (Fig. 8(a)) with those along the y-axis (Fig. 8(b)), a significant optical anisotropy of 2D BP can be found. When the incident light is along the x-axis, a wide absorption band in the region of 2–14 eV is clearly observed for the strainless case (see the black curve in Fig. 8(a)). This range is consistent with the absorption band predicted from the imaginary part of the dielectric function above. This indicates that the light within such an energy range will be strongly absorbed by the pristine monolayer BP. Considering the curves in detail, several relatively strong optical absorption peaks at 2.23, 6.67, and 8.78 eV can be recognized. The optical absorption curves for strains of –7%, –3%, 3%, and 7% are also shown in Fig. 8. Strain changes each absorption spectrum distinctively. The absorption band shrinks once the tensile effects are considered. Simultaneously, the heights of the absorption peaks are also affected by the strain. With an increasing tensile strain, the width of the absorption band becomes narrower. However, the compressive strain widens the absorption band, and the absorption peak heights increase as the compressive strain increases. In addition, for the BP systems under strain, there are obvious changes in the absorption edge. Compared with the unstrained single layer BP, the absorption edge under tensile strain (compressive strain) slightly shifts to a higher (lower) energy. This indicates that an interesting blueshift (redshift) of the absorption threshold can be observed in the strained systems. When the polarized light is introduced along the y-axis direction and the system is free of applied strain (the black curve in Fig. 8(b)), the absorption edge (threshold) of the primitive monolayer BP is found to be at 1.46 eV, below which there seems to be no optical absorption. This value is very close to the calculated band gap (1.509 eV). The intensity of the optical absorption coefficient increases as the compressive strain increases and the absorption region simultaneously widens, which indicates that the system is more likely to be excited under the influence of compressive strain. This effect is consistent with the reduction in the band gap observed with increasing strain. As can be seen from Figs. 3 and 5, the energy level of the monolayer BP conduction band is shifted to a lower energy by strain. Therefore, the electrons need to absorb less energy to undergo a transition, leading to the redshift of the absorption spectrum. To summarize, we can adjust the light absorption of monolayer BP by applying different strains. From Fig. 8, we know that the absorption band of monolayer BP mainly lies in the ultraviolet region, and the absorption coefficient is smaller in the infrared and visible regions. Especially, in the infrared region ($E_{gap.}$ < 1.6 eV), the absorption coefficient tends to zero. Considering the absorption and reflection spectra, monolayer BP has a good permeability over the whole infrared region. The ultraviolet regions of these spectra are greatly enhanced, indicating that the material strongly absorbs ultraviolet light, so can be used as an ultraviolet-protection or ultraviolet-detection material. Notably, nuclear energy control materials are required to perform in precisely the high-energy region for which monolayer BP has a high absorption coefficient [48].

### 3.4.4. Loss function

The loss function describes the energy loss of electrons passing through a homogeneous medium. The peak of the function is characteristic of the plasma oscillation; the corresponding oscillation frequency is called the plasma frequency [43]. The electron energy loss functions calculated with different strains along different directions are plotted in Fig. 9. The maximum energy loss peak of monolayer BP with no strain lies at an incident light energy of about 11.36 eV, and the peak corresponds to the plasma edge energy of the crystal. This energy corresponds to the sharp decline observed in the reflection spectrum, and the optical properties of the material relating to the SMT [49]. With the increase of tensile strain, the peak value of the energy loss function decreases and its position moves towards lower energies. The compressive strain induces the opposite: as this increases, the loss function is enhanced, and the loss range expands toward higher energies. This is also consistent with the blueshifts of the reflectance and absorption coefficients with increasing compressive strain. The loss of light in this system is only limited to a specific energy range, so monolayer BP crystals can be used to improve optical storage materials.

## 4. Conclusions

In the present paper, based on first-principles calculations within the framework of DFT, the best computational method

was selected to systematically study the electronic and optical properties of monolayer BP with different strains. From these calculations, we have drawn a number of conclusions:

(1) It is known that DFT within GGA or LDA generally underestimates the band gap of semiconductors. It is therefore essential to use alternative methods to correct the band gap. In this regard, we adopted the more accurate screened hybrid functional of Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) to calculate the band gap of BP. Bulk BP is a semiconductor with a direct band gap of 0.254 eV. When the bulk material is reduced to a monolayer, the band gap is increased to 1.509 eV, indicating that the decrease in thickness leads to a significant blueshift of the light absorption spectrum, but does not alter the direct band gap properties of the system.

(2) The compression acting on monolayer BP monotonically reduces the band gap of the system, and even leads to the transition of monolayer BP from a semiconductor to a metal, forming a gapless-type semiconductor when the compressive strain reaches 9%. It is noteworthy that tensile strain only adjusts the band gap value from 1.268 eV to 1.845 eV. Therefore, we can adjust the band gap by applying additional stress to meet different application requirements. In addition, the change of an external field can be monitored by the response of BP to the external stress.

(3) Based on the electronic properties, the optical properties of monolayer BP were analyzed in detail. The peaks of the dielectric function and corresponding electronic interband transitions were discussed. The calculated dielectric functions and absorption spectra along two different directions indicate that a significant optical anisotropy appears, giving a strong directionality dependence in the primitive and strained monolayer BP. Also, a blueshift phenomenon has been observed by analyzing the curves of the dielectric constant and absorption spectra with different compressive strains. Such strain causes the absorptivity, static dielectric constant, reflectivity, refractive index, and energy loss function of monolayer BP to increase. However, tensile strain decreases the intensity of these optical properties and induces a redshift. Therefore, we can adjust the optical parameters by applying strain. Furthermore, the optical properties can be derived from the behavior of monolayer BP under an applied stress.

In summary, our theoretical results imply that strain is an effective way to moderately modulate the electronic and optical properties of monolayer BP for various potential applications. Through this research, the understanding of the photoelectric properties of BP is further enhanced, which provides a useful theoretical basis for the practical application of BP as a photoelectric material.
