# Semiconducting properties of pyridyl appended linear dicarboxylate based coordination polymers: theoretical prediction via DFT study†

Faruk Ahmed, $^{a,b}$ Joaquín Ortega-Castro, $^{c}$ Antonio Frontera $^{*c}$ and Mohammad Hedayetullah Mir $^{*a}$

Coordination polymers (CPs) in recent times have emerged as active constituents in many semiconductor devices like light emitting diodes (LED), field effect transistors (FET), photovoltaic devices and Schottky barrier diodes. An intelligent choice of linkers, careful selection of metal ions and post synthetic modification (PSM) can provide a better pathway for charge transportation. However, a proper understanding of the charge transport mechanism in CPs is still inadequate due to the lack of considerable experimental and theoretical work. In this paper, we address the theoretical elucidation of semiconducting properties and a probable pathway for charge transportation in three of our previously published CPs using density functional theory (DFT). These results help us to recognize the orbitals that have major contributions in the formation of the valence band and also provide the most likely pathway for optimum electronic communication. In this regard, the role of hydrogen bonding and unpaired electrons of metal d-orbitals is also established.

## Introduction
Research in the field of coordination polymers (CPs) has witnessed a remarkable escalation in the past few decades owing to their easy structural tunability and diverse functionality. $^{1-12}$ A large volume of this research has been directed to the applicability of CPs in various fields such as gas storage, separation, magnetism, catalysis, light harvesting, drug delivery and sensing applications. $^{13-31}$ CPs are actually inorganic-organic hybrid materials composed of metal nodes and organic and/or inorganic spacer ligands through coordination bonds to provide an infinite array. The reasons for its success are the careful design strategy, post synthetic modification (PSM) and easy fabrication for desired properties and applications. It is important to mention that the use of CPs in the manufacture of electronic and optoelectronic devices is limited because these materials exhibited low electrical conductivity. However, only in recent years, new strategies have flourished to reverse this fact. $^{32-37}$ The key reason for which CPs have been mostly missing as semiconductor devices in the recent past is the fragile electronic coupling resulted from the poor orbital overlap and subsequent absence of a suitable pathway for charge transport. Hence, in the modern approaches, judicious selection of metal ions for efficient orbital overlap and elegant choice of ligands and their functionalization provide an extended pathway for the optimum charge transport. These provide much scope for the application of these materials in many semiconducting devices like light emitting diodes (LED), field effect transistors (FET), photovoltaic devices and Schottky barrier diodes. $^{38-41}$

Our group is interested in the rational design and synthesis of lower dimensional (1D or 2D) CPs by the utilization of monodentate aromatic ligands, where they coordinate in the perpendicular direction. The 1D chains or 2D planes further assemble through various non-covalent interactions such as C–H$\cdots\pi$, $\pi\cdots\pi$, hydrogen bonding or halogen$\cdots\pi$ interactions, which may provide a better pathway for charge transportation through space. $^{42-44}$ Again, metal d orbitals can take part in the formation of the valence band and thus participate in the charge transportation through the band mechanism. It is also significant to mention that the availability of unpaired electrons in the metal d orbitals has impacts on the electrical conductivity particularly for the $d^{9}$ configuration of Cu(II) systems. $^{35,45}$

There are three plausible mechanisms which may be utilized to explain the charge transportation in CPs. These are (i) charge transport through space utilizing non-covalent interactions like $\pi\cdots\pi$ stacking interactions, hydrogen bonding, etc., (ii) charge transport through the covalent bonds, generally observed in molecular wires, and (iii) charge hopping. $^{35,46}$ The first two types of mechanisms are generally based on band transport. In order to understand the charge transport phenomena and the various factors that control the electrical conductivity, our group is keenly devoted to synthesizing novel CPs for enhanced electronic and optoelectronic properties. However, a proper understanding of the charge transport mechanism in CPs is still scarce due to the lack of significant experimental and theoretical work. In this paper, we address the theoretical elucidation of semiconducting properties of some CPs previously published by our group using density functional theory (DFT).

Herein, to explain the electrical properties, we have selected three CPs, $[{\rm Cd(adc)(4-phpy)_2(H_2O)_2}]_n$ and $[{\rm Zn(adc)(4-phpy)_2(H_2O)_2}]_n$ (1 and 2) ($\rm H_2adc$ = acetylenedicarboxylic acid and 4-phpy = 4-phenylpyridine) and $[\{{\rm Cu_2(muco)_2(py)_4}\}\cdot4{\rm H_2O}\cdot2{\rm EtOH}]_n$ (3) ($\rm H_2muco$ = *trans, trans*-muconic acid or 1,3-butadiene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and py = pyridine), as a model for theoretical study. $^{47,48}$ Compounds 1 and 2 have 1D chain like structures, whereas compound 3 has a 2D sheet like structure (Fig. 1). However, all of them possess some common characteristics: (i) utilization of dicarboxylate ligands with a rigid backbone to bind the metals present in the structure while pyridine ligands are projected in the perpendicular direction and (ii) they reveal Schottky diode behavior in the metal-CP junction (Fig. 1). Besides, compounds 1, 2 and 3 exhibit photosensitivity and the observed conductivities under dark and illuminated conditions for compound 1 are calculated to be $1.53 \times 10^{-5}$ S m$^{-1}$ and $3.04 \times 10^{-5}$ S m$^{-1}$ respectively and for compound 2 $5.90 \times 10^{-6}$ S m$^{-1}$ and $7.78 \times 10^{-6}$ S m$^{-1}$ respectively, while those of compound 3 are $7.54 \times 10^{-5}$ S m$^{-1}$ and $1.99 \times 10^{-4}$ S m$^{-1}$ respectively. Nevertheless, they also present differences such as: (i) metals (Cd, Zn or Cu), (ii) size and conjugation of the linkers (adc and muco) and (iii) aromatic co-ligands (4-phpy and py).

### Computational details
The $P\bar1$ primitive triclinic crystal structures of compounds 1 and 3 and $C2/c$ monoclinic crystal structure of compound 2 were optimized with the DFT method using the CASTEP program code of Accelrys, Inc. $^{49}$ The atomic positions within the lattice were optimized, leaving the cell parameters fixed. The calculations were performed within the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) and the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation for the exchange-correlation functional. $^{50,51}$ Norm conserving pseudopotentials were used in this work with the relativistic treatment of the Koelling-Harmon. $^{52}$ A plane-wave basis set with 600 eV cutoff was applied. The $k$-mesh points over the Brillouin zone (BZ) were generated with parameters $1 \times 1 \times 1$ with the Monkhorst-Pack scheme. The energy tolerance for self-consistent field (SCF) convergence was $2 \times 10^{-6}$ eV per atom for all calculations. The long-range dispersion correction has been included in the calculations with the Grimme scheme. $^{53}$ Band structures were calculated along the k-vector of the first BZ of the crystal and partial density of states (PDOS) was plotted with respect to the Fermi level with a $3 \times 3 \times 2$ grid for compounds 1 and 3 and $3 \times 3 \times 3$ for compound 2.

## Results and discussion
### Electronic band gap measurement
The results derived from these calculations confirmed that compounds 1–3 are semiconductors (Fig. 2). The experimental band gaps determined using Tauc's equation for compounds 1 and 2 are 3.51 eV and 4.20 eV respectively while that of compound 3 is measured to be 1.67 eV.⁴⁷,⁴⁸ Considering DFT as a ground state theory and band gap as an excited state property, some degree of inaccuracy can be expected. As it is known, DFT tends to underestimate semiconductor band gap values in some cases up to 40%.⁵⁶ Although the band gap value is underestimated, it is considered that the shape of the bands is correct. Therefore, it is quite common to apply the scissor operator that equalizes the difference between the experimental and theoretical values.⁵⁷,⁵⁸ It has been verified that the studied semiconductors are of direct type, correlating adequately with the experimental findings. Compound 1 shows a direct band gap from the G branch to F point of BZ. This analysis also indicates that the direct band gap is very close to other two indirect ones from F to G and from Q to Z points of BZ. Fig. S1 (ESI†) shows the reciprocal lattices of all the systems studied herein. Regarding Zn and Cu materials, *i.e.* compounds 2 and 3, they have principally direct band gaps in G to Z and G to F points of their BZ.

### Study of partial density of states (PDOS)
Fig. 3 shows the PDOS of crystal cells of three CPs studied theoretically and the complete PDOS is shown in Fig. S2, ESI.† This analysis helps us to understand which atoms or molecules are mainly involved in the electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity of a material is due to the flow of electrons through its conduction band or holes in the valence bands. For this, it is necessary to promote an electron from one band to another. In a semiconductor, it requires an energy equivalent to the band gap energy $(E_g)$ to excite an electron to the conduction band. Within the possible models of electrical conductivity, the band transport should be the goal when aiming for the highest conductivity values while designing new electrical devices.³² The energy levels described above in the band diagram (Fig. 2) are one of the main factors to understand how these systems could conduct electricity. A molecular orbital analysis is performed for the three highest valence levels and the three lowest conduction levels of the band diagram. ### Dielectric function study
In order to understand the electronic structure of the solid- state material properly, it is important to compute and analyze the optical properties. They provide some information regard- ing the character of the bands. In each material, the frequency dependence of an incident photon can be studied by comput- ing the dielectric function $\varepsilon(\omega)$. The real part of $\varepsilon(\omega)$ gives information about the polarization degree of the material upon the application of an electric field. The imaginary part is a good indicator of the absorption of the incident photon in the material. We have used the computed band structure to measure the optical response (Fig. 7). To do so, we have selected a photon energy range of 0-16 eV. The peaks can be assigned to electronic transitions from the top of the valence band to the low energy in the conduction band. Due to the an- isotropy of the crystals we have decomposed the dielectric con- stant into three main directions where the light falls on the crystals $(\varepsilon_{xx}, \varepsilon_{yy}, \varepsilon_{zz})$. In compound 1 (top panel of Fig. 7), only the beginning of $\varepsilon_{zz}$ (red line) falls in the band gap value obtained experimentally (3.5 eV); however, $\varepsilon_{xx}$ and $\varepsilon_{yy}$ of the dielectric constant start at 3.8 eV. As seen in the graph, the peaks in all directions of polarization of light are similar with respect to their widths, presenting an absorption range between 3.4 and 8 eV. However, $\varepsilon_{zz}$ being the most intense and almost double than other components, they differ with respect to the intensity. This difference is attributed to the fact that the direction of incidence light mainly falls on the aromatic rings of the 4-phpy ligand. Compound 2 also has an aniso- tropic dielectric constant with respect to the direction of inci- dence of light. In this case, since it is a crystal with monoclinic

Theoretically through TDDFT, we can calculate both the energy levels of the first excited states and the changes they undergo in the crystalline structure. These geometric changes could be related with changes in the energy needed for the allowed electronic transition. If the population of the excited state is large enough, an increase in the electrical conductivity could be detected.⁵⁹ In this work we have used the same procedure that has been previously tested.⁶⁰⁻⁶² The main geometric parameters of the CPs are measured for their ground states as well as the first two excited states. Compound 1 exhibits better photosensitivity as compared to 2. TDDFT study reveals that in 1, there are only small geometric differences between the ground state and the calculated excited states (Table S1, ESI†). All these differences together contribute to a small drop of 0.04 eV between the top of the valence level and the bottom of the conduction when we consider the 1st excited state as a target. However, compound 3 (Table S2, ESI†) showed a decrease of 0.15 eV under the same conditions. In this case, we can highlight the geometric variations of Cu-O1, Cu-O2, Cu-N1 and Cu-N2 with an elongation of 0.05-0.07 Å as well as the generation of a new bond between Cu-O6 in the 1st and 2nd excited states (Table S2, ESI†). These facts can be correlated with the increase in the conductivity of these materials under illumination conditions. It is well-known that light is an attractive external stimulus because of its easy access and fast response time.⁶³ However, other aspects are not studied here and they may also be related to the increase in conductivity under light illumination. These are the interface metal/polymer electrode, electrode work function, carrier density, charge density of states, etc.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, we have theoretically studied three CPs with the help of DFT study that experimentally exhibit electrical conductivity in the semiconductor region and reveal Schottky barrier diode behaviour in the metal-CP junction. To get better insight into the charge transport mechanism, PDOS and orbital studies have been carried out. In this regard, the role of hydrogen bonding interactions in better charge transportation in 1 as compared to 2 is correlated. Again, the role of unpaired metal d electrons in charge transportation is also established in the case of compound 3 as revealed by the valence band which is dominated by 3d orbitals of the Cu atom and 2p orbitals of the carboxylate ligands. Moreover, all these compounds exhibit photosensitivity and can be potential candidates for the fabrication of optoelectronic devices. Theoretical analysis indicates that there occurs a considerable reduction in the energy required for the allowed electronic transition from the valence band to the conduction band under light illumination. Again, the geometric variations of bond lengths as well as the generation of a new bond in the 1st and 2nd excited states can be correlated with the increase in photoconductivity of compound 3. This observation will provide a deep understanding of the charge transportation in CPs and also offer a rational design strategy for the synthesis of CPs for the fabrication of new generation electronic and optoelectronic devices.
