# Polymorphs of Cobalt Oxide

Robin W. Grimes*

Davy Faraday Research Laboratory, The Royal Institution of Great Britain,
London, W1X 4BS, United Kingdom

K. Peter D. Lagerlöf*

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Case Western Reserve University,
Cleveland, Ohio 44106

Through controlled thermal decomposition of cobalt acetate tetrahydrate, cobalt monoxide can be synthesized with three different crystal structures: rock salt, zinc blende, and wurtzite. Using electron microscopy techniques, it is shown that the polymorphs exhibit characteristic crystallite morphologies; "tetrahedral" for the zinc blende type and "lath-like" for the wurtzite form. Atomistic simulation calculations predict the lattice energies of the three polymorphs to be relatively close, with the rock salt polymorph being most stable, followed by the wurtzite form and lastly zinc blende. [Key words: cobalt oxide, polymorphs, crystallography, morphology, lattice.]

## I. Introduction

However, it is possible to synthesize CoO in two additional polymorphs; these exhibit the cubic zinc blende ($F\overline{4}3m$) and hexagonal wurtzite ($P6_3mc$) structures.¹ It is immediately apparent that in these less familiar polymorphs both the oxygen and cobalt ions are tetrahedrally coordinated, while in the sodium chloride structure they are, of course, octahedrally coordinated.

To date, the zinc blende and wurtzite forms of CoO have been synthesized only via the decomposition of cobalt acetate (ethanoate), butanoate, or octanoate.¹⁻⁴ The decomposition proceeds either under vacuum¹⁻⁴ or in an inert atmosphere such as nitrogen¹² or argon.³ Temperature control is critical, since the zinc blende and wurtzite structures are formed only between 290° to 310°C, while the rock salt material forms above 320°C. After quenching to room temperature, the tetrahedrally coordinated oxides are completely stable in air.

In the original study of CoO polymorphs, Redman and Steward¹ concluded from X-ray diffraction analysis that their oxides were a mixture of zinc blende and wurtzite forms. Although they were able to increase the ratio of wurtzite to zinc blende through temperature control or decomposition from higher molecular weight carboxylates, the wurtzite polymorph was never seen to form without the presence of some zinc blende material. They suggested that this was the result of mixed stacking similar to that found in ZnS and SiC. Redman and Steward also noted the broadness of their diffraction lines and assumed that this was due to a small particle size.

More recent work⁴ has used in situ neutron diffraction and thermogravimetric analysis to study the decomposition process. Using a heating rate of 1°C/min, thermogravimetric data suggest that in decomposing to CoO the cobalt acetate loses acetate ligands by a two-step process. Beginning at 180°C, one in four acetate groups is lost. The remaining ligands begin to evolve at 240°C. The neutron diffraction study showed that different crystalline phases form at each stage in the decomposition of the acetate. Work is in progress to determine the decomposition mechanism in greater detail.

In this current study, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) techniques were used to establish the morphology of the small zinc blende and wurtzite crystallites formed as the end products of the decomposition. The structural study is complemented by lattice energy calculations which predict cohesive energies, lattice parameters, and dielectric constants for different cobalt oxide structures.

## II. Experimental Procedure
The oxides were prepared by heating cobalt acetate tetrahydrate* at 1°C/min in a high-purity argon† atmosphere. Two samples were produced corresponding to the different final decomposition temperatures of 290° and 310°C. In each case, once the final temperature was reached, the oxide powders were immediately moved to an unheated portion of the furnace and allowed to cool to room temperature in flowing argon.

Following cooling, the samples were investigated using powder X-ray diffraction. Although X-ray diffraction patterns⁴ suffered from peak broadening, the results were clearly consistent with the assumption that the oxide formed at 290°C had the cubic zinc blende structure and the oxide formed at 310°C was a mixture of wurtzite and zinc blende.

To prepare TEM samples, the oxide powders were ultrasonically dispersed in acetone. A copper grid, on which a holey carbon film had been deposited, was then dipped into the dispersion and dried in air. To check that neither the acetone nor ultrasonic dispersion had changed the powders, samples were prepared using water (with and without ultrasonic dispersion) and also by shaking powder onto the holey carbon grid. No difference between materials was observed, but the acetone/ultrasonic bath combination gave the best dispersion of oxide over the carbon film.

The TEM work was carried out on an electron microscope‡ using conventional imaging and convergent beam electron diffraction (CBED) techniques.

## III. TEM Observations

### (1) CoO Formed at 290°C
Samples of CoO formed at 290°C show the presence of only cubic zinc blende structure crystallites. The size of the crystallites varied slightly, but were generally in the range of 100 to 300 nm (0.1 to 0.3 μm). (This is small enough to be responsible for broad X-ray diffraction peaks.¹ˣ³) A typical crystallite is presented in Fig. 1(a), with its CBED pattern shown in Fig. 1(b). Figure 1 shows that the crystallite morphology is essentially "tetrahedral," that is, crystallites have well-defined {111} facets which give rise to a triangular projection along a ⟨111⟩ direction and evenly spaced thickness fringes. Some of the thickness fringes in Fig. 1(a) are uneven, suggesting that in this case the crystallite is not a perfect tetrahedron. However, other crystallites were observed with what appeared to be well-defined facets on all sides.

The CBED pattern in Fig. 1(b) is readily indexed as a cubic [111] zone, giving an experimentally determined lattice parameter of $a = 0.46$ nm. This agrees well with the lattice parameter determined by Redman and Steward,¹ recent powder X-ray measurements,⁴ and the theoretical prediction presented in Table I.

### (2) CoO Formed at 310°C
Samples prepared from CoO formed between 290° and 310°C (Fig. 2) show the presence of crystallites with a very different morphology from that observed in samples of CoO formed at 290°C. In fact, Fig. 2 shows that crystallites of two distinctly different morphologies are present: "tetrahedral"-shaped crystallites (T), which are present in the sample of CoO formed at 290°C (Fig. 1), and "lath"-shaped crystallites (L) which are not present in the sample of CoO formed at 290°C. The "tetrahedral"-shaped CoO crystallites were readily identified to have the cubic zinc blende structure, whereas the "lath"-shaped crystallites were determined to have the hexagonal wurtzite structure.

The morphology of the hexagonal wurtzite structure CoO crystallites is shown in more detail in Fig. 3(a), with the corresponding CBED pattern shown in Fig. 3(b). Generally, the length of these "lath"-shaped crystallites varied considerably, but was within the range 100 to 2000 nm (0.1 to 2.0 μm). The lack of fringes indicates that each "lath"-shaped crystallite has a fairly constant thickness. The CBED pattern can be indexed to belong to a [11$\overline{2}$0] zone, giving the lattice parameters $a = 0.34$ and $c = 0.53$ nm).

All the "lath"-shaped crystallites studied had a nonsymmetric morphology (see Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 3(a), one end of each crystallite had a well-defined "tip" of converging {1$\overline{1}$03} planes and, at the opposite end, a "flat" surface which corresponds to a ($\overline{1}$100) plane (Note: because of the $P6_3mc$ space group, the plane (1100) is not equivalent to the plane ($\overline{1}$100), whereas the plane ($\overline{1}$103) is symmetrically equivalent to the plane (1$\overline{1}$03).)

Since the "flat" end of the "lath"-shaped crystallites was often observed to be embedded in other crystallites, whereas the "tip" of converging {1$\overline{1}$03} planes was always free, it seems natural to assume that the "tip" indicates the growth direction. If this assumption is valid, it would appear as if the "lath"-shaped crystallites grow via a ledge mechanism where the ledges are on the {1$\overline{1}$03} planes.

Lastly, we wish to draw attention to the observation shown in Fig. 4 that the "tetrahedral"-shaped cubic CoO crystallites can "grow" together by the "stacking" of several crystallites (C). This stacking of several "tetrahedral"-shaped crystallites may result in the formation of growth faults.

## IV. Lattice Energy Calculations

### (1) Methodology
Lattice energy calculations (carried out using the METAPOC code⁵ˣ⁶) were used to investigate the relative stability of the three oxide polytypes by comparing the energy of a unit cell of each material. The unit cell energy is the sum of component energies of all ions in the unit cell.

The computational method is based on a description of the lattice in terms of effective potentials and is in essence a Born model of an ionic crystal.⁵ˣ⁶ Each ion in the unit cell interacts with all ions in the crystal. Long-range Coulombic potentials defined between ions in formal charge states (in this case $O^{2-}$ and $Co^{2+}$) are summed to infinity using the Ewald technique.⁶ Short-range interactions, which are also defined between ions, are parameterized into a Buckingham potential form.⁵ˣ⁶ Since these potentials are effectively zero beyond a few lattice spacings, we need consider only a finite number of the short-range interactions for each ion in the unit cell. This is achieved by defining a short-range cutoff parameter (in this case 3.8 lattice units) beyond which short-range interactions are defined to be zero.

Ion polarizability is treated using the shell model of Dick and Overhauser.⁷ In this model, a massless shell is allowed to move with respect to a massive core, the core and shell charges being connected by a harmonic spring. Displacement of the shell relative to the core gives a good description of electronic polarization, which is necessary to calculate high-frequency dielectric constants. This study employs the core and shell charges, spring constant value, and short-range parameters derived by Catlow et al.⁸ Using this ionic lattice description, atom starting positions and lattice vectors are minimized using Newton-Raphson procedures.⁶ This is important in the present context as it allows us to compare calculated lattice parameters with experimental values. When comparing calculated results with experimental values, we must bear in mind that this is essentially a zero K technique. However, since the model will be used only to reproduce low-temperature structural data and as we are concerned only with differences in lattice energies, in these contexts the approximation is appropriate.

In previous computational studies of alternative lattice structure stability, satisfactory results could not always be calculated using a Born-like model alone.⁹ When the different structures incorporated transition metal ions in different coordinations, superior results were obtained if the total energy included a specific contribution that accounted for the changes to $3d$ energy levels in the different crystal-field symmetries. This energy, the octahedral site preference energy (OSPE), as determined from spectroscopic data¹⁰ for the $Co^{2+}$ ion, is 0.32 eV. We include the OSPE into the lattice energy as follows:

$$\text{total lattice energy} = \text{ionic cohesive energy} + \text{OSPE}$$

We find the ionic cohesive energies for the rock salt and wurtzite polymorph to be very close. However, addition of the crystal-field OSPE decreases the stability of tetrahedrally coordinated cations. Consequently, the total lattice energy clearly favors the rock salt structure over the wurtzite polymorph (by 0.27 eV). A similar result was found by Navrotsky and Muan,¹¹ who, using activity-composition relations in the CoO-ZnO system, determined a value of 0.18 eV for the transformation energy between rock salt and wurtzite structures in CoO. After wurtzite, the next most stable polymorphs are zinc blende and inverse nickel arsenide, both of which are approximately 0.1 eV less stable. It is clear the cesium chloride structure is significantly less stable than all the other polymorphs.

### V. Discussion

The electron micrographs show that the crystallography of the polymorphs is manifested in the crystallite morphology. For the cubic zinc blende material, which forms at the lowest temperature, the particles take on a characteristic "tetrahedral" shape (see Fig. 1). The hexagonal wurtzite polymorph, formed at slightly higher temperatures, exhibits "lath"-shaped crystallites, one end of which is flat, the other pointed (see Fig. 3). Since clusters of the "lath"-shaped crystallites are connected at their flat ends, we have assumed (see Fig. 2) that the pointed end indicates the growth direction. It is also important to recall that wurtzite crystallites were never formed without some associated zinc blende material, whereas zinc blende crystallites were synthesized independently of any wurtzite material. In addition, and even after ultrasonic dispersion, wurtzite crystallites were often observed connected at their flat ends to what appeared to be clusters of small zinc blende crystallites. We therefore suggest that the wurtzite polymorph nucleates from preexisting zinc blende crystallites. Moreover, nucleation may occur at growth faults or at boundaries between overlapping zinc blende crystallites (as in Fig. 4) which provide low-energy nucleation sites for the hexagonal wurtzite form.

It is interesting to note that the material formed with zinc blende crystallites has a wurtzite structure rather than the inverse nickel arsenide structure (which has almost the same lattice energy as wurtzite) or a rock salt structure (which has a more favorable lattice energy, see Table I). We tentatively suggest that this is a consequence of cation coordination, since in both zinc blende and wurtzite structures, $Co^{2+}$ ions are tetrahedrally coordinated, while in inverse nickel arsenide and rock salt polymorphs, cations are octahedrally coordinated. This interesting observation will be investigated in future work.

As to why the zinc blende phase is the first polymorph to form, we can only assume that it is a consequence of unique features associated with the relatively low-temperature decomposition of the acetate and that the particular bonding in the intermediate decomposition product precludes the formation of other phases.

### VI. Summary

We have observed two polymorphs of cobalt oxide, both of which incorporate tetrahedrally coordinated cations. The morphology of the polymorphs can be distinguished by TEM and we have identified the low-energy planes in each case. Although the zinc blende material forms first (at 290°C), of the three experimentally observed polymorphs, lattice energy calculations predict this polytype to have the highest lattice energy.

The rock salt polymorph forms at a higher decomposition temperature (above $320^{\circ}C$). Lattice energy calculations predict this to be the most stable polymorph. We suggest that its formation at lower temperatures is prevented by the decomposition process which favors the formation of a material with tetrahedral coordination of cations.
