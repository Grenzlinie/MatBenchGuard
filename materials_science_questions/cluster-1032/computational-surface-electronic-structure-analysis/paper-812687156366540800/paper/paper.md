# Nitrogen adsorption on Rh(110)
V. R. Dhanak, $^{*}$ A. Baraldi, G. Comelli, $^{\dagger}$ K. C. Prince, and R. Rosei
Sincrotrone Trieste, Padriciano 99, 34012 Trieste, Italy

A. Atrei and E. Zanazzi
Laboratorio di Chimica-Fisica delle Superfici, Dipartimento di Chimica, Università degli Studi di Firenze, 50129 Firenze, Italy

(Received 18 May 1994)

The bonding geometry of nitrogen adsorbed on Rh(110) has been determined by means of low-energy-electron-diffraction $I$-$V$ analysis. The nitrogen atoms are found to be adsorbed in the long bridge site with rows of Rh atoms missing in the [001] direction, and with a metal-nitrogen bond length similar to that found on other metal surfaces. This gives an overall structure in which the Rh and the adsorbate form metal-nitrogen-metal chains. Ultraviolet photoemission shows that the valence-band emission induced by nitrogen consists of a single peak which disperses strongly to higher energy in one azimuth. This is consistent with a band due to nitrogen $p$ states which overlap most strongly along the chains.

## I. INTRODUCTION
The major industrial application of rhodium metal is as an exhaust gas catalyst for nitric oxide reduction. As part of our program of investigation of the surface chemistry of rhodium, we have studied the structure of nitric oxide, oxygen, and nitrogen adsorbed on Rh(110), which are reactants and intermediates in this reaction. Here we report on the structure and bonding of atomic nitrogen on Rh(110).

This system has been studied previously by low-energy electron diffraction (LEED), Auger spectroscopy, scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), and photoemission. $^{1-7}$ Nitrogen can be deposited either from an atomic nitrogen beam, by dissociation of ammonia and subsequent desorption of hydrogen, or by dissociating molecular NO and removing the oxygen with hydrogen. When the surface is exposed to NO at about 450-480 K, the NO dissociates into atomic nitrogen and oxygen. The sequence of LEED patterns observed with increasing coverage is $(3 \times 1)$ at 0.33 monolayers (ML), a mixed $(2 \times 1)$ and $(1 \times 2)$ at 0.5 ML and $c(2 \times 4)$ at 1 ML. STM studies $^{4,5}$ have shown that the $(3 \times 1)$ structure consists almost entirely of nitrogen-induced islands formed by Rh-N-Rh chains along the [001] direction with every third [001] row missing. The oxygen in this structure is believed to be disordered so that no LEED structure related to it is observed. For the mixed $(2 \times 1)$ and $(1 \times 2)$ structures, STM images show nitrogen-induced islands of chains in the [001] direction with every second row missing, while the oxygen forms ordered islands with rows in the $[1 \overline{1} 0]$ direction and every second $[1 \overline{1} 0]$ row missing. The two structures exist as clearly separated islands on the surface, which is consistent with the LEED pattern being a mixture of $(2 \times 1)$ and $(1 \times 2)$. Subsequent removal of the oxygen by reaction with hydrogen or CO results in either a $(3 \times 1)$ or $(2 \times 1)$ structure, for the initial $(3 \times 1)$ and mixed structures, respectively. In the present study, the $(2 \times 1)$ nitrogen structure has been examined using angle-resolved photoemission and LEED I-V analysis.

## II. EXPERIMENT
The experiments were performed in a turbo and titanium sublimation pumped vacuum chamber with double mu metal shielding, equipped with an angle resolving photoelectron spectrometer and LEED optics. The crystal was cleaned by cycles of sputtering, oxidation, and hydrogen reduction as in previous reports. $^{1-3}$ The $(2 \times 1)$ structure was prepared by exposure to $1 \times 10^{-6}$ mbar sec of nitric oxide at 450 K to give a good $(2 \times 1)$ LEED pattern. The oxygen was removed by reduction in hydrogen, either deliberately introduced or from the ambient background.

The photoemission spectra were acquired using the program Spectra (Ron Unwin Scientific) and a VG Fisons UV He lamp. He I radiation was used, incident at an angle of $60^{\circ}$ along the $[1 \overline{1} 0]$ azimuth. The work function of the sample was 5.1 eV. The LEED $I$-$V$ spectra were measured using the Autoleed program and data acquisition system of Fisons Ltd and Omicron rear view LEED optics.

The intensity of the diffraction spots as a function of the energy of the incident electrons (LEED $I$-$V$ spectra) was measured at normal incidence of the primary beam. The intensities of the symmetrically equivalent spots were averaged in order to compensate for small deviations from normal incidence. The LEED $I$-$V$ spectra were background subtracted and normalized to constant incident current.

The data were analyzed using the tensor LEED program. $^{8}$ The tensor LEED method allows several trial structures to be tested in a very efficient way. The nonstructural parameters (phase shifts, real and imaginary parts of the potential, Debye temperatures) were the same as in a previous LEED investigation of clean Rh(110) and O/Rh(110). $^{9}$ The phase shifts for N were derived from a muffin-tin potential of a fictitious "RhN" compound.

## IV. DISCUSSION

Despite the relatively small data set, the level of agreement between theory and experiment strongly favors the missing row model with $\mathrm{N}$ atoms in the long bridge site on the reconstructed surface. The residual discrepancies between calculated and experimental $I-V$ curves can be attributed to disorder in the surface layer and to incomplete formation of the superstructure. The other structural models tested in this analysis, based on an unreconstructed Rh substrate, can be ruled out. The LEED results are therefore in agreement with the previous STM studies of this system. $^{4,5}$

The most relevant aspect of the $\mathrm{Rh}\{110\}(2 \times 1)-\mathrm{N}$ structure is the missing row reconstruction induced by the adsorption of $0.5 \mathrm{ML}$ of nitrogen. The same type of $(2 \times 1)$ missing row reconstruction has been observed in the case of oxygen adsorption on the (110) surfaces of $\mathrm{Ag}$, $\mathrm{Cu}$, and $\mathrm{Ni} .{ }^{11}$ In contrast, $0.5 \mathrm{ML}$ of oxygen adsorbed on the (110) surface of $\mathrm{Rh}$ induces a zigzag of oxygen atoms along the $[1 \overline{1} 0]$ close-packed chains of $\mathrm{Rh}$ atoms producing a $(2 \times 2) p 2 m g$ periodicity. $^{9}$ The relaxation of the first interplanar spacing of the clean surface is removed both in the $\mathrm{Rh}\{110\}(2 \times 1)-\mathrm{N}$ and in the $\mathrm{Rh}\{110\}(2 \times 2)-\mathrm{O}$ phase. $^{9}$

We can compare the $\mathrm{Rh}\{110\}(2 \times 1)-\mathrm{N}$ structure with that formed by $\mathrm{N}$ adsorption on the (110) surfaces of $\mathrm{Cu}$, where a $(2 \times 3)$ superstructure has been observed. $^{12}$ The results of a LEED $I-V$ analysis for $\mathrm{N}$ adsorbed on $\mathrm{Cu}(110)$ indicate that the $(2 \times 3)$ phase consists of a missing row reconstruction of the substrate with $\mathrm{N}$ atoms occupying long bridge sites. $^{12}$ The metal-nitrogen bond length observed here, $1.89 \mathring{A}$, is similar to that observed on $\mathrm{Cu}(110)$. In this case the $\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{N}$ bond lengths to the first four copper atoms are $1.83,1.88,1.88$, and $1.90 \mathring{A}$, giving an average of $1.87 \mathring{A}$. On $\mathrm{Cu}(100)$, the nitrogen is coordinated to five $\mathrm{Cu}$ atoms, with an average bond length of $1.85 \mathring{A} .{ }^{12,13}$ This value may also be compared with the Rh-O bond length of $2.0 \mathring{A},{ }^{9,14}$ found for the $\mathrm{Rh}\{110\}(2 \times 2) p 2 m g$ structure. As expected, the more highly charged oxygen atom is slightly larger and so displays a slightly larger bond length.

From the results of structural determinations performed so far, it is apparent that the adsorption of $\mathrm{N}$ and $\mathrm{O}$ on the (110) surface of fcc transition metals, at least for some coverage and temperature conditions, produces a reconstruction of the substrate. Although the adsorption geometry and the periodicity may be different, the common feature of all these reconstructed phases is the missing [001] row of the substrate induced by chemisorption. For the specific case of $\mathrm{N}$ and $\mathrm{O}$ adsorbed on the $\mathrm{Rh}(110)$ surface, it is difficult to interpret the different bonding geometry of the adsorbate and the different kind of missing row reconstruction of the substrate and adsorbate. A possible factor which might explain the different behavior of $\mathrm{N}$ and $\mathrm{O}$ adsorbed on $\mathrm{Rh}$ is the slightly larger covalent radius of oxygen compared to the covalent radius of nitrogen which renders unfavorable the formation of $\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{Rh}$ chains along the [001] direction.

With a knowledge of the bonding structure of the surface, it is possible to interpret the band structure derived from the photoemission data. The nitrogen orbitals which are available for bonding are the three $\mathrm{N} 2 p$ levels, Fig. 5. These may form bonds and surface bands by hybridizing with the surface metal atom states, and these states may be occupied or empty. Adjacent $p_x$ orbitals will overlap more weakly than the $p_y$ orbitals along this azimuth. The N atoms are further apart in space along $\bar{\Gamma} \bar{X}'$ (by a factor 1.414) and there is no metal atom between the nitrogen atoms. Thus we expect the band due to the $p_y$ orbitals to have a stronger dispersion along $\bar{\Gamma} \bar{Y}'$ than that due to the $p_x$ states along $\bar{\Gamma} \bar{X}'$. A band due to $p_z$ states should show intermediate dispersion along both azimuths.

Using the procedure of Plummer and Eberhardt, $^{15}$ the direction of dispersion can be predicted for the case of direct overlap of the orbitals. At $\bar{\Gamma}$, the $p_x$ orbitals form a band which belongs to the irreducible representation $b_1$, and overlap with destructive interference. At $\bar{X}'$, they overlap constructively, i.e., the energy is lower than at $\bar{\Gamma}$. Thus the $b_1$ band will disperse to higher binding energy from $\bar{\Gamma}$ to $\bar{X}'$, and should show little dispersion along $\bar{\Gamma} \bar{Y}'$, but if dispersion is observable, it should be to lower binding energy. Similarly, the $b_2$ band due to the $p_y$ orbitals will disperse to higher binding energy along $\bar{\Gamma} \bar{Y}'$. The main band observed showed strong dispersion to higher binding energy along $\bar{\Gamma} \bar{Y}'$. It is therefore consistent with the dispersion expected from a $b_2$ band consisting of $p_y$ orbitals.

As noted above, other $p$-derived bands with nitrogen character may be expected in this system, but were not observed. There may be several reasons for their nonappearance: they may be unoccupied, or strongly hybridized with metal states and so very broad, or simply too weak to observe under the present conditions.

## V. CONCLUSION

In summary, using LEED $I$-$V$ analysis we have confirmed that the structure of a $(2 \times 1)$ nitrogen-induced phase on Rh(110) consists of Rh-N-Rh chains along the substrate [001] direction, with every second chain missing, and we have determined the structural parameters. The N-induced structure has been compared to the reconstructions on the neighboring fcc(110) transition-metal surfaces, and possible reasons for the observed difference have been indicated. One nitrogen-induced valence band was observed by UV photoemission, and assigned to orbitals overlapping along the chains. This interpretation depends on a knowledge of the structure and is consistent with it.
