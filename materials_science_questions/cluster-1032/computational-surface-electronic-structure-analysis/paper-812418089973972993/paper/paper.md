$F_{A D}^{s}$ describes the noninteracting adparticle having $M$ orbitals of interest. $E_{A i s}^{0}$ is the attractive core energy; $U_{i j}$ and $I_{i j}$ are the Coulomb and exchange integrals, respectively. The ionization energies for the unperturbed adparticle are then given by

$$
E_{A i s}=E_{A i s}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{M} U_{i j}\left\langle n_{A j-s}\right\rangle+\sum_{\substack{i, j=1 \\ i \neq j}}^{M}\left(U_{i j}-J_{i j}\right)\left\langle n_{A j s}\right\rangle,
$$

where the expectation values $\left\langle n_{a j s}\right\rangle$ are either zero or one, because $F_{A D}^{s}$ is diagonal. The assumption inherent in the given form of Eq. (2b) is that the isolated adparticle can be described by a single Slater determinant. $F_{L M}^{s}$ comprises $M$ metal orbitals, localized at the metal surface in the vicinity of the adparticle.

The indented metal is then described by the term $F_{I M}^{s}$. The wavefunctions $|k B s\rangle$ are orthogonal to all the localized orbitals contained in $F_{L M}^{s}$.

Because the unperturbed metal surface is represented by

$$
\sum_{s}\left(F_{L M}^{s}+F_{I M}^{s}+F_{L I}^{s}\right)=\sum_{s, k} \epsilon_{k} n_{k s},
\tag{3}
$$

the matrix elements $E_{B i s}$ and $V_{i i B}^{s}$ are given by

$$
E_{B i s}=\sum_{k}\langle B i s \mid k s\rangle \epsilon_{k}\langle k s \mid B i s\rangle,
\tag{4}
$$

$$
V_{i i B}^{s}=\sum_{k}\langle B i s \mid k s\rangle \epsilon_{k}\langle k s \mid l B s\rangle.
\tag{5}
$$

The electron-electron interaction is not contained explicitly in the terms $F_{L M}^{s}$ and $F_{I M}^{s}$, but is implicitly-in the form of an average potential-allowed for in the one-electron energies $E_{B i s}$ and $\epsilon_{k}$. This means that these orbital energies will not show a self-consistent shift upon coupling to the adparticle.

The physics of the chemisorption bond is contained in the terms $F_{A L}^{s}$ and $F_{L I}^{s}$. $F_{A L}^{s}$ gives the coupling within the surface molecules, whereas $F_{L I}^{s}$ describes the coupling between the surface molecule and the indented metal. The motivation for choosing this particular coupling has been discussed in Ref. 4, and is therefore not repeated here. An important point is that the localized metal orbitals are uniquely defined by the adsorbate metal coupling. They are those metal wavefunctions which give maximum overlap with the adorbitals:

$$
|B i s\rangle=\frac{1}{\langle A i s \mid B i s\rangle} \sum_{k}\langle k s \mid A i s\rangle|k s\rangle,
\tag{6}
$$

with
$$
\langle A i s \mid B i s\rangle=\left[\sum_{k}|\langle k s \mid A i s\rangle|^{2}\right]^{1 / 2}.
$$

The interaction terms $F_{A I}^{s}$ and $F_{L I}^{s}$ are modeled to reflect certain special features of a metal surface, such as, for example, the dipole layer. These terms are reasonable only for the interaction with the conduction and valence bands. They yield an incorrect description of the deeper metal levels and of wavefunctions above the vacuum level. This model is therefore adequate to describe those chemisorption systems where the adsorption energy arises from a short range interaction between the adorbitals and the valence and conduction bands of a metal. Excluded are physisorption systems, adsorbates with large dipole moments, and ionic adsorption. It is also not useful to describe the penetration of a particle into the bulk of the metal, because in this case the hopping matrix elements might change with respect to their relative importance as well as with respect to their absolute value.

Because this model is designed to describe the interaction only over a limited energy range, the problem of overcompleteness does not arise. A serious disadvantage is then that the high lying, one-electron eigenstates of the chemisorption system cannot be calculated using this model. As a consequence, the final states for emitted photoelectrons are not defined.

The one-electron Hamiltonian discussed here is of a somewhat unusual form. It is, for example, not the Hartree-Fock operator of a many-body Hamiltonian. This is due to the term $F_{A L}^{s}$ [Eq. (2e)]. A corresponding many-body operator has to comprise terms such as $\mathrm{const} \times \tilde{n}_{A k s} \tilde{a}_{A i s}^{\dagger} \tilde{a}_{B j s}$, which would imply that the Hartree-

Fock operator should contain const $\times \tilde{n}_{A k s}\langle\tilde{a}_{A i s}^{\dagger} \tilde{a}_{B j s}\rangle$.
Terms of this kind are missing in Eq. (2). However, the solution of Eq. (2) gives a rather good approximation to a many-body Hamiltonian, which describes the electron-electron interaction within the surface molecule in much greater detail than is apparent from Eq. (2). The formal similarity between the operator used here and the Anderson Hamiltonian is therefore highly misleading. This point was discussed in Ref. 4, to which the reader is referred for a deeper understanding.

The results obtained with the help of this model Hamiltonian seem to shed some new light on the properties of the chemisorption bond. Although they are in accordance with calculations for the jellium model in the density formalism and with some elaborate cluster calculations (see Sec. III), they suggest in some respects a way of thinking which differs from that indicated, for example, by the Anderson model $^{10-12}$ and from the way a chemical bond in, for example, a diatomic molecule is usually visualized. The model implies a reduced core hopping, leading to a dominance of the hopping induced by the electron-electron interaction. As a consequence the adsorption energy is not gained by accumulating the charge in the bonding region between adparticle and metal surface, but rather by effectively keeping the electrons apart, achieved through a correlated hopping, which minimizes the electron-electron repulsion.

There are drastic effects on the energy distribution of the emitted photoelectrons. The widths of the adorbital resonances (virtual levels) are rather small, $^{5}$ which means that the experimentally observed widths should essentially be due to vibrational effects, electron-hole excitations, and other many-body contributions not incorporated in this model.

The calculations yield generally large initial state screening effects and small final state relaxation effects. This is in contrast to the interpretation of the experimentally found photoelectron energy distribution that prevails in the literature. This point will be discussed further in the remainder of this paper.

### B. Treatment of the coupling between the surface molecule and the indented metal

The Hamiltonian is difficult to solve, because of the coupling of the surface molecule to the indented metal. Grimley and Pisani $^{8}$ suggest the use of a Dyson subroutine for treating this embedding problem.

In our work, $^{5,6}$ a different procedure is applied: A broad electron band is split into several degenerate levels by collecting all the metal wavefunctions in a small energy interval into a degenerate band energetically localized in the center of that interval. If the degenerate bands are labeled by the index $\kappa$, the metal part of the Hamiltonian (2) can be written in the form

$$
F_{L M}^{s}+F_{I M}^{s}+F_{L I}^{s}=\sum_{\kappa} \epsilon_{\kappa} \sum_{k, l, m} \tilde{a}_{l s}^{\dagger}\langle l s \mid k \kappa s\rangle\langle k \kappa s \mid m s\rangle \tilde{a}_{m s}. \quad (7)
$$

The operators $\{\tilde{a}_{l s}\}$ comprise the operators $\tilde{a}_{B i s}$ and $a_{I B s}$. In order to demonstrate the equivalence with the Hamiltonian used in Ref. 5 we define (using the notation of that reference)

$$
a_{B j s}=\left(S_{j s}\langle B i s \mid B j s\rangle\right)^{-1} \sum_{\kappa} S_{i \kappa s}\langle\hat{K} i \kappa s \mid \hat{K} j \kappa s\rangle \hat{a}_{\kappa j s}. \quad (8)
$$

With the corresponding orthogonal complement in band $\kappa$ one then writes

$$
\begin{aligned}
& F_{L M}^{s}+F_{I M}^{s}+F_{L I}^{s}=\sum_{\substack{i=1 \\
\kappa=1}}^{K} \hat{\epsilon}_{\kappa} \hat{n}_{i \kappa s} \\
& \quad +\sum_{i, j, \kappa} \epsilon_{\kappa} \Delta_{i j}^{s} \hat{a}_{i \kappa s}^{\dagger} \hat{a}_{j \kappa s}+\sum_{\kappa} \epsilon_{\kappa} \sum_{\lambda} \hat{n}_{\lambda \kappa s},
\end{aligned} \quad (9)
$$

where

$$
\Delta_{i j}^{s}=\langle\hat{K} i \kappa s \mid \hat{K} j \kappa s\rangle
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{A L}^{s}= & \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j, \kappa}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{M} U_{i l}\langle\hat{n}_{A l-s}\rangle+\sum_{\substack{l=1 \\
l \neq i}}^{M}\left(U_{i l}-J_{i l}\right)\langle\tilde{n}_{A l s}\rangle+E_{A i s}^{0}+E_{\kappa}\right] \\
& \times S_{i \kappa s}\langle\hat{K} i \kappa s \mid \hat{K} j \kappa s\rangle\left(\tilde{a}_{A i s}^{\dagger} \hat{a}_{\kappa j s}+\hat{a}_{\kappa j s}^{\dagger} \tilde{a}_{A i s}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

In this notation the Hamiltonian equation (2) is identical with Eq. (12) of Ref. 5 (see Ref. 13).

We now have a different larger surface molecule consisting of the orbitals $\{|A i s\rangle\}$ and $\{|\hat{K} i \kappa s\rangle\}$, which, however, in contrast to the original "embedded surface molecule" described by

$$
F_{S M}^{s}=F_{A D}^{s}+F_{L M}^{s}+F_{A L}^{s},
$$

is completely decoupled from the rest of the metal.

The question arises as to how many degenerate bands are necessary in order to simulate the broad electron band adequately. This convergence problem has been examined in Ref. 5. It was found that about 10 degenerate bands are sufficient for a 10 eV broad $s p$ band.

The Hamiltonian of the new decoupled surface molecule

$$
F_{A D}^{s}+F_{A L}^{s}+\sum_{i, \kappa} \epsilon_{\kappa} \hat{n}_{i \kappa s}+\sum_{i, j, \kappa} \epsilon_{\kappa} \Delta_{i j}^{s} \hat{a}_{i \kappa s}^{\dagger} \hat{a}_{j \kappa s}
$$

is diagonalized by standard methods.

For evaluating the adsorption energy one must remember that the Hamiltonian $=F^{t}+F^{s}$ contains certain electron-electron terms twice, and that these terms must therefore be subtracted once from the expectation value of the total energy.

### C. Parameterization

The electronic struction of the undisturbed adparticle and of the clean metal surface is needed as input.

The atomic orbitals for hydrogen and oxygen are taken as Slater orbitals with exponents determined by Slater's rules. The values for the core energies $E_{A i s}^{0}$, and the Coulomb and exchange integrals $U_{i j}$ and $J_{i j}$ are

chosen in such a way as to warrant the experimental ionization energies being reproduced in Koopmans' ap- proximation. Although this contradicts reality, this procedure is useful for comparison with chemisorption experiments, because this model is designed to de- scribe only the changes caused by chemisorption.

The wavefunctions used to represent the metal surface are given in the Appendix and are the same as used in earlier calculations. $^{6}$

If such a parameterization of the separated system is used, the adsorbate-metal interaction is then com- pletely determined by the overlap between the adorbitals and the metal wavefunctions.

For any particular adorbital $|A i s\rangle$ the dependence of the overlap on the energy of the metal functions is im- portant. This relationship is contained in the "effective density of metal states with respect to coupling to the adorbital $|A i s\rangle$ ," which was defined and extensively discussed in Ref. 5.

For the calculations reported here the $s p$ band of the metal is represented by a three-dimensional free elec- tron gas in order to estimate the overlap integrals. This approximation effects only the numerical values of the overlap integrals. The coupling itself was mod- eled to reflect the characteristics of a surface.

This paper studies chemisorption on transition metals with relatively narrow $d$ bands. The effective density of states of the $d$ band is therefore approximated by a $\delta$ function in energy.

The method of estimating the overlap with the $d$-band wavefunctions has been outlined in an earlier publica- tion. $^{5}$ It has been found convenient to introduce a parameter $B$ through
$$\langle A i s|\hat{K} i d s\rangle=B \sum_{\mathbf{m}}\langle A i s|d \mathbf{m}, \text { opt. }\rangle. \quad(12)$$
$m$ labels the lattice sites and $|d m$, opt. $\rangle$ is that $d$ orbital localized on the lattice site $m$, which gives maximum overlap with the adorbital $|A i s\rangle$.

B is the only parameter in this model concerning the adsorbate-metal coupling. If fixed for one adsorbate on a particular crystal plane of a metal, predictions can be made for different adsorbates and different crystal faces. The variation of $B$ for different transi tion metals can be estimated. $^{5}$ Values are given in Table II.

### D. Formalism for photoemission into plane wave final states
As was mentioned in Sec. II. A, a final state for emitted photoelectrons is not defined within this model. It is therefore only possible to examine initial state effects, the outgoing photoelectron being approximated by a plane wave. The initial state can have an impor- tant influence on the angular dependence and on the rel- ative intensities in the energy distribution curve. It is through this influence that the photoemission behavior reflects the properties of the chemisorption bond. Al- though it is not a crucial part of this work, we there- fore prefer to include optical matrix elements in the presentation of the results for the ionization energies instead of giving just the change of the one electron density of states upon adsorption. The treatment pre- sented here follows the lines of Refs. 14 and 15. The derived equations are dependent on the model and re- veal how the special properties are reflected in the $U P$  spectra.

For this model Koopmans' theorem represents a rather good approximation for estimating the ionization energies. $^{4}$ The photoemission intensity is therefore given by
$$W(\epsilon)=2 \pi \sum_{c, s}\left|\left\langle f^{-}|\tau| c s\right\rangle\right|^{2} \delta\left(\epsilon_{c s}+h \nu-\epsilon_{f}\right). \quad(13)$$
$|c s\rangle$ is a chemisorption state, $|f-\rangle$ is a plane wave, and $\tau$ is the operator for the electromagnetic field. The chemisorption state $|c s\rangle$ is a linear combination of theadorbitals $|A i s\rangle$ and the isolated metal states $|\hat{K} i \kappa s\rangle$ :
$$|c s\rangle=\sum_{i, \kappa}\left(A i s|A i s\rangle+B_{i \kappa s}|\hat{K} i \kappa s\rangle\right). \quad(14)$$

The Fourier transforms of the isolated metal states need some discussion here. The localized metal state $|\hat{K} i \kappa s\rangle$ has the form
$$|\hat{K} i \kappa s\rangle=D \int_{\epsilon_{\kappa}}^{\epsilon_{\kappa+1}} N(\omega) \sum_{1 \omega}\langle 1 \omega s|A i s| 1 \omega s\rangle d \omega, \quad(15)$$
where $N(\omega)$ is the density of states and $D$ is the normal ization constant. $1 \omega=(l_{z}(\omega), l_{\|})$ is the quantum number characterizing the eigenstates of the clean metal sur- face. The optical matrix element for transition into the plane wave $|f_{z}, f_{\|}\rangle$ is then
$$\begin{aligned}
\left\langle f_{z}, \mathbf{f}|\tau| \hat{K} i \kappa s\right\rangle= & D N_{1}(\epsilon) \Delta \epsilon \sum_{k_{z}}\left\langle k_{z}(\epsilon), \mathbf{f}_{\|}|A i s\rangle\right. \\
& \times\left\langle f_{z}, \mathbf{f}_{\|}|\tau| k_{z}(\epsilon), \mathbf{f}_{\|}\right\rangle,
\end{aligned}$$
where the sum is over those values of $k_{z}(\epsilon)$ which satis fy the bandstructure. $N_{1}(\epsilon)$ is the density of states in the direction $(f_{z}, f_{\|})$ in $k$ space and $\Delta \epsilon$ stands for the width of the $\kappa$ th band. The formula becomes exact for infinitely fine subdivisions into degenerate bands.

$l_{\|}$ indicates the parallel momentum, whereas $l_{z}$ does not have such a clear physical meaning. In the case of a degenerate $d$ band, $l_{z}$ can, for example, be taken to label the lattice planes parallel to the surface. Thiscase was treated in Ref. 6:
$$\begin{aligned}
\left\langle\mathbf{f}^{-}|\tau| \hat{K} i d s\right\rangle= & \frac{\hat{\epsilon} \cdot \mathbf{f}}{c} A_{0}\langle\mathbf{f}|d^{\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}\right)}| \\
& \times \sum_{\mathbf{m}}^{-i \mathbf{f} \mathbf{m}}\left\langle d \mathbf{m}^{\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}\right)}|A i s\rangle.\right.
\end{aligned}\qquad(17)$$

For the chemisorption of some diatomics on transi- tion metals this effect was shown to lead to a character- istic antiresonance in the photoemission difference spectrum. $^{6}$ In the case of a simple $s$ band the disper sion relation is taken to be
$$k_{z}^{2}(\epsilon)+f_{\|}^{2}=2 \epsilon, \quad(18)$$


where only one solution for $k_z(\epsilon)$ is of physical importance and hence the sum in Eq. (16) is restricted to one term. The state $|k_z(\epsilon), \mathbf{f}_{\parallel}\rangle$ is now represented by a tight-binding wavefunction:

$$
\left\langle\mathbf{r} \mid k_{z}, \mathbf{f}_{\|}\right\rangle=L^{-3 / 2} e^{i \varphi\left(\mathbf{R}_{a d}\right)} \sum_{\mathbf{m}} a_{\mathbf{m}}\left(k_{z}\right) \phi(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{m}) e^{i \mathbf{f}_{\|} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{m}}}, \quad(19)
$$

where $L$ is the dimension of the solid; $\mathbf{r}=(z, \rho)$; $\phi(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{m})$ is the atomic orbital; $a_{\mathbf{m}}(k_z)$ is the tight-binding coefficient; and $\mathbf{R}_{ad}=(R_{AM}, \mathbf{R}_{\parallel})$ is the position of the adparticle.

The phase factor $e^{i \varphi\left(\mathbf{R}_{a d}\right)}$ is of physical importance at this point. The plane wave metal states used to calculate the chemisorption orbitals represent a special choice of phase factors, determined by the origin of coordinate space, which was chosen to coincide with the position of the adparticle.

In order to allow qualitatively for the short mean free path of the photoelectrons in the solid, we assume the case of strong attenuation, where all photoelectrons originate either in the adparticle or in the topmost surface layer. This means that the summation of Eq. (19) is restricted to the term $n=0$. Particularizing now to a spherically symmetric, real valued adorbital leads to

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle f_{z}, \mathbf{f}_{\parallel}|\tau| \hat{K} i \kappa S\right\rangle= & 2^{-3 / 4} \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \cdot \mathbf{f} A_{0}}{L c} \sqrt{\frac{N_{1}(\epsilon)}{N_{2}(\epsilon)}} \epsilon^{-3 / 4} \\
& \times \exp -i\left[f_{z} R_{A M}+\varphi\left(\mathbf{R}_{a d}\right)\right] \\
& \times\left|\sin k_{z} a\right| \int d^{3} \mathbf{y} e^{-i \mathbf{r} \mathbf{y}} \phi(\mathbf{y}),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $a$ is the lattice constant perpendicular to the surface and $N_2(\epsilon)$ is the density of states comprising the metal wavefunctions with $\mathbf{k}$ vectors perpendicular to $(f_z, \mathbf{f}_{\parallel})$. A variation with the polar angle enters through the terms
$$
e^{-i f_{z} R_{A M}}, \quad e^{-i \varphi\left(\mathbf{R}_{a d}\right)}, \quad \sin k_{z} a.
$$

The first term incorporates the influence of the distance of the adparticle perpendicular to the surface; the second term contains the lateral position. The third term reflects the nature of the substrate.

Numerical data as represented below were derived by the use of Eqs. (17) and (20).

There is an inconsistency in this procedure in that the overlap integrals are evaluated with wavefunctions different from those used for the determination of the Fourier transforms. According to Eq. (15) this means that the isolated metal states are the same linear combinations of $s$-band basis functions as used for the calculation of the adsorption and ionization energies. The basis wavefunctions are, however, given in a different form, which is necessary, because there would be no photoemission from a electron gas without surface. $^{16}$

The described formalism for estimating the optical matrix elements is used only to detect large and obvious differences between the separated and the coupled chemisorption system. In particular, the question is answered, whether loss or gain of photoemission intensity is expected compared to the unperturbed adorbitals or metal wavefunctions.

## III. CHEMISORPTION OF ATOMIC HYDROGEN

### A. General remarks

A fundamental contribution to the theory of chemisorption was the treatment of hydrogen adsorption in the Anderson model by Newns. $^{11}$ In this work only the coupling of the $1s$ orbital to the $d$ band was considered.

A method suitable for the examinations of hydrogen chemisorption on an $s$-band metal is given by the jellium model combined with the density functional formalism. Corresponding calculations have been performed by Lang and Williams $^{17}$ and Gunnarson, Hjelmberg, and Lundqvist. $^{18}$ The results indicate that upon decreasing the distance between the $\mathrm{H}$ atom and the surface of the $1s$ level becomes broadened and somewhat shifted to lower ionization energies; beyond the equilibrum position the $1s$ level drops towards the bottom of the band. The LDF calculations were performed for an electron density corresponding to that of aluminum $(r_s=2$ a.u.). At the equilibrium position an adsorption energy of $1.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ is obtained, which is about $60\%$ of the experimental value found for several transition metals, thus indicating the importance of the interaction with the $s$ band.

In order to determine the most important kind of interaction, our model was applied to the case where the $1s$ orbital couples only to the $d$ band. If that value of $B$ is used, which gives reasonable results for the chemisorption energies of diatomics, $^6$ then an adsorption energy of only $2\ \mathrm{kcal/mole}$ is calculated, which compares unfavorably with the experimental value of $63\ \mathrm{kcal/mole}$. $B=0.814$ would be necessary in order to yield the correct adsorption energy. However, even then striking discrepancies with experiment would result: The energy profile for $\mathrm{H/Ni\ (111)}$, for example, would exhibit a rather pronounced structure with energy variations of up to $22\ \mathrm{kcal/mole}$. LEED experiments have revealed, however, that an ordered adsorption phase exists only at low temperatures; at room temperature, disordered adsorption is observed. This means that the activation energy for diffusion should be smaller than about $3\ \mathrm{kcal/mole}$. With the same value of $B$ adsorption energies of $100\ \mathrm{kcal/mole}$ are obtained for $\mathrm{H/Ni\ (100)}$ and $\mathrm{H/Ni\ (110)}$. This too is in disagreement with experiment, where similar values have been measured for the three closest packed faces. The situation is qualitatively the same for $\mathrm{Cu}$ and $\mathrm{Pd}$.

These findings indicate that the $1s-d$ coupling cannot be the dominating interaction. The model for hydrogen chemisorption which we propose instead couples the $1s$ hydrogen orbital to the valence $s$ band and the unoccupied $2p$ orbitals of the hydrogen atom to the $d$ band.

### C. The photoelectron difference spectrum

The measured photoelectron spectra for H/Pd(111) and H/Ni(111) are displayed in Figs. 1 and 2 in comparison with the calculated difference energy distribution curves for special angles of emission (for the experimental conditions, cf. Ref. 21). The width of the peaks in the theoretical spectra has been introduced artificially by folding with a Gaussian of width 1 eV.

In the energy distribution curves three kinds of structure are distinguished. There is an antiresonance (decrease of photoemission intensity after adsorption) near the Fermi level accompanied by a small resonance at slightly higher ionization energies. The antireso-nace is due to the interference between $d$-band wave-functions and adorbitals, as discussed in detail in Ref. 6 for some diatomic adsorbates. It is more a matrix element effect than just a depletion of states. However, this decrease of emission from the $d$ band is not as universal for an adsorbed hydrogen atom as it is for the chemisorption of the investigated diatomics, because the coupling to the unoccupied $p_{z}$ orbital has a counterbalancing influence. The strong structure found experimentally is reproduced by the theory only if the hydrogen atom penetrates into the surface. The energy minimum as calculated within this model is, however, obtained for adsorption exactly in the first layer of metal atoms.

In Fig. 2 [hydrogen on Ni(111)] the intensity scale for the theoretical spectra has been multiplied by a factor 1/2 compared to Fig. 1. Figure 2 demonstrates that large changes in the emission from the $d$-band region might occur, despite the rather weak coupling to the $d$ band. Comparing this with Fig. 1c, we remark that a direct conclusion about the strength of the interaction with the $d$ band cannot be drawn from the observed change of emission from the $d$ band. A connection of this kind has been suggested recently. $^{22}$ Figures 1 and 2 demonstrate in fact that the optical matrix elements (and hence the observed intensities) are extremely sensitive to the geometry, the photon energy, and the angle of emission.

### D. Adsorption energy and energy profiles
The adsorption energies predicted for the most favor- able locations of the H atom are listed in Table II. If compared with experiment they are in the correct order of magnitude. For Ni the chemisorption is predicted to the strongest on the (100) face. This is in contrast to the chemisorption of the diatomics, where the (110) face generally turns out to give the largest binding ener- gy. This theoretically predicted trend is confirmed by experiment, $^{24}$ which for H/Ni(110) indicates a somewhat weaker bond (62 kcal/mole) than for H/Ni(100) and H/Ni(111) (both 63 kcal/mole). The reason as it emerges from these model calculations is that all $2p$ orbitals are unoccupied on the hydrogen atom, whereas for the diatomics the lowest unoccupied molecular or- bital has no $p_{z}$ component. The additional component enlarges and modifies the "total overlap" $T_{2p-d}$ and thus amplifies the structure dependent contribution of the back donation from the $d$ band to the adsorbate.

The energy profiles for H/Ni are reproduced in Figs.5, 6, and 7. In these cases the parameter $B$ was read- justed in such a way that the maximum binding energy on the (100) face reproduces the experimental value of63 kcal/mole. An interesting point to note is that for all calculated energy profiles with atomic hydrogen or oxygen as adsorbate the energy minimum never occurs exactly at one of the symmetrically preferred sites (on top, bridge, or center of the unit cell). There are al- ways small, shallow troughs around these symmetry points, where adsorption is energetically more favor- able. If in the remainder of this paper it is said that the adsorption site is predicted to be at one of those symmetry points, this has to be understood in the sense just outlined.

The prominent feature of the energy profiles for chemisorbed hydrogen is that the adsorption energy on the (111)- and (100) faces varies by less than 3 kcal/ mole. The reason is the weak $p-d$ coupling, which in our model is the only interaction depending on the ad- sorption geometry. Such a small variation is in accord- ance with experiment.

At room temperature no additional diffraction pat- terns appear in LEED investigations. $^{24}$ Measurements of the intensities indicate disordered adsorption. Be- low 270 K, however, hydrogen chemisorption on Ni(111) causes a $(2 \times 2)$ structure. $^{25}$ The rapid order-disorder transformation indicates that the activation barrier for surface diffusion does not exceed a few kcal/mole.

Recently a dynamical analysis of the LEED intensity for the $(2 \times 2)$ structure of H/Ni(111) was performed. $^{26}$ It suggests the position of the hydrogen atom in the center between three Ni atoms $\sim 1$ Å above the first metal layer. This would be in agreement with the ener- gy profile of Fig. 5. The calculated distance above the surface is $0.3$ Å, which appears to be too small and is simply determined by the maximum overlap between

the hydrogen $2p$ orbitals and the metal $d$ wavefunctions. The energy profile for H/Ni(100) (Fig. 6) exhibits almost no variation of the adsorption energy along the [10] directions, thus preventing any preferred adsorption sites. Recent attempts $^{27}$ to observe the formation of ordered overlayers with this system were negative down to temperatures of 120 K, thus indeed suggesting a rather high mobility of the hydrogen atoms on this plane.

The energy profile of the (110) face (Fig. 7) shows somewhat stronger variations. In the (10) direction diffusion is possible with a very small activation energy. The maximum in the center of the unit cell is not caused by the hardcore diameter of $1\mathring{A}$. The reason is that the overlap between the $p_{z}$ orbital and the Ni $d_{z2}$ orbital changes sign at small distances. On the (110) face hydrogen chemisorbs in an ordered $1\times2$ phase even at room temperature. Taylor and Estrup, $^{28}$ however, concluded from their experiments that this LEED pattern arises from a distortion of the configuration of the topmost nickel atoms. In fact, Demuth $^{29}$ was unable to reproduce satisfactorily the experimental LEED intensities by dynamical calculations for various adsorbate configurations on an undistorted substrate lattice. The best agreement was obtained with a reconstructed surface model, however without identification of the location of the H atoms.

The theoretical results for hydrogen chemisorption on copper are very similar to those on nickel. The effective density of states was constructed with the data of Table IV in Ref. 5. Table II gives the calculated adsorption energies, which are of the same order of magnitude as for H/Ni. The somewhat weaker $p-d$ interaction is compensated by the stronger $1s-4s$ coupling, which is even more dominating than for H/Ni.

The result that hydrogen chemisorption on Ni and Cu is dominated by the $1s-4s$ interaction has also been found in the EHT cluster calculations by Fassaert et al., $^{30}$ in the ab initio treatment of the HNi diatomic by Melius et al., $^{20}$ and in recent SCF-$X_{\alpha}$ calculations. $^{31}$ Reference 20 is of particular significance, because it includes correlation effects and hence avoids the disadvantages of the conventional restricted HF calculations. It is pointed out there that the $d$ orbitals prefer to become localized, if inclusion of configuration interactions opens this possibility. The character of the H-Ni bond as established by that ab initio calculation is in many respects similar to the character of the hydrogen chemisorption bond as obtained in our model calculations. Aside from the localization of the $d$ orbitals, Melius et al. $^{20}$ also find the coupling of the $3d$ orbitals to the unoccupied $2p$ orbitals. The comparison of the Mulliken population analysis gives (the values of Melius et al. are given in parentheses) 1s: 0.11(0.19); 2p: 0.02(0.06). There is a small charge transfer to the hydrogen atom, which in a simple minded picture would predict an increase of the surface potential upon adsorption, as found in experiment.

For hydrogen chemisorption on palladium our model yields a significantly reduced strength of the coupling between the $1s$ orbital and the valence $s$ band, as compared to H/Ni and H/Cu. Table II of Ref. 4 allows an estimate of the energy gain from this coupling as $\frac{1}{4}S_{occ}^{2}$ $\times(\epsilon_{Mo}-E_{1s}^{0}-U)$, where $\epsilon_{Mo}$ means an averaged ionization energy of the occupied part of the $s$ band. The subsequent coupling to the unoccupied part contributes approximately $\frac{1}{4}S_{unocc}^{2}(\epsilon_{1s}-\epsilon_{Mu})$ [cf. Eq. (50) of Ref. 4] with $\epsilon_{1s}=E_{1s}^{0}+U\langle n_{A-s}\rangle$. Because roughly $\epsilon_{Mo}-E_{1s}^{0}\approx0$ and $\epsilon_{1s}-\epsilon_{Mu}\approx4-5$ eV it is clear that $-\frac{1}{4}S_{occ}^{2}U$ is the main contribution. Figure 5 of Ref. 5 reveals that $S_{occ}=\int_{0}^{\epsilon F}n_{1s}(\epsilon)d\epsilon$ is obviously much larger for Ni than for Pd, because in the case of Pd the effective density of states is predominantly above the Fermi level. The occupied part is significantly smaller than for Ni. Simply speaking this means that the electron transfer into the unoccupied $1s$ spin-orbital determines the energy gain, which is proportional to the occupied part of the effective density of states. For H/Pd the decrease of the total energy caused by this effect is 27 kcal/mole as compared to 56 kcal/mole for H/Ni. Table II reveals that the order of decreasing adsorption energy is $E_{ad}(110)>E_{ad}(100)>E_{ad}(111)$, which is characteristic for the $p-d$ interaction on Pd.

As a consequence the energy profiles (Figs. 8 and 9) show a much larger variation of the adsorption energy compared to Ni. ### B. Parameterization

Within the present model the ionization of an isolated oxygen atom is described in Koopmans' approximation. This gives rise to some complications, because a single Slater determinant is generally only an eigenstate of the operators $S_z$ and $L_z$, but not of $S^2$ and $L^2$. The chosen Coulomb- and exchange matrices are listed in Table I. In the Koopmans picture there are then three different ionization energies corresponding to the removal of a $2p$ electron and reproducing the experimental values. The assignment to the states of the O$^+$ ion is given in Fig. 10.

The situation is less favorable for the removal of a $2s$ electron (Fig. 11). In this case Koopmans' approximation can only yield two ionization energies. There is no ambiguity for the quartet state $^4P$ of O$^+$, which is

![](./images/812418089973972993_10.jpg)

FIG. 10. Configurations of the $2p$ shell for the neutral oxygen atom (a) and the positive ion (b) under the assumption of Russell-Saunders coupling. Spin-orbit coupling is negligible.

reproduced with the given parameterization. The other Koopmans ionization leaves the ion in an (unrelaxed) state, which contains components of all three states $^2D$, $^2S$, $^2P$. The ionization energies for the "Koopmans shake-up processes" are calculated in order to achieve a proper assignment. We gain the $2s$-$2p$ exchange energy once (6 eV) and lose the $2p$-$2p$ exchange energy once (1.66 eV), thus leading to a reduction of the ionization energies of 4.34 eV for both the configuration $c$ and $d$ compared to the configuration $b$. This means that the second Koopmans ionization energy should be identified with the state $^2P$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
H= & \sum_{i, \sigma} E_{A i}^{0} n_{A i \sigma}+\sum_{i, j} U_{i j} n_{A i \uparrow} n_{A j \downarrow}+\sum_{i, j}\left(U_{i j}-J_{i j}\right)\left(n_{A i \uparrow} n_{A j \uparrow}+n_{A i \downarrow} n_{A j \downarrow}\right) \\
& +\epsilon_{d} \sum_{k, \sigma} n_{k d \sigma}+\sum_{k, \sigma} \epsilon_{k} n_{k \sigma}+\sum_{k, p, \sigma}\left(V_{p k d} C_{p \sigma}^{\dagger} C_{k d \sigma}+\text { h. c. }\right)+\sum_{k, \sigma}\left(V_{s k} C_{s \sigma}^{\dagger} C_{k \sigma}+\text { h. c. }\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The coupling was constructed in analogy to the treatment of the other adsorbates. The $2s$ orbital was assumed to interact only with the $s$ band, whereas the $2p$ orbitals were coupled to the $d$ band.

The results of the unrestricted Hartree-Fock calculations were qualitatively the same for all substrates, namely, a depletion of the occupied orbitals and an occupation of the empty orbitals by roughly 0.2 of an electron per orbital. This has a drastic effect on the one-electron energies given by

$$
E_{A i s}=E_{A i}^{0}+\sum\left[U_{i j}\left(\left\langle n_{A-s}\right\rangle+\left\langle n_{A s}\right\rangle\right)-J_{i j}\left\langle n_{A s}\right\rangle\right].
$$

The order of magnitude of the reduction of the Coulomb repulsion seen by every single electron is estimated to be $(5-2) \times 0.2 \times 15 \mathrm{eV}=9 \mathrm{eV}$. This is in obvious contrast to uv-photoemission experiments, where no additional emission in the range between 8 and 20 eV below the Fermi level is observed.

The effect of relaxation, as contained in the Anderson model, is to increase the ionization energies. Although the word "relaxation" is often used in the literature to indicate a *decrease* of the ionization energy by lowering the energy of the ionized state, this meaning does not follow from the definition of relaxation as an improvement over Koopmans' ionization energies. The fact that relaxation in the Anderson model tends to increase the ionization energies has already been discussed for the case of hydrogen chemisorption on copper. $^{35}$ For oxygen the adsorption on copper with its fully occupied $3d$ band is also convenient for an estimate of the relaxation energies.

In this case the interaction of the $2p$ electrons with the $d$ band is exactly described by unrestricted Hartree-Fock, as far as the ground state is concerned. (The interaction of the $2s$ orbital with the $sp$ band is a decoupled problem, which can be treated separately.) Figure 12 gives all possible configurations for distributing five electrons over tight spin-orbitals. The diagonalization of the Hamiltonian in the Hilbert space spanned by these Slater determinants yields the exact eigenstates of the ion. Because of the degeneracy of various configurations, it remains to diagonalize one $(2 \times 2)$-matrix and two $(3 \times 3)$-matrices. This has the consequence that two of the $2p$-derived ionization energies increase, whereas the third one remains trapped between them. The hole excitation spectrum is reproduced schematically in Fig. 13. The residua have not been calculated. The above argumentation has been performed for an orthogonal Anderson model; however, including overlap does not remedy the obvious deficiencies.

## E. Adsorption energy and energy profiles

The interaction of the oxygen $2s$ electrons with the $s$ band of nickel yields an energy gain of 28 kcal/mole. Including the $p-d$ coupling leads to the following ener- gies for the M-O bonds: (111): 49 kcal/mole; (100): 55; (110) 54. This means that oxygen would not chemi- sorb on Ni, because $2E_{ad}$ would be smaller than the dis- sociation energy of $O_{2}$ (120 kcal/mole). This result clearly contradicts experiment, where strong adsorp- tion is observed; although no exact data are known for $E_{ad}$ (due to interference with the onset of bulk oxidation), it certainly exceeds the value for Pd (~90 kcal/mole). A different parameterization of the oxygen atom (Cou- lomb integrals) cannot remedy the situation. The cal- culations for oxygen chemisorption on copper give very similar results.

The $p-(\pi-)d$ interaction is generally more important for adsorption on Pd than on Ni. This is due to the less contracted character of the $4d$ orbitals giving rise to a larger overlap. The greater value for the parameter $B$ enhances this effect.

The experimentally derived trends of the adsorption energy are reasonably well reproduced with respect to the variation of the nature of the substrate, the crystallographic orientation of the surface and with respect to various adsorbates on the same substrate. The only striking disagreement occurs with oxygen chemisorption on copper and nickel. It should be mentioned that other physical quantities (e.g., ionization energies, energy profiles) are predicted in reasonable agreement with experiment also for O/Ni.

If one wants to speculate on the reasons for discrepancies with experiment, it must first be pointed out that the neglected interaction of the unoccupied $3s$ orbital with the $s$ band might be of importance. A similar problem occurred for the chemisorption of hydrogen with respect to the importance of the $2s$ orbital. This was discussed in Sec. III.B, where the orthogonalized $2s$ orbital was found to have negligible influence. For the most localized, orthogonalized $3s$ orbital of the oxygen atom there is, however, a considerable interaction with the $s$ band, which might easily account for the deficit in adsorption energy. This neglected interaction would not be uniform over the series of treated metals. The gain in energy is proportional to the electron transfer into the orthogonalized $3s$ orbit and is therefore largest for metals having a high occupied effective density of $s$-band states. Hence it might lead to a considerably enhanced binding energy for oxygen on Cu, Ni, and Ag. and to a smaller increase for O/Pd.

The experimentally found ionization energies could be verified for nearly all investigated adsorption systems. This point deserves some more attention in connection with the common discussion of the experimental results in terms of "relaxation" shifts. We observe that—for obvious reasons—no reliable calculation exists, which gives support to this interpretation by comparing a realistic Hartree-Fock treatment to an even better calculation.

Our model interprets the reduction of the ionization energies in terms of large screening shifts, which overcompensate the relatively small bonding shifts. To be more precise these expressions are defined as follows: Let $\epsilon_{Ais}$ be the Hartree-Fock orbital energy corresponding to an eigenstate of the Fock operator $F^s$, and $|Ais\rangle$ the unperturbed adorbital. Then the screening shift is defined by
$$\Delta \epsilon_{s}=\langle Ais\left|F^{s}\right|Ais\rangle-E_{Ais}$$
and the bonding shift
$$\Delta \epsilon_{b}=\epsilon_{Ais}-\langle Ais\left|F^{s}\right|Ais\rangle.$$

Only the electron-electron interaction contributes to the screening shift, whereas the bonding shift arises mainly through the positive core potential. It is a particular property of the model used here that it is possible to assign these specific physical meanings to the defined quantities. For a profound understanding and the connection with the adsorption energy the reader is referred to Ref. 4.

Table III collects the ionization energies for the examined systems and displays the respective screening and bonding shifts. The screening shift is generally an order of magnitude larger than the bonding shift and is seen to be responsible for the ubiquitous decrease of the ionization energies upon adsorption. Some examples demonstrate that the adsorption energy has a rather minor influence on the peak position. These conclusions are fully supported by experiment.

Shifts of the excitation energies found through this procedure should not be confused with relaxation shifts, which have to be small in our model and are not considered. A proper calculation as indicated is, however, extremely difficult, especially if it is combined with an estimation of the excitation probabilities. No attempt in this direction was therefore made.

One comment might, however, be appropriate. For a large number of adsorption systems energy loss in the region between 13 and 15 eV was found experimentally. Table IV demonstrates that within this model this universal loss can be traced back to the same physical origin: The excitation of an electron from the $d$ band to the unoccupied $p-$ or $\pi$ orbitals. This state of the excited chemisorption system corresponds asymptotically (i.e., for large separation between the metal surface and the adparticle) to a resonant electron scattering state of the neutral adsorbate as discussed, for example, in Ref. 19. This state will probably have a width of several eV. Upon desorption it would therefore rapidly decay into the (possibly excited) neutral adparticle and a free electron. It is important to notice that this excited chemisorption state is not asymptotically identical with the (stable) negative ion, obtained by transferring one electron to the affinity level. It is only for the higher lying resonant levels that the nature of the adsorbate is of less importance and an approximately adsorbate independent energy loss can be expected.

The given theoretical values result from Koopmans' theorem within the restricted basis. The basis was, however, modeled to describe the ground state and therefore basis effects might reduce these values by several eV.

The limitations of the present work have been emphasized throughout this paper. With respect to the photoemission spectra there is the disadvantage that the calculated widths of the adsorbate induced peaks are much too small. This might, for example, reflect the neglect of vibrational structure and of many-body effects (particle-hole excitations). There is also the restriction to practically homopolar (neutral) chemisorption bonds. Heteropolar chemisorption would require the inclusion of the coupling to the plasmons (image charge effects). This opens possibilities for extensions and further theoretical investigations.
