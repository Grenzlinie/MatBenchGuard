## Abstract
Donor doping is commonly applied for softening of the piezoelectric and dielectric properties and facilitation of polarization switching in the ubiquitous Pb(Zr, Ti)O₃ [PZT] ceramics. The origin of the donor-dopant effects is not entirely clear. (Pb,Ba)ZrO₃ [PBZ] is a related ferroelectric material, its perovskite A-site being partially occupied by the larger Ba⁺² cation, less prone to evaporation than Pb⁺², and the B-site is occupied entirely by the valency-stable Zr⁺⁴. Here we report on our studies of Nb⁺⁵ doping effects in (Pb,Ba)ZrO₃. Similarly, to past observations on La⁺³ and Nb⁺⁵ doped PZT, we find a strong reduction in relative density of PBZ when the doping is <0.5 atomic %. This is accompanied by lattice parameter reduction, enhanced PbO loss, smaller grain size and deterioration of dielectric, piezoelectric and polarization switching properties, the latter being opposite of expected softening effect. All those observations can be interpreted in terms of the Nb entering A-site at small concentrations. This is supported by ab-initio calculations and analysis of the possible defect reaction equations. The structure and microstructure of PBZ with Nb>0.2% are consistent with Nb⁺⁵ entering the B-site and softening effects are observed. The study supports the scenario of hardening due to domain walls pinning by V_Pb-V_O divacancies and softening upon decrease in their concentration.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The electromechanical properties of ferroelectric materials can be modified by doping in order to meet the requirements imposed by applications.¹ In lead-based Pb⁺²B⁺⁴O₃ perovskite materials, heterovalent cation dopants replace, depending on ion radii, the Pb⁺² and B⁺⁴ (for example zirconium or titanium) sites, creating Pb, B, or O site vacancies. High valence dopants (La⁺³, Nb⁺⁵) are donors due to the excess of electrons, and their substitution to A and B sites, respectively, creates A- or B-site vacancies.²⁴ Conversely, ions like Na⁺ and Fe⁺³ act as acceptors, and their substitution into the Pb and B sites, respectively, creates O-vacancies.⁴⁶ This is well-known in Pb(Zr,Ti)O₃ (PZT) ceramics, in which even a small amount of dopant affects macroscopic properties and makes the ferroelectric hard or soft. However, previous investigations in La-doped PZT systems showed that an addition of very low dopant concentrations leads to high porosities and therefore to low and irreproducible electromechanical properties.⁷ Consequently, in terms of application, those compositions were not of interest and no further investigations have been performed.

Lead zirconate, PbZrO₃ (PZ), is the first identified antiferroelectric and an end-member of the highly useful

solid-solution series $PbZrO_3$–$PbTiO_3$ (PZT). At room temperature PZ is antiferroelectric but at elevated temperatures a ferroelectric (FE) phase exists over a very narrow temperature range $(230$-$233^\circ$C).$^8$ The FE phase can become dominant by partial replacement of $Zr^{+4}$ ions for $Ti^{+4}$ ions (PZT ceramics) or by replacing $Pb^{2+}$ by $Ba^{2+}$ ions $(Pb,Ba)ZrO_3$ (PBZ). In the latter case, the temperature ranges of this intermediate ferrioelectric phase increases with increased Ba concentration. At 23% of barium, the material is a normal ferroelectric, with rhombohedral structure at room temperature and phase transition into paraelectric phase takes place at $130^\circ$C.$^9$ The material presents the opportunity to study the influence of doping in a Pb-based material different from the classical PZT. This has been rarely done and would help to generalize the concepts of softening and hardening in piezoelectric materials by doping. The doping studies were previously mainly done either in Pb-based (such as $PbTiO_3$ or Pb $(Zr,Ti)O_3)$ or lead-free $(KNbO_3$, $BaTiO_3$, $BiFeO_3)$ materials.

Here, we report a detailed analysis of the properties of PBZ ceramics with various Nb content, in order to understand the influence of the dopant on the microstructural development of the material (porosity, grain size, and chemical homogeneity) and the related electromechanical properties for low and high percentage of dopants.

## 2 | EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

The samples were prepared by conventional mixed oxide solid-state reaction method. PbO (99.99% purity PbO Sigma-Aldrich, GmbH, Steinheim, Germany), $ZrO_2$ (99.99% purity Sigma-Aldrich), $BaCO_3$ (Alfa Aeser 99.95% purity, GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany) and $Nb_2O_5$ (99.99% purity Nb2O5 Alfa Aesar) were mixed for 24 hour, using zirconia balls as the grinding media in stoichiometric amounts according to the formula $(Pb_{0.77}Ba_{0.23})(Zr_{1-x}Nb_x)O_3$ (PBZ-xNb) with $x$=0, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.0015, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.0155, 0.0 2. The reaction sequence to obtain PBZ and PBZ-Nb was analyzed, with the adequate mixture of the precursors, by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) in air with heating and cooling rates of $10^\circ$C/min. Our objective is to show relative changes rather than performing a quantitative analysis: due to difficulties in calibration, the results displayed are provided in microvolts and it was not converted in a heat flux difference.

Samples were prepared as described in.$^{10}$

TEM was performed with a Philips CM300 instrument using an acceleration voltage of 300 kV and the microstructural features were analysed in a Jeol/5800LV scanning electron microscope (SEM). Fracture images were obtained for ceramics without any etching process and surface images were done in samples polished with diamond past (5 $\mu$m, 3 $\mu$m ,1 $\mu$m successively) and thermally etched at $1050^\circ$C for 1 minute (cooling and heating rate $20^\circ$C/min).

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) was carried out on powders as well as pellets of the materials using a Bruker D8 (Bruker-AXS GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany) DISCOVER X-ray diffractometer with monochromatic CuK$\alpha$l radiation (1.540596 Å) and a position sensitive detector in the $2\theta$ range from $5^\circ$ to $85^\circ$. Lattice parameters were calculated from XRD patterns assuming a rhombohedral perovskite-type structure, using a least mean square method.$^{11}$

For electrical measurements, Au electrodes were sputtered on polished major surfaces of the disk shaped samples (0.5-1 mm thickness and -8 mm diameter). The dielectric permittivity was measured as a function of temperature at several frequencies of the driving field with amplitude 1 Vrms using an HP 4284A Precision LCR meter, while sample was placed in a Delta 9023 temperature chamber or a CTI-cryogenics refrigerator model 22. Measurements were made during cooling, with the rate of 2 K/min at various frequencies from 1.0 kHz to 1.0 MHz.

Piezoelectric properties were measured using gold-coated polished ceramic disks, which were poled using a DC field of 2.5 kV/mm applied for 20 minutes at $30^\circ$C in silicon oil. Charge constant $d_{33}$ was measured using a Berlincourt $d_{33}$-meter. The polarization-electric field behavior was characterized using a modified Sawyer-Tower circuit at 10 Hz and various electric fields with amplitudes from 5 to 30 kV/cm.

### 2.1 | First-principles methodology

We used density-functional theory with the PBEsol exchange-correlation functional $^{12}$ using ultrasoft pseudopotentials $^{13}$ and a plane-waves basis set, as implemented in the Quantum ESPRESSO distribution.$^{14}$ The kinetic energy and charge density cutoffs utilized are 60 Ry and 600 Ry respectively and a 2*2*2 shifted Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh is used for Brillouin-zone sampling in the 135-atom 3*3*3 supercells of $PbTiO_3$ used. Atomic positions are allowed to relax until forces on atoms are smaller than $10^{-4}$ Ryd/bohr. The calculated ground state lattice parameter and c/a ratio of tetragonal $PbTiO_3$ are 3.88 Å and 1.072 respectively. This is in very good agreement with experiments (3.90 Å and $1.064^{15}$). The total energy of the $V_{Pb}''' - V_O^{\bullet\bullet}$ defect associate in the presence of Nb substituent ($E_{V_{Pb}'''-V_O^{\bullet\bullet}-Nb_{Ti}^\bullet}$) is obtained by selecting the lowest energy configuration among multiple configurations of the $V_{Pb}''' - V_O^{\bullet\bullet} - Nb_{Ti}^\bullet$ defect associate. The charge state of the defects is specified using the Kröger-Vink notation. These charge states have been shown to be the most stable in other first-principles calculations and EPR measurements.$^{16}$ In the case of supercells containing a charged defect (eg $E_{Nb_{Ti}^\bullet}$), a compensating jellium background of opposite charge is inserted to remove divergences.

## 3 | RESULTS

Figure 1A depicts the DSC curve for a mixture of PbO, $\text{ZrO}_2$, $\text{BaCO}_3$, and $\text{Nb}_2\text{O}_5$ used to form the PBZ and PBZ-xNb. At $200^\circ\text{C$-$400^\circ\text{C}$, water and organics decomposition occur that can be associated to a large peak in the DSC curve, while the DSC event at $522^\circ\text{C}$ is associated with the allotropic transition $\gamma\text{-BaCO}_3\rightarrow\alpha\text{-BaCO}_3.^{17}$ A change in the derivative around $800^\circ\text{C}$ is associated with the solid-state reaction between PbO, $\text{ZrO}_2$, and $\text{BaCO}_3$. These results indicate that the PBZ is formed in the range of $800^\circ\text{C$-$1000^\circ\text{C}$. The best processing range for the kinetics of this solid-state reaction was determined to be between $850^\circ\text{C}$ and $900^\circ\text{C}$. A comparison between the DSC curves of pure PBZ and PBZ with 0.1%Nb (PbO, $\text{ZrO}_2$, $\text{BaCO}_3$, and $\text{Nb}_2\text{O}_5$) shows, in the Nb containing powder, an additional peak around $600^\circ\text{C}$. This can be an indication of formation of an intermediate pyrochlore phase, recurrent in materials based on Nb and Pb $^{18,19}$ (eg, $\text{Pb}_3\text{Nb}_4\text{O}_{13}$). However, no pyrochlore phase was detected in the XRD of the final powders after calcination. Similar results were obtained for the other doped samples. All powders were thus calcined at $850^\circ\text{C}$.

A typical example of a sintering shrinkage curve is given in Figure 1B. At low temperatures, only thermal expansion of the green body is recorded. When $800^\circ\text{C}$ is reached, the shrinkage process begins, and the material starts to sinter. The maximum shrinkage rate takes place at $1300^\circ\text{C}$ for undoped PBZ samples and at a lower temperature for Nb doped samples ($1210^\circ\text{C}$ for 0.1% of Nb) (see linear shrinkage rate, continuous curve at Figure 1B). These results indicate that the PBZ ceramics could be sintered in the range from $1150^\circ\text{C}$ to $1350^\circ\text{C}$. Density analysis indicated the best sintering conditions to be $1250^\circ\text{C}$ during 2 hours. The sintering rate reveals two peaks for the doped samples containing 0.2% of Nb or less, whereas the pure one and the samples with more than 0.2% of Nb reveal only one peak, as shown for PBZ-0.1%Nb in Figure 1B. Taking into account that the melting point of PbO is around $\sim$$900^\circ\text{C}$, the peak at lower temperatures (around $900^\circ\text{C}$) is assumed to be associated with formation of a PbO-rich liquid phase, which could not be detected in the final samples, $^{20}$ but reflects in the grain morphology of the samples (Figure 4).

We have considered other defect reactions and only the two discussed below are in agreement with all-experimental data. It is clear from Equation (1) and (2) that both undoped and Nb-doped samples contain lead vacancies, which are conducive for effective densification. The fact that number of oxygen vacancies is reduced in Nb-doped material apparently does not affect much the densification process (Figure 3). It is useful to recall again that the anomaly happens only with donor but not acceptor dopants. $^{24}$ This clearly indicates that the phenomenon is linked to formation of lead and oxygen vacancies, which are favored by donor and acceptor dopants, respectively. A reduced concentration of $\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{P b}}^{\prime\prime}-\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}$ divacancies would decrease diffusion of material during sintering and lead to a low density. Apparently, this concentration of lead vacancies enables sufficient diffusion needed for densification.

The relationship between the nature of dopants and propensity of Pb- based perovskite oxides to lose PbO suggests an alternative way to look at the origin of softening and hardening. Undoped Pb-based materials naturally exhibit a certain concentration of $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ divacancies. As shown in a recent first principal study, $^{26}$ the divacancies interact with the polarization within the domains pining domain walls and the undoped material behaves as hard.$^{27}$ The result on formation energy of $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ presented here shows that in acceptor doped materials the energy formation of these divacancies is drastically reduced, leading thus to an increased concentration of $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ pairs relative to undoped material, which, together with acceptor-oxygen vacancy complexes pin domain walls and make material harder than undoped material. In soft materials, a donor cation is balanced by lead vacancies creating loose complexes which only weakly interact with polarization. More importantly, the concentration of $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ is reduced in donor doped materials owing to a larger formation energy of the divacancies, making the material softer than the undoped one. Thus, while it is commonly assumed that the origin of hardening and softening is related solely to different concentrations of oxygen vacancies in these materials, we propose that the origin is rather linked to different concentration of $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ divacancies. Whereas any atomic defect, including oxygen vacancies, will act as a pinning centre for domain walls, $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ have been shown to be particularly effective in the pinning.$^{26}$

We propose a second mechanism to explain a minimum in density for low concentration of donor dopants. Let's assume that at low concentration of donor dopant (<0.2 mol%), a fraction of existing lead vacancies is filled with the doping cations. This would lead to compensation of Nb partially by B-site vacancies, $V_{\mathrm{Zr}}$, and partially by A site vacancies, $V_{\mathrm{Pb}}^{\prime\prime}$ (equation 4):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\mathrm{Pb}, \mathrm{Ba})\left(\mathrm{Zr}_{1-x} V_{\mathrm{Zr}_{x}}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\right) \mathrm{O}_{3-2 x} V_{\mathrm{O}_{x}}^{\bullet\bullet}+\frac{x}{2} \mathrm{Nb}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{5} \rightarrow \\
&\left(\mathrm{Pb}_{1-y-\frac{x}{2}} \mathrm{Nb}_{\mathrm{Pb}_{x}}^{\bullet\bullet\bullet} V_{\mathrm{Pb}_{y-\frac{x}{2}}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\left(\mathrm{Zr}_{1-x} V_{\mathrm{Zr}_{x}}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\right) \mathrm{O}_{3-y} V_{\mathrm{O}_{y}}^{\bullet\bullet}+\uparrow\left(y+\frac{x}{2}\right) \mathrm{PbO}
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

As we mentioned earlier, B-site vacancies are designed in the doped system by adding (1-x)Zr. If any of xNb that should fill the remaining $x$ B sites does not go to B site, $V_{\mathrm{Zr}}$ must occur.

Since B site vacancies are expected to be less mobile than Pb vacancies, the reduction in concentration of $V_{\mathrm{Pb}}$ would decrease the volume diffusion rate and lower the relative density, which in those samples indeed reaches only 80%. However, with a further increase in additive content, the Nb ions will substitute the Zr ions at the B site of the perovskite structure, as is the usual case. In that case, Pb vacancies are created (equation 2) to reach electroneutrality, enhancing again the volume diffusion and increasing the sintered density (about 97-98% of the theoretical). ## 4 | SUMMARY

Like several other donor doped Pb-based perovskites, PBZ exhibits strong anomalies in properties and low densification at low concentrations of donor dopant. We show that these anomalies are closely related to the mechanism of softening. One can thus summarize possible mechanisms, by which the site position of dopant ion $Nb^{5+}$ influences the structural, microstructural, dielectric, and ferroelectric characteristics of PBZ, as following:

1. At low concentrations, Nb enters into A site: The resulting decrease in concentration of lead vacancies and creation of $V_{Zr}$ vacancies which are not as mobile as A site vacancies, leads to incomplete densification and changes the structure by distorting the unit cell, causing deterioration of dielectric and piezoelectric properties.

2. Addition of Nb beyond $\approx 0.2\%$, at which it enters B site, reduces concentration of $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ divacancies. At these large concentrations, $Nb_{Zr}$ is compensated by $V_{Pb}$ and the diffusion rate is sufficient for nearly full densification. $Nb_{Zr}$ and $V_{Pb}$ are weakly coupled and are not as effective in pinning domain walls as $V_{Pb}$-$V_O$ divacancies. The material is thus electrically and mechanically softer than undoped material.
