# Highly Stable and Active Solid-Solution-Alloy Three-Way Catalyst by Utilizing Configurational-Entropy Effect

Kohei Kusada,* Dongshuang Wu, Yusuke Nanba, Michihisa Koyama,*
Tomokazu Yamamoto, Xuan Quy Tran, Takaaki Toriyama, Syo Matsumura, Ayano Ito,

Since 1970, people have been making every endeavor to reduce toxic emissions from automobiles. After the development of a three-way catalyst (TWC) that concurrently converts three harmful gases, carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HCs), and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), Rh became an essential element in automobile technology because only Rh works efficiently for catalytic NOₓ reduction. However, due to the sharp price spike in 2007, numerous efforts have been made to replace Rh in TWCs. Nevertheless, Rh remains irreplaceable, and now, the price of Rh is increasing significantly again. Here, it is demonstrated that PdRuM ternary solid-solution alloy nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit highly durable and active TWC performance, which will result in a significant reduction in catalyst cost compared to Rh. This work provides insights into the design of highly durable and efficient functional alloy NPs, guiding how to best take advantage of the configurational entropy in addition to the mixing enthalpy.

A three-way catalyst (TWC) that currently converts three harmful gases, carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HCs), and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) is an absolutely essential technique for the sustainable society. Rh has been an essential element in TWC because only Rh is able to efficiently catalytically reduce NOₓ.[1,2] However, since Rh is scarce metal and its price is very fluctuating, significant efforts have been made to develop alternative TWC catalysts to Rh. Nevertheless, Rh is still irreplaceable, and nowadays, the price of Rh marked an all-time record high.

One of the most attractive advantages of the solid-solution alloy is the ability to continuously control its electronic structure, affecting most of the physical and chemical properties by tuning the composition of the constituents. Therefore, by mixing elements A and B, alloy AB could have a unique property not possessed by either A or B or an enhanced property owing to the synergistic effect of A and B. As a fascinating example, solid-solution alloy NPs of Pd and Ru, which are located next to Rh in the periodic table of elements, exhibit $NO_{x}$ reduction activity in the TWC reaction superior to that of Rh which is known as the best practical $NO_{x}$ purification catalyst. $^{[9,10]}$ Since Rh has been one of the most expensive metals, much more expensive than Pd or Ru, the PdRu alloy showing a better $NO_{x}$ reduction activity was expected to be an alternative catalyst to Rh. However, the PdRu homogeneous solid-solution alloy structure transforms at high temperatures into an equilibrium segregated structure due to the immiscibility of Pd and Ru, and the catalyst loses its high $NO_{x}$ reduction activity (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The PdRu alloy is converted into a mixture of Pd-rich face-centered cubic (fcc) phase and Ru-rich hexagonal close-packed phase by exposure to high-temperature TWC reaction conditions. Serious loss of the catalytic activity occurs, and the complete conversion temperature of $NO_{x}$ becomes $100\ ^{\circ}C$ higher than that of the homogeneous PdRu solid-solution alloy.

To overcome this thermal stability issue, we have focused on the configurational-entropy effect. Recently, bulk high-entropy alloys have drawn much attention as a new type of structural material due to their great mechanical properties derived from the higher configurational-entropy effect. High-entropy alloys, in general, are solid-solution alloys consisting of five or more elements with equal or relatively large compositions. $^{[11-14]}$ On the other hand, in the case of NPs, considering the large surface-to-volume ratio, it can be assumed that even a third element can effectively stabilize the solid-solution phase at high temperatures by means of the large surface entropy in addition to the configurational entropy. Therefore, we introduced a third element (M) to stabilize the solid-solution structure in the form of PdRuM NPs at high temperatures. In addition, an enhancement of the $NO_{x}$ reduction activity could be expected by alloying M with PdRu. There are some studies reporting the enhanced catalytic activity by alloying three elements. $^{[15,16]}$ Here, we report the synthesis of PdRuM solid-solution alloy NPs through a chemical reduction method and demonstrate that the third element can effectively stabilize the solid-solution alloy structure at TWC reaction temperatures. Furthermore, PdRuIr shows better $NO_{x}$ reduction activity than PdRu, resulting in a cost-reductive alternative TWC to Rh. We also performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to reveal the effect of the third element.

We synthesized ternary PdRuM solid-solution alloy NPs through a chemical reduction method that concurrently reduced the constituents' ions (see the Experimental Section).

We fixed the Pd:Ru:M ratio to 1:1:1 because $Pd_{x}Ru_{1-x}$ showed the highest catalytic activity for $NO_{x}$ reduction at Pd:Ru = $1:1,^{[10]}$ and we chose Rh, Ag, Ir, Pt, and Au as candidates for the third element M. Figure 1 shows high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images of the obtained PdRuM NPs and the corresponding Pd, Ru, and M elemental maps obtained by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). These results revealed that all three kinds of elements were distributed in all of the particles; that is, the obtained NPs formed ternary solid-solution structures. However, Ru tends to locate more shell side in some particles maybe due to the relatively slower reduction speed of Ru ion compared to the others. The metal compositions and mean diameters of the synthesized NPs were characterized by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis and STEM images, respectively (Tables S2 and S3, Supporting Information). The crystal structures of the PdRuM NPs were confirmed by synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis at the BL02B2 beamline, SPring-8 $^{[17]}$ (Figure S2, Supporting Information). All of the PdRuM NP diffraction data showed a single fcc pattern, confirming the solid-solution structure. Furthermore, the electronic structures of PdRuM NPs were experimentally observed by hard X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (HAXPES) (Figure S3, Supporting Information), and their characteristic profiles are consistent with the theoretical results (Figure S4, Supporting Information), supporting the formation of solid-solution alloys of PdRuM NPs.

The obtained NPs were deposited onto a $\gamma\mathrm{-Al_{2}O_{3}}$ support to prepare $1\ \mathrm{wt\%}$ of metal-supported catalysts with a standard impregnation method (see the Experimental Section). By using these PdRuM catalysts, the catalytic activity for the TWC reaction was investigated with a fixed-bed flow reactor (see the Experimental Section). The supplied reaction mixture was adjusted to be a simulated automotive exhaust with a theoretical air-to-fuel ratio such that the CO and $\mathrm{C_{3}H_{6}}$ in the fuel were stoichiometrically combusted by NO and $\mathrm{O_{2}}$. Many nations and communities are implementing tighter emission regulations, which require new catalyst technology to convert $NO_{x}$, CO, and HC at lower temperatures because most emission gases are released at lower temperatures just after the initiation of engine operation. Therefore, new types of catalysts showing high conversion for three harmful species at lower temperatures are urgently needed worldwide. Figure 2; and Figure S5 (Supporting Information) shows the temperature dependence of $NO_{x}$, CO, and $\mathrm{C_{3}H_{6}}$ conversions. The $NO_{x}$ conversion rate on all the catalysts increased with the reaction temperature. All the catalysts show high catalytic activity with a low temperature in the first cycle compared to binary PdRu in $NO_{x}$ reduction, CO oxidation, and HC oxidation. In particular, PdRuIr and PdRuRh showed excellent activities for the conversion of all three harmful species at low temperatures. Surprisingly, although the binary PdRu lost its activity in the second cycle, PdRuIr converted $NO_{x}$, CO, and HC at low temperatures. In the second cycle, the difference in the temperatures corresponding to $50\%$ conversion ($T_{50}$) of $NO_{x}$ between PdRu and PdRuIr became much larger by $\approx 70\ ^{\circ}C$. While PdRuIr, PdRuRh, and PdRuPt maintained their high catalytic activities in the second cycle, the activities of PdRuAg and PdRuAu worsened, as did that of the binary PdRu catalyst (Figure S6, Supporting Information). To investigate the catalytic stability of PdRuM, we performed a 20-cycle TWC test for the PdRu and PdRuIr catalysts. Notably, PdRuIr maintained its excellent catalytic activity even in the 20th cycle and did not show any obvious degradation (Figure 3a,c; and Figure S11, Supporting Information). In contrast, the PdRu catalyst showed serious degradation in the 2nd cycle due to the decomposition of the solid-solution structure, as shown in Figure 3b,c; and Figure S11(Supporting Information). To confirm our expectation that the third element can effectively stabilize the solid-solution structure due to the entropy effect, STEM-EDX analysis was performed on the PdRuIr catalyst after the 20 cycle of the TWC test. Figure 3d clearly shows that the solid-solution structure of PdRuIr is maintained. In contrast, PdRuM catalysts that experienced degradation of the catalytic activity in the second cycle (M = Ag or Au) showed serious phase segre-gation into PdM and Ru even after the 2nd cycle because Agand Au are miscible with Pd but immiscible with $Ru^{[18,19]}$ (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The three Gibbs triangle representations of local composition distributions of PdRuIr (as-prepared, after 1st cycle, and 20th cycles) show the structural change that the three elements mix more homogeneously as TWC test cycle increases. The factor of Pd-Ru change from -0.69 to -0.34 through the TWC cycle test, suggesting that Pd and Ru were slightly segregated in the as-prepared NPs but mixed well after the heating.

We fixed the ratio of Pd to Ru to 1:1 and chose noble metals including Rh, Ag, Ir, Pt, and Au as the third elements in the synthesis. Figure 4 shows the composition dependence of the excess energies at 0 K of the PdRuM solid-solution and the phase-segregated models. In the case of Rh, Ir, or Pt, the excess energy of the ternary solid solutions becomes lower than that of the binary solid solution with increasing content of the third element. In contrast, the ternary solid solution of PdRuM (M = Ag or Au) becomes unstable compared to the binary solid solution. However, none of them provides a stable solid-solution phase with lower excess energy than the segregated model at 0 K.

Given the Gibbs free energy $(G = H-TS)$, it is obviously expected that the solid-solution alloys with Pd:Ru:M = 1:1:1 gain the most benefits from the configurational entropy compared to the others because the configurational entropy is maximal at the composition of Pd:Ru:M = 1:1:1. The Gibbs free energy $G$ becomes lower at higher temperatures due to the second term $TS$ in the equation. Therefore, the excess energies of the solid-solution models become lower at higher temperatures than those of the phase-segregated models in each alloy system (Figure 5a; and Figure S17, Supporting Information). The critical temperature $(T_C)$ at which the solid-solution model is converted into the equilibrium phase is $\approx$600 K for PdRuIr NPs (Ir = 33 at%). It is noteworthy that this $T_C$ is significantly lower than that of the binary PdRu model by 900 K. Figure 5b shows a summary of the $T_C$ values of PdRuM, which revealed that the ternary alloy NPs with Pd:Ru:M = 1:1:1 (M = Ir, Pt, or Rh) are able to thermodynamically stabilize the solid-solution structure at temperatures above $\approx$600 K. On the other hand, the atomic diffusion becomes pronounced as the temperature of the metal approaches its Tammann temperature that is usually taken to be half of its melting point. $^{[23]}$ When $T_C$ is well below the Tammann temperatures of monometals, solid-solution NPs are thermodynamically unstable below $T_C$ but can maintain their structure because the diffusion is not significant. Since the Tammann temperatures of these metals are above 900 K, it is expected that PdRuIr, PdRuPt, and PdRuRh ternary alloy NPs can maintain the solid-solution structure under the TWC reaction conditions and exhibit excellently stable catalytic activities, even though the solid-solution phases are nonequilibrium under ambient conditions.

We verified that the solid-solution phase in NPs was extremely stabilized by the combination of enthalpy of mixing and the large configurational entropy by alloying the third element. We also demonstrated that the obtained alloy catalyst exhibited not only dramatically enhanced stability but also excellent activities for the TWC reaction compared to the binary PdRu catalyst. In addition, in the case of PdRuIr, the cost reduction of raw materials is now approximately 90% that of Rh. This finding critically impacts the automobile industry depending on the at-risk material Rh and contributes to the development of a new type of TWCs to satisfy tighter emission regulations.

## Experimental Section
Chemicals: Poly(N-vinyl-2-pyrrolidone) (PVP, K30, MW ≈ 40 000), $RuCl_3{\cdot}nH_2O$, $AgNO_3$, $RhCl_3{\cdot}3H_2O$, triethylene glycol (TEG), diethyl ether, acetone, and ethanol were purchased from Wako (Japan). $K_2PdCl_4$, $HAuCl_4{\cdot}3H_2O$, and $K_2PtCl_4$ were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). $IrCl_4{\cdot}nH_2O$ was purchased from Mitsuwa Chemicals (Japan). All the chemicals and reagents were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of PdRuM NPs and Binary Alloy NPs: In a typical synthesis of PdRuM NPs (Pd:Ru:M = 1:1:1, in the case of M = Ir), PVP (1665.0 mg) was dissolved in triethylene glycol (TEG, 300 mL), and the solution was heated to $230\ ^{\circ}C$ in air with magnetic stirring. Furthermore, $K_2PdCl_4$ (163.2 mg), $RuCl_3{\cdot}nH_2O$ (130.6 mg), and $IrCl_4{\cdot}nH_2O$ (196.9 mg) were dissolved in deionized water (20 mL). The aqueous mixture solution was then slowly added to the TEG solution. The solution was maintained at $230\ ^{\circ}C$, while adding the solution. After cooling to room temperature, the prepared NPs were separated with acetone and diethyl ether by centrifugation. Other PdRuM (M = Ag, Au, Rh, Pt) NPs and binary alloy NPs were prepared by changing the metal precursors. The details of the synthesis conditions for PdRuM NPs and binary alloy NPs are summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Synthesis of Monometallic NPs: Monometallic Pd, Ru, and Rh NPs were prepared by chemical reduction methods using $K_2PdCl_4$, $RuCl_3{\cdot}nH_2O$, and $RhCl_3{\cdot}3H_2O$ as the metal precursors, respectively, and PVP as the stabilizing agent. In the syntheses of these NPs, the metal precursors and PVP were dissolved in TEG at room temperature. The solution was then heated to 180, 200, and $150\ ^{\circ}C$ for Pd, Ru, and Rh NPs, respectively, and maintained at these temperatures for 2 h. After the reaction was complete, the prepared NPs were separated by centrifugation. The details of the synthesis conditions for these NPs are summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

General Characterizations: Synchrotron X-ray diffraction patterns were measured at the BL02B2 beamline, SPring-8, Japan. $^{[17]}$ Samples were sealed in $\emptyset$0.3 mm glass capillary tubes and tested at RT over a range of $2.0$–$78^{\circ}$ with a step size of $0.006^{\circ}$. Preparation of PdRuM Catalysts: To investigate the TWC activities, PdRuM NPs supported on $\gamma\ce{-Al_{2}O_{3}}$ catalysts were prepared by wet impregnation. After stirring, the suspended solutions were heated to 60 °C and dried under vacuum.

Catalytic Test: Powders of the $\gamma\ce{-Al_{2}O_{3}}$-supported catalysts were pressed into pellets at 52 MPa for 5 min. They were then crushed and sieved to obtain grains with diameters of 250–500 μm. For each test, 200 mg of catalyst grains were loaded into a tubular quartz reactor (internal diameter = 7 mm), and quartz wool was packed around the catalyst. In addition, a k-type thermocouple was inserted into the catalyst bed to measure the temperature for feedback control of the system. A feed-gas mixture containing NO (1000 ppm), CO (6000 ppm), $\ce{C_{3}H_{6}}$, (555 ppm), $\ce{O_{2}}$ (6000 ppm), $\ce{H_{2}}$ (2000 ppm), $\ce{CO_{2}}$ (13.9%), and He (balance) was passed over each catalyst (total flow rate = 200 mL min⁻¹; space velocity = 60 000 h⁻¹). The gas mixture was designed to emulate the air-to-fuel ratio of automotive emissions. The catalyst bed was heated from ambient temperature to 600 at 10 °C min⁻¹ under the flowing reaction mixture. The outlet gas composition was continuously monitored with a chemiluminescence NOₓ analyzer and a nondispersive infrared gas analyzer (VA3000, HORIBA).
