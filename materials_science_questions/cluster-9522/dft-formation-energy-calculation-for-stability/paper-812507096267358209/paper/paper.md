# Anion Constructor for Atomic-Scale Engineering of Antiperovskite Crystals for Electrochemical Reactions

Sehyun Lee, Jae Young Jung, Injoon Jang, Daeil Choi, Myeong Jae Lee, Dong Wook Lee, Jue-Hyuk Jang, Jeong Hee Lee, Haneul Jin, Kyungmin Im, Eungjun Lee, Seung-hoon Kim, Nam Dong Kim, Soo-Hyoung Lee, Yun Sik Kang, Hee-Young Park, Dongwon Chun, Hyung Chul Ham, Kug-Seung Lee, Docheon Ahn,* Pil Kim,* and Sung Jong Yoo*

Among the Pt group metals, Pd has been considered the most efficient for application in electrocatalysts as an alternative to Pt. Despite the comparable electrochemical activities of Pd and Pd-metal alloys, they are vulnerable to liquid acidic electrolytes, leading to degradation of catalytic activity. Pd–Ni alloys have been used to enhance catalytic activity because the electronic structure of Pd can be easily changed by adding Ni. In other studies, N atoms have been introduced for more stable M–Ni catalysts by inducing the formation of Ni₄N species; however, the structural analysis and the role of nitrogen have not been fully understood yet. Herein, the Pd–Ni alloy nitride with a unique crystal structure shows a promising catalytic activity for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). The nitride PdNi nanoparticles have a novel monolithic antiperovskite structure of chemical formula $(Pd_xNi_{1-x})NNi_3$. The unique antiperovskite crystal $(Pd_xNi_{1-x})NNi_3$ possesses superior ORR activity and stability, originating from the downshifted d-band center of the monolayer Pd/antiperovskite surface and the lower formation energy of the antiperovskite core nanocrystal. Consequently, $(Pd_xNi_{1-x})NNi_3$, as a Pt-free Pd-based electrocatalyst, overcomes the stability issue of Pd under acidic conditions by achieving 99-times higher mass activity than commercial Pd/C, as shown by the durability test.

## 2. Results and Discussions
### 2.1. Structural Analysis of Atomic-Engineered PdNNi

The extraordinary precursor molar ratio of Pd:Ni (1:3) produces a novel crystal structure upon postheat treatment in ammonia atmosphere (Figure 1a). The disordered $PdNi_{3}$ crystals were transformed into long-range-ordered crystal structures via high-temperature lattice reorientation of atoms in ammonia environment as the first step of atomic engineering (as-prepared crystal). In general, the optimized degree of Pd-Ni alloying for ORR is thought to be more than the 3:1 molar ratio, as per the volcano plot of face-centered cubic crystals (fcc). $^{[12]}$ However, the alloying degree of Pd to Ni is less than 1:3 for the novel crystal produced in our study. As the second step of atomic engineering, surface modification of the as-prepared crystal was conducted to obtain a Pd-enriched surface; the thus-prepared sample is denoted as ae-PdNNi (atomic-engineered PdNNi). $^{[13]}$ To characterize the crystal structure of ae-PdNNi, synchrotron X-ray high-resolution powder diffraction patterns (HRPD) were obtained, which could be indexed to antiperovskite crystal structure $(ABX_{3})$, as shown in Figure 1b and Table S1 in the Supporting Information (PDF#36-1300). Furthermore, Rietveld refinement results presented good agreement between the observed and calculated patterns (Figure 1b; Table S1, Supporting Information) and all the refined parameters and reliability factors obtained were also reasonable enough to trust the antiperovskite structure model used for structure analysis (Table S1, Supporting Information). This structural analysis revealed that instead of Ni, Pd occupies 70% of the A sites located at the corners in the unit cell of the antiperovskite structure (Figure 1c). Therefore, it was concluded that the composition of ae-PdNNi is $(Pd_{0.7}Ni_{0.3})NNi_{3}$. This is consistent with the lattice parameter of ae-PdNNi (3.79 Å) being less than that of bulk Pd (3.89 Å) because the ionic radius of Pd is larger than that of Ni. $^{[14]}$ The antiperovskite unit cell (Figure 1c) has an octahedral $Ni_{3}N$ structure with an A site on the corner. In addition, the average crystallite size of the nanoparticles is 13.9 nm, which is similar to the average particle size in Figure S8 in the Supporting Information, obtained by microstructural analysis using Rietveld refinement (Figure 1b).

The electronic structure and bonding information of ae-PdNNi were obtained by X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis (Figure S1, Supporting Information) and were compared with those of the disordered $PdNi_{3}$ crystal (denoted as PdNi/C). The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) shows that the oxidation states of Pd in ae-PdNNi were higher than those in PdNi/C; therefore, we can conclude that the nitrogen in the crystal structure withdraws some electrons from Ni, thereby weakening the electron donation to Pd. The manifestation of Pd-O bonding in the antiperovskite structure is an additional reason for the higher oxidation state of Pd. To investigate the peak position and intensity of the XANES spectra in detail, the second derivatives of normalized absorption spectra were used (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). The higher intensity of the first negative peak at 24 353 eV indicates s-d orbital transition, corresponding to crystal structure changes of antiperovskite ae-PdNNi. $^{[15]}$ The extended XAFS (EXAFS) spectra and the fitting results showed that Pd-Pd bonding of the Pd metallic phase was absent in ae-PdNNi, suggesting a monolithic crystal of antiperovskite structure (Figure 1c,d; Table S2, Supporting Information). In addition, the Ni K edge XANES and EXAFS in Figure S9 in the Supporting Information can support the nitride structure of ae-PdNNi because positive shift of ae-PdNNi in XANES is consistent with the existence of Ni-N bonding peak at 1.4 Å in EXAFS. From the HRPD and EXAFS data, the interatomic distances can be visualized in the octahedron of the antiperovskite structure, as shown in Figure 1d.

From the filtered high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images obtained along the [001] zone axis (ZA) (Figure 2a), we can directly confirm the atomic arrangement corresponding to antiperovskite crystal structures. Furthermore, we can verify the existence of atoms at the octahedral $Ni_{3}N$ and A sites in the octahedral corner from the filtered STEM images (Figure 2b), in which the diffraction pattern (inlet) matches the representative crystal phase in HRPD. Although both Pd (70%) and Ni (30%) atoms occupy the A site, it is apparent only for the Pd due to the intrinsic limitation of STEM analysis, influenced by the higher electron density of Pd than that of Ni. For the verification of N at the center of octahedral $Ni_{3}N$, we performed STEM image simulation (Figure 2c) based on the crystallographic information file (CIF) obtained by Rietveld refinement because the intensity profile in HAADF-STEM image relies on the atomic number. $^{[16]}$ The histogram of statistical distribution of the three atom sites (yellow square in Figure 2b,c) in both the experimental and simulated images is shown in Figure 2d,e. The obtained intensity difference between the center and side atomic positions is 18.2%, almost identical to that of simulated one, indicating that N indeed occupies the center of the octahedral site. When we compare the difference of HAADF intensities with another simulation models (Figure S6, Supporting Information), the $\Delta$HAADF intensity is significantly mediated by the species at the central position. ### 2.2. ORR Performances of Pd-Based Catalysts in Acidic Medium

To evaluate the ORR activity, we implemented a half-cell test in acidic medium (0.1 м $HClO_4$), as shown in Figure 3a. In general, Pd and Pd-transition metal alloy electrocatalysts are vulnerable to acidic conditions due to their intrinsically low dissolution stabilities. The commercial Pd/C possesses poor activity and stability after 3000 cycles of accelerated durability test (ADT), as shown in Figure 3b. In the case of PdNi/C, the low crystallinity with various crystal phases was observed in the range of $40^\circ$-$50^\circ$ as shown in Figure S7 in the Supporting Information. The PdNi/C possess more than three crystal phases along with the unalloyed Pd and Ni phases. Therefore, previous studies based on PdNi alloys in Table S3 in the Supporting Information were adopted to compare ORR activity rather than PdNi/C. The antiperovskite structured ae-PdNNi, however, exhibits superior mass activity (1.46 A $mg_{Pd}^{-1}$) and stability owing to the electronic effect of intermetallic Pd-Ni alloy and nitriding in competition with oxidation of metal species during ORR.$^{[17]}$ After the ADT, ae-PdNNi achieves 99-times higher mass activity than commercial Pd/C and shows greater retention rate than other Pd-based electrocatalysts (Figure 3c). The ORR results experimentally prove that the oxygen reductive activity and stability are contingent on the antiperovskite crystal core structures. To the best of our knowledge, the ORR activity and stability of antiperovskite materials in acidic media are far better than those of previously reported Pd-based electrocatalysts,$^{[18-25]}$ except those with unique nanomorphology (Figure 3d; Table S3, Supporting Information). We further investigated the excellent ORR activity of antiperovskite catalysts by computational calculations and surface characteristics.

### 2.3. Computational Study to Investigate the Activity and Stability

As shown in Figure 4a, the bulk formation energy of the antiperovskite materials were in the range from -0.95 to -1.05 eV, much lower than that of the fcc-structured PdNi$_3$ alloy (0.02 eV). The positive formation energy of PdNi$_3$ alloy suggested that it is thermodynamically unstable and cannot be synthesized via conventional methods, whereas Pd-Ni antiperovskite materials are thermodynamically stable. ## 3. Conclusion
In summary, we demonstrate an intermetallic nitriding alloy from an extraordinary molar ratio of precursor and post-treatment that could be regarded as a convergence of two promising strategies. The anion stabilized ae-PdNNi overcomes an operating challenge in acidic medium of Pd-based electrocatalysis. The completely different crystal structure significantly changes the intrinsic properties of the ae-PdNNi that facilitates excellent electrocatalytic activity and durability. DFT calculations and experimental results show that the ORR activity and durability of ae-PdNNi can be improved through proper d-band center shift and catalytic surface in operando. Besides, the atomic-scale engineered electrocatalyst can be tuned to versatile electrocatalysts by introducing various components for targeted electrochemical reactions, as briefly demonstrated in this report. Our findings will stimulate subsequent research on compositionally engineered electrocatalysts for various chemical reactions and new concepts to improve the intrinsic activity of materials.

## 4. Experimental Section
Materials: Vulcan carbon was purchased from Cabot. Ethanol ($C_2H_5OH$), nitric acid ($HNO_3$), sulfuric acid ($H_2SO_4$), and isopropyl alcohol (IPA) were purchased from Samchun Chemical. The Nafion perfluorinated resin solution (5 wt% contents in alcohols), perchloric acid ($HClO_4$, 70%), sodium borohydride ($NaBH_4$), palladium(II) acetylacetonate ($Pd(acac)_2$), nickel(II) acetylacetonate ($Ni(acac)_2$), iron(III) acetylacetonate ($Fe(acac)_3$), iridium(III) acetylacetonate ($Ir(acac)_3$), and ruthenium(III) acetylacetonate ($Ru(acac)_3$) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Palladium nitrate ($Pd(NO_3)_2$) was purchased from Kojima Chemicals. Commercial Pt/C was obtained from Johnson Matthey Co. Commercial Pd/C was obtained from Premetek Co.

Material Synthesis: For the acid treatment of carbon, 2 g of Vulcan carbon was treated at 90 °C for 3 h in a solution of 6 M $HNO_3$ and 6 M $H_2SO_4$. After it cooled to room temperature (25 °C), it was washed with 1 L of deionized (DI) water and stored in 80 °C dry oven. Furthermore, 100 mL of EtOH with 0.2 g of acid-treated carbon was sonicated for 30 min, after which it was vigorously stirred for an additional 30 min. The solution was transferred to a 250 mL three-neck flask and heated to 70 °C. In a 100 mL beaker, 0.0860 g $Pd(acac)_2$ and 0.2175 g $Ni(acac)_2$ (≈1:3 metal ratio) were stirred into 50 mL of EtOH until completely dissolved. The solution of 10 mL EtOH with 0.35 g $NaBH_4$ was added in the carbon solution that was heated to 70 °C in the three-neck flask. The precursor solution was immediately injected into the carbon solution with a syringe pump at a feed rate of 5 mL min⁻¹. After the entirety of the precursor solution was injected, the reaction was allowed to take place for an hour and products were filtered using DI water. The product powder (PdNi/C, determined to be 25 wt% via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, ICP-MS) was kept overnight for drying in an oven at 80 °C. As a postheat treatment, 50 mg of PdNi/C was loaded into a furnace. To obtain the disordered PdNi/C sample, 1 h of heat treatment was conducted at 600 °C with a ramping rate of 5 °C min⁻¹ under $N_2$ atmosphere and spontaneously cooled to room temperature. The as-prepared antiperovskite was synthesized through the same heat treatment process under $NH_3$ (99.95%) atmosphere. However, when the temperature reached 300 °C, the $NH_3$ gas flow was replaced with $N_2$. Utilizing atomic-scale engineering, the Ni at the surface of the as-prepared antiperovskite was replaced with Pd. After the heat treatment, the powder was transferred to the reactor without exposure to air or any pollutant. Subsequently, 9.7 mg of $Pd(NO_3)_2$ dissolved in 100 mL DI water was added dropwise with a syringe pump at a feed rate of 5 mL min⁻¹. Following this, the reaction was allowed to progress for 1 h and filtered using DI water. For investigation of various antiperovskite candidates, PdNFe₃, IrNNi₃, and RuNNi₃ were prepared using the same procedure by keeping metal ratio (≈1:3 ratio) but replacing the metal precursor to $Fe(acac)_3$ for PdNFe₃, and $Ir(acac)_3$ and $Ru(acac)_3$ for IrNNi₃ and RuNNi₃, respectively.

X-Ray Powder Diffraction: High-resolution synchrotron powder X-ray diffraction data of the prepared samples were obtained at the 9B HRPD beamline of PLS-II. The HRPD data were collected at room temperature for $2\theta$ values in the range of 20-130, with a step size of 0.01 at a wavelength of 1.5167 (1) Å. Rietveld refinement was performed using FullProf software.$^{[45]}$ HRPD impurity peaks of a negligible amount of Pd were observed in the XRD patterns of the prepared samples.

Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy: The antiperovskite ae-PdNNi particle images were obtained by Cs-corrected STEM (FEI; TitanTM 80-300). For the background noise reduction, the obtained HAADF-STEM images were filtered by 2D Wiener filtering algorithm implemented in a software package (HREM Filter Pro, HREM research). Furthermore, a multislice HAADF-STEM simulation was carried out using Dr. Probe software.$^{[16]}$

X-Ray Absorption Fine Structure: XAFS was measured at 8C nanoprobe XAFS beamline (BL8C) of Pohang Light Source (PLS-II) in the 3.0 GeV storage ring, with a ring current of 400 mA. The X-ray beam was monochromated by a Si(111) double crystal, where the beam intensity was reduced by 30% to eliminate higher-order harmonics. Following this, the X-ray beam was delivered to a secondary source aperture, where the beam size was adjusted to 0.5 mm (v) × 1 mm (h). XAFS spectra were collected in transmission and fluorescence modes. The obtained spectra were processed using Demeter software. The EXAFS spectra were fitted in a Fourier-transform range of 3-14 Å⁻¹ with a Hanning window applied between 1.2 and 3.3 Å. The amplitude reduction factor (So2) was set to be 0.9. Pd $L_3$-edge and Ni K-edge XANES spectra were obtained at PLS-II using the 8C beam line.

X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy: To estimate the surface metal species and thickness, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was carried out at 8A1 beamline of Pohang synchrotron sources with 950, 1000, and 1100 eV of photon energy.

Density Functional Theory: The calculations reported herein were carried out on the basis of spin-polarized DFT within Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE), as implemented in the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP). The projector augmented wave (PAW) method with a plane wave basis set was used to describe the interaction between core and valence electrons. An energy cutoff of 400 eV was applied for the plane wave expansion of the electronic eigenfunctions. The calculated equilibrium lattice constant for fcc bulk Pd was 3.94 Å, which was similar to the experimental results of 3.86 Å.$^{[14]}$ To model bulk PdNi₃ and the antiperovskite-structured $ABX_3$ (A and X denote Ni and Pd, respectively), some of the Ni atoms in the bulk Ni ($Fm3m$) and $Ni_4N$ ($Pm3m$) were replaced with Pd. For $(Ni_{0.3}Pd_{0.7})NNi_3$ bulk, which is obtained by multiplying the PdNNi₃ supercell by 2, 2, and 5 in the x, y, and z directions, respectively, some of the Ni atoms were replaced with Pd to match the ratio of Ni and Pd in the A-site from 3 to 7. To find the equilibrium lattice parameter of these unit cells, the total energies of the bulk were calculated by changing the lattice parameter. Using these values, equilibrium lattice parameters with the lowest total energy were estimated. For simulating the Pd/ae-PdNNi termination slab model, the vertical z vector of $(Ni_{0.3}Pd_{0.7})NNi_3$ bulk unit cell was elongated, corresponding to eight atomic layers by a vacuum space for periodic image separation. The two bottom layers of the five-layered slab were fixed, and the upper three layers were fully relaxed. This was attained using the conjugate gradient method, until residual forces on all the constituent atoms became smaller than 0.05 eV Å⁻¹. For Brillouin zone integration, $(8 × 8 × 8)$, $(4 × 4 × 2)$, and $(4 × 4 × 1)$ Monkhorst-Pack meshes of k-points were used to calculate the optimized geometry and total energy of $ABX_3$, $(Ni_{0.3}Pd_{0.7})NNi_3$ bulk, and the $(Ni_{0.3}Pd_{0.7})NNi_3$

slab model, respectively. For the electronic structure, d-band center, and Bader charge calculations, the k-point mesh was doubled to $(16 × 16 × 16)$, $(8 × 8 × 4)$, and $(8 × 8 × 1)$, respectively. The d-band center was calculated as follows

$$
\mu=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\varepsilon \times \rho) \mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \rho \mathrm{d} \varepsilon}
\tag{2}
$$

where $\varepsilon$ and $\rho$ represent an energy level and the electron density at that energy level, respectively.

Electrochemical Measurement: A CHI 700C electrochemical workstation was used for electrochemical measurements. The electrochemical measurements were performed in a three-electrode system using Pt wire as counter electrode, Ag/AgCl (3 м NaCl) as reference electrode, and a glassy carbon rotating disk electrode (RDE) (disk geometric area was $0.2472 \mathrm{~cm}^{2}$) as working electrode. The ink for the RDE tests was composed of the catalyst (10 mg), $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(50 \mu \mathrm{L}), 5$ wt% Nafion solution in alcohol $(50 \mu \mathrm{L})$, and IPA $(4000 \mu \mathrm{L})$. The catalysts were loaded onto RDE at $15 \mu \mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{Pd}}$ per $\mathrm{cm}^{2}$ based on the ICP data. After the ink on the RDE was dried, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted in $\mathrm{N}_{2}$-saturated $0.1 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{HClO}_{4}$ solution at a scan rate of $100 \mathrm{mV} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ with a potential range (vs reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) of 0.05-1.2 V. For the ORR measurement, the electrolyte solution was bubbled through with $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ gas until it was saturated with $\mathrm{O}_{2}$. Moreover, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted at a scan rate of $10 \mathrm{mV} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ under a potential range (vs RHE) of 0.115-1.115 V. The ADT following the DOE protocol was carried out under the potential range of $0.6-1.0 \mathrm{~V}$ at a scan rate of $100 \mathrm{mV} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ under $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ flow $(50 \mathrm{~mL} \mathrm{~min}^{-1})$. After 3000 cycles of ADT, ORR LSV curves were obtained in a manner similar to the initial measurement. The ECSA of Pd-based catalysts were obtained from the stripping of CO. For the CO stripping, the acidic $0.1 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{HClO}_{4}$ electrolyte was saturated by CO gas (99.99%) bubbling for 30 min. The working electrode potential coated with Pd-based catalysts was held at $0.1 \mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{RHE}}$ for 10 min under CO bubbling. And, the CVs were measured at a scan rate of $20 \mathrm{mV} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ after $\mathrm{N}_{2}$ bubbling for at least $20 \mathrm{~min}$. The ECSAs were calculated from the first CV results by integrating the charges of CO stripping and subtracting the background charge of the second CV results. It was assumed that the CO coverage charge density was $420 \mu \mathrm{C} \mathrm{cm}^{-2}$.
