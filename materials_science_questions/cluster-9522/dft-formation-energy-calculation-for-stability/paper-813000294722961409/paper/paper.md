## ABSTRACT
The microstructure variation and oxidation behavior of a hot-rolled and quenched Ti-24Al-11Nb (at%) intermetallic alloy with and without protective Ni-20Cr (at%) coating were studied in the air at a cyclic annealing temperature of 600 °C and 900 °C. The phase transformations monitored up to 120 h of alloy oxidation and oxidation products were examined using different experimental techniques. The oxidation kinetics was determined by recording the mass gain vs. time data and oxidation activation energy was evaluated. It was found that the annealing temperature and deposited coating significantly affect the scale formation and growth. The alloy showed better oxidation resistance at 600 °C, irrespective of the protective coating application. The higher temperature promoted the formation of thicker and multi-layered scale predominantly composed of Nb-doped TiO₂ and Al₂O₃ oxides, which cracked and spalled causing oxidation rate for an order of magnitude higher than that at 600 °C. The presence of AlN decelerated the oxide scale growth, which obeyed a parabolic rate law. It was also noted that a two-phase $α_2+β$ microstructure changed to a greater extent. With increasing temperature, the $α_2$→$β$ phase transformation occurred more intensive and new $α_2''$ and O-Ti₂AlNb phase appeared. The Ni-20Cr coating reduced the oxidation rate at both temperatures and improved the scale-spallation resistance. The compact Cr₂O₃ layer formed on the coated alloy led to the preferential Al₂O₃ formation, increasing its amount in mixture with Nb-enriched TiO₂ oxide. Thin TiN layer detected beneath the oxide scale also supported the formation of slow-growing Al₂O₃, leading to a decrease in the oxidation rate. The external scale suppression contributed to the greater diffusion zone enrichment, resulting in the faster O-Ti₂AlNb formation. The experimentally observed O-Ti₂AlNb formation was confirmed by *ab initio* modeling. Furthermore, additional structures were predicted and studied using first-principles calculations in the O-Ti₂AlNb compound.

## 1. Introduction
The Ti-Al intermetallic alloys, such as $α_2$-Ti₃Al based alloys, are recognized as promising structural materials for high-temperature applications due to their low densities and better mechanical performance at elevated temperatures than conventional Ti alloys [1]. Exceptional properties of these intermetallic materials made them extremely attractive for aerospace and automotive applications. However, a serious limitation to the broad industrial usage of Ti₃Al-based alloys can be attributed to their low room-temperature ductility [2] and poor oxidation resistance during exploitation in the air at high temperatures [3].

Limitations of Ti-Al intermetallics led to their extensive development over the past few decades. One of the effective ways to improve their properties is obtainment of two-phase $α_2+β$ microstructure with the optimal $α_2/β$ ratio. Namely, alloys based on $α_2$-Ti₃Al intermetallic compound cannot be used as single-phase materials due to its high room-temperature brittleness. The $β$ phase, present in microstructure as ordered $β_2$ (B2) phase [2,3], enhances the alloy room-temperature ductility and hot deformability [1,4]. Additionally, the presence of the $β$ phase contributes to the alloy oxidation resistance improvement. Higher $β$ phase volume fraction ensures higher alloy oxidation resistance at the elevated temperatures. Increase in the $β$ phase fraction can be achieved by additional alloying with $β$-stabilizing elements, such as Nb, V, Mo and Ta, which broaden the region of the $β$ phase stability [5]. Among alloying additions, the Nb replacing Ti in alloy composition, is identified as the most effective element. Thus, Ti₃Al-based alloys are most commonly three-component systems with chemical composition based on Ti-(23-25)Al-(8-18)Nb (in at%), although they can be additionally alloyed with two or three elements [6].

However, alloying with Nb can lead to the formation of additional phases which in great merit contributes to the complexity of phase transformations in these alloys. Apart from the hexagonal close packed (hcp) $α_2$ with a DO₁₉ structure and body-centered cubic (bcc) $β$

(disordered structure) or B2 (ordered structure) phases, the orthorhombic O-phase (based on the stoichiometry $Ti_2AlNb$) is found to exist in Ti-Al-Nb equilibrium phase diagram [7-11]. The O-phase is stable over quite a broad composition range [12]. It forms by solid-state reactions involving either $\alpha_2$ or $\beta$ (B2) or through the ternary equivalent of a peritectoid reaction $\alpha_2$+B2$\rightarrow$O, which gives rise to the three-phase $\alpha_2$+B2+O phase field [11]. During cooling the alloy passes through the $\alpha_2$+B2, $\alpha_2$+B2+O and O+B2 phase fields, so that the sequence of transformation is complicated [7].

The O-phase can appear in the microstructure only as a result of slow cooling or after subsequent heat treatment of the quenched material [1,13,14]. Its appearance in the microstructure could lead to the improvement of the alloy high temperature properties [15]. Thus, the Ti-Al-Nb alloys containing a significant amount of the O-phase show the excellent creep resistance [10]. Furthermore, the presence of this orthorhombic phase may enhance tensile properties and high-temperature oxidation resistance. The influence of O-phase on the oxidation behavior of $Ti_3Al$-based alloys is not well documented. However, it is believed that the O-phase formation contributes to the lower oxidation rate. Since the alloy creep and oxidation resistance increases with the O-phase volume fraction and decreases with the volume fraction of the $\alpha_2$ phase, the nature of the present phases and their amount depending on the alloy composition and heat treatment are very important for the alloy applications. The morphological features were also proven to play an important role in the $Ti_3Al$-Nb alloys behavior. Wang et al. [10] reported that both the increased thickness and larger volume of the O-phase laths resulted from the age treatment of a quenched Ti-22Al-25Nb (at%) alloy at 840 °C, were possible reasons for the greater creep resistance than in the case of age treatment at 760 °C. The morphology and distribution of the O-phase vary with its formation reaction [10,11]. Therefore, in order to obtain a suitable microstructure, it is necessary to conduct a systematic investigation on the phase evolution, tensile, creep and oxidation behavior of the Ti-Al-Nb alloys.

Even though additional alloying of titanium aluminides with $\beta$-stabilizing elements showed some beneficial effects [16], further improvement of their high-temperature oxidation properties can be achieved by applying diverse treatments, such as thermomechanical processing and protective coating deposition [17-20]. The application of coatings may improve the oxidation resistance of these alloys, without the negative effects on their mechanical properties [19]. A variety of diffusing and depositing coatings was used as the protective coating on the $Ti_3Al$-based alloys. However, many kinds of the coatings are not quite effective to improve the $Ti_3Al$-based alloy oxidation resistance. Thus, the beneficial effect of the $Al_2O_3$ thin film deposited on a Ti-24Al-14Nb-3V-0.5Mo-0.3Si alloy by sol-gel processing became weak after a long time isothermal oxidation at 1000 °C [19]. On the other hand, this coating decreased the thickness of the Nb/Al enrichment layer in the alloy adjacent to the oxide/alloy interface by reducing the scale growth rate. However, Li et al. [20] reported that the $Al_2O_3$ oxide film applied on the surface of $Ti_3Al$-11Nb alloy by an electrodeposition technique has a detrimental effect on the alloy oxidation in air at 800-1000 °C and its scale-spallation resistance. However, the oxidation rate of this alloy can be reduced by $CeO_2$ and $Y_2O_3$ films. The applications of these surface oxide coatings can also improve the formation of Nb/Al-enriched layers, although the exact phase structure of these layers was not identified. Nevertheless, all of the mentioned procedures could be reflected in microstructural variation and in that way influence the alloy performances at elevated temperatures and in turn their industrial applications.

As mentioned above, the occurrence of the O-phase may have a great impact on the $Ti_3Al$-Nb alloys behavior at room and high temperatures. Hence, the O-$Ti_2AlNb$ phase, with a Cmcm (no. 63) space group and Pearson symbol oC16 [9,12,21] appearing in $O_1$ and $O_2$ variants [9,12], has received intensive attention in previous studies [7-9,11,12,21,22]. Much of that attention has been given to the investigations of the electronic structure of the $O_2$-phase variant, its stability and mechanical properties [7-9,22]. In recent years, the ab initio calculation method has been successfully used to determine the properties of some intermetallic compounds [23]. Earlier calculations of the $\alpha_2$, B2 and O-$Ti_2AlNb$ phase structural stability and mechanical properties by using ab initio density functional theory (DFT) were related to the ground state (T = 0 K) [7-9]. More recently, Hu et al. [22] used the DFT combined with quasi-harmonic approximation (QHA) to predict the equilibrium volume, elastic and thermodynamic properties of the ordered O-$Ti_2AlNb$ phase under temperatures in range from 0 to 1300 K. It was found that O-phase shows good high-temperature mechanical and thermal stability, which is important for elevated temperature applications. Holec et al. [24] showed that the ab initio calculation method can be also used to study the preferential site occupations of ternary elements in B19 phase observed in the $\gamma$-TiAl-based intermetallics. However, most of those theoretical studies have focused on properties rather than on structural determination, characterization and prediction, and in so far published experimental observations the O-phase structure is described by using partial occupancy for Ti and Nb atoms. In the industrial practice, the achievement of the enhanced oxidation resistance is often more important than the enhanced mechanical properties. Therefore, it is necessary to study the existence of the O-phase and optimized O-phase structure to better understand its role in the $Ti_3Al$-based alloys oxidation behavior.

In the present work, the formation of O-$Ti_2AlNb$ phase in the $Ti_3Al$-based alloy with and without protective coating during cyclic annealing in air at elevated temperatures was investigated. The kinetics and mechanisms of oxidation process promoting the O-phase appearance were established by conducting a detailed characterization of alloy microstructure evolution and formed oxide scale. Finally, three different ab initio approaches were used in order to calculate and characterize the O-phase. Additionally, not-yet synthesized structure candidates where proposed.

## 2. Experimental details
### 2.1. Materials and experimental methods

The material selected for this investigation was hot-rolled Ti-24Al-11Nb (at%) sheets with a thickness of 0.5 mm [25,26]. The hot-rolling was conducted in the ($\alpha_2$+$\beta$) phase field at 1050 °C. The samples with a size of 15 × 15 mm were sectioned from the hot-rolled sheets, solution treated at 1200 °C and subsequently water quenched. After heat treatment, all samples were ground to 1000-grit SiC paper and cleaned for 30 min in an ultrasonic bath with ethanol.

Before cyclic oxidation, a set of metallographically prepared samples was coated with protective Ni-20Cr (at%) layer. Nonporous 1 $\mu$m thick layer was deposited by applying direct current (d.c.) $Ar^+$ ion sputtering technique. The coating deposition was performed in Ar atmosphere at 0.1995 Pa using a Balzers Sputron II vacuum system supplied by Balzers AG, Lichtenstein. During the coating process with a sputtering rate of 8.7 nm/min, the temperature was kept constant at 150 °C. In order to eliminate the contamination, the substrate was pre-cleaned and then ion-etched.

Coated and uncoated samples were cyclically oxidized in air at 600 °C and 900 °C. The samples were kept in a tube furnace at a selected temperature for 24 h and then furnace-cooled down to the room temperature. This process is defined as one cycle and the cycle was successively repeated five times. Mass gain measurements were conducted at certain time intervals of oxidation using a Mettler electronic balance with an accuracy of ± 0.0001 g.

The microstructure of hot-rolled and quenched alloy was examined before and after oxidation annealing using a Reichert Jung optical microscope (OM) and a JEOL JSM-6460LV scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) Oxford Instruments INCA X-sight system operating at 25 kV. For this purpose, the samples were ground on progressively finer grades of SiC

### 2.2. Computational methods

Full-scale structural optimization on ab initial level was performed
using the CRYSTAL 17 code [27,28] based on a Linear Combination of
Atomic Orbitals (LCAO). In our calculations, an all-electron basis set
(AEBS) labeled Ti_86-51(3d)G_muscat_1999 [29] was chosen for Ti
atoms. After basis set optimization two additional exponents were
added (sp 0.14 and d 0.14), which in total makes [5s4p2d] basis set for
titanium. For aluminum, an AEBS type [4s3p1d] basis set was used as in
Refs. [30-32]. In the case of Nb, effective core pseudopotential (ECP)
by Hay and Wadt labeled Nb_SC_HAYWSC-31(31d)G_baranek_2013
[33] was used. Final [3s3p2d] optimized basis set include additional sp
0.20 exponent.

Local optimizations employed analytical gradients with respect to
the atom positions [34] and the cell parameters [35], as well as local
optimization routine [36]. These local optimizations were performed
using different ab initio methods, which should confirm the quantitative
validity of the obtained results [37-39]. The hybrid B3LYP functional
(Becke's three parameter functional [40] in combination with the cor-
relation functional of Lee, Yang and Parr) and Density Functional
theory (DFT) calculations were utilized. With regard to DFT calcula-
tions, we used the local density approximation (LDA) with Perdew-
Zunger (PZ) correlation functional [41] and the generalized gradient
approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) func-
tional [42]. For the integration over the Brillouin zone, a k-point mash
of $8×8×8$ was generated using the Monkhorst-Pack scheme [43].
The energy convergence tolerance was set as $10^{-7}$ eV/atom for all of the
calculations.

In order to analyze the symmetry of the calculated structures, the
algorithms SFND [44] and RGS [45] implemented in the program
KPLOT [46] were used. The comparison of the calculated crystal
structures was performed using the CMPZ algorithm [47] also im-
plemented in the KPLOT program. Investigated structures were visua-
lized using the VESTA [48] software.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Initial microstructure

A typical microstructure of the hot-rolled Ti-24Al-11Nb alloy after
quenching from 1200 °C is shown in Fig. 1a. It consists of two phases:
the $\beta$ phase matrix (dark) and $\alpha_2$-Ti₃Al intermetallic phase (light) dis-
tributed along grain boundaries and throughout the $\beta$ matrix. Their
presence is verified by XRD analysis. The XRD patterns reveal that $\alpha_2$
and $\beta$ phases are the only existing phases, Fig. 1b. Also, the intensity of
peaks indicates that the $\beta$ phase is the main constituent phase. Fast
cooling from above the $\beta$-transus temperature leads to the diffusion-
controlled formation of coarse Widmanstätten $\alpha_2$ platelets within the
former $\beta$ phase. The transformation of $\beta$→$\alpha_2$ follows the
$\{0001\}_{\alpha2}||\{110\}_{\beta2}$ and $\langle11\overline{2}0\rangle_{\alpha2}||\langle101\rangle_{\beta2}$ orientation relationship [49]. The
measured results show that only 40 vol% of $\beta$ phase is transformed into
the $\alpha_2$ phase upon quenching. Thus even high cooling rate is not suf-
ficient to fully retain the high-temperature $\beta$ phase. However, such
microstructure is not thermally stable and subsequent heat treatment at
elevated temperatures can prompt the phase transformations of re-
tained metastable $\beta$ phase.

### 3.2. Microstructure evolution and oxidation during cyclic annealing

During the cycle annealing of the quenched Ti-24Al-11Nb alloy, the oxidation process takes place along with the alloy microstructure changes. The composition and morphology of the formed oxide scale, kinetics of its formation and microstructure variation strongly depend on the oxidation conditions. Among the various factors, deposited Ni-20Cr protective coating exerts the greatest influence on the oxidation behavior and phase transformations upon annealing. This is particularly pronounced at the higher annealing temperature when it accelerates the O-Ti₂AlNb intermetallic compound formation.

#### 3.2.1. Annealing temperature of $600\,^{\circ}\text{C}$

The microstructures of the uncoated alloy after oxidation at $600\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ for different annealing times are similar to the initial microstructure. As can be seen from AFM images shown in Fig. 2, where the $\beta$ phase is displayed as bright-colored areas, the two-phase $\alpha_{2}+\beta$ microstructure is maintained even after 120 h of annealing time. Due to the limited driving force of phase transformation at this relatively low annealing temperature, the new phases do not appear. It is obvious that the differences only being on the shape, size and volume fraction of the $\alpha_{2}$ phase. In the early stages of annealing, the $\alpha_{2}$ platelets become shorter and thicker. Since the low aspect ratio $\alpha_{2}$ platelets have less total interfacial energy compared with that of the big aspect ratio $\alpha_{2}$ platelets, the energy reduction provides the driving force for the $\alpha_{2}$ platelets morphology change. By increasing the annealing time to 120 h, the $\alpha_{2}$ platelets transforms into near equiaxed $\alpha_{2}$ grains to minimize the alloy free energy. Moreover, some $\alpha_{2}$ phase transforms into the $\beta$ phase leading to the smaller volume fraction of $\alpha_{2}$ phase.

It is also found that the hot-rolled and quenched alloy shows good oxidation resistance in the air when the annealing temperature is $600\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. Although the alloy surface was not protected with a coating, the formation and growth of oxide scale are relatively slow. Fig. 3a shows the oxidation kinetics as a function of the mass gain versus annealing time. It can be seen that the mass gain increases with increasing the annealing time, but after 120 h of oxidation the total mass gain is only $3\,\text{mg/cm}^2$. The mass gain curve can be described with a kinetic model given as follows [1]:
$$
(\Delta m)^{n}=k_{p} t \tag{1}
$$
where $\Delta m$ is the mass gain per unit surface area, $k_{p}$ is oxidation rate constant, $n$ is the oxidation rate power exponent and $t$ is the oxidation time.

The values of both $k_{p}$ and $n$ can be evaluated from a linear regression fitting of $\log\ (\Delta m)$ vs. $\log\ t$. The oxidation kinetic model is linear when $n$ equals 1.0, while is parabolic when $n$ equals 2.0 and so on [1].

According to Schaeffer [2], only $n = 2.0$ corresponds to parabolic rate. If the slopes range from 1.18 to 2.13, the oxidation kinetics for the Ti₃Al-based alloys is mixed. Although different kinetic models can be applied to define oxidation rate, the parabolic rate law valid under certain ideal conditions [50] was considered as the basis for interpretation of experimental data obtained in the previous studies [19,51,52]. However, most of the investigators observed a significant deviation from the parabolic behavior at higher temperatures. Thus, Zhu et al. [19] noted the parabolic oxidation rates for the blank and Al₂O₃-coated Ti-24Al-14Nb-3V-0.5Mo-0.3Si alloy isothermally annealed in the air at a temperature of 900 °C, while the oxidation rate of the blank alloy at 1000 °C deviated from the parabolic law. When the cyclic oxidation was carried out, the inflection of the mass gain curve corresponding to the blank alloy occurred at 800 and 900 °C. A non-parabolic oxidation process at higher temperature was observed earlier than that at 800 °C. An effect similar to breakaway oxidation leading to a linear rate law was also reported by Becker et al. [52]. Wiedemann et al. [53] even concluded that the rate of the complex oxidation behavior of Ti-14Al-21Nb (mass %) alloy changes two or more times, depending on the temperature and exposure time. The $k_p$ and $n$ values obtained in the present study are listed in Table 1. As can be seen, the oxidation kinetic of the uncoated alloy at 600 °C follows the linear rate law. A linear kinetic curve indicates that the oxide scale formed during cyclic annealing is not protective because the cracking and spallation of oxide scale intensify oxidation process [19]. Namely, after extensive spallation the alloy surface is again exposed to the oxidizing environment and oxidation process continues, resulting in the oxidation rate increase.

This is supported by the cross-sectional SEM observations of the uncoated alloy oxidized for different annealing times. After the first cycle, the oxide layer is not formed perfectly yet. Namely, the continuous oxide layer is not visible on the alloy surface after 24 h of annealing time, while the XRD patterns show the presence of only $α_2$ and $β$ phases.

However, the EDS analysis demonstrates that the alloy surface is rich in oxygen, Fig. 4a. Detailed SEM/EDS examination of the cross-section reveals that the oxidation is taken place. Discrete isolated scale islands could be observed at some locations, Fig. 4b. Since the oxide scale does not completely cover the uncoated alloy surface, it is difficult to detect its presence by the XRD analysis. It can be assumed that the thin continuous oxide scale layer is indeed formed, but the most of the oxide scale spall during the cooling after oxidation. The second possible reason may be an uneven distribution of alloying elements in $α_2$ and $β$ phases, resulting in non-uniform oxide layer formation due to the difference in the Gibbs formation energy for $Al_2O_3$, TiO, $TiO_2$ and $Nb_2O_5$ oxides [50,54]. The formation energy values for less protective $TiO_2$ (rutile) and protective $Al_2O_3$ oxides in the reaction of pure Ti and Al with oxygen are similar [55]. On the other hand, the lower equilibrium oxygen partial pressure of $Al_2O_3$ ($p_{Al_2O_3}$) compared to that of $TiO_2$ ($p_{TiO_2}$) favors the formation of $Al_2O_3$. Based on the calculated the Al and Ti-activities ($a_{Al}$ and $a_{Ti}$) in Ti-Al intermetallic compounds, it is found that the formation of a continuous external $Al_2O_3$ scale can be achieved only when the Al concentration is over 50 at% [19]. Addition of Nb [56,57] increases the activity ratio ($a_{Al}/a_{Ti}$) in the alloy, so that the complete $Al_2O_3$ scale can be formed even when the Al concentration is less than 50 at% [52]. $Nb_2O_3$ oxide does not exist in scale when the Nb content is in the range of 10-15 at% [19,57,58]. The Nb addition at this level promotes the formation of $Al_2O_3$ oxide and incorporates into the $TiO_2$ oxide, suppressing the growth of scale [57]. The occurrence of $Nb_2O_5$ in Ti-Al-Nb alloy with both lower and higher Nb content leads to a drastic increase in the oxidation rate due to the high diffusion rate of oxygen in this oxide [19,50].

From the cross-sectional SEM image (Fig. 4b) and elemental X-ray maps (Fig. 4c), it can be observed that the scale islands formed on the uncoated alloy surface are composed of a dense $Al_2O_3$ oxide. Although the $Al_2O_3$ oxide forms more preferably than $TiO_2$ oxide, Al-activity is not high enough so that a continuous $Al_2O_3$ scale layer does not exist. Immediately beneath it, the thick diffusion-affected zone enriched with Ti, Al, Nb, O and N can be distinguished. During annealing, Ti, Al and Nb diffuse outward, while O and N diffuse inward. Due to its higher oxygen affinity, Al is oxidized preferentially. With the increasing oxidation time, the amount of $Al_2O_3$ scale continuously increases. Consequently, the subsurface zone becomes more and more depleted with Al and enriched with Ti and Nb. The subsurface enrichment results in solid-solution hardening, which causes a significant increase in the microhardness, Fig. 5.

By increasing the annealing time to 24 h, microhardness near the scale/alloy interface exhibits the value which is two times higher than the value on the same location before oxidation. The formation of $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ oxide enriched with $\mathrm{Nb}$ consumes $\mathrm{Ti}$ and $\mathrm{Nb}$, so that depletion becomes more pronounced. Since the main part of the oxide scale, with an overall thickness of about $15 \mu \mathrm{m}$, consists of the $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ oxide exhibiting faster growth rate, doping of $\mathrm{Nb}$ in defect structure of $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ is not enough limited. Although the substitution of $\mathrm{Nb}^{5+}$ for $\mathrm{Ti}^{4+}$ in $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ should decrease the concentration of the vacancies and in turn diffusion of oxygen [19,20], the growth of $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ in the oxide scale proceeds causing the intensive cracking of the scale. As a result the oxidation rate increases. Furthermore, the some of $\mathrm{Nb}$ dissolved in $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ may have an influence on the formation of the nitrides, showing a beneficial effect on the oxidation resistance [20,51]. The nitride layers along the scale/alloy interface act as a diffusion barrier thereby may be helpful in slowing down the growth of $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ oxide and preventing further oxidation. The elemental X-ray maps in Fig. 6c demonstrate that the N enrichment exists, but nitrides are not detected. Since the oxides are more stable than nitrides, the formation and maintenance of nitrides are possible if low partial pressure ratio $\left(p_{\mathrm{O}_{2}} / p_{\mathrm{N}_{2}}\right)$ is achieved [20]. Apart from the local consumption of oxygen [20], the application of coatings may contribute to the decrease in oxygen partial pressure [19].

The protective Ni-20Cr coating applied in the present study is quite effective to improve oxidation resistance of quenched Ti-24Al-11Nb alloy, which is accompanied by the morphological changes in the alloy microstructure. The equiaxed $\alpha_{2}$ phase grains homogeneously distributed within the $\beta$ matrix are observed even after the first 24 h of annealing, Fig. 7a. The finer $\alpha_{2}$ grains and greater volume fraction of $\beta$ phase indicate that coating deposition enhances the phase transformations. The volume fraction of the $\beta$ phase continuously increases with prolonging annealing time, while the volume fraction of $\alpha_{2}$ phase decreases. When the annealing time reaches 120 h, the more equiaxed $\alpha_{2}$ phase is dissolved and volume fraction of the $\beta$ phase increases, but the globular particles of new phase also appear.

As can be seen from the AFM images presented in Fig. 7b, the microstructure of coated alloy after the final annealing cycle consists of equiaxed $\alpha_{2}$ grains (in dark) surrounded by a $\beta$ matrix (in gray) and fine precipitates (in light) separating out from the $\beta$ phase. Since the new phase particles are extremely fine and present in a very small amount, it is difficult to determine their nature by XRD and EDS analysis. However, the possibility of the O-Ti₂AlNb phase formation cannot be excluded. The $\beta(\mathrm{B} 2) \rightarrow \mathrm{O}$ transformation can occur in quenched and aged alloys, containing as little as 15 at% Nb [11]. According to Banerjee et al. [21], only the O-phase and $\alpha_{2}$ phase coexisted in the Ti-25Al12.5Nb alloy that was furnace cooled from $1100{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. On the other hand, the practical alloys contain the $\alpha_{2}$, O-phase and some volume fraction of the B2 phase [22]. Hence, the intragranular O-phase particles

### 3.3. Structure prediction and modeling on ab initio level

The complicated structure of the O-Ti₂AlNb phase, which we observed in the oxidized alloy microstructures, has been mostly characterized by using partial occupancy (see Fig. 16a [12]). The applied ab initio modeling showed that this problem can be successfully overcome and the resulting optimized O-phase structure is shown in Fig. 16b.

In the literature, the O-phase structure is described by the layer or sub-lattice model [7,9,12,59]. However, we performed a detailed structural analysis and characterized O modification in the new manner presented in Fig. 17. Thus, Fig. 17a shows 6-fold coordination of Ti atoms, while Nb atoms are coordinated by four Al atoms, as represented in Fig. 17b. Finally, in Fig. 17c Ti atoms are surrounded by four Nb and four Al atoms, creating distorted cubes and in total 8-fold coordination for titanium.

Predicting which crystalline structure can exist in a chemical system usually requires a global exploration of its energy landscape or/and data mining searches [60,61]. The most common exploration methods were described in detail elsewhere. [31,62]. After detailed structural analysis and data mining based searches, two related structure candidates were found. We would like to highlight two predicted models, which have never been experimentally observed or calculated before in the Ti₂AlNb system.

Two crystalline modifications derived from the AlCu₂Mn structure or so-called Heusler structure (Strukturbericht designation L2₁ and Pearson symbol cF16) [63,64] were proposed. Heusler compounds have face-centered cubic (fcc) structure with Fm-3m (no. 225) space group. First predicted "H1" (Heusler-1) phase was created by replacing Cu atoms by Ti atoms, and Mn atoms with Nb atoms, while Al atoms remained in their original Wyckoff positions (Fig. 18).

Finally, "H2" (Heusler-2) phase was created by replacing atomic positions of Al for Nb, and vice versa, from the "H1" (Heusler-1) modification, as shown in Fig. 19. Both predicted Heusler structures have the 8-fold coordination of titanium by Al and Nb atoms, as in the related O-phase. Furthermore, H1 and H2 phases show perfect cubes polyhedra with high cubic symmetry, compared to the distorted cubic arrangement and low orthorhombic symmetry in the O-phase, which makes them ideal structure candidates in the Ti₂AlNb (see Figs. 17–19).

Table 2 shows structural data for the calculated Ti₂AlNb modifications investigated using LDA, GGA and B3LYP method. When comparing experimental results (a = 6.09 Å, b = 9.57 Å, c = 4.67 Å) and the results of ab initio calculations, the best agreement was found with the hybrid B3LYP approach (Table 2). We note that H1 and H2 predicted phases show virtually the same unit cell parameters and atomic positions. When comparing total energies of the calculated modifications, we observe O-phase as the lowest energy minimum, while H1 and H2 show indistinguishable energy ranking, which is to be expected since there is no change in the unit cell (Tables 2, 3).

In short, presented ab initio calculations for O-phase are confirmed by experimental observations at high temperatures. Also, two additional modifications (labeled H1 and H2) are proposed, which should have an equal chance of existence in the Ti₂AlNb compound.

the experimental and theoretical results the following conclusions can be drawn:

1.  The cyclic oxidation of Ti-24Al-11Nb alloy with two-phase $\alpha_{2}+\beta$ microstructure in the air at 600 °C and 900 °C leads to significant microstructural changes and degradation of the alloy. During annealing for 120 h at both test temperatures, external oxidation occurs, while the presence of the $\beta$ phase in the higher amount achieved by quenching is effective in preventing internal oxidation. The alloy oxidation is accompanied with the several phase transformations leading to the appearance of new phases in both uncoated and coated alloy microstructure.

2.  The oxidation kinetics, composition and morphology of the formed scale vary greatly with annealing temperature. The alloy shows better oxidation resistance at 600 °C. Although the linear rate law is obeyed, the mono-layered scales are thinner and more adherent than that formed at 900 °C. The oxidation rate constant $k_{p}$ value for the higher temperature is more than an order of magnitude larger than in the case of oxidation at 600 °C. The oxide scale growth on alloy annealed at 900 °C obeys the approximately parabolic law, indicating a change in the external scale formation mechanism. At 900 °C, the very thick scales are multi-layered with porous structure and cracks. The layers detachment resulting in significant scale spallation reduces the alloy oxidation resistance.

3.  Main oxidation products are Al₂O₃ and Nb-doped TiO₂ oxides, irrespective of the annealing temperature. Formation of Al₂O₃ oxide, showing positive influence on the alloy oxidation resistance, is supported by the presence of thin AlN and TiN films beneath the oxide scale.

4.  The AlN and TiN films formed in early cyclic oxidation stages confirm the previous findings that Nb promotes the easier formation of protective nitride layers, representing the diffusion barrier. The Ni-20Cr coating deposition also favors nitride formation.

5.  The applied thin Ni-20Cr coating remarkable improves the alloy oxidation resistance, even at 900 °C, through the compact Cr₂O₃ oxide layer formation and Ni ions incorporation in porous external scale. The activation energy for oxidation of the coated alloy $E_{a}$ is 325.7 KJ/mol, while in the case of uncoated alloy value is 641 KJ/mol, indicating that the oxidation process is not controlled by the same mechanism. The formed protective Cr₂O₃ oxide increases the content of slow-growing Al₂O₃ in the mixed oxides, decreasing the alloy oxidation rate especially during cyclic annealing at 600 °C. The scales on the coated alloy are thinner, compact and adherent so that the scale-spallation resistance is improved.

6.  The uncoated alloy microstructure also varies greatly with the annealing temperature and duration. At 600 °C, the $\alpha_{2}\rightarrow\beta$ phase transformation occurs. With oxidation progress, the $\alpha_{2}$ phase volume fraction decreases and $\alpha_{2}$ platelets morphology changes into near equiaxed grains. At higher temperature, more $\alpha_{2}$ phase transforms in $\beta$ phase while new $\alpha_{2}''$ and O-Ti₂AlNb phases appear. With prolonging oxidation time, the O-Ti₂AlNb phase fraction increases due to the intensive outward diffusion of Ti, Al and Nb. The O-Ti₂AlNb phase has distinct morphologies because it forms by different solid-state reactions.

7.  The beneficial effect of Ni-20Cr coating deposition also reflects in more intensive phase transformations. Apart from the $\alpha_{2}\rightarrow\beta$ phase transformation, the $\alpha_{2}''$ dissolves and O-Ti₂AlNb phase forms faster and in the higher amount due to the greater enrichment in the subsurface diffusion zone as a consequence of the preferable formation of protective Cr₂O₃, Al₂O₃, AlN and TiN layers.

8.  The experimentally observed O-Ti₂AlNb phase is confirmed by *ab initio* calculations. The complex O-Ti₂AlNb structure is successfully characterized and calculated, followed by detailed crystallographic and structural analysis.

9.  Additional structure candidates are proposed and calculated on *ab initio* level from the structure related Heusler compounds.
