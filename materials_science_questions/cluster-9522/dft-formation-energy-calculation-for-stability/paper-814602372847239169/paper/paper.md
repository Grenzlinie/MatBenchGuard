## ABSTRACT

The concentrations of Sn on the rolling surface $(S_{N})$, the perpendicular to transversal direction surface $(S_{T})$ and the perpendicular to rolling direction surface $(S_{R})$ of Zircaloy-4 (Zr-Sn alloy) and B alloy (Zr-Sn-Nb alloy) specimens which possess similar texture structure were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) at room temperature, 773 and 873 K, respectively. The results show that surface segregation of Sn on surfaces of Zircaloy-4 specimens at 773, 873 K and B alloy specimens at 873 K is anisotropic due to the difference of surface energy among $S_{N}, S_{T}$ and $S_{R}$ surfaces. The level of Sn surface segregation for B alloy specimens is much higher than that for Zircaloy-4 specimens at 873 K although the Sn content of B alloy is lower than Zircaloy-4. It is suggested that the surface segregation of Sn is enhanced by Nb in $\alpha$-Zr matrix. And that is verified by the results of the heat of mixing of $Zr_{53}Sn_{1}$ and $Zr_{52}Sn_{1}Nb_{1}$ models calculated by density functional theory (DFT).

## 1. Introduction

It is well know that the surface energy is higher than that of bulk due to the less of atom coordination number and there are some dangling bonds. In order to decrease the surface energy, some alloying element atoms in the bulk are found to segregate on the surface. The segregation of alloying element atoms in alloy and the doping atoms in ceramic and semiconductor on the surface of materials will be presented during the process or under special condition. The surface segregation could change the surface atom composition and structure and many kinds of surface properties [1,2].

A lot of work about the segregation on material surface has been reported since 1960s. Such as, Tan et al. [3] found that Sn segregated on the Al-Sn alloy surface at 573 K. Prasad et al. [4] reported that Sn enriched on the surface in Sn-Zn and Al-Sn systems and Zn segregated on the surface in Al-Zn system. Kumar et al. [5] employed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and auger electron spectroscopy (AES) techniques to study the segregation of Ni, Sn and Ag, Ge on Cu-9.7 at.% Ni-2.3 at.% Sn and Ag-39 at.% Cu-1.6 at.% Ge alloy specimen surfaces when all alloy specimens were heated in the vacuum system. Campbell et al. [6] and Correr et al. [7] used XPS method to detect the segregation of Sr and Ba on the doped MgO ceramic specimens. Tinkov et al. [8] found that the law of Mo and Co segregated in the outmost layer and the underlying layers of surface for the alloy specimens was different, whether the specimens were annealed at 1073 K or not, and increasing of Co concentration and decreasing of Mo concentration in the near-surface region for the non-annealed specimen after heating the specimen. Wynblatt et al. [9] suggested that the effect of Au on the segregation of Ag in the ternary Cu-Ag-Au alloy was not obvious, while the effect of Ag on the segregation of Au was obvious. Zhou et al. [10] used AES technique to investigate the segregation of Sn on Zircaloy-4 specimen surfaces at an elevated temperature in the range of 673-873 K, and the content of Sn on the surface increased with the increase of holding time. Zhang et al. [11] found the segregation of Fe on the surface of Zr-50 ppm Fe single crystal specimen at a temperature in the range of 573-1090 K.

In addition to the recitation above, many researchers studied the alloying element atoms segregation on different orientation planes of a single crystal. Plessis et al. [12] employed AES and low energy electron diffraction (LEED) methods to study segregation energy of (1 1 1), (1 1 0) and (1 0 0) surfaces of Cu single crystals at a temperature in the range of 660-840 K, and the results showed that they were different. Zhang et al. [13] investigated the segregation of H on (0 00 1) and (1 0 1 0) planes of Zr single crystals by static secondary ion mass spectroscopy (SSIMS) and AES, and the segregation energy of these two planes were 10.1 and 10.7 kcal/mol, respectively. Steigerwald et al. [14] found that the chemical compositions on the surfaces of 38 grains with different orientations in

* Corresponding author.

a Ni-0.1 at.% Au alloy at four different temperatures in the range of 1083–1173 K were different, and showed an anisotropic character of surface segregation.

There have been relatively few attempts to study the surface segregation behaviour on different surfaces of the same zirconium alloy specimens having texture structure. Up to now, number of reports showed that the diffusion of alloying element atoms along perpendicular to c-axis and parallel to c-axis direction in Zr single crystal was anisotropic [15–21]. So we suggest that the surface segregation of some alloying element atoms in zirconium alloys could also be anisotropic. In order to validate it, we investigated the surface segregation behavior of Sn on three different surfaces of zirconium alloy specimens which possess texture structure by XPS technique. Furthermore, the density functional theory (DFT) method was used to calculate the heat of mixing of both $Zr_{53}Sn_{1}$ and $Zr_{52}Sn_{1}Nb_{1}$ models to explain the effect of Nb addition on the surface segregation behaviour of Sn.

## 2. Experimental

### 2.1. Specimen preparation

In order to investigate the surface segregation of Sn in zirconium alloys, Zircaloy-4 and B alloy with the addition of Nb were adopted. The chemical compositions of these two alloys are given in Table 1. Two pieces of specimens about $24 \times 15 \times 2$ mm were cut from each alloy plate.

The rolling surface ($S_{\text{N}}$), the perpendicular to the transversal direction surface ($S_{\text{T}}$) and the perpendicular to the rolling direction surface ($S_{\text{R}}$) specimens about $7 \times 2 \times 1$ mm for XPS test were sliced from one specimen of each kind of these two alloys. The schematic diagram of XPS specimen preparation is shown in Fig. 1. And another piece of specimen of each alloy was used for texture determination by electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) analysis. All of the specimens were annealed at 853 K for 10 h to obtain a fully recrystallized structure. Prior to annealing, all specimens were ground using 800, 1200 grit SiC paper, and pickled in a solution of 30 vol.%$\text{HNO}_{3}$, 30 vol.%$\text{H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4}$, 30 vol.%$\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$ and 10 vol.% HF, and then cleaned with deionized water finally.

### 2.2. EBSD analysis

Before EBSD test, the annealed specimens were electro-polished in a solution of 20 vol.% $\text{HClO}_{4}$ and 80 vol. $\% \text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{OH}$ at about 273 K with 25 V DC. The grain size of these two alloy specimens after annealing at 853 K for 10 h was similar and smaller than $15 \, \mu\text{m}$. Fig. 2 clearly shows that the grain size of B alloy specimen is obviously smaller than $15 \, \mu\text{m}$. Apollo300 SEM equiped with EBSD was used to measure the specimen texture. EBSD data was collected from $100 \times 80 \, \mu\text{m}$ scans on the rolling surface of each specimen at a scanning step length of $0.5 \, \mu\text{m}$.

### 2.3. XPS determination

In order to detect the chemical compositions on the specimen surfaces, XPS (Escalab 250 xi) analysis was carried out with a monochromatic Al K$\alpha$ source under a pressure of $4.98 \times 10^{-8}$ mba. After 2000 eV $\text{Ar}^{+}$ sputtering for 50 s to remove the contamination on the surface, the survey scans of XPS spectrum acquired from surface with a binding energy range of 0–1486.6 eV and the detail spectra of Zr 3d, Fe 2p, Cr 2p, O 1s, Sn 2d, Nb 3d, C 1s were acquired at room temperature. Then the spectra were in situ acquired again after increasing the temperature to 773 and 873 K and holding for 10 min, respectively. The spot size of X-ray beam was $500 \, \mu\text{m}$, which was only one-fourth of the specimen width. So there was no doubt that all of the spectra were acquired from the specimen surface. The aid of our work was only to detect atomic concentration on the surfaces instead of the chemical state of the alloying element and therefore the curve fitting of the peak was not necessary in our work. Atomic concentrations on the surfaces of these two alloy specimens were calculated by Avantage software (vision 5.934).

### 2.4. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation

In order to illustrate the effect of Nb on the surface segregation of Sn, the DFT method was used to calculate the heat of mixing of Zr–Sn and Zr–Sn–Nb systems. The Kohn–Sham equations were solved using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation package (VASP) [22–25]. The valence electron and core interactions were described using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [26]. Electron exchange and correlation were described using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA) functional with a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV [27]. Integrations in reciprocal space were made according to the Monkhorst–Pack (MP) scheme, and a reasonably converged grid spacing and numbers of k-points were used, such as MP $5 \times 5 \times 5$ for a $3 \times 3 \times 3$ (54 atoms) cell. Song et.al [28] illustrated that the value of the heat of mixing has a little variation when the cell was larger than $3 \times 3 \times 3$. Therefore, the $3 \times 3 \times 3$ (54 atoms) cell was used to in our work since it is convenient for calculation and it is sufficient to illustrate the law of Nb effect on the Sn diffusion.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Specimen texture

Fig. 3 shows $\{0001\},\{11 \overline{2} 0\}$ and $\{10 \overline{1} 0\}$ pole figures of Zircaloy-4. RD and TD mean rolling and transversal directions of the specimen, the normal direction (ND) of the rolling surface is perpendicular to the paper. Pole figure is used to characterize a definite crystal plane of all grains in specimen project into the specimen coordinate system (ND, RD and TD) [29]. For instance, the $\{0001\}$ pole figure presents the distribution of the definite $\{0001\}$ plane of all grains in the specimen. So we can know that the normal direction of $\{0001\}$ plane of all grains in Zircaloy-4 is nearly parallel to the ND in terms of Fig. 3. On the other hand, the normal direction of $\{11 \overline{2} 0\}$ and $\{10 \overline{1} 0\}$ planes of all grains in Zircaloy-4 specimen are nearly parallel to the RD and TD. Therefore, it is concluded that Zircaloy-4 specimen possess $(0001)[10 \overline{1} 0]$ and $(0001)[11 \overline{2} 0]$ texture. And the texture of B alloy specimen is nearly the same as Zircaloy-4 specimen. The values of $\{0001\},\{10 \overline{1} 0\}$ and $\{11 \overline{2} 0\}$ texture factor for $S_{N}, S_{R}$ and $S_{T}$ surfaces $(f_{N}, f_{R}$ and $f_{T})$ of these two alloy specimens are showed in Table 2. The value of $\{0001\}$ texture factor for $S_{N}$ surface $f_{N\{0001\}}$ are larger than 0.77 for all specimens and the values of $\{10 \overline{1} 0\}$ and $\{11 \overline{2} 0\}$ texture factor for $S_{N}, S_{R}$ and $S_{T}$ surfaces of Zircaloy-4 specimen are nearly the same as that of B alloy specimen. From the values shown in Table 2, it also can be known that these two alloy specimens have nearly the same $(0001)[10 \overline{1} 0]$ and $(0001)[11 \overline{2} 0]$ texture.

### 3.2. Surface segregation anisotropy of Sn

Fig. 4 shows some XPS spectra of our test alloy specimens. The XPS whole spectrum acquired from $S_{T}$ surface of B alloy specimen at 873 K after holding for 10 min is shown in Fig. 4a. Zr, Sn, Fe, C and O are found on this surface except Nb and Cr. It is presumably that the content of Nb and Cr on the surface is lower than the resolution of XPS technique (0.1 at.%). By calculating, chemical composition of the surface includes $55.75\%$ Zr, $2.82\%$ Sn, $28.31\%$ C, $12.14\%$ O and $0.99\%$ Fe (at.%). Fig. 4b shows the Zr 3d and Sn 3d XPS spectra on $S_{N}, S_{R}$ and $S_{T}$ surfaces of Zircaloy-4 specimen at 773 K after holding for 10 min. The binding energies 484.3 eV for Sn $3d_{5/2}$ and 492.8 eV for Sn $3d_{3/2}$ are shown in Fig. 4b. The peak of Sn 3d acquired from $S_{N}$ surface is not raised but that acquired from $S_{R}$ and $S_{T}$ surfaces are obviously raised. It is revealed that the concentrations of Sn on $S_{R}$ and $S_{T}$ surfaces are higher than that on $S_{T}$ surface for Zircaloy-4 specimen at 773 K. Fig. 4c shows the Zr 3d and Sn 3d XPS spectra acquired form $S_{N}$ surface of B alloy specimen at room temperature, 773 and 873 K. It is clear that the peak of Sn 3d at 873 K is the largest. In other words, the concentration of Sn on $S_{N}$ surface of B alloy specimen at 873 K is the highest in that detected at these three temperatures. In this part, only some XPS spectra of our test specimens are shown due to limited space. The concentrations of Sn on $S_{N}, S_{R}$, and $S_{T}$ surfaces of Zircaloy-4 and B alloy specimens at room temperature, 773 and 873 K were calculated through the XPS spectra by Avantage software. In order to eliminate the influence of other impurities, the ratio of Sn atom percentage to Zr atom percentage $(C_{Sn}/C_{Zr}$, signed as $r)$ was used to characterize the Sn surface segregation. And the result will be shown in the following Fig. 5.

Fig. 5 shows the values of $r$ on $S_{N}, S_{R}, S_{T}$ surfaces of these test alloy specimens at room temperature, 773 and 873 K. The two red broken lines in the graphs stand for the values of $r$ for Zircaloy-4 and B alloy bulk specimens in the original, and they are $9.1 \times 10^{-3}$ and $6.9 \times 10^{-3}$, respectively. In this study, we only discuss the surface segregation of Sn because the surface segregation of Fe, Cr, Nb could be affected by the diffusion of these alloying elements from the second phase particles (SPPs) [30,31] into the $\alpha$-Zr matrix due to the variation of the solid solution limitation of these alloying elements at different temperatures. The SPPs composed by Zr-Fe-Cr or Zr-Fe-Cr-Nb atoms exist inevitably in zirconium alloys. Therefore, the surface segregation of Fe, Cr, Nb in zirconium alloy specimens could not be accurately detected.

At room temperature, each $r$ is slightly higher than the initial value of the bulk specimens. It is possible that there is a Sn segregation layer which formed during annealing at 853 K has not been absolutely removed by $Ar^{+}$ sputtering. On the other hand, the values of $r$ for the three surfaces of each alloy specimen are nearly identical, which implies that the Sn segregation on different surfaces can be studied in the same condition at the beginning.

In the case of B alloy specimens, as shown in Fig. 5b, the values of $r_{S_N}$, $r_{S_R}$ and $r_{S_T}$ at 773 K are nearly equal to these at room temperature. [33] reported that the surface energy of (0001), $(10\bar{1}0)$, and $(11\bar{2}0)$ planes of zirconium crystal, which were calculated by modified embedded atom method (MEAM) were 2250, 3106.6 and $2646.7\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^2}$, respectively. Therefore, the content of Sn on $S_N$ surface is the least in that on the three surfaces for Zircaloy-4 specimen when the surface segregation occurred, and the tendency of segregation on different surfaces of specimens would be in order of $S_N < S_T \approx S_R$, and the surface segregation of Sn for this specimen is anisotropic. This is consistent with the result obtained in Zircaloy-4 specimens as shown in Fig. 5a. In addition, the value of $r_{S_N}$ is slightly decreased at 773 K when compared with that at room temperature. It is probable that the segregated Sn on the surface could be evaporated at 773 K [34], and the quantity of evaporation maybe slightly larger than that of segregation.

According to the order of surface energy for the three surfaces, B alloy specimen should also have a similar law of surface segregation as Zircaloy-4 specimen at 873 K, but it is unexpected that the level of Sn surface segregation for B alloy specimen is much higher than that for Zircaloy-4 specimen although the Sn content of B alloy is lower than that of Zircloy-4, as shown in Fig. 5b. We consider that the other alloying elements like Fe, Cr and Nb may play a key role in it. Because all factors of these two alloys are nearly identical except the content of Fe, Cr and Nb alloying elements.

It well known that the solid solubility of Fe and Cr alloying elements in $\alpha$-Zr is extremely low. On the contrary, the solid solubility of Sn alloying element in $\alpha$-Zr is relatively high. It is reported that the solid solubility of Fe is 120 ppm at 1093 K and the solid solubility of Cr is 200 ppm at 1133 K in $\alpha$-Zr [35]. Therefore, the Fe and Cr contents of the zirconium alloys adopted in present research are over the limitations of the solubility, and Zr-Fe-Cr SPPs can be precipitated, and all of the Sn in these two alloys absolutely solute in $\alpha$-Zr. Then the effect of Fe, Cr in $\alpha$-Zr matrix on the diffusion of Sn is the same for these two alloys. Consequently, the alloying element Nb is the only one factor to affect the surface segregation of Sn in B alloy specimens.

Furthermore, the diffusion of alloying elements would be influenced by the interaction among them in the alloy [36-42]. Based on the former assertions, the interaction between alloying elements also occurred in zirconium alloys and the surface segregation of Sn could be affected by the addition of Nb. As the surface segregation of Sn at 873 K in B alloy specimen is more obvious than that in Zircaloy-4 specimen, it could be suggested that the addition of Nb could prompt the diffusion of Sn. But why the surface segregation of Sn in B alloy specimens is nearly absent at 773 K? We consider that the interfaces between SPPs and $\alpha$-Zr matrix may play an important role in this phenomenon.

Fig. 6 is the electron micrograph observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which shows the distribution of the SPPs in Zircaloy-4 (a) and in B alloy (b) specimens annealed at 853 K for 10 h. Contrast in TEM images can arise due to the differences in the phase of the electron waves scattered through a thin specimen. The contrast is very sensitive to many factors: the appearances of the image varies with small changes in the thickness, crystal orientation, or scattering factor of the specimen, and variations in focus or astigmatism of the objective lens [43]. The orientation and scattering factor of the SPPs are different with the matrix, thus the contrasts of the SPPs and the matrix are different. And all the dark dots signify SPPs in our test alloy specimens as shown in Fig. 6. It is obvious that the number of the SPPs in B alloy is much more than that of Zircaloy-4 since some $\beta$-Nb SPPs precipitated in B alloy due to the addition of Nb [31].

A lot of work carried out by atom probe tomography (APT) technique has been reported that various elements segregated in interfaces between SPPs and matrix in austenitic precipitation strengthened stainless steel, reactor pressure vessel (RPV) model steel and Nb-V micro-alloying steel after different heat treatments [44-49]. Based on these studies, it is no doubt that Sn would segregate in the interfaces between SPPs and $\alpha$-Zr matrix in our test alloy specimens. Therefore, more of Sn in B alloy specimens could segregate in the interfaces between SPPs and $\alpha$-Zr matrix during the heating in the first instance at 773 K due to more SPPs/matrix interfaces. With going on increasing the temperature, the activity of Sn which enriched in the interfaces between SPPs and $\alpha$-Zr matrix increase and then they diffused to the surface through the grain boundaries and crystal lattice. Consequently, the surface segregation of Sn in B alloy specimens is obvious at 873 K, but on the contrary at 773 K.

where $E((N$-$1)$Zr+$1$Sn) is the energy of a supercell containing $(N$-$1)$Zr atoms and one Sn atom, $E((N$-$1)$Zr+$1$Nb) is the energy of a supercell containing $(N$-$1)$Zr atoms and one Nb atom, $E((N$-$2)$Zr+$1$Sn+$1$Nb) is the energy of a supercell containing $(N$-$2)$Zr atoms and one Sn atom and one Nb atom, $E_\text{ref}$ is the energy of a supercell containing $N$ Zr atoms, and $E(Sn_\text{ref})$ and $E(Nb_\text{ref})$ are the cohesive energies of β-Sn and Nb with body-centred cubic (Body-centred cubic, BCC) structure.

Table 3 gives the heat of mixing at different substitutive conditions, and two conditions of Nb and Sn atoms at the near est neighbor are considered, $a$ direction (as shown in Fig. 7, $E_\text{m}$ (Sn,Nb → Zr)) and $c$ direction ($E_\text{m}$ (Sn, Nb → Zr)). The negative value of $E_\text{m}$ (Sn → Zr) illustrates that Sn atoms prefer to dis perse in $\alpha$-Zr matrix. The positive value of $E_\text{m}$ (Nb → Zr) reveals that Nb atoms prefer to precipitate in $\alpha$-Zr matrix. The value of $E_\text{m}$ (Sn, Nb → Zr) is less than the sum of $E_\text{m}$ (Sn → Zr) and $E_\text{m}$ (Nb → Zr), showing that Sn and Nb prefer to gather together in the $\alpha$-Zr matrix. Our results are in line with the values from Miedema's model calculated by Takeuchi and Inoue [51] for the heat of mixing of Zr-Sn, Zr-Nb and Sn-Nb systems are -43, 4 and -1 kJ/mol, respectively. Moreover, all of the values of the heat of mixing of Zr₅₂Sn₁Nb₁ model are higher than that of Zr₅₃Sn₁ model. It is indicated that Zr₅₃Sn₁ model is more stable than Zr₅₂Sn₁Nb₁ model. On the other hand, the diffusion rate of Nb in $\alpha$-Zr is approximate one order of magnitude faster than the self-diffusion rate of Zr in $\alpha$-Zr from the experimentally measured values [16,52]. Based on the three aspects that Sn prefers to contact with Nb in $\alpha$-Zr matrix, Zr₅₃Sn₁ model is more stable than Zr₅₂Sn₁Nb₁ model and the diffusion rate of Nb is faster in $\alpha$-Zr than that of Zr. We can deduce that the atoms of Sn in $\alpha$-Zr matrix after adding the Nb diffuse faster than the atoms of Sn in $\alpha$-Zr matrix without Nb element. Namely, the diffusion rate of Sn in $\alpha$-Zr matrix is promoted by the addition of Nb. This results in that the surface segregation of Sn is more obvious in Zr-Sn-Nb system than that in Zr-Sn system under the same condition, which is well consistent with our experimental results.

## 4. Conclusion

Sn would segregate on the surface of Zircaloy-4 (Zr-Sn alloy) and B alloy (Zr-Sn-Nb alloy) specimens at elevated temperature since the surface energy is higher than that of bulk, and the Sn surface segregation for these two alloy specimens which possess (0001) [10$\overline{1}$0] and (0001) [11$\overline{2}$0] texture is anisotropic due to the difference of surface energy among (0001), (10$\overline{1}$0) and (11$\overline{2}$0) planes of zirconium crystal. Furthermore, the surface segregation of Sn for Zr-Sn-Nb alloy is more obvious than that for Zr-Sn alloy since the diffusion of Sn is prompted by the addition of Nb. This is verified by the result of heat of mixing calculation.
