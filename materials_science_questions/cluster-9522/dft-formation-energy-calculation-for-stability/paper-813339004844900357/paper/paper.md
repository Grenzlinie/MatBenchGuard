# Structural stability and generalized stacking fault energies in $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys: Relation to dislocation properties

J. Huang, H. Xing* and J. Sun*

School of Materials Science and Engineering, Shanghai Jiaotong University, Shanghai 200240, PR China

Received 6 November 2011; accepted 17 January 2012
Available online 25 January 2012

The structural stability and generalized stacking fault energies of the {112}<111> and {110}<111> slip systems in $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys with various valence electron numbers were surveyed by ab initio calculations. The structural stability and unstable stacking fault energies along these slip systems decrease monotonously with reducing valence electron number in these alloys. The structure of the dislocation core indicates there is a strong tendency of for the screw dislocations to split into partials in Ti-25Nb (at.%) alloy.

Keywords: Titanium alloys; Dislocation structure; Ab initio calculation; Generalized stacking fault energy

$\beta$ Ti alloys have attracted much attention for application as biomaterials [1,2]. Recently, Saito et al. [3] developed a group of multifunctional $\beta$ Ti alloys, called gum metals. These Ti-Nb-based alloys simulta- neously show low Young's modulus, superelasticity, high strength, etc., after substantial cold working. The unique properties of gum metals were originally attrib- uted to a dislocation-free plastic deformation mecha- nism. Li et al. [4] also proposed that these alloys would reach an ideal shear strength before conventional dislocation slip is activated. However, subsequent stud- ies have shown that gum metals deform plastically via conventional dislocations, twinning and stress-induced phase transformation [5-8]. Very recently, Castany et al. [9] observed screw dislocations with jerky motion during in situ tensile test in gum metals. They considered the low mobility of the screw dislocations to be due to their spreading core structures that have to recombine to glide.

Generally the $1/2<111>$ screw dislocation cores spread into several planes of the $<111>$ zone in body centered cubic (bcc) metals. The most important dislocation core feature is the possible splitting into par- tial dislocations separated by stacking faults. Vitek [10] originally introduced the $\gamma$-surface in order to provide a comprehensive description of stacking fault energy in bcc materials. They found that no stable intrinsic stack- ing faults exist in bcc materials on the {110} or {112} slip planes. A further criterion has also been proposed to predict the core structures by comparison with the specific values in generalized stacking fault energy (GSFE) curves [11]. For gum metals, Bodylev et al. [12] suggested a nonplanar splitting of the core of screw dislocations and calculated the energy difference be- tween the nonsplit and split dislocation configurations. Bodylev et al.'s model treated the GSFE as approximat- ing to a sinusoidal function, though the actual GSFE usually has varies from this.

To our knowledge, no data for stacking fault energies in the $\beta$-type Ti alloys have been reported in the litera- ture. Additionally it has been reported that the deforma- tion behavior of $\beta$ Ti alloys with the bcc structure strongly depends on the stability of the $\beta$ phase. In this paper, we calculated the structural stability and GSFE of the $<111>\{110\}$ and $\{112\}$ slip systems in $\beta$ Ti Nb alloys as a function of the Nb content, i.e. the va- lence electron number $(e/a)$. Based on the GSFE results, the structures of screw dislocation cores were deter- mined using the Duesbery-Vitek criterion. Furthermore, the energy difference between the nonsplit and split dis- location configurations was evaluated by introducing the calculated GSFE into Bodylev et al.'s model.

Brillouin zone (BZ) integration follows the Methfessel-Paxton scheme with the smearing width chosen so that the "-TS" term is less than 0.5 MeV/atom⁻¹ [16]. The periodic supercells of $e_1 \times me_2 \times ne_3$ with $e_1=[111]a_0/2$, $e_2=[0-11]a_0$, and $e_3=[2-1-1]a_0$ were used to establish a slab with 12 atomic layers to calculate the stacking fault energy. The stacking fault in the <111>{110} or {112} slip systems can be generated by shifting the upper half of the crystal relative to the lower half with different displacement vectors along the <111> direction on the {110} or {112} plane, respectively. We then allowed all atoms of the supercell to fully relax in the direction vertical to the slip plane. The GSFE were obtained from the energy difference between the supercells with and without displacement per unit area. The maximum GSFE, i.e. the unstable stacking fault energy $\gamma_{\text{us}}$, was achieved at a displacement vector equal to $e_1/2$. The calculations were performed for β Ti-Nb alloys containing 25, 50 and 75 at.% Nb. Thus, $e/a$ is 4.25, 4.5 and 4.75 for these Ti-Nb alloys, respectively. The ab initio calculations were also performed for pure Nb with the bcc structure and with $e/a=5.0$.

To survey the structural stability of these β Ti-Nb alloys, the total energies were first calculated for the bcc structure as a function of the cell volume and for the hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure as a function of the cell volume and the $c/a$ ratio as well, respectively. The elastic constants were calculated for the β Ti-Nb alloys with bcc structure using volume-conserving tetragonal and monoclinic strains. The total energy difference of the Ti-Nb alloys between the bcc and hcp structure and the tetragonal shear modulus $C'=(C_{11}-C_{12})/2$ of the β Ti-Nb alloys as a function of $e/a$ are shown in Figure 1a. The calculated tetragonal shear moduli of these β Ti-Nb alloys as a function of $e/a$ are in good agreement with other theoretical calculations in the literature [17]. It can be seen that the energy difference and $C'$ of the β Ti-Nb alloys decrease with decreasing $e/a$ and approach zero when $e/a$ reaches 4.25, which is associated with the transition from bcc to hcp structure. These properties are approximately proportional to each other for the β Ti-Nb alloys, as exhibited in Figure 1b, which is behavior typical of transition metals [18]. The calculated results indicate that the structural stability decreases with reducing $e/a$ in the β Ti-Nb alloys.

Thus, $G_{111}$ decreases with decreasing structural stability of the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys, which has a good agreement with our previous results [5]. Vitek et al. [11] indicated that the maximum value of $\gamma$ is strongly related to the cohesive energy of materials. The cohesive energy of the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys also decreases with reducing $e/a$ [21]. These findings are in good agreement with the result calculated in this work.

The unstable stacking fault energy difference between the {110}&lt;111&gt; and {112}&lt;111&gt; slip systems still remains in the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys when $e/a$ falls. However, when $e/a$ reaches 4.25, the unstable stacking fault energies are 307 and $296\ \text{mJ m}^{-2}$ in the {110}&lt;111&gt; and {112}&lt;111&gt; slip systems, respectively, and the difference between these unstable stacking fault energies can be nearly ignored in the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys. This suggests that the overall slip planes change from {110}&lt;111&gt; at $e/a=5.0$ to both {112} and {110}&lt;111&gt; at $e/a=4.25$. This change in slip plane upon alloying is similar to the effect of Re on the dislocation core structure in W [22]. As suggested by Castany [9], the deformation rate in gum metals is mainly controlled by the motion of screw dislocation, and nonscrew dislocation is only correlated with pinning obstacles. Therefore, we only focus on the core structure of screw dislocations in the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys.

The nonplanar core structure of screw dislocations in bcc transition metals has been widely accepted, and is usually employed to explain their complex plastic deformation behaviors. Duesbery and Vitek [11] proposed a criterion that relates the {110} GSFE to the $b=1/2<111>$ screw dislocation core structure. The criterion is that if $\gamma(b/3)/2\gamma(b/6)>1$, the screw dislocation core has a 6-fold symmetric, nondegenerate structure. Pure Nb metal meets this criterion and has a 6-fold symmetric, nondegenerate screw dislocation core structure, as illustrated in Ref. [11]. The ratios of $\gamma(b/3)$ to $2\gamma(b/6)$ for the $\{110\}<111>$ slip system in the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys were calculated from Figure 2, as shown in Figure 3. The result for pure Nb metal is in good agreement with those obtained by Duesbery and Vitek [11]. It can be further seen from Figure 3 that the ratios of $\gamma(b/3)$ to $2\gamma(b/6)$ in {110} increase with decreasing $e/a$ in the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys. These imply that the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys, like pure Nb metal, have 6-fold symmetric, nondegenerate screw dislocation core structures, with small twinning–antitwinning asymmetry and weak interaction with the nonglide applied stress component. For $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys with lower stacking fault energy and a higher ratio of $\gamma(b/3)$ to $2\gamma(b/6)$ in {110}, the screw dislocation of $1/2[111]$ can be considered to be split into six partial dislocations of $a/12[111]$ more easily, which spread more extensively in the $(1-10)$, $(10-1)$, $(01-1)$, $(11-2)$, $(1-21)$ and $(-211)$ planes, respectively. In this case, an increased shear stress tending to move the screw dislocation is required because all of the extended partial dislocations must contract before motion can occur. In addition, the lower energy of such stacking faults results in larger spreading widths of the cores under applied stress [11]. This indicates that the Peierls stresses decrease with decreasing structural stability of the $\beta$ Ti–Nb alloys.

Following Bodylev et al.'s model [12], we replace the summed energy of the generalized stacking faults with the values from our calculations. The total energy change between nonsplit and split dislocation configurations can be given as follows:

For $N=3$ (where $N$ is the number of resultant partials)
$$
\Delta W=-\frac{G a^{2}}{16 \pi}\left(\ln \frac{4 r^{2}}{3 a^{2}}+2\right)+3 \gamma_{b / 3}^{110} r \tag{1}
$$

For $N=6$
$$
\Delta W=-\frac{5 G a^{2}}{32 \pi}\left(\ln \frac{2 r}{a \sqrt{3}}+1\right)+6 \gamma_{b / 6}^{\{110\}} r \tag{2}
$$

For $N=12$
$$
\Delta W=-\frac{11 G a^{2}}{128 \pi}\left(\ln \frac{4 r^{2}}{3 a^{2}}+2\right)+6 \gamma_{b / 12}^{\{110\}} r+6 \gamma_{b / 12}^{\{112\}} r \tag{3}
$$

In addition, the minimum values of $\Delta W(r)$ for 12-par tial splitting in various Ti-Nb alloys and pure Nb are summarized in Figure 4b. This shows that the dependences $\Delta W(r)$ for 12-partial splitting decreases with decreasing structural stability of the $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys. Moreover, only $\Delta W(r)$ for 12-partial splitting in Ti-25Nb can reach a negative value, meaning that there is a strong tendency for screw dislocations to split into 12 partials in Ti-25Nb alloy. The subsequence of this type of core configuration will result in so-called jerky motion of screw dislocations. This is attributed to the presence of alloying element Ti into pure Nb which en hances the sessile-glissile transition compared with pure Nb [9]. The low mobility of the screw dislocations de rived from their spreading core structures means that they can recombine to glide. Based on the dislocation mechanism proposed by Hsiung and Lasilla [23], these splitting partials could recombine and be related to the easy occurrence of mechanical twinning and stress-in duced phase transformation in metastable $\beta$ Ti alloys with $e / a$ around 4.25. Additionally, the deformation mechanism usually changes from slip to mechanical twinning or stress-induced martensitic transformation with decreasing stability of $\beta$ Ti alloys [24]. It has been proposed that these phenomena are also closely related to low stacking fault energies [25].

In summary, the structural stability and GSFE of the $\{112\}<111>$ and $\{110\}<111>$ slip systems in $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys with various $e / a$ were surveyed by ab initio calcu lations. The results show that the structural stability and unstable stacking fault energies along these two slip sys tems decrease with reducing $e / a$ in $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys. The vanishing difference of the unstable stacking fault energy in Ti-25Nb indicates that the overall slip planes change from $\{110\}<111>$ to both $\{112\}$ and $\{110\}<111>$. The ratios of $\gamma(b / 3)$ to $2 \gamma(b / 6)$ in $\{110\}$ increase with decreasing $e / a$ in $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys. For the $\beta$ Ti-25Nb al loy, with lower stacking fault energy and higher ratio of $\gamma(b / 3)$ to $2 \gamma(b / 6)$ in $\{110\}$, the screw dislocations of $1 /$ 2[111] split into six partial dislocations of a/12[111] more easily. On the other hand, the dependence $\Delta W(r)$ for 12-partial splitting decreases with decreasing struc tural stability of the $\beta$ Ti-Nb alloys and only $\Delta W(r)$ for Ti-25Nb alloy can achieve a negative value. This indicates that there is a strong tendency for the screw dislocations to split into 12 partials in Ti-25Nb alloy, which corresponds to recent experimental observations.
