# Origin of the stability of two-dimensional perovskites: a first-principles study
Yi Yang, Fei Gao, Shiwu Gao* and Su-Huai Wei*

It usually costs energy to create a surface, so to reduce the instability of a material, it is often desirable to reduce the surface area. However, it has been reported recently that the instability of lead halide perovskites, which are promising materials for high efficiency and low cost solar cells, can be improved by reducing the dimensions of the perovskites from the three-dimensional (3D) bulk to that of two-dimensional (2D) films. So far, there is a lack of general understanding about this unusual behavior, i.e., why a 2D slab cut out of the 3D bulk material could be more stable than the 3D material. Here, we carry out a systematic and comparative study on the thermodynamic stability of 2D and 3D perovskites using the first-principles method based on density functional theory. Taking $CsPbBr_3$ and $MAPbI_3$ as prototype examples, we show that the thermodynamic stability of the 2D perovskite originates from the asymmetric surface properties of this material: the AX-terminated 2D $APbX_3$ are more stable than the $PbX_2$-terminated 2D counterpart due to fewer surface dangling bonds. The MAI-terminated 2D $MAPbI_3$ becomes even more stable than its 3D counterpart due to relaxation of the hydrogen bonds at the surface. Our results thus provide deep insights into the stability of 2D slabs versus 3D host materials, which shall be useful for improving the stability of perovskites and designing new 2D materials.

## Introduction
Lead halide perovskites are emerging as promising solar cell materials due to their excellent optoelectronic properties and the low-cost in producing these materials.¹⁻⁴ Power conversion efficiencies (PEC) of over 22% for solar cells based on lead halide perovskites have been achieved.⁵ However, the long-term performance of perovskite solar cells is hindered by the intrinsic thermal instability of the perovskites,⁶⁻⁸ which has limited their use in wide commercial application.⁵,⁹ Interestingly, recent experimental studies found that (quasi-)two-dimensional (2D) perovskites show much better stability than their 3D bulk counterparts.¹⁰⁻¹² These layered perovskites crystallize into a Ruddlesden-Popper phase.¹⁰,¹² For example, 2D $CsPbBr_3$ with a few layer thickness¹³,¹⁴ and 2D $CH_3NH_3PbI_3$ ($MAPbI_3$) with thickness down to a single unit cell have been synthesized in experiments.¹⁵ The degradation speed of 2D perovskites is much slower than their 3D materials.¹¹,¹²

However, the thermodynamic stability of these 2D perovskites is not well understood.¹¹,¹⁶,¹⁷ It is not clear why a slab cut out of a 3D material could be more stable than their 3D counterpart. Moreover, the surface thermodynamic properties of lead halide perovskites are also far from clear. In general, there is a lack of systematic understanding about relative thermodynamic stability between 2D slabs and 3D host perovskites.

In this work, we conduct a systematic study on the thermodynamic properties of 2D and 3D perovskites using the first-principles method based on density functional theory (DFT). Two prototype $APbX_3$ perovskites, $CsPbBr_3$ and $MAPbI_3$, are used as model systems for this purpose. The formation energy and surface energy of these perovskites are calculated and used to characterize the stability between 2D and 3D structures. It is found that AX-terminated 2D $APbX_3$ are thermodynamically more stable than the $PbX_2$-terminated structures due to fewer dangling bonds in the former case. In addition, significant surface relaxation, such as in the MAI-terminated 2D $MAPbI_3$, also contributes to the stability of the 2D structures. Our findings are in agreement with experiments, and provide insight into the puzzling experimental observations.

We then discuss the thermodynamic stability of 2D perovskites using two typical materials, orthorhombic CsPbBr₃ and MAPbI₃ (Fig. 1). Experimental studies have shown that 2D perovskites consist of the stacking of alternate AX and PbX₂ layers.¹³,¹⁵ The 2D slabs can be obtained by cutting through the (001) surface of the perovskites. These (001) surfaces are general to many perovskite phases, including the cubic phase. Fig. 1 displays the 2D orthorhombic CsPbBr₃ as an example. When orthorhombic CsPbBr₃ is cut through the (001) plane, two different surfaces, CsBr-terminated and PbBr₂-terminated, appear as the termination layer. Such a cutting gives two kinds of symmetric slabs: CsBr-terminated and PbBr₂-terminated (see Fig. 1(c) and (d)). The structures of 3D and 2D orthorhombic MAPbI₃ are similar to those of these orthorhombic CsPbBr₃. 2D perovskites with thickness of a monolayer (L1), double-layers (L2), and triple-layers (L3) are considered (see Fig. 1(c) and (d)). We calculate the formation energies of 2D CsPbBr₃ and MAPbI₃ with respect to the stable binary phases, CsBr (CH₃NH₃I) and PbBr₂ (PbI₂) under AX-rich and BX₂-rich conditions, respectively (see the Methods section). All 2D structures are calculated in a $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2}$ in-plane cell, as indicated in Fig. 1(a). The slab with a vacuum layer (>14 Å) extends periodically along the $c$ direction.

To better understand the origin of the termination-dependent (AX- or PbX₂⁻) stability of 2D perovskites, we calculate the surface energy of APbX₃ perovskites. The calculated surface energies of the (001) surfaces for the orthorhombic CsPbBr₃ and MAPbI₃ are listed in Table 2. Note that the surface energy of the AX-terminated surface is much lower than that of the PbX₂-terminated surface for both unrelaxed and relaxed cases. Moreover, the MAI-terminated surface shows extremely low surface energy that can be even negative. This suggests a very unusual stability of the MAI-terminated surface. These results indicate that the better stability of AX-terminated 2D perovskites, especially for MAI-terminated 2D MAPbI₃, is due to the lower surface energy of AX-terminated surfaces.

To reveal the origin of the difference in surface energy between AX-terminated and PbX₂-terminated surfaces, we first focus on unrelaxed CsPbBr₃ and MAPbI₃ surfaces (ideal cases), where atoms are located at the positions as in their bulk materials. From Table 2, the differences in surface energies between two types of unrelaxed surfaces are 0.16 eV for CsPbBr₃ and 0.25 eV for MAPbI₃. The lower energy of the AX-terminated surface could be attributed to the fact that the AX-terminated surface has less surface dangling bonds and moreover, the A-X bond is weaker than the Pb-X bond due to its high ionicity. Larger ionicity usually results in lower surface energy, as demonstrated for common semiconductors.¹⁹ From Table 2, it is also seen that relaxation plays an important role in decreasing the surface energy. The surface energies of CsBr- and PbBr₂-terminated surfaces are reduced by 0.08 eV and 0.06 eV, respectively. Notably, the surface energy of the MAI-terminated surface is decreased by 0.24 eV, and becomes negative after relaxation. In contrast, the relaxation of the PbI₂-terminated surface shows a much smaller effect with a reduction of only 0.04 eV. These results indicate that surface relaxation produces a larger effect on decreasing the surface energy of the AX-terminated surface than the PbX₂-terminated surface, especially for the MAI-terminated surface. Therefore, apart from the difference of bonding characters between two types of surfaces, surface relaxation enlarges the difference of surface energies between two types of surfaces, especially for MAPbI₃. In MAPbI₃, relaxation largely enhances the stability of the MAI-terminated 2D structure. We then check the structural changes after relaxation of the MAPbI₃ surfaces to find out the origin of the distinct relaxation effect on the two types of surfaces. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the MA molecules at the surface reorient with the NH₃ fragment pointing into the inner layers after relaxation for the MAI-terminated 2D MAPbI₃. In contrast, changes in the surface structure are negligible for the PbI₂-terminated surface (see Fig. 3(b)). These results are consistent with the fact that a much larger relaxation effect on surface energy is present for the MAI-terminated than the PbI₂-terminated surface. It is also reasonable due to the more number of degrees of freedoms in the MA subunit in comparison with the PbI₂. Relaxation in CsPbBr₃ slabs leads to the rumpling of CsBr and PbBr₂ atomic layers, *i.e.* relative displacement of cations and anions along the *c* direction. For example, with relaxation, Cs⁺ moves inward toward the center of the slab, while Br⁻ moves outward for the surface CsBr layer.

To find out the reason of reorientation of MA molecules, we measure the H⋯I bond lengths for H atoms bonded to the N atom. In γ-MAPbI₃, there exist three hydrogen bonds (N-H⋯I) per MA-ion: one with the length of 2.55 Å and other two with the length of 2.56 Å. One of the three hydrogen bonds is broken when the slab is cut from the bulk. Relaxation enhances the hydrogen bonding interaction at the surface for the MAI-terminated slab, while this effect is missing in the PbI₂-terminated slab. Fig. 3(c) shows the detailed structure changes with bond lengths marked together with atomic structures. It is seen that relaxation leads to the appearance of two more N-H⋯I bonds with the bond length of 2.69 and 3.00 Å. The length of the shorter hydrogen bond is in the range of strong hydrogen bonding, as demonstrated in previous studies.²⁰,²¹ Another relatively weak hydrogen bonding also contributes to the interaction of the NH₃ fragment with the iodine anion. Therefore, the strong hydrogen bonding interaction at the surface lowers the total energy of MAI-terminated slabs, which contributes to the lowered formation energy of MAI terminated slabs. The effect of hydrogen-bonding on stabilizing hybrid perovskites has also been found in the orthorhombic bulk.²⁰,²² Based on DFT calculations, *Lee et al.* found that hydrogen bonding stabilizes the tilt of octahedra in the orthorhombic phase.²² Moreover, a barrier for MA rotation along its C-N direction is calculated to be 102 meV per MA-ion, implying the strong hydrogen-bonding in the orthorhombic phase.²⁰

In contrast, the formation energies of $\text{PbI}_2$-terminated 2D $\text{MAPbI}_3$ increase significantly from its bulk value, and therefore, formation of $\text{PbI}_2$ terminated 2D slabs is not expected.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, we studied the stability of 2D lead halide perovskites by using the DFT method. Our results show that AX-terminated 2D $\text{APbX}_3$ are thermodynamically more stable than the $PbX_2$-terminated 2D structures. Surprisingly, although the formation energies of 2D $\text{CsPbBr}_3$ and $\text{PbI}_2$-terminated $\text{MAPbI}_3$ are higher than their 3D counterpart, as expected, the stability of MAI-terminated 2D $\text{MAPbI}_3$ becomes comparable to or even better than their 3D bulk. We find that the thermodynamic stability of the 2D AX-terminated perovskites originates from the asymmetric surface properties of these materials: fewer surface dangling bonds and more ionicity of the AX layer, as well as significant surface relaxation. These factors lead to low surface energy for the AX-terminated surface. Particularly, in $\text{MAPbI}_3$, we find that structural relaxation enhances the hydrogen bonding interaction at the surface and thus enhances the stability of MAI-terminated 2D $\text{MAPbI}_3$. The observation that one surface could be more stable than the complementary one is a universal phenomenon, which can be utilized to design more stable structures such as 2D MAI-terminated slabs. Our finding thus provides insights into the thermodynamic stability of 2D *versus* 3D materials. Experimental tests of our predictions are called for.

## Methods

All calculations were done within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP).${}^{23-25}$ The projector augmented-wave (PAW) method was used to describe the electron-core interaction.${}^{26}$ PBEsol was used as the exchange-correlation functional in structural relaxations and calculations of total energy.${}^{27}$ A kinetic energy cutoff of 350 eV and 500 eV were used to perform calculations for $\text{CsPbBr}_3$ and $\text{MAPbI}_3$, respectively. A Mon- khorst-Pack mesh of $8 \times 8 \times 8$ was used for cubic perovskite cells and scaled meshes were used for other bulks and slabs. Our test calculations show that spin-orbital coupling (SOC) does not change our main conclusion and thus SOC is not considered in the calculations. Formation energy, surface energy, and cleavage energy were calculated according to the following definitions.

The formation energies of 3D and 2D perovskites ($\text{CsPbBr}_3$ and $\text{MAPbI}_3$) were calculated with respect to decomposed component phases ($\text{CsPbBr}_3$: $\text{PbBr}_2$/CsBr; $\text{MAPbI}_3$: $\text{PbI}_2$/MAI). The initial structures of bulk $\gamma\text{-CsPbBr}_3$ and $\gamma\text{-MAPbI}_3$ were taken from experimental studies and were relaxed.${}^{28,29}$ All crystal structures of binaries studied here are taken at their most stable polymorph under ambient conditions; particularly, for the ground state structure of MAI, we used a tetragonal structure.${}^{30}$

The formation energies ($E_f$) of 3D and 2D perovskites are defined as follows:

3D bulk:
$$
\mathrm{E}_f(\mathrm{ABX}_3) = E_{\mathrm{ABX}_3} - E_{\mathrm{AX}} - \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{BX}_2} \tag{1}
$$

2D $n$ layer (Ln) AX-terminated $\text{ABX}_3$ under AX-rich conditions (in contact with a reservoir of bulk AX):
$$
E_f(\mathrm{A}_{n+1}\mathrm{B}_{n}\mathrm{X}_{3n+1}) = \left[E_{\mathrm{A}_{n+1}\mathrm{B}_{n}\mathrm{X}_{3n+1}} - (n+1)E_{\mathrm{AX}} - n\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{BX}_2}\right]/n \tag{2}
$$

2D $n$ layer (Ln) $\text{BX}_2$-terminated $\text{ABX}_3$ under $\text{BX}_2$-rich conditions (in contact with a reservoir of bulk $\text{BX}_2$):
$$
E_f(\mathrm{A}_{n}\mathrm{B}_{n+1}\mathrm{X}_{3n+2}) = \left[E_{\mathrm{A}_{n}\mathrm{B}_{n+1}\mathrm{X}_{3n+2}} - nE_{\mathrm{AX}} - (n+1)E_{\mathrm{BX}_2}\right]/n \tag{3}
$$
where $E$ is the total energy of 3D bulks, 2D slab ternary perovskites, and related bulk binaries. According to the definitions, negative formation energies indicate that the ternary system is thermodynamically stable.

Surface energy is calculated by simply considering the chemical potential of component binary phases of perovskites. This method has been used to calculate the surface energy of oxide perovskites.${}^{31}$ Using $\text{CsPbBr}_3$ as an example, the surface energy is calculated as follows:
$$
E_{\text{surf}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(E_{\text{slab}} - N_{\text{CsBr}}\mu_{\text{CsBr}} - N_{\text{PbBr}_2}\mu_{\text{PbBr}_2}\right) \tag{4}
$$
where $E_{\text{slab}}$ is the total energy of the slab, $N_{\text{CsBr}}$ and $N_{\text{PbBr}_2}$ are the numbers of formula unit of CsBr and $\text{PbBr}_2$ in the slab, and $\mu_{\text{CsBr}}$ and $\mu_{\text{PbBr}_2}$ are the corresponding chemical potentials. The factor of 1/2 accounts for two identical surfaces

existing in the slab. Since the surface is in equilibrium with the bulk, the chemical potential $\mu_i$ should satisfy the following conditions:

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{CsBr}}+\mu_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}=\mu_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}=E_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}} \tag{5}
$$

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{CsBr}} \leq E_{\mathrm{CsBr}} \tag{6}
$$

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}} \leq E_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}} \tag{7}
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{CsBr}}, E_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}$ are the total energies of bulk binary and ternary compounds. $E_{\mathrm{f}}=E_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}-E_{\mathrm{CsBr}}-E_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}$ is the formation energy of the ternary compound with respect to the binary compounds. We can see that only one chemical potential is an independent variable. The variation range of chemical potential is set by avoiding the precipitation of either bulk CsBr or bulk PbBr$_2$. For example, the condition $\mu_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}=E_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}$ corresponds to a system in equilibrium with PbBr$_2$ bulk (PbBr$_2$-rich condition), while $\mu_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}=E_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}+E_{\mathrm{f}}$ corresponds to the PbBr$_2$-poor condition or CsBr-rich condition.

For symmetric (001) slab of perovskites, using eqn (5), (4) can be rewritten as follows:

$$
E_{\text {surf }}^{\mathrm{CsBr}}=1 / 2\left(E_{\text {slab }}^{\mathrm{CsBr}}-N_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}} E_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}-\mu_{\mathrm{CsBr}}\right) \tag{8}
$$

for the CsBr-terminated slab, and

$$
E_{\text {surf }}^{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{\text {slab }}^{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}-N_{\mathrm{CsBr}} E_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}-\mu_{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}\right) \tag{9}
$$

for the PbBr$_2$-terminated slab. Based on these two equations, we calculate the surface energies of CsPbBr$_3$ and MAPbI$_3$ using triple-layer slabs. Our test calculations for CsPbBr$_3$ slabs show that the triple-layer slab is thick enough to obtain converged surface energy. Thus, triple-layer slabs with two types of terminations for both CsPbBr$_3$ and MAPbI$_3$ are used to calculate the surface energy. Note that the surface energies of AX-termi- nated surfaces are calculated at the chemical potential condi- tions of their most stable states, i.e. the AX-rich condition (in equilibrium with the AX bulk), while those of PbX$_2$-terminated surfaces are calculated at the corresponding PbX$_2$-rich chemical potential conditions.

We also calculate the cleavage energy $(E_{\mathrm{cl}})$ of two AX- and PbX$_2$-terminated surfaces. Using CsPbBr$_3$ as an example, we obtain

$$
E_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{\text {slab }}^{\mathrm{CsBr}}+E_{\text {slab }}^{\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}}-N_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}} E_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}\right) \tag{10}
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{CsPbBr}_{3}}$ is the total number of formula unit of bulk CsPbBr$_3$ in two slabs. Note that $E_{\mathrm{cl}}$ is independent of chemical potential. In this definition, $E_{\mathrm{cl}}$ is the energy cost in cleaving the bulk CsPbBr$_3$ along the (001) surface. The $E_{\mathrm{cl}}$ of CsPbBr$_3$ and MAPbI$_3$ is calculated in the triple-layer thick slab, with which converged cleavage energy can be obtained.
