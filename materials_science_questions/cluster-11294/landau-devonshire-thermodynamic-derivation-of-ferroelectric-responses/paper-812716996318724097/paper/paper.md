## ABSTRACT
In this work, based on thermodynamic theory, we theoretically derived the elastocaloric coefficient at the zero electric field, including the first kind of elastocaloric coefficient at constant polarization and the second kind of elastocaloric coefficient arising from the piezoelectric effect. In addition, using the Landau-Ginzburg-Devonshire approach, we predicted a giant elastocaloric effect in poly(vinylidene fluoride- trifluoroethylene) [P(VEF-TrFE)] copolymer films with a large elastocaloric strength, which is one or two orders of magnitude larger than typical elastocaloric materials reported in the literature. The adiabatic elastocaloric temperature change is found to be huge [~32.2 K for P(VEF-TrFE) 65/35 and 65 K for P(VEF-TrFE) 70/30] under a uniaxial compressive stress (-100 MPa) due to negative piezoelectricity. Moreover, even a low tensile stress (~15 MPa) can induce a modest elastocaloric effect (~-1.3 K), which is in good agreement with the pre- vious literature [Patel et al., Appl. Phys. Lett. 108, 072903 (2016)]. Our study not only stimulates further investigations on elastocaloric effects in ferroelectric polymers but also offers opportunities for elastocaloric solid-state refrigeration.

## I. INTRODUCTION
During the past decade, great efforts have been made to develop solid-state refrigeration, mainly in order to be used instead of the present compression cooling technologies that cannot be scaled down to nano/microscales because of performance degrada- tion upon downscaling from the macroscale. $^{1,2}$ As a result, rapid progress in research on caloric [magnetocaloric (MC), electro- caloric (EC), elastocaloric (eC), and barocaloric (BC)] effects has been made. $^{3-5}$ Large EC effects induced by electric field have been found to exist in ferroelectric materials. $^{6-8}$ Shape memory alloys have demonstrated eC and MC effects in response to external stress and magnetic field, respectively. $^{9-13}$ Some materials exhibit multi caloric properties, such as multiferroics having at least two ferroic orders, which can support more than one type of caloric effects. Multicaloric effects, i.e., the coupling of EC, MC, eC, and BC effects, are expected to enhance the cooling performance, which have been extensively investigated in their respective class of mate- rials. $^{1,9,10}$ For ferroelectric materials reported for giant EC, since the order parameter (polarization) and structural transitions are closely coupled, it is to be expected that they could possess a large eC effect. Wang et al. studied the EC effect induced by multidomain to mono- domain transition in ferroelectrics using a phase field model. $^{14}$  Wu et al. investigated the effect of out-of-plane strain on the EC performances of a poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene)[P(VDF-TrFE)] vertical heteroepitaxial film using the nonlinear ther- modynamic theory. $^{15}$ Pan et al. studied the microstructure of electro caloric materials by using piezoresponse force microscopy. $^{16,17}$  Indeed, the eC effect has been demonstrated in ferroelectric materials. $^{2,4,18-25}$ But most investigations are focused on ceramic perovskite oxide ferroelectrics such as $Ba_{x}Sr_{1-x}TiO_{3},^{18}PbTiO_{3},^{19}$  $BaTiO_{3},^{20}PZT,^{21}(Ba_{x}Ca_{1-x})(Zr_{y}Ti_{1-y})O_{3}(BCZTO),^{22-24}$ and xPb $(Mg_{1/3}Nb_{2/3})O_{3}-1-xPbTiO_{3}^{25}$ Apart from shape memory alloys

and perovskite oxides, ferroelectric polymers reported for giant $EC^{7}$ effects are also another important potential elastocaloric material due to other advantages such as ease of fabrication, low cost, and low processing temperatures. Compared with alloys or ceramics, polyvi- nylidene difluoride (PVDF)-based polymers are more environmen- tally friendly and show a high caloric performance but only require low stress values. Besides, ferroelectric polymers have a large mass specific heat but small volumetric specific heat, and thus ferroelectric polymer materials are suitable for large-scale cooling applications. Atthe early stage, large elastocaloric effects in polymers such as rubber $^{26}$  are not surprising due to the high strain-induced entropy changes associated with the polymer chains. Crystalline solids do not exhibit this contribution, which was first investigated by Kuhn. $^{27}$ However, recently, Patel et al. $^{28}$ demonstrated large eC effects (adiabatic temperature change $\Delta T=-1.8 ~K$ ) induced by small tensile stress (15 MPa) in polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF)-based polymers at 298 K, whichis attributed to nonpolar crystalline $\alpha$ or $\gamma$ phase to polar crystalline $\beta$  phase transformation. Yoshida et al. $^{29}$ reported the eC propertiesof poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene-chlorotrifluoroethylene)[P(VDF-TrFE-CTFE)] terpolymer with a reversible adiabatic temper- ature variation of 2.15 K at a strain of $12 \%$ . They thought that the pure entropic elasticity related to the amorphous phase is responsible for the eC effect. However, until now, the theory behind the origin of large eC effects in ferroelectric polymers has been not reported. In the present work, combining the thermodynamic and Landau- Ginzburg-Devonshire theories, we study the effect of different applied uniaxial stresses on phase transition, polarization, and eC effect of P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 and 70/30 that have negative piezoelectricity as a typical representative of ferroelectric PVDF-based polymers. Indeed, besides the tensile stress-induced large eC effect, $^{28}$ we find that low compressive stress can give rise to a giant eC effect in PVDF-based polymer because of its negative piezoelectricity. This finding offers prospects for eC solid-state refrigeration in ferroelectric polymers.

## II. THERMODYNAMIC THEORY OF eC EFFECTS
### A. General thermodynamic of eC for ferroelectric materials
In this work, we assume that the thermodynamic theory of deformable ferroelectric materials under external stress $\sigma_{j}$ is within the continuum approximation. The thermodynamic fundamentalequation is as follows:
$$d U=T d S+\sigma_{j} d \varepsilon_{j}+E_{\alpha} d P_{\alpha},\qquad(1)$$
where U is the internal energy, S is the entropy, T is the tempera- ture, $P_{\alpha}$ is the polarization, $E_{\alpha}$ is the external electric field, and $\varepsilon_{j}$ is the deformable (liner) strain.
Using the elastic Gibbs function $G=U-T S-\sigma_{j} \varepsilon_{j}$ , we have
$$d G=-S d T-\varepsilon_{j} d \sigma_{j}+E_{\alpha} d P_{\alpha}.\qquad(2)$$

Therefore, according to the Maxwell relation, we have
$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial \sigma_{j}}\right)_{P, T}=\left(\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{j}}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma, P}.\qquad(3)$$

For ferroelectrics, external stress not only gives rise to the change of volume leading to the entropy change but also induces the change of polarization due to the piezoelectric effect, which also leads to the entropy contribution. Therefore, the isothermal eC coefficient can be obtained at E= 0 by
$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial \sigma_{j}}\right)_{T E}=\left(\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{j}}{\partial T}\right)_{\sigma P}+\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P_{\alpha}}\right)_{T \sigma}\left(\frac{\partial P_{\alpha}}{\partial \sigma_{j}}\right)_{T E},\qquad(4)$$
where
$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P_{\alpha}}\right)_{T \sigma}\left(\frac{\partial P_{\alpha}}{\partial \sigma_{j}}\right)_{E T}=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E_{\gamma}}\right)_{\sigma T}\left(\frac{\partial E_{\gamma}}{\partial P_{\alpha}}\right)_{\sigma T}\left(\frac{\partial P_{\alpha}}{\partial \sigma_{j}}\right)_{T E}.\qquad(5)$$

So the isothermal eC coefficient under E=0 can be expressed by
$$\alpha_{j}^{E}=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial \sigma_{j}}\right)_{T E}=\alpha_{j}^{P}+p_{\gamma}^{T} \beta_{\gamma \alpha}^{T} d_{\alpha j}^{E}.\qquad(6)$$

Here, the first term of the right-hand side of (6) is the first kind of eC coefficient (at constant polarization). The second term is the second kind of eC coefficient arising from the piezoelectric effect. $\alpha_{j}^{P}$ is the thermal expansion coefficient (or the first kind of eC coefficient) at constant polarization, $\alpha_{j}^{E}$ is the eC coefficient at the zero electric field, $p_{\gamma}^{T}$ is the pyroelectric coefficient (or electro caloric coefficient), $\beta_{\gamma \alpha}^{T}$ is the dielectric stiffness, and $d_{\alpha j}^{E}$ is the pie zoelectric constant at zero electric field. According to Eq. (6), because of the negative pyroelectric coefficient and positive dielec- tric stiffness, the negative piezoelectric coefficient leads to the posi- tive elastocaloric coefficient, which has the same sign with the first elastocaloric coefficient. Therefore, the negative piezoelectric coeffi- cient will enhance the total elastocaloric coefficient.
As a result, the isothermal eC entropy change and adiabatic eC temperature change at the zero electric field can be written as
$$\Delta S=\int_{\sigma_{i}}^{\sigma_{f}} \alpha_{j}^{E} d \sigma,\qquad(7)$$

$$\Delta T=-\frac{T}{C \rho} \int_{\sigma_{i}}^{\sigma_{f}} \alpha_{j}^{E} d \sigma.\qquad(8)$$

Here, T is the work temperature, C is the thermal capacity, $\rho$  is the density, $\sigma_{i}$ is the initial applied stress, and $\sigma_{f}$ is the final applied stress.
### B. Landau-Ginzburg-Devonshire theory of eC effect for P(VDF-TrFE) ferroelectrics
For PVDF-based polymeric film samples that are freestandingin $x_{1} x_{2}$ plane $(\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=0)$ but subjected to a uniaxial stress $\sigma_{3}$  along $x_{3}$ direction, the standard elastic Gibbs energy function G is solid in the present study, where the minima of G corresponds to the stable thermodynamic states at fixed mechanical stresses.

The elastic Gibbs free energy of PVDF-based polymers can be written as a function of polarization and stress, $^{30}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
G= & \frac{a}{2} P^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4} P^{4}+\frac{\gamma}{6} P^{6}-\frac{1}{2} s_{11}\left(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{3}^{2}\right) \\
& -s_{12}\left(\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}+\sigma_{2} \sigma_{3}+\sigma_{3} \sigma_{1}\right)-\frac{1}{2} s_{44} \sigma_{6}^{2} \\
& -Q_{11} \sigma_{3} P^{2}-Q_{12}\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\right) P^{2},
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

where $P$ is the dielectric polarization. $a$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ are the Landau coefficients, $a$ obeys $a = a_0(T-T_0)$ based on the Curie-Weiss law, where $T_0$ is the Curie-Weiss temperature. $s_{11}$, $s_{12}$, $Q_{11}$, and $Q_{12}$ are the elastic compliances and electrostrictive constants, respectively. The values of coefficients for P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 and 70/30 in the present calculation are listed in Table I. $^{31-36}$

The Curie temperature $T_\text{c}(\sigma_3)$, as a function of an applied uniaxial stress $\sigma_3$ ($\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\sigma_4=\sigma_5=\sigma_6=0$, $\sigma_3\neq0$) or hydrostatic pressure $\sigma_h$ ($\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\sigma_3=\sigma_h\neq0$, $\sigma_4=\sigma_5=\sigma_6=0$), refers to the paraelectric-ferroelectric transition. It varies with applied external mechanical stresses,

$$
T_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\sigma_{3}\right)=T_{0}+\frac{3 \beta^{2}}{16 a_{0} \gamma}+\frac{2 Q_{11} \sigma_{3}}{a_{0}},
\tag{10a}
$$

$$
T_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\sigma_{h}\right)=T_{0}+\frac{3 \beta^{2}}{16 a_{0} \gamma}+\frac{2\left(Q_{11}+2 Q_{12}\right) \sigma_{h}}{a_{0}}.
\tag{10b}
$$

Using the relation $-S=\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}$, we have

$$
-S=\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}=\frac{1}{2} a_{0} P^{2}-s_{11} \sigma_{3} \frac{\partial \sigma_{3}}{\partial T}-Q_{11} P^{2} \frac{\partial \sigma_{3}}{\partial T}.
\tag{11}
$$

Assuming that the polymeric film has a volume $\Omega$, an area $A$ in the $x_1x_2$ plane, and a thickness $h$ in the $x_3$ direction and the fixed external force $F$ is along the $x_3$ direction, $\partial\sigma_3/\partial T$ can be expressed as

$$
\frac{\partial \sigma_{3}}{\partial T}=\frac{\partial\left(\frac{F}{A}\right)}{\partial T}=-\frac{F}{A^{2}} \frac{\partial A}{\partial T}.
\tag{12}
$$

Therefore, using $\frac{\partial A}{A\partial T}\approx2\alpha_l$, $\alpha_l$ is the thermal expansion coefficient; the isothermal eC entropy change and adiabatic eC temperature change can be written at the zero initial stress as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta S= & -\frac{1}{2} a_{0}\left[P^{2}\left(\sigma_{3}, T\right)-P^{2}(0, T)\right]-2 \alpha_{l} s_{11} \sigma_{3}^{2} \\
& -2 \alpha_{l} \sigma_{3} Q_{11} P^{2}\left(\sigma_{3}, T\right),
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
$$

$$
\Delta T=-\frac{T \Delta S}{C \rho}.
\tag{14}
$$

Equation (13) clearly indicates the contribution to entropy from polarization change, elastic, and piezoelectric effects. The first term on the right-hand side of (13) is the contribution of the stress field-induced polarization change. The second term is the contribution of the elastic effect. The third term shows the contribution of the piezoelectric effect.

### III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

In order to better investigate the eC effects of PVDF-based polymers, here, we choose P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 and 70/30 two compositions as examples; firstly, the thermodynamic equilibrium polarization states should be taken into account, which are obtained by minimizing the elastic Gibbs function $G$. To describe the eC effects near the ferroelectric phase transition, it is useful to develop the external stress-temperature phase diagram, where the Curie temperature $T_\text{c}$ is a function of external stress $\sigma_3$. In the present calculation, the Curie temperature $T_\text{c}$ can be obtained by Eq. (10). Using Eq. (10), we calculated the phase diagram for the P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 and 70/30 freestanding films under different external stresses $\sigma_3$ along the $x_3$ direction. Ferroelectric transitions reflect changing atomic positions, so mechanical stress affects the corresponding phase transitions. Figure 1 shows the external stress-temperature phase diagram of the P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 and 70/30 freestanding films. As can be seen, the Curie temperature $T_\text{c}$ has a linear variation with external mechanical stresses. $T_\text{c}(\sigma_3)$ rises with increasing magnitude of compressive stress ($\sigma_3<0$) and decreases with increasing tensile stress ($\sigma_3>0$) due to $Q_{11}<0$ (or negative piezoelectric constant $d_{33}=2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{33}Q_{11}P<0$). As a result, $T_c(\sigma_3)$ rises for compressive stress and decreases for tensile stress. A stress-inducted giant eC temperature change arises from stress enhanced entropy contribution [see Figs. 5(a) and 5(c)]. As a result, uniaxial compressive stress leads to a giant eC effect.

This is because uniaxial tensile stress decreases electric polarization due to anomalous negative piezoelectricity. It is worthwhile to note that our theoretical prediction value at low temperatures is consistent with the experimental value in Ref. 28. For example, at applied tensile stress $\sigma_{3}=15 \mathrm{MPa}$, the eC isothermal temperature change of P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 is $\sim-1.3 \mathrm{~K}$ at $25^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, while the experimental data for PVDF at the same condition is $-1.8 \mathrm{~K}$. However, at high temperature, our calculation for P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 is significantly different from the experimental data for PVDF in Ref. 28. This difference may be due to the difference in chemistry of materials as well as the structure phase transition. In this work, our considered phase transition is ferroelectric-paraelectric transition, while in Ref. 28, the transition is from the nonpolar crystalline $\alpha$ or $\gamma$ phase to polar crystalline $\beta$ phase. So we suggest that the stress-induced ferroelectric-paraelectric transition is expected to be studied experimentally for eC in ferroelectric polymers.

## IV. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, we have theoretically predicted the eC effects in P(VDF-TrFE) 65/35 and 70/30 polymers based on thermodynamic theory and the Landau-Ginzburg-Devonshire approach. A remarkably giant eC response $[\Delta T_{\sigma 3} \sim 32.2 \mathrm{~K}$ for P(VEF-TrFE) 65/35 and 65 K for P(VDF-TrFE) 70/30] was predicted under a uniaxial compressive stress of 100 MPa. Alternately, the eC strength was also found to be 1-2 orders of magnitude larger than other eC materials including shape memory alloys and ferroelectric materials. Most importantly, the elastocaloric coefficient at the zero electric field is derived theoretically for the first time, including the first kind of elastocaloric coefficient at constant polarization and second kind of elastocaloric coefficient arising from the piezoelectric effect. Our findings will provide guidance for the investigation of the eC effect in ferroelectric materials, particularly ferroelectric polymers.
