# ON THE EFFECTS OF STITCHING IN CFRPs--I. MODE I DELAMINATION TOUGHNESS

Kimberley A. Dransfield, $^{a}$ Lalit K. Jain $^{b}$ \& Yiu-Wing Mai $^{a *}$

$^{a}$ Centre for Advanced Materials Technology, Department of Mechanical and Mechatronic Engineering, University of Sydney, Sydney, NSW 2006, Australia
$^{b}$ Cooperative Research Centre for Advanced Composite Structures Ltd, 361 Milperra Road, Bankstown, NSW 2200, Australia

(Received 9 May 1997; revised 31 October 1997; accepted 20 November 1997)

## Abstract
Stitching advanced carbon-fibre composites in the through-thickness direction enhances the interlaminar fracture toughness. The mode I delamination toughness of a carbon/epoxy composite, both unstitched and stitched, was experimentally determined by using double-cantilever beam specimens. Specimens with varying stitch density, thread diameter and thread type were studied. The steady-state toughnesses, $G_{I R s}$ , of unstitched and stitched specimens have been compared. The addition of stitching was found to create an improvement in toughness of up to 15 times. The energy-dissipating crack propagation processes were determined and critical parameters identified. In addition, experimental results were compared with theoretical predictions from a micromechanics-based model developed previously and good agreement was found.

## 1 INTRODUCTION
Traditional laminated structures generally exhibit poor interlaminar fracture toughness and thus are susceptible to delamination when subjected to interlaminar stresses. Interlaminar stresses typically arise from manufacturing defects, low-velocity impact damage or geometric discontinuities such as free edges, notches, ply termination, bolted or bonded joints. $^{1}$ In some circumstances, this may lead simply to a substantial decrease in the structural integrity whilst in others it may be responsible for the catastrophic failure of the component. Until recently most research in developing damage-tolerant composites has focussed on materials improvements, i.e. on the use of tougher matrices, better fibre/matrix interfaces or interleaving concepts. $^{2-4}$ An alternative approach to bringing about substantial improvements in the damage tolerance of composites structures requires a modification of the fibre architecture. By the use of existing textile technologies such as stitching, knitting, weaving or braiding, through-thickness reinforcement can be introduced into the laminate. $^{5-17}$

Textile fibre geometries which have through-thickness reinforcement are called three-dimensional (3D) fabrics. The fabrication of a fabric by knitting, weaving or braiding can be done in a one-step process with the z-direction fibres being introduced simultaneously. Damage to the in-plane fibres can therefore be avoided. Stitching, on the other hand, requires at least one additional step with the through-thickness fibres being introduced after the lay-up of the laminate and the sewing process is responsible for an amount of in-plane fibre damage. However, whilst a considerable improvement in the mode I fracture toughness is possible with weaving, knitting and braiding, these methods create large resin pockets throughout the structure. This reduces the volume fraction of the in-plane fibres compared with an analogous two-dimensional (2D) laminate resulting in a large decrease in the in-plane properties. Perhaps the biggest advantage of stitching compared to the other methods of through-thickness reinforcement is its versatility. Stitching utilises traditional materials and fabrication processes and components can be manufactured from either a prepreg or preform layup. Stitching need not be limited only to reinforcing laminates but may also be used to improve the strength of lap joints. $^{18}$

As a consequence of the geometry of a 3D laminate, the biggest improvement in properties will be observed in mode I. In this paper the effects of stitching density, thread type and thread diameter on the mode I interlaminar fracture toughness have been evaluated and compared to those of a 2D laminate having similar material system and in-plane stacking sequence. In addition, experimental data is compared with theoretical predictions.

## 2 MATERIAL SYSTEMS

### 2.1 Fabric and resin
Carbon/epoxy panels with mid-plane inserts were fabricated from Ciba-Geigy 'UNIWEAVE' Injectex fabric and GY260 resin. A 'UNIWEAVE' fabric consists of a large percentage of 3000-filament tow carbon warp yarns (in this case 90% by weight) held together by 1000-filament tow carbon fill yarns in the weft direction.

Following the stacking, some of the dry fabric lay-ups were stitched through-the-thickness at 90° to the warp direction. Modified lock stitch was employed (Fig. 1) so as to avoid the disruption to the laminate arising from any interior looping processes between the bobbin and needle threads. The through-thickness reinforcement penetrates the in-plane yarns hence some fibre breakage and misalignment occurs at each stitching needle penetration point (Fig. 2). The presence of fibre damage and misalignment results in the formation of resin-rich pockets around the thread.

The ply orientation of both the unstitched and stitched materials were essentially unidirectional. In other words, all the plies had the same lay-up orientation with warp direction along the direction of crack propagation. All the dry fibre preforms were infiltrated with the Ciba- Geigy GY260 epoxy resin in conjunction with HY917 hardener and DY070 accelerator with 100, 90 and 1 weight proportions, respectively, by a resin transfer moulding (RTM) technique. The laminates were cured for 4 h at 80°C and then postcured for 4 h at 160°C.

All test coupons were approximately 150 mm long by 25 mm wide and 14 plies thick. All specimens were taken from single panel of dimensions 30 mm×35 mm for each stitch density and type of stitch thread. These specimens were milled after the cutting procedure to ensure a consistent cross-section and to eliminate any damage. The average specimen thickness for both the stitched and unstitched materials was 2.97 mm. The mid-plane delamination was introduced during the layup by means of a 12 μm thick Teflon strip inserted between the 7th and 8th plies.

## 3 EXPERIMENTAL TECHNIQUE

The standard double cantilever beam (DCB) test is an ideal test to determine the mode I interlaminar fracture toughness, $G_{\text{Ic}}$. This test utilises specimens of 3 mm thickness, 25 mm width and 150 mm in length. The specimens contained a mid-plane insert of $12\,\mu\text{m}$ thick teflon to act as a delamination in one end. The specimens were manufactured so that the crack length from the loading point to the crack tip was 25 mm.

The load was introduced through the use of aluminium T-tabs fixed to the specimen end containing the delamination (Figs 3 and 4). Following grinding with 200 grade emery paper and subsequent cleaning with acetone of both the specimen and tabs, the T-tabs were bonded to the specimen with a 24 h rapid cure epoxy. A simple jig (Fig. 3) was employed to ensure alignment. This methodology was found to be adequate with the unstitched specimen. However, when the stitched specimens were tested, failure of a specimen cantilever in bending rather than self-similar crack propagation between the plies occurred. A new DCB specimen configuration based on the design developed by Guenon et al.$^{6}$ was consequently used.

This specimen, termed the tabbed DCB (TDCB), utilises long aluminium tabs bonded along the specimen length to prevent failure of the composite cantilevers (Figs 3 and 4). The thickness of the aluminium required to reinforce the composite without yielding was calculated by estimating the value of $G_{\text{Ic}}$ using results from previous studies.$^{6,7}$ This, coupled with a knowledge of

the yield strength, $\sigma_{y}$, of the aluminium, the thickness, $h_{\mathrm{c}}$ and modulus, $E_{\mathrm{c}}$, of the composite and the Irwin-Kies equation for mode I provided an approximation of $6 \mathrm{~mm}$ for the minimum thickness of aluminium, $h_{\mathrm{a}}$, required. In the present study, readily available $10 \mathrm{~mm}$ thick aluminium tabs were used. In this case, again, to ensure good adhesion between the long aluminium tabs and the specimen, both surfaces were lightly ground with 200 grade emery paper and subsequently cleaned with acetone. The tabs were also grooved in the $\pm 45^{\circ}$ directions (Fig. 3). Initially Hysol 9303 and later 3M DP490 adhesive were used to bond the reinforcing aluminium tabs to the specimen. To aid load introduction T-tabs were fixed to the long aluminium tabs using both adhesive and bolts. T-tabs were preferred to drilling a hole through the long tab to facilitate alignment of both the tabs and the specimen in the fixture. To ensure consistency with the results, the unstitched specimens were tested both with and without the long aluminium tabs.

Following the specimen manufacture, a razor blade was used to wedge open the initial delamination to form a sharp mode I precrack and to ensure no bonding through the teflon film. This procedure produced an initial crack length of approximately $25 \mathrm{~mm}$. The sides of the specimen were coated with a thin layer of typists' white correction fluid to aid measurement of the initial crack length and to assist visual observation of the crack propagation during the test.

![](./images/811037273662423040_5.jpg)

Fig. 4. Schematic representation of (a) untabbed specimen and (b) tabbed specimen for mode I indicating specimen and tab dimensions.

Subsequently, the DCB specimens, both untabbed and tabbed, were pin loaded in tension using Instron grips in a servohydraulic Instron 4302 materials testing machine. A $10 \mathrm{KN}$ load cell was employed to apply the load to the specimen through the fixture and T-tab. Displacement control mode was used with a cross-head speed of $1 \mathrm{~mm} \mathrm{~min}^{-1}$. A data logger was used to continuously record the load, displacement and time. A travelling microscope with a light source enabled visual examination of the specimen throughout the test and measurement of the crack length. The specimen was loaded until the crack propagated several millimetres. It was then unloaded and the crack tip marked. This process was repeated 10-15 times for each specimen. For each variable four to six specimens were tested. The crack lengths, defined as the distance from the loading point to the crack tip, were measured using the travelling microscope. The accuracy of these measurements was inspected using digital callipers.

## 4 ANALYSIS

The interlaminar fracture toughness was determined in terms of the mode I critical strain energy release rate,

$G_{\text{IR}}$ (which may be regarded as $G_{\text{IC}}$ in the case of unstitched laminates). There are three commonly used data reduction techniques; the compliance method, the direct method and the area method. The compliance methods use the load-displacement plot to determine any non-ideal factors, the direct method requires knowl- edge of load-displacement plot as well as material proper- ties for such determinations, and the area method employs changes in compliance to account for non-ideality. Sig- nificant problems with applying the compliance and area methodologies to stitched composites have been found. $^{17}$ Thus the data was analysed using the direct method.

If it is assumed that each cracked half behaves as a cantilever beam of length a, then the deflection, $\delta$, between the two cracked beams may be estimated by summing a bending deflection term with a shear deflec- tion term as follows:

$$
\delta=\frac{2 P\left(a+\chi h_{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{3}}{3 E_{\mathrm{c}} I_{\mathrm{c}}}+\frac{12 P\left(a+\chi h_{\mathrm{c}}\right)}{5 G_{12 \mathrm{c}} h_{\mathrm{c}} w} \quad(1)
$$

where $P$ is the applied load, $a$ is the crack length, $E_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the flexural modulus, $E_{\mathrm{c}} I_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the flexural rigidity, $w$ is the beam width, $h_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the beam thickness and $G_{12 \mathrm{c}}$ is the transverse shear modulus. The factor $\chi$ is a correction factor which takes into account the additional deflection due to crack tip rotation of the beams. The compliance which is the ratio of deflection over load is then given by

$$
C=\frac{2\left(a+\chi h_{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{3}}{3 E_{\mathrm{c}} I_{\mathrm{c}}}+\frac{12\left(a+\chi h_{\mathrm{c}}\right)}{5 G_{12 \mathrm{c}} h_{\mathrm{c}} w} \quad(2)
$$

The mode I critical energy release rate, $G_{\text{IR}}$, is defined by the Griffith's energetic fracture criterion (or Irwin- Kies equation) as follows:

$$
G_{\mathrm{IR}}=\frac{P^{2}}{2 w} \frac{\mathrm{d} C}{\mathrm{~d} a} \quad(3)
$$

Upon substitution for the compliance from eqn (2) into eqn (3), $G_{\text{IR}}$ can be simplified as follows:

$$
G_{\mathrm{IR}}=\frac{P^{2}}{w}\left[\frac{\left(a+\chi h_{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{c}} I_{\mathrm{c}}}+\frac{6}{5 G_{12 \mathrm{c}} h_{\mathrm{c}} w}\right] \quad(4)
$$

From this expression it is possible to determine a set of mode I energy release rate values, $G_{\text{IR}}$, as a function of crack growth, $\Delta a$, if the load $P$ required to cause crack propagation is known. When $G_{\text{IR}}$ is plotted against the crack length, $a$, a curve is produced. This plot is termed an $R$-curve. From this curve an asymptotic value, the steady-state $G_{\text{IRs}}$, can be determined. The correction factor, $\chi$, which accounts for the crack tip root rotation was approximated to be $0 \cdot 6 .^{25}$

To account for the long aluminium tabs required by the stitched DCB specimen, in the above expressions, the beam thickness $h_{\mathrm{c}}$ is replaced by $h_{\mathrm{c}}+h_{\mathrm{a}}$ where $h_{\mathrm{a}}$ is the thickness of the aluminium tab. The flexural rigidity $E_{\mathrm{c}} I_{\mathrm{c}}$ is replaced by $E I$ and the shear modulus $G_{12 \mathrm{c}}$ is replaced by $G_{12}$ for the composite-aluminium beam. To determine the flexural rigidity of the aluminium-com- posite beam, consider a cross-section (Fig. 5) where $w$ is the width of the composite and $w(E_{\mathrm{a}}/E_{\mathrm{c}})$ is the equiva- lent width of the aluminium. The expression for $EI$ may be written as

$$
E I=E_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\sum \frac{w h^{3}}{12}+\sum A(y-\bar{y})^{2}\right) \quad(5)
$$

where

$$
\bar{y}=\frac{\sum A y}{\sum A} \quad(6)
$$

is the distance from the designated origin to the neutral axis, $y$ is the distance of the mid-point of each compo- nent from the same origin and $A$ is the cross-sectional area of each component. Thus the expression for flex- ural rigidity may be simplified as

$$
\begin{aligned}
& E I=E_{\mathrm{c}}\left[\frac{w h_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}{12}+w h_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\bar{y}-\frac{h_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\right)^{2}+w\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{E_{\mathrm{c}}}\right) \frac{h_{\mathrm{a}}^{3}}{12}+w\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{E_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)\right. \\
& \left.\times h_{\mathrm{a}}\left(\frac{h_{\mathrm{a}}}{2}+h_{\mathrm{c}}-\bar{y}\right)^{2}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

and for $\bar{y}$ as

$$
\bar{y}=\left[\frac{w h_{\mathrm{c}} \frac{h_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}+w\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{E_{\mathrm{c}}}\right) h_{\mathrm{a}}\left(h_{\mathrm{c}}+\frac{h_{\mathrm{a}}}{2}\right)}{w h_{\mathrm{c}}+w\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{E_{\mathrm{c}}}\right) h_{\mathrm{a}}}\right]
$$

![](./images/811037273662423040_6.jpg)

Fig. 5. Schematic representation of the cross-section of a CFRP laminate reinforced with an aluminium tab utilised in deriving eqn (7).

The interlaminar shear modulus of the system, $G_{12}$, may be given approximately by the inverse rule of mixtures $^{26}$ as

$$
G_{12}=\frac{\left(h_{\mathrm{c}}+h_{\mathrm{a}}\right)}{\left(\frac{h_{\mathrm{c}}}{G_{12 \mathrm{c}}}+\frac{h_{\mathrm{a}}}{G_{a}}\right)} \tag{1}
$$

where $G_{a}$ is the shear modulus of the aluminium. In determining flexural rigidity, $E I$, and the shear modulus, $G_{12}$, $E_{a}$ of the 6061 aluminium was taken as 68.9 GPa and $G_{a}$ as $26.9 \mathrm{GPa}^{27}$

## 5 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### 5.1 Load-displacement curves
Observation of the load-displacement curves obtained from stitched specimens (Fig. 6(b-d)) shows that initially there is an increase in the crack propagation load as the crack grows. This is caused by the development of the stitch bridging zone. After the bridging zone is fully developed, the steady-state interlaminar fracture is attained and with further crack propagation the stitches start breaking and the crack propagation load decreases with increasing crack length. The crack propagation part of the curve generally exhibited stable crack propagation behaviour consisting of successive load drops followed by crack arrest. This is termed a 'stick-slip' behaviour. In the case of the unstitched specimen a rising $R$-curve (increase in fracture toughness with crack length) was also observed. However, there was a continual decrease in the crack propagation load with increasing crack length (see Fig. 6(a)) for both the untabbed and tabbed samples.

The load-displacement curves of the stitched specimens also show an appreciable amount of permanent deformation. Observation of the crack tip with the travelling microscope revealed that the cracks did not close completely when the load was removed. To determine the reasons for this feature, the aluminium tabs were examined. However, there was no evidence of plastic deformation in the tabs or the adhesive. A comparison of the applied stresses to the yield stress of the aluminium also showed that the yield stress was never exceeded. Thus the fracture surfaces of the tested specimen were examined. It was established that most of the stitches did not break in the plane of the crack (Fig. 7(a))

### 5.2 Experimental results
The $R$-curves for the different material variables were determined. This required the flexural modulus and shear modulus to be measured for each specimen. The flexural moduli were estimated from the load-displace- ment plots of specimens without delamination loaded in 3-point bend test. The results are given in Table 4. The out-of-plane shear moduli were estimated to be 5.8 GPa. The $R$-curves are shown in Figs 8-10. The steady-state mode I delamination toughness values, $G_{IRs}$, are pre sented in Table 5.

It is immediately obvious that the addition of through-thickness reinforcement by stitching produced a significant improvement in the mode I delamination fracture toughness. (Figs 8(b), 9 and 10). To determine the major energy absorbing mechanisms responsible for the improvement, delamination surfaces of the stitched specimens were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Evidence of thread breakage and subsequent pull-out was found (Fig. 7). Thus it was postulated that several processes were involved in increasing the toughness. Debonding of the thread/ matrix interface allowed elastic stretching of the thread. This created fibre bridging of the crack by the stitch lines providing crack closure forces until thread failure and pull-out occurred.

The unstitched specimens also exhibited $R$-curve behaviour (Fig. 8(a)). This incremental increase in the fracture toughness was due to some fibre bridging effect caused by the in-plane carbon fibres. Unlike the crack closure forces provided by the stitches, the in-plane fibre bridging lead to minor improvements in toughness only. A study of the fracture surface using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) found some evidence of fibre brid- ging (Fig. 11).

## 6 MICROMECHANICAL MODEL

Two simple micromechanics-based models using a stress intensity factor approach have been developed by Jain and $Mai^{28}$ to predict the effect of stitching on mode I delamination toughness. In the first model each stitch was considered as an independent (discontinuous) through-thickness reinforcement. In this case, the expression for crack closure traction contained terms for frictional slip and thread pull-out. In the second model, the effect of interconnected stitching was incorporated. This model utilised terms for frictional slip, elastic stretching, and thread rupture in the crack plane. How- ever, as described previously the thread fracture beha- viour of the stitched double cantilever beam specimens was found experimentally to consist of (i) thread debonding (ii) elastic stretching of the thread (iii) thread rupture, and (iv) thread pull-out. Thus to accurately predict the behaviour of stitched specimens under mode I loading it was necessary to combine the mod- els. In this modified model, the thread fails at the embedded end after elastic stretching and subsequently pulls out.

In these models, several assumptions were made to simplify the analysis. The stitch thread was assumed to have cylindrical geometry. The frictional shear stress at the thread/matrix interface was assumed to have a con- stant value and the effect of fibre abrasion or matrix crumbling during thread stretching and pull-out was ignored. The deformation in the matrix was also neglected. The tensile strength of the thread was assumed, reasonably, to be single valued. Additionally it was assumed that the support ahead of the crack front

To simulate the failure mechanisms observed in the case of tabbed DCB specimens, it was assumed that the effective thread length available for elastic stretching was equal to the laminate thickness $(2h_{c})$ and that available for pull-out was half the laminate thickness, $h_{c}$, as the stitch breaks at one end and then pulls out.

Using Euler-Bernoulli linear elastic beam theory, the governing differential equation for the crack opening displacement, $\delta$, (which is twice the beam deflection) may be expressed as follows

$$
E_{\mathrm{c}} I_{\mathrm{c}} \frac{\mathrm{d}^{4} \delta}{\mathrm{d} t^{4}}=\left\{\begin{array}{cl}
-2 P(t) & 0<\mathrm{t} \leq \Delta a \\
0 & t>\Delta a
\end{array}\right.
\tag{10}
$$

where $P(t)$ is the equivalent closure traction resulting from the force in the bridging stitches. This closure traction can be obtained from the following expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(t)= & 2 S_{\mathrm{D}} \tau \pi \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{f}}\left[\left(Y(t)+\frac{F_{\mathrm{t}}}{\tau \pi \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{f}}}\right) U_{1} U_{2}+\left(h_{\mathrm{c}}-S(t)\right)\right. \\
& \left(1-U_{2}\right) U_{3} U_{4}\left(1-U_{5}\right)+\left(h_{\mathrm{c}}+\frac{F_{0}(t)+F_{t}}{\tau \pi \mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{f}}}\right) \\
& \left.\left(1-U_{2}\right) U_{4} U_{5}\right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
$$

The parameter $S_{\mathrm{D}}$ represents the number of stitches per unit area, $\tau$ is the pull-out shear stress of the thread/matrix interface, $d_{f}$ is the thread diameter, $t$ is the distance from the crack tip, $Y(t)$ is the slip length, $F_{t}$ is the pre-tension load in the thread, $F_{0}$ is the load at the embedded end, $S(t)$ is the slippage of the embedded thread end and $U_{1}$ to $U_{5}$ are the step functions following the Heaviside definition and are given as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& U_{1}=U_{1}\left(L_{\mathrm{c}}-Y(t)\right) \\
& U_{2}=U_{2}\left(h_{\mathrm{c}}-Y(t)\right) \\
& U_{3}=U_{3}\left(h_{\mathrm{c}}-\delta(t)\right) \\
& U_{4}=U_{4}\left(L_{\mathrm{c}}-h_{\mathrm{c}}\right) \\
& U_{5}=U_{5}\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{fu}} A_{\mathrm{f}}-\left[F_{0}(t)+F_{t}\right]\right)
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
$$

where $L_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the critical embedded length $(=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{f}} \sigma_{\mathrm{fu}} / 4 \tau), \sigma_{\mathrm{fu}}$ is the thread failure strength and $A_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the cross-sectional area of the thread. Further details on the model and on the expressions for $Y(t)$ and $S(t)$ may be found in Ref. 28.

The step functions $U_{1}$ and $U_{4}$ consider the case where the embedded length of the thread exceeds the critical embedded length and the thread just stretches followed by rupture without pull-out. The purpose of step function $U_{2}$ is to differentiate between activation of frictional slip and elastic stretching or thread pull-out as the governing mechanism. The step function $U_{3}$ accounts for threads which have completed pull-out and thus do not contribute further to crack bridging. Finally the step function $U_{5}$ governs the condition of thread breakage at the embedded end prior to thread pull out.

The closure traction provided by the bridging stitches may then be utilised to determine the crack growth resistance, $K_{\mathrm{IR}}$, as a function of crack growth, $\Delta a$, as follows:

$$

\tag{13}
$$

where $K_{\mathrm{Ic}}$ is the intrinsic fracture toughness of the stitched composite which is taken as the steady-state value for the unstitched composite. Inclusion of the matrix $R$-curve (i.e. matrix process zone) in addition to the fibre bridging zone for fibre cements has been analysed by

## 7 CONCLUSIONS
It has been determined that the addition of through- thickness reinforcement in the form of stitches produces significant improvements in the mode I delamination toughness. These improvements are found to be depen- dent on the thread type, thread diameter and the stitch density. To understand the cause of the improvement in toughness with stitching the thread failure processes have been identified. The major contributing failure mechanisms have been identified as (i) debonding of the thread/matrix interface (ii) elastic stretching of the thread, providing crack closure forces (iii) thread failure and pull-out.

A theoretical model developed by Jain and $Mai^{28}$  using linear elastic beam theory and the stress intensity factor approach was compared with the experimental results. The correlation between the theoretical predic- tion and experimentally determined toughness was found to be good.
