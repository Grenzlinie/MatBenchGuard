A preliminary study on the fatigue failure of continuous fiber reinforced composites under multiaxial stress has been presented. A model used to predict the direction of the critical plane of composite laminae under multiaxial cyclic stress is developed. First, two types of planes in the unidirectional lamina are defined in order to discriminate the different failure mechanisms, i.e. fibre failure and inter-fibre failure. Based on planes of different types, the concept of effective stress is proposed to determine the direction of the critical plane. Second, fatigue experiments of filament wound composites under biaxial loading are carried out and the failure planes are described. ## INTRODUCTION

Continuous fiber reinforced composites as a range of advanced engineering materials have been effectively employed in various structural systems, such as aircraft, automobiles, and power plants. These structures are often subjected to complex service loading conditions, in which two or three dynamic or static principal stresses may exist. Furthermore, when a multidirectional laminate structure is loaded uniaxially, the resulting stress distributions in its individual laminae are inherently multiaxial [1]. For reliable operation and efficient design of these engineering structures, therefore, it is important to assess their fatigue behavior under complex multiaxial stress conditions.

Although there is an extensive amount of research on multiaxial fatigue of metals, research in the same field on fiber reinforced composite materials is much less complete [2]. The reasons for the situation are the inherent inhomogeneity of composites and the complexity of multiaxial loading. Metals usually exhibit one failure mode (cracking), while composites may exhibit a combination of complex failure modes such as fiber breakage, delamination, matrix cracking, and fiber-matrix debonding. This combination of failure modes has contributed to the complexity of predicting the fatigue characteristics of composites [3]. Furthermore, the fatigue failure process of composites must be more complicated under multiaxial loading than under uniaxial loading. Fatigue degradation would be accelerated due to the stress coupling effect on material damage under multiaxial stresses, for instance, axial and shear stresses along the fiber direction applied to composites [4].

Although the study of multiaxial fatigue behavior of composites is urgent due to the lack of understanding of this extremely complex problem, fatigue testing of composites of all configurations and loading conditions is difficult, both cost- and time-wise. Thus, the establishment of a fatigue life prediction model, both structure and loading mode independent, becomes important steps in composite fatigue design.

The idea of using polynomial failure criteria for predicting fatigue failure of composite laminates under multiaxial stress states has been used by many authors [5-11]. These criteria are usually generalizations of known static failure theories taking into account factors relevant to the fatigue life of the structure, such as number of cycles and loading frequency. However, these fatigue failure criteria need at least three S-N curves and some static strengths for their implementation.

Recently, the critical plane approach, which was proposed by Findley [12] and Brown and Miller [13] in multiaxial fatigue studies for metals, have been introduced into studies for composites by Shen et al. [14-16]. Good correlation with multiaxial fatigue data of composites has been obtained under a variety of loading conditions and geometrical configurations. The advantages of this approach are its ability to facilitate the construction of fatigue-life plots. The criteria based on the critical plane approach need just one parameter-life curve, therefore, it is more useful for applications in engineering.

In general, investigation on the multiaxial fatigue damage models based on the critical plane differs in two aspects, i.e. definition of the critical plane and the assumed kind of damage parameters. In previous study [14-16], planes parallel to the fiber direction are defined as the critical planes. However the real fracture planes sometimes are different under various loading conditions. In this study, a method is proposed to determine the direction of the critical plane of composites under multiaxial stress.

# THE CRITICAL PLANE OF COMPOSITES UNDER MULTIAXIAL STRESS

Ply-by-ply method is usually applied to fatigue analysis of composite laminates [17]. How to determine the critical plane of unidirectional constituent laminae under multiaxial cyclic plane stress, therefore, is a basic step for fatigue analysis based on the critical plane approach.

Once a crack is formed in the metal during multiaxial fatigue loading progress, it usually propagates along the initial direction until the ultimate failure occurs. The fracture plane, therefore, is usually consistent with the initial cracking plane. In practice, damage on each plane is calculated by a parameter in terms of stress, strain, or energy, and the plane on which the parameter reaches its maximum is often defined as the critical plane [18]. However, composites are anisotropic and their fatigue mechanisms are more complicated than metals. Because the mechanical properties of fiber is much higher than matrix, fatigue cracks are preferential formed in the matrix. When the cracks propagate onto the surface of fiber, they may terminate its initial direction and the debonding or delamination may take place. When the damaged area, which is called pseudocrack by Takemura and Fujii [19], becomes a certain size, the final failure occurs suddenly as macrocrack progresses unstably. Obviously, the initial cracking plane is often different from the ultimate failure plane unless the initial cracking is parallel to the fiber direction. Therefore, it is unsuitable to apply the method used to determine the critical plane of metals directly to composites. In this study, the final failure plane is regarded as the critical plane for composites rather than the initial plane, and the critical plane under multiaxial stress is determined in view of macromechanics.

Schematically drawn in Fig. 1a, is an element of a lamina under multiaxial plane stress in a Cartesian coordinate system. The 1-axis is conveniently chosen to be parallel to the fiber direction, the axis-2 is normal to the fiber direction on the midplane, and 3-axis coincides with the outside normal of the surface. A general proportional loading path can then be described by the variation of the following plane stress state.

$$
\sigma=\left(\begin{array}{ccc}
\sigma_{11} & \sigma_{12} & 0 \\
\sigma_{12} & \sigma_{22} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{array}\right)=\sigma_{22}\left(\begin{array}{lll}
\varsigma & \lambda & 0 \\
\lambda & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{array}\right), \quad(1)
$$

where $\varsigma$ is the first biaxiality ratio and $\lambda$ is the second biaxiality ratio. $\varsigma$ and $\lambda$ remain constant, while $\sigma_{22}$ varies between $\sigma_{22}^{\min }$ and $\sigma_{22}^{\max }$ in a stabilized cycle.

Given any proportional stress path, the variation of stresses on any plane in the lamina can be obtained by matrix transformations. As shown in Fig. 1b, the $1^{\prime}-2^{\prime}$ plane for example is reached by first rotating the 1-2 plane counterclockwise about 3-axis by an angle $\beta$, which is followed by a second counterclockwise rotation about the new $1^{\prime}$-axis by an angle $\gamma$. Therefore, any plane in the lamina can be indicated by angles $\beta$ and $\gamma$. For example, the plane parallel to the fiber direction can be identified by $\beta=0$, and the plane perpendicular to the fiber direction has $\beta=\gamma=\pi / 2$.

The stress matrix in the new $1^{\prime}-2^{\prime}-3^{\prime}$ coordinate system can then be expressed by

$$
\sigma^{\prime}=F^{T} \sigma F, \quad(2)
$$

where
$$
F=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\beta & -\sin\beta\cos\gamma & \sin\beta\sin\gamma \\
\sin\beta & \cos\beta\sin\gamma & -\cos\beta\sin\gamma \\
0 & \sin\gamma & \cos\gamma
\end{pmatrix}
$$
and $F^{T}$ denotes the transpose matrix of $F$.

The stresses on the $1'-2'$ plane are
$$
\sigma_{1'3'}=\left[\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22})\sin2\beta-\sigma_{12}\cos2\beta\right]\sin\gamma,\quad(3a)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{2'3'}=-\left[\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22})\cos2\beta-\sigma_{12}\sin2\beta\right]\\
\times\sin\gamma\cos\gamma,\quad(3b)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{3'3'}=-\left[\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22})\cos2\beta-\sigma_{12}\sin2\beta\right]\\
\times\sin^{2}\gamma,\quad(3c)
\end{aligned}
$$

To facilitate analyzing, the planes in a lamina are classified into two types. Type $I$ planes are those cut the fibres, while Type $II$ planes are those parallel to the fiber direction. In other words, Type $I$ planes can be identified by $\beta\neq0$, while Type $II$ planes can be identified by $\beta=0$. It's obvious that planes of Type $I$ compose of fiber, matrix, and interface, while those of Type $II$ only compose of matrix and interface.

Generally, there are two kinds of failure modes for laminae under fatigue loading, i.e. fiber failure and interfiber failure. Although a real plane may not occur for fiber failure mechanism, it is assumed here, for simplification, that these two failure modes correspond to two types of planes defined above, respectively. The mechanical properties along the fiber direction are different from that along other directions. Therefore, damages caused by the fatigue loading may be different although the stresses on different planes are equal. To determine the direction of the critical plane, effective stresses are defined based on two types of planes and the plane which has the maximum effective stress is regarded as the critical plane.

### Definition of the Effective Stress on Type $I$ Planes

Some researchers think that fiber-failure mode of the lamina is caused by the tensile stress along the fiber direction [6, 20], it is assumed, therefore, that failure of Type $I$ planes is caused by the normal tensile stress on the plane.

The normal stress on any Type $I$ plane, $\sigma_{I,n}$, can be calculated by $Eq.3$, namely
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{I,n}=\left[\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}+\sigma_{22})-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22})\cos2\beta-\sigma_{12}\sin2\beta\right]\\
\times\sin^{2}\gamma,\ (4)
\end{aligned}
$$
when $0<\beta<\pi$ and the effective stress on the Type $I$ plane is defined as the tensile stress $\sigma_{I,t}$ normalized by the static tensile strength on the plane
$$
\sigma_{I}^{\text{eq}}=\sigma_{I,t}/\sigma_{I}^{f},\tag{5}
$$
where $\sigma_{I,t}=\sigma_{I,n}$ when $\sigma_{I,n}>0$, $\sigma_{I,t}=0$ when $\sigma_{I,n}\leq0$,

Because all the Type $I$ planes composes of fibre and matrix of the same fraction, it is assumed that the tensile strengths on the Type $I$ planes are equal and can be approximated by the tensile strengths of composites laminae along the fibre direction, i.e. $\sigma_{I}^{f}=\sigma_{II}^{f}$.

The planes on which $\sigma_{I}^{\text{eq}}$ reaches its maximum, denoted by $\beta$ and $\gamma$, can be derived by $Eqs.4$ and $5$
$$
\beta=\frac{3}{4}\pi-\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\frac{\varsigma-1}{2\lambda},\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}\quad\text{when }\sigma_{12}>0,\quad(6a)
$$

$$
\beta=\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\frac{\varsigma-1}{2\lambda},\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}\quad\text{when }\sigma_{12}<0,\quad(6b)
$$

$$
\beta=\frac{\pi}{2},\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}\quad\text{when }\sigma_{12}=0\text{ and }\sigma_{11}>\sigma_{22},\quad(6c)
$$
and the shear stresses on those planes are equal to 0.

To facilitate expression of the maximum normal tensile stress on the planes described by $Eq.6$, two so-called biaxiality factors $r_{1}(\varsigma,\lambda)$ and $r_{2}(\varsigma,\lambda)$ are introduced and defined as
$$
r_{1}(\varsigma,\lambda)=\frac{(1+\varsigma)+\sqrt{(1-\varsigma)^{2}+4\lambda^{2}}}{(1+\varsigma)-\sqrt{(1-\varsigma)^{2}+4\lambda^{2}}},\tag{7a}
$$

$$
r_{2}(\varsigma,\lambda)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+(2\lambda/\varsigma)^{2}}}{1-\sqrt{1+(2\lambda/\varsigma)^{2}}},\tag{7b}
$$
and loading conditions are divided into three groups, i.e. (1) $\sigma_{22}\neq0$; (2) $\sigma_{22}=0$ while $\sigma_{11}\neq0$; (3) $\sigma_{11}=\sigma_{22}=0$. The maximum normal tensile stress are expressed as follows

(1) If $\sigma_{22}\neq0$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{I,t}^{\text{max}}=\sigma_{I,n}^{\text{max}}=\left[\frac{\varsigma+1}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varsigma-1}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right]\sigma_{22}^{\text{max}}\\
\text{when }r_{1}(\varsigma,\lambda)\leq R_{22}<1,\quad(8a)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{I,t}^{\text{max}}=\sigma_{I,n}^{\text{max}}=\left[\frac{\varsigma+1}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varsigma-1}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right]\sigma_{22}^{\text{max}}\\
\text{when }R_{22}<r_{1}(\varsigma,\lambda)\quad\text{and}\quad\varsigma>\lambda^{2},\quad(8b)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{I,t}^{\text{max}}=\sigma_{I,n}^{\text{max}}=\left[\frac{\varsigma+1}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varsigma-1}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right]\sigma_{22}^{\text{min}}\\
\text{when }R_{22}<r(\varsigma,\lambda)\text{ and }\varsigma\leq\lambda^{2},\quad(8c)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{I,t}^{\text{max}}=\sigma_{I,n}^{\text{max}}=\left[\frac{\varsigma+1}{2}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varsigma-1}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right]\sigma_{22}^{\text{min}}\\
\text{when }R_{22}>1\quad\text{and}\quad\varsigma\leq\lambda^{2},\quad(8d)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\sigma_{I,t}^{\text{max}}=0\quad\text{when }R_{22}>1\text{ and }\varsigma>\lambda^{2}\tag{8e}
$$

(2) If $\sigma_{22}=0$ but $\sigma_{11} \neq 0$,

$$
\sigma_{I, t}^{\max }=\sigma_{I, n}^{\max }=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{\varsigma}\right)^{2}}\right) \sigma_{11}^{\max }
$$

when $r_{2}(\varsigma, \lambda) \leq R_{I I}<1, \quad(8 f)$

$$
\sigma_{I, t}^{\max }=\sigma_{I, n}^{\max }=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{\varsigma}\right)^{2}}\right) \sigma_{11}^{\min }
$$

when $R_{I I}<r_{2}(\varsigma, \lambda), \quad(8 \mathrm{~g})$

(3) If $\sigma_{11}=\sigma_{22}=0$ but $\sigma_{12} \neq 0$

$$
\sigma_{I, t}^{\max }=\sigma_{I, n}^{\max }=\sigma_{12}^{\max } \quad \text { when } R_{12}^{2}<1, \quad \text { (8h) }
$$

$$
\sigma_{I, t}^{\max }=\sigma_{I, n}^{\max }=-\sigma_{12}^{\min } \quad \text { when } R_{12}^{2} \geq 1, \quad \text { (8i) }
$$

Substituting Eq. 8 into Eq. 5. The maximum effective stress on the Type $I$ plane under multiaxial stress can be determined by

$$
\left(\sigma_{I}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{\max }=\sigma_{I, t}^{\max } / \sigma_{I}^{f}. \quad \text { (9) }
$$

## Definition of the Effective Stress on Type II Planes

It is assumed that failure of Type $I I$ plane is caused by the tensile and shear stresses on the plane. Under multiaxial plane stress, the normal and shear stresses on Type $I I$ planes can be calculated by

$$
\sigma_{I I, n}=\sigma_{22} \sin ^{2} \gamma, \quad(10)
$$

$$
\tau_{I I}=\sqrt{\left(\sigma_{12} \sin \gamma\right)^{2}+\left(\sigma_{22} \sin \gamma \cos \gamma\right)^{2}}, \quad(11)
$$

and the effective stress on the Type $I I$ plane is defined as follows

$$
\sigma_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\sqrt{\left(\sigma_{I I, n}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{2}+\left(\tau_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{2}}, \quad(12)
$$

where
$$
\sigma_{I I, n}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\sigma_{I I, t} / \sigma_{I I}^{f},
$$

$$
\tau_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\tau_{I I} / \tau_{I I}^{f},
$$

and $\sigma_{I I, t}$ is the normal tensile stress on the Type $I I$ plane and determined by

$$
\sigma_{I I, t}=\sigma_{I I, n} \quad \text { when } \sigma_{I I, n}>0,
$$

$$
\sigma_{I I, t}=0 \quad \text { when } \sigma_{I I, n} \leq 0,
$$

where $\sigma_{I I}^{f}$ and $\tau_{I I}^{f}$ are tensile and shear fracture strengths on the Type $I I$ plane, respectively. When a lamina is subjected to uniaxial static tensile stress normal to the fiber direction or uniaxial shear stress parallel to the fiber direction, fracture planes are those denoted by $\beta=0$ and $\gamma=\pi / 2$ [20]. It is assumed here that the Type $I I$ plane is isotropic and $\sigma_{I I}^{f}$ and $\tau_{I I}^{f}$ can be approximated by tensile strengths $\sigma_{22}^{f}$ and shear strength $\sigma_{12}^{f}$, respectively.

By substituting Eqs. 10 and 11 into Eq. 12, the effective stress on Type $I I$ plane can be expressed as follows

$$
\sigma_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\sigma_{22} \sqrt{\frac{\sin ^{4} \gamma}{\left(\sigma_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\lambda^{2} \sin ^{2} \gamma+\sin ^{2} \gamma \cos ^{2} \gamma}{\left(\tau_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}}}
$$

when $\sigma_{22}>0, \quad(13 \mathrm{a})$

$$
\sigma_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{\left|\sigma_{22} \sin \gamma\right|}{\tau_{I I}^{f}} \sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\cos ^{2} \lambda} \quad \text { when } \sigma_{22}<0, \quad(13 \mathrm{~b})
$$

$$
\sigma_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{\left|\sigma_{12} \sin \gamma\right|}{\tau_{I I}^{f}} \quad \text { when } \sigma_{22}=0 . \quad(13 \mathrm{c})
$$

The plane on which effective stress reaches its maximum among Type $I I$ planes, denoted by $\gamma$, can be derived by Eq. 13. For facilitating expressing, loading conditions are divided into three groups, i.e. (1) $\sigma_{22} \neq 0$; (2) $\sigma_{22}=0$ while $\sigma_{12} \neq 0$; (3) $\sigma_{22}=\sigma_{12}=0$. Then, $\gamma$ and the corresponding maximum effective stress are given by

(1) If $\sigma_{22} \neq 0$,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\gamma=\frac{1}{2} \operatorname{arc} \cos \frac{\left(\tau_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}+\left(\lambda \sigma_{22}^{f}\right)^{2}}{\left(\tau_{I I}^{f}\right)-\left(\sigma_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}} \text { and } \\
\left(\sigma_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{\max }=\frac{\left(\lambda^{2}+1\right) \sigma_{I I}^{f}}{2 \tau_{I I}^{f} \sqrt{\left(\sigma_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}-\left(\tau_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}}} \sigma_{22}^{\max }
\end{gathered}
$$

when $0 \leq R_{22}<1$ and $\lambda^{2}<1-2\left(\frac{\tau_{I I}^{f}}{\sigma_{I I}^{f}}\right)^{2}, \quad(14 \mathrm{a})$

$$
\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2} \quad \text { and } \quad\left(\sigma_{I I}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)^{\max }=\frac{\sqrt{\left(\tau_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}+\left(\lambda \sigma_{I I}^{f}\right)^{2}}}{\sigma_{I I}^{f} \tau_{I I}^{f}} \sigma_{22}^{\max }
$$

when $0 \leq R_{22}<1$ and $\lambda^{2} \geq 1-2\left(\frac{\tau_{I I}^{f}}{\sigma_{I I}^{f}}\right)^{2}, \quad(14 \mathrm{~b})$

$$
\begin{gathered}

\end{gathered}
$$

$$
\begin{gathered}

$$

\begin{gathered}

## EXPERIMENTAL INVESTIGATIONS

### Testing of Composites Under Multiaxial Fatigue Loading

Multiaxial fatigue experiments were carried out by filament-wound composite tubular specimens under biaxial tension/torsion in-phase proportional loading.

E-glass fiber was used as reinforcement and the matrix material used was epoxy. Mechanical properties of these matrix and reinforcement materials are given in Table 1. The fibers were wetted by passing through a resin bath for impregnation just before they were wound onto the mandrel of calibrated dimension. Helical winding was used for the desired angle of $\pm\theta$ and a reinforcement of glass-cloth/epoxy resin over a length of 50 mm were circumferentially wound at both ends of each filament wound tube. Compo- nents were cured first at $80^\circ$C for 4 h and at $120^\circ$C for 2 h. The wound tubes were then withdrawn from the mandrels and machined to the designed dimensions. A tubular specimen is shown in Fig. 3a, with 21 mm internal diameter and 220 mm long with the gauge length of 60 mm. Each specimen had reinforcement parts of glass-cloth/epoxy with outside diameter of 57.5 mm at both ends. Three types of specimens containing $55\% \pm 2\%$ volume fraction glass fibers with different configurations, namely, $(\pm35^\circ)_3$, $(\pm55^\circ)_3$, and $(\pm70^\circ)_3$, were used in this investigation.

### Failure Mechanism

When filament wound tubular specimens were loaded under tension/torsion biaxial cyclic loading, small white regions were firstly initiated at the surface of the specimen. As the number of cycles increased, white regions would grow wider and deeper along the fiber winding direction, which would develop several macroscopic cracks. As the test proceeded, one macroscopic crack could develop faster than others, and the final failure would occur along that fracture one. Generally, it was observed that more than one white zone may initiate and propagate, but only one of them dominates and leads to the final failure. Macroscopic morphology of the final failed surfaces for the biaxial fatigue loading at $R_{\text{globe}} = 0$ and $\lambda_{\text{globe}} = 0.5$ is shown in Fig. 5. We can find that all the cracking are between the fiber and matrix, parallel to the fiber winding directions. In fact, almost the same failure modes were observed for specimens under other loading conditions in this study.

### Comparison of Critical Plane With the Theoretical Prediction
Assuming the stress-strain relation of filament wound composites under biaxial stress is elastic, the stresses in the component plies of filament wound composites under biaxial loading are calculated by classic laminate theory. It should be mentioned that the elastic modulus of the constituent laminae used here are calculated using the rule of mixtures and Halpin-Tsai relationship.

By comparing the $(\sigma_{I}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ and $(\sigma_{II}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ in each component lamina calculated by Eqs. 8, 9, and 14, it is found that $(\sigma_{II}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ is greater than $(\sigma_{I}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ in the same lamina for all the constituent laminae. It means that all the critical planes predicted under our testing conditions are Type II.

Furthermore, applying the stresses in the constituent laminae to Eqs. 8, 9, and 14, and comparing the values of $(\sigma_{II}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ in constituent laminae with each other, we can obtain the maximum $(\sigma_{II}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ among them. In this study, there are only two fiber orientations for each tubular specimen, therefore, it is assumed that failure of the tubular specimen would occur if component laminae with one fiber orientation fail, and the critical planes in plies which have the maximum $(\sigma_{II}^{\text{eq}})^{\text{max}}$ are consistent with the final fracture planes.

As shown in Table 2, the shear strength $\sigma_{12}^{f}$ of the lamina is larger than the tensile strength $\sigma_{22}^{f}$. Under this condition, therefore, the direction of the critical plane can be determined by the simplified Eq. 14, namely

(1) when $\lambda^{2}<1$ and $1>R_{22}>-\frac{2\sqrt{(\tau_{II}^{f})^{2}+(\lambda\sigma_{II}^{f})^{2}}}{(1+\lambda^{2})\sigma_{II}^{f}}$, or $\lambda^{2}\geq1$,
$$\gamma_{c}=\pi/2, \tag{17a}$$

(2) under other conditions,
$$\gamma_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\arccos(-\lambda)^{2}. \tag{17b}$$

The calculated biaxiality ratios in the constituent laminae under the testing conditions are listed in Table 3. Applying the stress conditions to Eq. 17, the predicted critical planes by the model are all $\gamma_{c}=90^{\circ}$. Obviously, the predicted results are in agreement with the experimental results obtained in our tests. It can be concluded, therefore, that the model proposed in this study may be a promising method to predict the direction of the critical plane of a composite lamina under multiaxial stress.

The failure progress in reality is very complicated due to the inherent inhomogeneity of composites and the complexity of multiaxial loading. As a consequence, the direction of the critical plane could only be predicted approximately and more experimental tests are needed for further verification.

## CONCLUSIONS

(1) Two types of planes in composite laminae are defined for investigation on multiaxial fatigue of composites based on the critical plane approach, and the concept of effective stresses is proposed in order to determine the direction of critical plane of composites under multiaxial cyclic stress.

(2) After several biaxial fatigue tests of the filament wound glass fibre/epoxy composite tubular specimens, the fracture planes are described. It is shown in the results that the direction of the critical planes derived from the model is close to that of the fracture planes in reality, which is parallel to the fiber winding direction for the materials and test conditions in this study.
