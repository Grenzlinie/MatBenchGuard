# J-shaped stress/strain curves and crack resistance of biological materials

K Kendall† and K N G Fuller‡

† ICI, PO Box 8, Runcorn, Cheshire, UK
‡ Malaysian Rubber Producers Research Association, Brickendonbury, Herts, UK

Received 12 August 1986, in final form 21 May 1987

Abstract. The influence of the stress/strain curve shape on the cracking of a brittle elastic material is analysed in terms of the energy balance theory of fracture mechanics. Both r-shaped and J-shaped elastic curves are investigated. For some cracking tests, for example the trouser tear and lubricated cutting tests, the shape of the stress/strain curve is found to have little effect on the crack extension force; this is determined mainly by the fracture surface energy of the material. However, in other tests such as tension, the shape of the stress/strain curve does influence the fracture stress, but not by a large amount when compared with the effect of reinforcement. Whether an r-shaped or J-shaped curve leads to a higher fracture stress depends on such factors as the fracture energy and the size of the pre-existing crack. It thus appears that no general statement can be made about the effect of the shape of the stress/strain curve upon the crack resistance of materials, contrary to views expressed previously in the literature.

## 1. Introduction

In his book on Structures, Gordon (1968) suggested that materials exhibiting J-shaped stress/strain curves resisted cracks better than linear elastic materials. He wrote 'materials with this type of stress-strain curve are extremely difficult to tear', and used this idea to explain the unusual crack resistance of biological materials such as skin, artery or gut. Atkins and Mai have echoed this view in their work *Elastic and Plastic Fracture* (1985) saying that 'most animal tissues are in fact exceptionally resistant to cracking ... these biological materials have a J-shaped elastic curve'.

There are several difficulties with these arguments. The first problem is that the test for determining the cracking resistance of these complex materials has not properly been defined. Rupture has variously been assessed in tension or in tearing tests (Veronda and Westman 1970). Another point of confusion is the criterion for resistance to cracking. Should we accept the fracture surface energy criterion to rank these materials, in which case biological membranes would not be special (fracture energies between 1 and $20\ \text{kJ}\ \text{m}^{-2}$) as noted by Atkins and Mai? Or should we rank them in order of fracture stress for a particular crack length, or perhaps in terms of energy stored at fracture?

The theory of fracture mechanics suggests that for brittle substances the fracture surface energy provides a fundamental measure of the resistance to cracking which is independent of test geometry and the shape of the stress/strain curve. Other criteria of crack resistance such as the fracture stress are dependent on the test geometry. This paper uses the energy balance approach of fracture mechanics to show that stress criteria can also be dependent upon the shape of the stress/strain curve, the extent and sense varying critically with the test geometry and various test parameters.

First it is demonstrated that some tests of cracking should not be much influenced by the shape of the elastic curve. Two other tests are shown to be significantly affected. One of these is a readily analysable mode II test geometry which is used to illustrate the complex influence of non-linearity on the fracture stress; J-curve materials fail at lower stresses than linear or r-curve substances in certain circumstances, but require higher stresses under others. The second test studied is the tension test. Using the approximate analysis of Rivlin and Thomas (1953) the influence of non-linearity is again shown to be complex and dependent on such factors as the fracture energy and the size of the crack.

### 3. The effect of geometry

One facet of the question which needs clarification is the influence of test geometry. Certain cracking tests, such as the trouser-leg tear test, or the lubricated cutting test (figure 3) do not depend significantly on the elastic properties of the material, merely converting the potential energy of the applied load into surface energy of the crack (Kendall 1979). Thus, for tearing, the work done by the two loads $F$ in propagating the crack a distance $c$ is $2 F c$ and the area of material fractured is $b c$ where $b$ is the ligament between the deep notches. Balancing the potential and surface energies $F=b R / 2$ where $R$ is the fracture surface energy of the material, identical to the fracture toughness defined by Atkins and Mai. Strain energy does not come into this calculation because it remains constant as the crack progresses, providing there is insignificant extension of the arms (Kendall 1975a). This is true whatever the shape of the stress/strain curve. Therefore, a J-shaped or r-shaped stress/strain curve will not influence the

In contrast, the propagation of a crack through a tensile test specimen must be influenced by the elastic properties of the material because the strain energy changes around the crack as it lengthens. For linear materials, this idea is illustrated in the Griffith (1920) criterion of cracking, $\sigma=(E R / \pi c)^{1 / 2}$ for a crack of length $2 c$ in an infinite sheet. Because of this difficulty, a simpler geometry akin to cracking of a lap shear adhesive joint (Kendall 1975b) is first considered.

### 4. New geometry
Consider the geometry of figure 3(c). A sheet of material is clamped, slit with a razor and then pulled parallel to the slit. Here the cracking force depends on elasticity because it is dominated by the strain energy stored in the stretched strip, originally of length $c$, but now extended to a length $c+x$ by the force $F$ which just propagates the crack. From equation (1) the stress in the stretched material is
$$
\sigma=F / b d=\varepsilon(x / c)^{n}. \quad (2a)
$$

Thus the elastic strain energy is
$$
\int F \mathrm{~d} x=\int b \varepsilon d(x / c)^{n} \mathrm{~d} x=b \varepsilon x^{n+1} d /(n+1) c^{n}.(2b)
$$

The surface energy term is $b c R$. For a fixed grip cracking condition, the total energy is
$$
U_{\mathrm{t}}=b c R+b \varepsilon x^{n+1} d /(n+1) c^{n}. \quad (2c)
$$

Applying the principle of energy conservation, $\mathrm{d} U_{\mathrm{t}} /$ $\mathrm{d} c=0$, gives the cracking stress
$$
\sigma=\varepsilon\left(\frac{(n+1) R}{n \varepsilon d}\right)^{n /(n+1)}. \quad (2d)
$$

It is interesting that this same condition also applies under constant load testing despite the non-linear elasticity.

### 5. Calculation of the effect of non-linearity
Using equation (2d) it is possible to evaluate the effect of $n$ on cracking stress, keeping the other factors constant. The modulus parameter $\varepsilon$ was chosen to be $10^{6} \mathrm{~Pa}$ since this is a near typical figure for both rubber and skin (figure 2). The fracture surface energy $R$ was selected to be $5000 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~m}^{-2}$ because this is a reasonable value for skin and also because this gave roughly the same value of failure strain, around $\epsilon=1$ for all the materials, whatever the value of $n$. The width $b$ of the strip was set at $1 \mathrm{~mm}$ and the thickness $d$ at $10 \mathrm{~mm}$. Then the value of $n$ was changed to calculate the effect of stress/strain curve shape on fracture stress, keeping everything else constant. The curve in figure 4 shows that the influence of the J-shaped stress/strain curve with these chosen conditions is to make the crack propagate at a lower stress than for a linear or r-shaped material. In other words, J-shaped stress/strain curves can make materials fail at lower stress, in contradiction to statements in the literature. The effect is small for practical values of $n$, giving an effective halving of fracture stress as $n$ rises from 0.2 to 4 , a minor contribution compared to what can be achieved through addition of fillers or reinforcements. For example, colloidal silica changes the fracture stress of silicone rubber by a factor around 100. Similarly, glass fibre changes the cracking stress of epoxy resins by more than an order of magnitude.

\frac{b \varepsilon c^{n+1} d}{(n+1) c^{n}}=\frac{b \varepsilon c d}{(n+1)}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\right)^{(n+1) / n}=\frac{b c R}{n}. \tag{4}

## 6. Application to tension testing

Analysis of failure for a cracked tension test piece is more difficult, but can be achieved using the approximate result due to Rivlin and Thomas (1953) which defines the change in total strain energy $-\Delta W$ associated with the introduction of a crack of length $c$ into a long thin strip at constant extension
$$
-\Delta W=K(\lambda) c^{2} h W_{0} \tag{5}
$$
where $h$ is the thickness, $W_{0}$ is the strain energy density in the uniformly extended part of the test piece remote from the cut, and $K(\lambda)$ is a slowly varying function of the extension ratio $\lambda$ in the uniformly extended region. Equation (5) assumes plane stress deformation and a crack much shorter than the width of the strip. According to the energy balance fracture criterion, the fracture energy $R$ is given by
$$
R=2 K(\lambda) c W_{0}. \tag{6}
$$

For a material obeying the power law stress/strain relation of equation (1)
$$
W_{0}=\frac{\varepsilon(\lambda-1)^{n+1}}{(n+1)}. \tag{7}
$$

The function $K(\lambda)$ can be approximately represented by (Lake 1970)
$$
K(\lambda)=k / \lambda^{1 / 2} \tag{8}
$$
where $k$ is a constant just less than $\pi$. Combining equations (6), (7) and (8) results in an expression for the extension ratio at failure
$$
\frac{(\lambda-1)^{n+1}}{\lambda^{1 / 2}}=R(n+1) / 2 k c \varepsilon \tag{9}
$$
which can be solved numerically to determine the fracture stress with the aid of equation (1).

As with the mode II geometry considered in $\S\S 4$ and 5 the influence of non-linearity on cracking stress is seen to be complicated. The magnitude of the effect and whether it is a J-shaped or an r-shaped curve that increases the stress depends upon the fracture energy and on a dimension of the test piece, in this case the crack length.

J-type material cracks at lower stress than the linear or r-type material, whereas high values of $R/\varepsilon d$ lead to higher cracking stress for the J material but at the expense of much lower strain. Strain energy at failure is always lower for a J-curve material. For tension tests the behaviour depends upon the value of $R/c$ where $c$ is the crack length. For low values, J-type materials fracture at lower stresses, whereas the situation is reversed for high values.

Thus it is not generally true to say that materials with a J-shaped stress/strain curve are extremely difficult to tear.

### 7. Conclusions

The force needed to crack a brittle, non-linear elastic material depends on the test geometry. Non-linearity has little influence on certain cracking tests, such as the trouser tear or lubricated cutting tests in which cracking force depends mainly on fracture surface energy. It is recommended that such tests be used to define the cracking resistance of non-linear materials.

In other tests the non-linear elasticity does have an influence on the fracture condition but the picture is complex.
