# Analysis of the Effect of Strain Hardening on Central Bursting Defects in Drawing and Extrusion

Central bursting defects in a strain-hardening material are analyzed. A criterion has been derived for the conditions that promote these defects, as a function of the coefficient of strain hardening, the percent reduction, the die semicone angle, and the coeffi- cient of friction. The criterion was experimentally verified for homogeneous materials.

## Introduction

METAL FORMING by flow through conical converging dies is used in such processes as wire drawing, open die extrusion, hydrostatic extrusion, and conventional extrusion. Fig. 1 shows a typical die-billet setup for such processes (chamber ex- trusion is excluded from the following discussion). A rigid billet of an original radius $R_{0}$ is forced through the conical portion which has a semicone angle $\alpha$ , and emerges at a reduced final radius $R_{f}$ in the form of a rigid bar. The final radius is deter mined by the size of the cylindrical portion of the die.

The length $L$ of the cylindrical portion is provided because of die manufacturing requirements, and for dimensional stability of the product. During forming, the emerging material rubs against the cylindrical portion, introducing friction losses; thus the length $L$ is kept small.

In the drawing process the stress $\sigma_{x f}$ provides the required power, while $\sigma_{x b}$ may or may not be zero; for extrusion, the stress $\sigma_{x b}$ provides the power, and $\sigma_{x f}$ may or may not be zero.

Any forming process is designed to form a product of a pre- determined cross section from a given billet. Because the dis- cussion in this paper is limited to the case of producing round bars, the cross sections are represented by the various radii. The reduction of a bar through conical angles may be carried in one or more steps, depending on the available machines and the power requirement. Generally, it is desirable to perform the processes in as few steps as possible. The final product should be sound and smooth surfaced.

In any particular process, the reduction is determined before- hand, and the die angle can be adjusted for the process.

Past experience has shown that the semicone angle of the die,α, has a pronounced effect on the energy required for the process, or, in other words, on the drawing or extrusion stress. The angle also affects the soundness of the product. The effect of the semicone angle on the force required and on the product was analyzed in previous works $[1,7-9,12]^{1}$ and is summarized in Fig. 2 for drawing stress $\sigma_{x f}$ versus semicone angle $\alpha$ . In the sound flow region in Fig. 2, the drawing stress changes con- tinuously with the semicone angle and exhibits a minimum at some cone angle designated as the optimal cone angle $\alpha_{opt }$ .

The relations between die cone angle and critical, dead zone, and optimal cone angles are shown in Fig. 3. In addition to the external phenomena discussed, there is a region overlapping the sound flow and dead zone regions where the material may undergo internal ruptures, forming defectscalled "central bursts." These defects are shown in Fig. 4 and are the subject of this work.

The central bursting defect is analyzed in reference [1]. The analysis was made for a nonstrain-hardening material, called "Mises' material," a nonstrain-hardening material which under- goes no elastic deformation and consequently no volume change during deformation. Mises' material obeys Mises' stress devi- ator-strain rate relation and Mises' yield criterion. The stress combination that causes Mises' material to enter the plastic range and the flow law in the plastic range are defined, but no limit is set on the amount of flow possible. A criterion for the conditions that promote the central bursting defect in this kind of material was derived.

The goal of this work is to define criteria for the occurrence of central bursting defects as a function of the strain-hardening coefficient, cone angle, reduction, and friction.

An upperbound approach for energy expended to cause the flow of strain-hardening material through conical converging dies is presented in reference [2]. The present analysis accounts for strain hardening in the manner introduced in reference [2]. The flow of metal through a conical converging die is shown in Fig. 1. A wire or rod of initial radius $R_{0}$ is pushed (extruded) or pulled (drawn) through the conical portion of the die. Plastic deformation occurs in Zone II in Fig. 1. During the plastic de- formation, strains are introduced and, as a result, the material strain hardens. No plastic deformations occur in Zones I and III. The analysis assumes linear strain-hardening effect with no elastic deformation and consequently no volume change during deformation.

The solution presented in reference [1] assumes a velocity field containing central bursts, as shown in Fig. 6. The same velocity field is assumed in the present analysis. However, while in reference [1] nonstrain-hardening Mises' material was treated, the present study treats the strain-hardening material. Computing the internal power of deformation and shear and friction losses evaluates the power required for flow with central bursting. If the flow with central bursting, as computed, re- quires less power than the flow without central bursting, actual flow will occur with central bursting.

The conditions for which a velocity field with central bursting prevails are computed and presented in both mathematical and graphical form (Fig. 7) and the results are compared with the results of experimental work.

### Effective Flow Stress

The effect of strain hardening on the flow stress of the material can be approximated by

$$
\sigma_{\text {eff }}=S(1+\beta \Phi)
\tag{5}
$$

Equation (5) shows a linear relationship between the flow stress and the effective strain. The effective strain as the function of the toroidal coordinates $(r, \theta$, and $\varphi)$ is found through

$$
\Phi=\int_{t=0}^{t} \dot{\Phi} d t
\tag{6}
$$

The instantaneous effective strain rate $\dot{\Phi}$ is given by

$$
\dot{\Phi}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} \dot{\epsilon}_{i j} \dot{\epsilon}_{i j}}
\tag{7}
$$

Since

$$
d r=v d t
\tag{8}
$$

it follows by equation (2) for $v$ that

$$
d t=\frac{d r}{v}=-\frac{r}{v_{f} r_{f} \cos \theta} \cdot \frac{r \sin \theta+R_{i}}{r_{f} \sin \theta+R_{i}} d r
\tag{9}
$$

The assumed velocity field is such that each material point at an angular position $\theta$ moves along a radial line, decreasing the position coordinate $r$ but keeping $\theta$ constant. Substituting equation (9) into equation (6) replaces the variable $t$ with the variable $r$ over which the integration is performed.

The effective strain in Zone II, as computed in Appendix 1 of references [14, 15], is

## The Criterion
Substituting the values of $\dot{W}_{i}, \dot{W}_{s 1}, \dot{W}_{s 2}, \dot{W}_{s 3}, \dot{W}_{s 4}, \dot{W}_{a}$, and $\dot{W}_{b}$ from equations (14), (18), (21), (24), (28), (29), and (30) into equations (31) and (32) and dividing by the common term $S \pi v_{f} R_{f}^{2}$, the applied stress required to perform the deformation is obtained.

For wire drawing,

$$
\frac{\sigma_{x f}}{S}=\left(-\dot{W}_{b}+\dot{W}_{i}+\dot{W}_{s 1}+\dot{W}_{s 2}+\dot{W}_{s 3}+\dot{W}_{s 4}\right) \frac{1}{S \pi v_{f} R_{f}^{2}}
\tag{33}
$$

And for extrusion,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sigma_{x b}}{S}=\left(\dot{W}_{a}-\dot{W}_{i}-\dot{W}_{s 1}-\dot{W}_{s 2}-\dot{W}_{s 3}-\dot{W}_{s 4}\right) & \frac{1}{S \pi v_{f} R_{f}^{2}} \\
& \times \frac{\left(\frac{R_{0}}{R_{f}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{R_{i}}{R_{f}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\frac{R_{0}}{R_{f}}\right)^{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{R_{i}}{R_{f}}\right)^{2}\right]}
\end{aligned}

When $R_{i} / R_{f}=0$, equations (33) and (34) (after substituting the various terms) reduce to the old solution, equation (42) of reference [2]. In order for central bursting to occur, the required force must decrease with an increase in $R_{i} / R_{f}$ from zero up; that is, a slight increase in $R_{i} / R_{f}$ should cause a reduction in the required drawing (or extrusion) stress. The criterion for central bursting as presented in equation (37) defines two zones, one in which any combination of cone angle, friction, reduction ratio, and coefficient of strain hardening will result in central bursting, and another in which central bursting will not occur. The two zones are distinguished by sets of limiting conditions for which the expression of equation (37) equals zero. Equation (37) can thus define sets of conditions for the limit case. The equation was programmed and by a method of successive approximations, these sets of conditions were computed.

Previous work [1] defined a danger zone in which central bursting may occur, depending on material properties. That is, if the material is hard, and the strain-hardening coefficient $(\beta)$ is zero, central bursting will occur in the danger zone.

The strain-hardening property of the material was taken into consideration in the present work; thus one can say that a homogeneous and isotropic material for which the strain-harden- ing coefficient $(\beta)$ is given will burst centrally if worked in the region defined to the right of the appropriate solid line in Fig. 8.

As can be noted in Fig. 7, as well as in Fig. 8, an increase in the coefficient of strain hardening $(\beta)$ generally decreases the fracture zone. In other words, a particular combination of cone angle and reduction in area, which causes central bursting in non- strain-hardening material, will not cause it in a strain-hardening material. For example, this effect may be noted in Fig. 7 for30 percent reduction in area and 20-deg cone angle.

However, for very large reductions and cone angles, the situa- tion becomes more involved. Interaction between central bursting and the formation of a dead zone takes place, as dis- cussed next.

A well-known phenomenon in deformed material is the forma- tion of dead zone. When the die angle is too large, the material adjacent to the die adheres to the die and is immobilized. It forms a dead zone acting as a die of a smaller cone angle. The material then flows over this surface rather than the surface of the die. The occurrence of dead zone in a nonstrain-hardening material was analyzed in references [7, 8], and a criterion for the formation of dead zone was presented. The critical cone angle is defined as that angle which determines whether a dead zone will occur or will be prevented. When the die cone angle

## Experimental Study

The aim of the experimental work was to verify the criterion for nonstrain-hardening materials $(\beta=0)$ as presented in refer ence [1] and Fig.5.

The experimental procedure was based on the fact that the coefficient of strain hardening in metals changes with the amount of cold working. As the metal is deformed, the coefficient of strain hardening reduces until it becomes essentially zero. A general description of an extended stress-strain curve is shown in Fig. 9. The analysis was carried out assuming linear strain- hardening behavior,

$$
\sigma_{\text {eff }}=S(1+\beta \Phi) \quad(5)
$$

The coefficient of strain hardening $(\beta)$ can be approximated from the extended stress-strain curve for every reduction as

$$
\beta=\frac{\Delta\left(\frac{\sigma_{\text {eff }}}{2}\right)}{\Delta \Phi}
$$

For a cold-worked material, the ideal true strain introduced is defined as

$$
\Phi=2 \ln \left(\frac{R_{0}}{R_{f}}\right)
$$

From Fig. 9 it can be seen that, when a material is cold-worked extensively (like $\phi_{2}$ in Fig. 9), the coefficient of linear strain hardening becomes very small, and the material can be assumed to be nonstrain hardening.

The coefficient $\beta$ from the extended stress-strain curve can be approximated by the following method (Fig. 9): for annealed material cold working introduces $\Phi_{1}$ , effective strain, and the coefficient of strain hardening will be

$$
\beta_{1}=\frac{1}{\Phi_{1}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}}\right)
$$

However, because the analysis is related to strain hardening in the course of the actual test, when a material that was precold- worked to $\Phi_{1}$ is cold-worked again to $\Phi_{2}$ , the coefficient of strain hardening will be

$$
\beta_{2}=\frac{1}{P_{2}-\Phi_{1}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)
$$

## Conclusions

A criterion for the occurrence of central bursting in a homogeneous strain-hardening material was developed, assuming linear strain hardening. The analytical solution was based on the approach presented in reference [1] for a nonstrain-hardening material. The criterion is presented analytically [equation (37)] and graphically (Fig. 7).

The experimental study showed that central bursting never occurs when homogeneous materials are cold-worked in the safe zone predicted in reference [1] for a nonstrain-hardening material. For unhomogeneous materials (e.g., 12L14 alloy), it was found that an analysis should be developed to take inclusions into consideration.

The criterion for safe zone for nonstrain-hardening materials thus defines a zone for cold working so that central bursting will

not occur in homogeneous materials. For materials that strain harden, the safe zone is generally increased. However, the cri- terion developed for central bursting in strain-hardening mate- rials should be used with caution, and should be regarded more as a qualitative measure. The criterion developed is a function of the strain-hardening coefficient, $\beta$, and the actual determina tion of this coefficient in metals is somewhat ambiguous.
