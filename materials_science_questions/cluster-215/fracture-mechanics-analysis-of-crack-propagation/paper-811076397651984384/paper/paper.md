# AN APPROACH TO EXPLAIN THE STAGE I/II BEHAVIOUR OF THE DELAYED HYDRIDE CRACKING VELOCITY VS. $\boldsymbol{K_I}$ CURVE FOR Zr-2.5 Nb

D. Yan and R.L. Eadie

Department of Chemical and Materials Engineering, University of Alberta, Edmonton, Alberta, Canada T6G 2G6

(Received January 26, 2000)
(Accepted February 25, 2000)

Keywords: Hydrogen embrittlement; Kinetics (theory and modeling); Diffusion (theory and modeling); Stress corrosion; Computer simulation

## Introduction

For several cracking mechanisms of metallic materials, the crack growth rate exhibits a three-stage curve relationship with the applied stress intensity, as given in Fig. 1. Such cracking mechanisms may include stress corrosion cracking (SCC) and hydrogen induced cracking (HIC) of steels and titanium alloys (1-3), and delayed hydride cracking (DHC) of zirconium alloys (4). One characteristics for these cracking mechanisms is that the crack nucleates and propagates for a finite distance in each crack increment (2,4). Depending on the relative size of the crack increment to the plastic zone at the crack tip, the crack growth kinetics is dominated by either elastic (stage I) or plastic (stage II) stress field (2). For DHC, this crack increment is the hydride length at the crack tip or striations measured by fractography (4). In this work, based on the concept and measurement that there is a critical length of hydride cluster for fracture at different stress intensity $K_I$, a new approach is proposed to explain the stage I and II behaviour of DHC velocity vs. $K_I$ curve for Zr-2.5 Nb alloy. This approach may be considered as an extension of existing explanations (2,4) to the three-stage crack growth rates.

The Zr-2.5 Nb alloy is a structural material for constructing the pressure tubes of the primary heat transport system in CANDU (Canadian Deuterium Uranium) nuclear reactors. This alloy, despite its excellent combination of mechanical properties and low neutron absorption, is sometimes susceptible to a fracture mechanism called delayed hydride cracking (DHC). Since the first observation of cracking in Zr-2.5 Nb pressure tubes in the early 1970's, extensive research has been conducted in this area. In general, DHC has been accepted as a repeated process that involves stress-induced hydrogen diffusion, hydride precipitation and hydride fracturing (4). The process usually begins at the tip of preexisting cracks in the alloys that act as stress raisers. Usually, the stress intensity factor, $K_I$, is used to characterize the stress state at the crack tip.

The DHC velocity of Zr-2.5 Nb alloy is dependent on $K_I$, and usually exhibits a three-stage curve, as shown in Fig. 1. In stage I, the DHC velocity is very sensitive to $K_I$, and increases rapidly with small increases in $K_I$. There is a threshold value for $K_I$, i.e. $K_{IH}$, below which DHC will not happen. In stage II, the DHC velocity is relatively insensitive to $K_I$, and forms a plateau. Early research work (4) suggested that stage I behaviour is mainly controlled by the elastic stress field at crack tip, while stage II behaviour is controlled by the plastic yielding at the crack tip, and this explanation is in agreement

Recent numerical simulations (5) calculated the average DHC velocity with respect to $K_I$ by assuming a constant hydride length for fracture, which may be appropriate for large $K_I$. Theoretical work (6,7) and experimental observations at low $K_I$ (8) showed that there is a dependence of hydride (or striation) length on $K_I$.

Experimental work (9) was performed by the authors to study the critical hydride cluster length at the crack tip for both $K_I < K_{IH}$ and $K_I > K_{IH}$ in the region near $K_{IH}$ and it was proposed that the occurrence of DHC is controlled by two factors: one is the maximum length to which the hydride cluster can grow, and the other is the critical length of hydride cluster required for fracture. We find that this concept not only extended the discussion in (6,7) regarding the threshold behaviour of DHC, but may provide a different approach for explaining the stage I/II behaviour of the DHC velocity vs. $K_I$ curve if we do some simple analysis. This approach may be considered as an extension of existing explanations (2,4).

## Results and Discussion

### 1. Experimental Results

In DHC of Zr-2.5 Nb alloys, the hydride cluster at the crack tip has a critical length, which is a function of the stress intensity factor $K_I$ and other material parameters. When $K_I > K_{IH}$, the hydride cluster must grow to this critical length before it will fracture. On the other hand, when $K_I < K_{IH}$, there is a maximum length to which the hydride cluster can grow. This is because the hydride growth is a self-limiting process, since the driving force for hydrogen diffusion decreases as the hydride grows (6). This length is insufficient for fracture i.e. less than the critical length.

Fig. 2 (9) gives the hydride cluster lengths observed at different $K_I$. The dotted line gives the lengths of the single hydride clusters at the crack tip, measured after the specimens were held under load for sufficiently long times. These hydride clusters were not fractured. The solid line gives the lengths of the first hydride clusters at the crack tip, and these hydride clusters were fractured. One sees that as $K_I$ increases, the hydride cluster length first increases for low $K_I$ and then reverses direction when $K_I$ exceeds $6.0\ \text{MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$. The solid line gives the critical length of the (first) hydride cluster that can be fractured at different applied $K_I$. The dotted line shows the maximum length to which the hydride cluster can grow. For small $K_I$ this length is insufficient for fracture. The intersection of the dotted and solid lines defines the threshold for DHC.

This argument can be extended to explain the stage I and stage II velocity behaviour for DHC. Time-dependent growth is considered in Fig. 3. The dotted line on the top gives the maximum length

## 2. Theoretical Analysis

Although a more accurate analysis of the hydrogen diffusion at the crack tip requires finite element analysis tools, a simple and easy-to-handle numerical method by using finite differential method and a cylindrical approximation to the stress field at crack tip has been developed in (5). This method gives results that are in reasonable agreement with experiment. In summary, this method assumes the stress field is cylindrically symmetric under plane strain at the crack tip (4,5), and ignores the change of stress due to hydride formation. The Fick's first and second laws for hydrogen diffusion are given as equations (1) and (2):

$$
J(r, t)=-D_{H}[\nabla C(r, t)-C(r, t) \nabla \Phi(r)], \quad L_{h}(t)<r<L, \tag{1}
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial C(r, t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left[-\frac{1}{r} J(r, t)\right], \quad L_{h}(t)<r<L, \tag{2}
$$

where J(r, t) is the hydrogen flux at distance r from the crack tip and at time t; $D_{H}$ is the hydrogen diffusivity; C(r, t) is the hydrogen concentration; $\Phi(\mathrm{r})=-(p(r) \cdot V_{H}) / R T$ is the hydrostatic stress potential, where p(r) is the hydrostatic stress (positive for tensile stress), $V_{H}$ the partial molal volume of hydrogen, R the gas constant and T the temperature in Kelvin; $L_{h}(t)$ is the length of hydride at the crack tip as a function of time, and L is the distance of the boundary used in calculation (about the diffusion distance $\sqrt{D_{H} t}$). The boundary conditions are given in equations (3) and (4) as:

$$
C\left(L_{h}, t\right)=C_{H}^{P}, \tag{3}
$$

and

$$
J(L, t)=0, \tag{4}
$$

where $C_{H}^{P}$ is the hydrogen precipitation solvus.

By using this numerical method from (5), we calculated the maximum length to which the hydride cluster can grow at the crack tip at $150^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, and the hydride cluster lengths corresponding to different diffusion times, as given by the dotted lines in Fig. 4. In our calculation, the initial hydrogen concentration was assumed to be 96 percent of the hydrogen precipitation solvus at $150^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. This is to simulate the effect of thermal cycle in which the specimen was heated to a peak temperature about $60^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ above the test temperature in order to obtain hydrogen saturation in the specimen (9). In real situations, hydride clusters are wedge-shaped (9), and this hydride thickness should increase with diffusion time, but will be in the range of $2$–$3\ \mu$m for most cases. However, the hydride thickness used in the calculation will only slightly affect the resulting overall DHC velocities, but will not affect the conclusions we draw about the stage I/II transition of the DHC velocities. This is because the latter is decided by the critical hydride cluster length curve, as we will see in the following discussion. The DHC velocities can thus be calculated from the intersections of the dotted lines and the solid line in Fig. 4, and are given in Fig. 5. In Fig. 5, one can see that the position of the stage I/II transition in DHC velocity vs. $K_I$ curve is determined by the turning point of the critical hydride cluster length vs. $K_I$ curve, where the critical length of hydride cluster starts to increase abruptly (as $K_I$ decreases).

The critical length of the hydride cluster, as given by the solid line in Fig. 2 and 4, was measured in experiments. The theoretical explanation for this critical hydride length for fracture still remains controversial, due to a lack of clarity in the hydride fracture criteria (6,7). Both the critical stress model (6) and the energy balance model (7), however, predict a curve for critical hydride length similar to the solid line in Fig. 2 and 4. It was proposed by early research (4) that the stage I behaviour is mainly controlled by the elastic stress field at the crack tip and the stage II behaviour is caused by the plastic yielding at the crack tip. For DHC, this is related to the relative size of the plastic yielding zone at crack tip to the length of hydride cluster (4). Stage I corresponds to the situation when the length of hydride cluster is longer than the size of plastic yielding zone, and for stage II the situation is the opposite. To understand this, one may calculate $r_P = 1/6\pi\ (K_I/\sigma_{ys})^2$, which is the approximate size of plastic yielding zone for plane strain, for different $K_I$, and plot it with the curve of the critical hydride length, as in Fig. 6. Here $\sigma_{ys} = 630$ MPa is the yield stress of Zr-2.5 Nb at $150^\circ$C. In Fig. 6, one sees that, at about $K_I = 8$ MPa$\sqrt{m}$, the hydride cluster length equals the size of plastic yielding zone. This $K_I$, as marked by the arrow in Fig. 5, approximately defines the transition point between stage I and stage II of the DHC velocity vs. $K_I$ curve.

What this analysis suggests is that when the size of the damage zone necessary for fracture is less than the plastic zone size, whether it be hydrides accumulating as in DHC or hydrogen accumulating as in HIC, damage accumulates rapidly and is not very dependent on $K_I$ (stage II). At lower $K_I$, however, the size of the necessary damage zone extends outside the plastic zone, into the elastic region where the stresses are not as elevated as they are in the plastic zone. Then the damage accumulation, which is driven by the stress gradients, becomes more dependent on $K_I$ and the cracking velocity vs. $K_I$ curve shows a much steeper slope (stage I). This would also correlate with the experimental observations (8) that at large $K_I$, the hydride size is small and its exact shape is not readily observable, but at near $K_{IH}$, the wedge-shaped hydrides and their variations are observable.

## Conclusions
(1) Based on experimentally measured critical lengths of hydride cluster for fracture, a new approach has been proposed to explain the stage I and stage II behaviour of DHC velocity vs. $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ curve.
(2) The DHC velocities at different $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ can be determined from the intersections between the curve of critical hydride cluster length and the curves of the length of hydride cluster that can grow at the crack tip for different times.
(3) The $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ corresponding to the stage I/II transition in the DHC velocity vs. $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ curve corresponds to the $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ where the critical length of hydride cluster for fracture starts to increase abruptly (as $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ decreases), as given in Figs. 4 and 5.
(4) Analysis shows that at the $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ corresponding to the stage I/II transition, the size of the plastic zone at the crack tip equals the critical length of the hydride cluster. This result is in agreement with early research that proposed that the stage I and II behaviour is related to the relative size of the plastic zone at crack tip to the length of hydride cluster.
(5) A hydrogen diffusion analysis based on partial differential methods was conducted to calculate the length of hydride cluster at crack tip for different diffusion times, and by using the experimental data of critical length for hydride fracture, the DHC velocity vs. $K_{\mathrm{I}}$ curve was simulated. The simulation supports the contention.
