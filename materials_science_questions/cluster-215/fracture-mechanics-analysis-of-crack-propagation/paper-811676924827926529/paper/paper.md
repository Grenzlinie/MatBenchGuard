## ABSTRACT
In the lamellar form of the ordered intermetallic TiAl, mismatches at the lamellar interfaces, of order 1%, give rise to internal stresses and to interfacial dislocations. The geometry of the microstructure ensures extreme plastic anisotropy. Three main trends are evident in the plastic tensile properties: Both the yield and fracture stresses are low when deformation occurs in the plane of the lamellae and they are high when deformation occurs across the lamellae, the ductility is high when the tensile axis lies close to the lamellar plane and low when the tensile axis is nearly normal to the lamellar plane. The variations of the yield stress and the fracture stress are explained principally by the effects of Schmid factors and anisotropies in the Hall- Petch (or Stroh) stresses resulting from the lamellar shapes. The variation in ductility may be understood in terms of a competition between the ductile propagation of dislocations across interfaces and the creation of Stroh cracks at the heads of dislocation pile-ups.

## INTRODUCTION
When a thin film is deposited hetero-epitaxially onto a substrate, elastic strains in the film take up the lattice mismatch until the film reaches a critical thickness whereupon the elastic strains are partially relieved by the appearance of interfacial dislocations [1,2]. In multilayer devices the mismatches at each interface contribute to the elastic strains in any layer. The mismatch strains commonly lead to fabrication problems like buckling, debonding and delamination of the deposited layers. In metals, somewhat similar microstructures may be made by controlled phase transformations. Such transformation-engineered materials have constituents whose composition is fixed by phase equilibrium considerations and which may be epitaxially related across low index planes. These materials may be described as stacks of thin hetero-epitaxial films with unusual mechanical properties.

In this paper we describe an epitaxially related two phase $\alpha_2$-Ti₃Al (DO₁₉) + $\gamma$-TiAl (L1₀) layered structure in which both constituents are ordered intermetallics. Its attractions in the aerospace industry are that it has a low density and it is exceptionally ductile for an intermetallic [3]. The lamellar films are ~1µm thick and the lattice mismatches are ~1% which implies that most of the mismatch is accommodated by interfacial dislocations but a fraction of the mismatch is taken up by elastic strains. The resulting stresses are comparable in magnitude with the yield stress of the material but they have no effect on slip in the plane of the lamellae. The lamellar morphology promotes deformation (slip and twinning) in the plane of the lamellae and deformation across the lamellae is obstructed by the lamellar interfaces, by the mismatch dislocations and by the internal stresses. Consequently both the yield behavior and the fracture behavior are strongly anisotropic. Many of the observations can be rationalized by applying the Hall-Petch theory of yield and the Stroh theory of crack formation to a material with exceptionally flat pancake shaped grains.

## FORMATION AND STRUCTURE
The lamellar $\alpha_2$-$\gamma$ microstructure forms on cooling the $\alpha$-hcp Ti (disordered form of Ti₃Al) parent phase. Lamellar plates are obtained by $\alpha$ ->$\gamma$ or $\alpha_2$ ->$\gamma$ transformation through the operation of Shockley partial dislocations sweeping alternate basal planes. Each sweep produces two layers of $\gamma$ crystal. The {111} planes of $\gamma$ are parallel to the (0001) plane of $\alpha$ and the close packed directions of $\gamma$ nearly line up with the close packed directions of $\alpha$. But, because $\gamma$ is slightly tetragonal (c/a = 1.02 at 300K), the alignment cannot be perfect. with the result that there are interfacial dislocations between the grains and elastic stresses in the grains. Three physically distinct γ/γ interfaces exist, the 60⁰, 120⁰ and 180⁰ (twin) types but there is only one type of α₂/γ interface. α₂ lamellae are very large but γ lamellae are broken into domains, typically 40μm across, which are 120⁰ variants of each other. The domain boundaries, unlike the lamellar boundaries, are generally not crystallographic. Most of these features are illustrated in Fig.1. PROPERTIES OF LAMELLAR AND DOMAIN BOUNDARIES.

The triangles in Fig.1 on each γ lamella, which indicate the atomic structure of a {111} plane, are isosceles, not equilateral. The atomic spacing in two directions is $\sqrt{(a^2+c^2)/2}$ and it is $a/\sqrt{2}$ in the third. The triangle in the α₂ plate is equilateral with atomic spacing a'/2 where a' is one lattice parameter of α₂. No two of these three spacings are the same; consequently mismatches, of order (c/a - 1)/2 ~1%, are present at all interfaces except the γ/γ 180⁰ interface which is coherent [5]. These mismatches are accommodated principally by interfacial dislocations, [6].

Figure 1. Lamellar geometry in PST α₂/γ TiAl. Note the domain structure of the lamellae.

At the γ/γ 60⁰ and 120⁰ interfaces, the strains are shears in the plane of the interface and are an example of a Matthews shear boundary [2]. The interfacial dislocations are sets of parallel screws with spacing ~2b/(c/a - 1) ~100b, where b is the Burgers vector of the screws. According to the Frank and van der Merwe theory [1] the dislocations take up most of the mismatch, but leave a residual elastic strain in the lamellae of order b/h. In this case, of screw dislocations with a spacing much smaller than the lamellar thickness h, the original theory is hardly altered by the recent reformulation of Willis et al. [7]. The elastic strain of b/h implies an elastic shear stress of Gb/h and convergent beam electron diffraction measurements [5] have detected these stresses in γ lamellae. Because of the flat plate geometry the stresses are uniform through the lamellae

At the γ/α₂ interfaces the mismatch shears are identical to those at γ/γ interfaces but, in addition, there is a biaxial expansion in γ and a biaxial compression in α₂. These latter strains, which are of the same order as the shear strains, require a cross grid of edge interfacial dislocations as well as the single set of screw dislocations.

In addition to the interfacial dislocations and the elastic stresses in the lamellae, the interfaces have another interesting property. Referring to Fig.1, it is clear that when one lamella, e.g. the 0⁰ lamella interfaces with the 120⁰ lamella, the atomic bonding across the interface is abnormal: The numbers of Al-Al bonds, Ti-Ti bonds and Ti-Al bonds differ from their bulk values. This has the effect that the gamma surface of the interface is not the same as the gamma surface of an ordinary {111} plane. In particular, shifts of one lamella relative to its neighbor of <112>/6, <112>/3 and <110>/2 incur no energy penalty to first order [8]. This conclusion, reached by a simple bond counting argument, has been confirmed by atomistic estimates[9]. Another way of stating this result is that the APB, CSF and SISF energies at a γ/γ 120⁰ interface are all close to zero. Similar effects occur at α₂/γ interfaces and at γ/γ 60⁰ interfaces but not at γ/γ 180⁰ interfaces [8]. The alterations to the gamma surface may have three consequences:

(i) Dislocations which consist of close-coupled partial dislocations when travelling parallel to an interface can dissociate very widely and therefore move more easily if they actually travel in the interface. This applies also to climb dissociation and climb mobility [8].
(ii) Dislocations travelling across a lamella tend to stick to an interface because they can lower their energy there by wide dissociation or by core spreading [9].
(iii) The alteration to the gamma surface modifies the core structure of dislocations in the interface and makes them more mobile than in the bulk [9].

## MECHANICAL PROPERTIES

Tensile stress strain curves of lamellar TiAl have been published by Inui et al. [3] and are reproduced in Fig.2. Three trends are evident in this figure: The yield stress and the fracture stress are both strongly orientation dependent with a minimum when $\phi$ (the angle between the tensile axis and the lamellar plane) is ~45°. The third trend is that the ductility is high when $\phi$ is small and falls drastically when $\phi$ exceeds ~60°. The trend in the yield stress has been explained [3] by considering the operation of hard and soft modes of deformation, the Hall-Petch effect and Schmid factors. The second trend, in the fracture stress, indicates that fracture is linked to yield and this may be rationalized by the Stroh model [10], see below. The third trend appears to result from detailed crystallographic considerations.

One of the critical concepts is that of soft versus hard mode deformation. In Fig.2 the three specimens B, C and D deform in the plane of the lamellae, like easy glide of single crystals. The mean free path of the dislocations is large (the domain size) and the yield stress is low, being determined by that of the softest lamella or interface Specimens A and N deform in hard mode: The Schmid factors on soft mode systems are zero, deformation (slip or twinning) cuts across lamellar boundaries, mean free paths are small, the yield stress is high and (for compatibility reasons) even the hardest lamella must deform. In both modes, five components of the yield stress can be isolated: The Peierls stress, $\tau_P$ and the solid solution stress $\tau_S$ (which together comprise the yield stress of a single crystal $\tau_0$), the Frank Read stress $\tau_F$ to operate a dislocation source, the internal stress $\tau_I$ from the lamellar mismatches and the Hall Petch stress $\tau_H$ caused by the finite crystal size. In the soft mode the resolved $\tau_I$ is zero, $\tau_F$ is negligible and so the yield stress $\tau_y$ is $(\tau_0 + \tau_H)$. In the hard mode $\tau_F$ and $\tau_I$ are finite but, when the lamellar thickness is ~1µm they are an order of magnitude less than $\tau_H$ so again $\tau_y$ is close to $(\tau_0 + \tau_H)$ [8] If the lamellae were much less than 1µm thick, $\tau_F$ and $\tau_I$ would both become more significant relative to $\tau_H$ and the analysis would consequently change. It should be noted that the values of both $\tau_0$ and particularly $\tau_H$ are different in soft and hard modes. The models for estimating the yield and fracture stresses are based on the observation that both slip and twinning dislocations are

obstructed by interfaces, lamellar interfaces in hard mode and domain interfaces in soft mode, see Fig.3. The dislocations are piled up at the interfaces; they may either propagate deformation across the interface (ductility, the Hall Petch effect) or they may open up mode 1 cracks (fracture, Stroh effect). The two effects are viewed as being in competition, if the stress is low for propagation the material is ductile, but if it is low for fracture, the material is brittle.

### Hall Petch Effect
Nakano et al. [11] have shown that the yield stress of lamellar TiAl obeys the Hall Petch equation

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}=\tau_{0} / \mathrm{S}+\mathrm{B} / \ell^{1 / 2} \tag{1}
$$

where $\tau_{0}$ is the critical resolved shear stress of a single crystal, S the Schmid factor of the operative system and $\ell$ the mean free path of the dislocations. In soft mode $\ell$ is the domain size and in the hard mode $\ell$ is a little larger than h, the lamellar thickness. The measured values for B are $0.25 \pm 0.1$ MPa$\sqrt{\mathrm{m}}$ in soft mode and $0.44 \pm 0.08$ MPa$\sqrt{\mathrm{m}}$ in hard mode [11]. Semiquantitative theoretical values have been given for B which are somewhat lower than the experimental values [8]. The reason why B is low in soft mode deformation is that the interfaces are $120^{\circ} \gamma / \gamma$ interfaces which are easier for dislocations to penetrate than the variety of $\gamma / \gamma$ and $\gamma / \alpha_{2}$ interfaces faced by hard mode dislocations. $\tau_{0}$ has at least seven different theoretical values, depending on exactly which dislocations control the deformation: Perfect, super, twin or interfacial dislocations in $\gamma$ and dislocations gliding on basal, prism or pyramidal planes in $\alpha_{2}$. The measured value of $\tau_{0}$ contains some average of these individual values.

### Stroh effect
The Stroh theory [10] concentrates on the tensile, rather than the shear, stresses close to a pile up of edge dislocations. A pile up of dislocations, driven by an effective stress $(\tau-\tau_{0})$ against a lamellar or domain interface barrier and of length $\ell$ generates a tensile stress at a point $(r, \theta)$ from the pile up head of

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}=(3 / 2)(\ell / \mathrm{r})^{1 / 2}(\tau-\tau_{0}) \sin \theta \cos (\theta / 2) \tag{2}
$$

The maximum stress is on a plane inclined at $\cos ^{-1}(1 / 3)=71^{\circ}$ from the slip plane and on the tensile side of the edge dislocations This maximum value of $\sigma_{n}$ is $2(\ell / 3 r)^{1 / 2}(\tau-\tau_{0})$. Stroh then invokes the Griffith critical stress to grow a crack of length r, which is $\sigma_{n}=(8 E \gamma_{s} / \pi r)^{1 / 2}$ where E is Young's modulus and $\gamma_{S}$ is the surface energy. Eliminating r, and using a tensile applied stress, the fracture stress is:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}=(1 / S)\left(\tau_{0}+A / \ell^{1 / 2}\right) \tag{3}
$$

where $A=(6 E \gamma_{S} / \pi)^{1 / 2}=0.6 \mathrm{MPa} \sqrt{\mathrm{m}}$ in TiAl.This is an upper limit for A because edge dislocations, which are the most effective crack-formers are assumed and because cleavage is taken to occur on the plane with the maximum tensile stress whereas in fact TiAl tends to cleave on crystallographic planes $\{111\}$ and $\{100\}$, see [12].

The two equations, (1) for plastic yield and (3) for semi-brittle failure are virtually identical ( to the accuracy of the theory) and this satisfactorily explains the fact that yield and fracture stresses show similar orientation dependences, Fig.2. Since the two processes are in competition, it is not surprising that TiAl is sometimes ductile and sometimes brittle. The constants $S, \tau_{0}, A$ and B in equations (1) and (3) are all sensitive to the crystallography of the lamellae and so, to make estimates of $\sigma_{y}$ and $\sigma_{f}$, this must be taken into account.

**Comparison with Experiment.**
The crystallography of this problem seems very complicated, for example, there are seven separate values of $\tau_{0}$ and S is different for each of the 16 deformation systems (12 slip and 4 twin) in each of the 6 differently oriented lamellae. The constant B depends on which of the 4 types of interface are considered and A depends on the 4 characters of the piled up dislocations ( 0 and $60^{\circ}$ for glide dislocations, $30^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$ for twin dislocations) and on the angle between the slip plane and the cleavage plane. In addition, twinning is directional so a tension/compression asymmetry may be anticipated. These complications come about because lamellar TiAl is a polycrystal about which we have unusually full knowledge: The shapes, sizes and relative orientations of grains are all known exactly. A full calculation, using these variables and then averaging appropriately for the material as a whole, would be lengthy.

As a first attempt we consider only $\gamma$ material and only one lamella but with 5 different tensile axes corresponding to one set of published experiments [3]. $\tau_{0}$ values used are 40,60,80 MPa for perfect, twin and super dislocations. These stresses are in the ratios determined by Couret [13] by in situ microscopy at 300K. Twin dislocations are assumed to form pile ups in the same way as glide dislocations. $\ell$ is given the values $1.2 \mu \mathrm{m}$ in hard mode and $40 \mu \mathrm{m}$ in soft mode. $A=$ $0.15 \pm 0.05 \mathrm{MPa} \sqrt{\mathrm{m}}, \mathrm{B}=0.2 \mathrm{MPa} \sqrt{\mathrm{m}}$ in soft mode and $0.3 \mathrm{MPa} \sqrt{\mathrm{m}}$ in hard mode. With these parameters, the deformation system with the lowest yield stress for each tensile axis is determined and, using this system, values are obtained for $\sigma_{y}$ and $\sigma_{f}$. The calculations predict, in agreement with experiment, that lamellae in orientations B, C and D deform in soft mode while A and N are bound to deform in hard mode. It should be noted, however, that there is a difference between the hard modes of specimens A and N: In N the value of $\ell$ is definitely the lamellar thickness for all hard deformation systems while in A the dislocations on the hard systems have two slip distances, the lamellar thickness and the domain size. The calculation was simplified so that $\ell=1.2 \mu \mathrm{m}$ in both cases.

The fact that the Stroh and Hall-Petch equations (1) and (3) are alike and therefore that the orientation dependences of the yield and fracture stresses are similar, agrees with experiment.

The final experimental trend, that specimens A, B and C are ductile while D and N are brittle in tension, while all specimens are ductile in compression [14] is too subtle for the 'one lamella' model used here. The key parameter which decides whether $\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}$ is lower or higher than $\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}$ is $\mathrm{A}$ in eqn.(3). In the calculations $\mathrm{A}=0.15 \pm 0.05 \mathrm{MPa} \sqrt{\mathrm{m}}$, the error bar (Fig.3) being to indicate that $\mathrm{A}$ is not known accurately. In a fuller model A will be calculated, it depends on the character of the piled up dislocations and the relative orientations of the cleavage and slip planes. Two qualitative explanations for the brittleness of specimens D and N are:
(i) Comparing the two hard mode specimens: $\mathrm{N}$ has six equally stressed slip systems producing $60^{\circ}$ pile ups and a twin system producing $90^{\circ}$ pile ups while specimen A can deform by transmitting screws through the lamellar boundaries. The asymmetry in ductility might be explained in this way. The only brittle soft mode specimen, D, might be brittle because the $[1 \overline{1} 0](\overline{1} 11)$ hard mode system producing $60^{\circ}$ pile ups operates in places because of its high Schmid factor.
(ii) In specimen $\mathrm{N}$ alone, the $\alpha_{2}$ plates are forced to deform on pyramidal planes which require a very high yield stress, $750 \mathrm{MPa}$ which is higher than $\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}$ for this specimen.[14]. The specimen consequently cracks instead of deforming.

## CONCLUSIONS
1. The orientation dependence of the yield stress is caused partly by Schmid factor considerations and partly by the Hall Petch anisotropy. The two effects contribute roughly equally to the factor of 5 between the softest and hardest specimens.
2. The orientation dependence of the fracture stress is similar to that of the yield stress because the Hall Petch and Stroh equations are similar in form.
3. Lamellar TiAl is brittle when the tensile axis is nearly normal to the lamellar plane for two reasons: (i) because edge pile ups form in these specimens and (ii) because $\alpha_{2}$ plates are forced to deform on pyramidal planes.
