## Abstract
In many materials, including tin–lead eutectic solder, a substantial portion of their fatigue life is spent in accumulation of randomly distributed microcracks. An assembly of discrete interconnected elements (e.g. grains or phases) approximates the solid in this paper. Percolation theory is employed to derive critical microcrack density and to predict size effect. Self-similarity in microcrack nucleation leads to a simple power law for lifetime prediction. Its application to the fatigue of tin–lead eutectic solder shows a good agreement with experimental data. © 1999 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

**Keywords:** A. Microcracking; A. Percolation; B. Polycrystalline material; B. Cyclic loading

## 1. Introduction
This paper uses methods of statistical physics, micromechanics and applied mathematics to model damage governed by nucleation of microcracks such as occurs in eutectic tin–lead solder. This approach is based on experimental observations and the methodology of damage mechanics given in the book by

PII: S0022-5096(99)00014-9

Krajcinovic (1996). It derives from the understanding that the nature of such damage is stochastic due to the level of randomness of both defect geometry and material microstructure.

Under cyclic loading the last stage before final rupture is enlargement of one or several major macrocracks in the material. If the exact locations and sizes of these macrocracks are known, then the material behavior can be modeled with the help of fracture mechanics and finite element methods knowing the fatigue crack growth kinetics vs. $\Delta K$ or $\Delta J$. This approach is useful if the material already has macroscopic flaws or cracks; however, when the damage evolution is dominated by the nucleation of new microcracks rather than expansion of the existing ones, the application of percolation theory to an array of cracks gives an estimation of the critical microcrack density. This leads to a microstructural based model for a failure criterion and for failure prediction, which is presented here.

The model is well suited for application to 63Sn–37Pb eutectic solder, since the experimental data, as will be explained later, show that evolution of damage during a substantial portion of its fatigue life is governed by microcrack nucleation with the rate of nucleation being almost constant (e.g. see Cutiongco, 1991 for detailed experimental results). The observed microcracks originate at interfaces between phases and between grains and remain microcracks because of microstructural obstacles to growth. The salient trends in the experimental data can be determined despite the natural scatter, and the model predictions of these trends are quite good.

## 2. Nucleation dominated damage and estimation of its termination

An often used measure of fatigue damage in strain controlled experiments is the decline in peak stress. A typical curve showing the evolution of peak stress with cycling for 63Sn–37Pb solder has three distinct regions representing different stages of fatigue (Fig. 1). The first stage is the shortest, usually no more than 100 cycles, and is characterized by an initial sharp drop in peak stress followed by continuous reduction at a declining rate. The second stage has the longest

![](./images/811039780132356097_2.jpg)

Fig. 1. Evolution of damage in Sn–Pb eutectic solder.

duration and is typified in this solder by almost constant rate of decline; that is, this region is represented by a nearly straight line. It is noted that a long steady state stage in fatigue damage evolution has been observed in other materials as well, e.g. in 1018 steel (Erber et al., 1997). Finally, the third stage shows the accelerated decline in peak stress leading to ultimate failure. Similar to creep, these three stages can be called transient, steady state and tertiary fatigue. The initial rapid drop in peak stress is due to microstructural changes. The microstructure, except for microcracking, is in steady state after the initial drop.

Steady state creep is usually explained on the basis of a balance between the competing processes of strain hardening and recovery. Steady state fatigue in eutectic tin-lead solder, on the contrary, cannot be attributable to a balance between hardening and microcrack softening, since no isotropic hardening was observed in experiments, and kinematic hardening should be excluded due to the symmetry between loading and unloading passes. An absence of isotropic hardening in a near eutectic solder (61Sn-39Pb) was also reported by McDowell et al. (1994), where this phenomenon is explained in Section 4.

Microcrack nucleation and growth are the two basic modes governing fatigue damage evolution processes in solids. A constant rate of peak stress decline during steady state fatigue excludes microcrack growth as the main source of damage evolution. Otherwise the rate would increase with crack length following a Paris-like relation. Cutiongco (1991) observed microcracks in criss-crossing shear bands on the surface of tested 63Sn-37Pb solder. The microcracks were located between Sn-Sn grain boundaries and Sn-Pb phase boundaries. The network of microcracks eventually link-up to form macrocracks. This physical evidence allows us to make an assumption that microcrack growth in 63Sn-37Pb solder is limited to the area of grain facets, and damage evolution during steady state fatigue is dominated by microcrack nucleation.

The microstructure of a polycrystalline material can be considered as an assembly of discrete interconnected elements, such as grains or phases. In the lattice approximation of such an assembly (Fig. 2) each grain or phase is replaced by a node connected to its neighbor by a link. If the majority of microcracks is in interfaces, then the presence or absence of a link will represent either an intact or cracked grain or interphase boundary. The geometry of such a lattice of links depends on the particular microstructure. Percolation theory studies the effects of random disorder, i.e. in this instance missing links, in a system, such as a lattice, that itself can be either ordered or random. One of the most important concepts in percolation theory is the percolation threshold or transition. In the lattice approximation of a solid the percolation threshold occurs when an infinite array of missing links appears. For a solid saturated with microcracks it corresponds to the situation where the cluster of linked microcracks traversing it forms a bore-like hole. This is called conductivity percolation and Krajcinovic (1996) pointed out its difference with elastic percolation in a three-dimensional (3-D) solid, i.e. a solid which is characterized by the loss of rigidity or fracture of a body into two or more fragments.

The use of percolation theory is justified when microcrack nucleation at the

![](./images/811039780132356097_3.jpg)

Fig. 2. Lattice approximation of a solid.

grain or interface boundaries is the main source of damage as observed in 63Sn–37Pb solder. Though the conductivity percolation does not imply an immediate structural failure, it is attractive to identify it with a transition in the damage evolution process to one that is macrogrowth dominated, since it indicates that microcrack density has reached the level where formation of one or several macrocracks has occurred. The presented of macrocracks is likely to change the governing damage mode from one dominated by microcrack nucleation to one dominated by macrocrack enlargement. Determination of the end of the nucleation governed stage is important, since it is a logical choice for a failure criterion in a safe design practice.

Formation of one or several major macrocracks has been observed during the experiments at the point marked as $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ in Fig. 1, when the curve changes its slope. Examples from experiments are given in Figs. 3–6, and they will be discussed in Section 5. An evolution of damage is governed by microcrack nucleation up to this point, so that change of slope in the damage evolution curve is used here as a failure criterion. This is a conservative criterion, since when macrocracks are formed a specimen may only have reached from 60–80% of its total life (which ends at the elastic percolation). The conductivity percolation in 3-D is defined by the formation of a bore-like hole through the specimen. It is also characterized by the formation of several large clusters of linked failures which are assumed to correspond to clusters of microcracks. If these large clusters of microcracks can be considered to be macrocracks, then it appears appropriate to link conductivity percolation with formation of macrocracks and, as a result, with the change in a slope of a damage evolution curve.

The shape of each microcrack depends on the particular grain or interphase boundary it lays along. For simplification it is assumed here that all microcracks

Thus, the overlapping (intersecting or permeating) of microcracks is unlikely and the critical density reported by Charlaix (1986) should only be used for comparison with the one derived for non-overlapping microcracks.

Charlaix (1986) has also argued that the value of $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ is quasi-invariant, i.e. almost independent of the microstructural details, similar to other invariants such as the critical number of bonds per site for bond percolation and critical volume for site percolation. If we assume that this invariance holds for non-overlapping microcracks as well, then the value of $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ determined for one particular microstructure can be used for any other microstructure.

Consider the microstructure as represented by a simple cubic lattice with edge length $L=\lambda l$, where $l$ is the link length and $\lambda$ is some large number (this calculation is similar to that of Krajcinovic, 1996, for a square lattice). The total number of links within this volume is $N=(z / 2) \lambda^{3}$, where $z=6$ is the coordination number for a simple cubic lattice. The number of failed (missing) links at percolation is $N_{\mathrm{f}}=p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}} N$, where the bond percolation threshold, $p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}$, is equal to the probability that a given link (bond) in the lattice will be broken at threshold. For a simple cubic finite lattice its value, $p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}=0.247$, is given by Zallen (1983). The total volume of the lattice is $V=(\lambda l)^{3}$, and the microcrack radius is $a=l / 2$. Then the critical density of non-overlapping circular microcracks is equal to

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=n a^{3}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{f}}}{V}\left(\frac{l}{2}\right)^{3}=\frac{p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}(z / 2) \lambda^{3}}{(\lambda l)^{3}}\left(\frac{l}{2}\right)^{3}=\frac{p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}} z}{16}=0.093. \tag{2.1}
$$

It is interesting that the value of $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ for non-overlapping cracks is half of the value of $\omega_{\mathrm{c}}$ for overlapping cracks.

Since the number of cycles to failure, as will be shown in Section 4, is proportional to the effective critical microcrack density, a solid with smaller $\lambda$ will last longer than one with larger $\lambda$ under identical loading conditions. This fact is of importance in applications where size of structural components is one of the major design criteria, e.g. microelectronics.

### 3. Effective properties of a damaged solid

To account for the presence of microcracks it is convenient to introduce a microcrack compliance tensor, $S_{i j k l}^{*}$, which gives an increase in elastic strain due to microcracks in a solid body under constant stress, $\sigma_{k l}$, i.e.

$$
\Delta\left(\varepsilon_{i j}-\varepsilon_{i j}^{i n}\right)=S_{i j k l}^{*} \sigma_{k l},
\tag{3.1}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{i j}$ and $\varepsilon_{i j}^{i n}$ are, respectively, total and inelastic strain tensors. The sum of an elastic compliance tensor of undamaged material, $S_{i j k l}$, and microcrack compliance tensor, $S_{i j k l}^{*}$, is called an effective compliance, $\bar{S}_{i j k l}$, i.e.

$$
\bar{S}_{i j k l}=S_{i j k l}+S_{i j k l}^{*}.
\tag{3.2}
$$

An effective stress tensor of the damaged material is given by

$$
\bar{\sigma}_{k l}=\bar{S}_{i j k l}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon_{i j}-\varepsilon_{i j}^{i n}\right)=\bar{S}_{i j k l}^{-1} S_{i j m n} \sigma_{m n}.
\tag{3.3}
$$

Under uniaxial loading the effective stress is given by

$$
\bar{\sigma}=\sigma \frac{\bar{E}}{E}
\tag{3.4}
$$

and in pure shear

$$
\bar{\tau}=\tau \frac{\bar{\mu}}{\mu},
\tag{3.5}
$$

where $\bar{E}$ and $\bar{\mu}$ are the effective elastic and shear moduli and $E, \mu$ are the elastic and shear moduli in the undamaged material.

The effective compliance can be estimated based on the assumed microcrack distribution and properties of the undamaged material. Cutiongco (1991) observed microcracks in criss-crossing shear bands, which occurred at approximately $30-60^{\circ}$ angles from the direction of applied uniaxial load on the surface of tested 63Sn–37Pb solder specimens. The shear bands had finite width, each one containing a particular number of grains or phases. The microcracks were location between Sn–Sn grain boundaries and Sn–Pb phase boundaries and their position appeared to be quite random, as can be seen from Fig. 2. An assumption of isotropy in microcrack distribution is made because the damage evolution is governed by random microcrack nucleation on interfaces that are not distributed in a definite pattern and because their growth is minimal and limited by microstructural obstacles as observed. This assumption is no longer valid when microcrack growth becomes more dominant than nucleation and individual microcracks begin to join forming macrocracks. At this point, a change of slope in the damage evolution curve in Fig. 1 is observed and taken as failure criterion. Evolution of damage during and after macrocrack formation is outside the scope of this paper.

Budiansky and O'Connell (1976) used the self-consistent model to derive expressions for the effective elastic and shear moduli as functions of microcrack density, $\omega$, for bodies containing random distribution of flat circular cracks as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{E} & =E\left[1-\frac{16\left(1-(\bar{v})^{2}\right)(10-3 \bar{v})}{45(2-\bar{v})} \omega\right], \\
\bar{\mu} & =\mu\left[1-\frac{32(1-\bar{v})(5-\bar{v})}{45(2-\bar{v})} \omega\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.6}
$$

where the effective Poisson's ratio, $\bar{v}$, can be found from

$$
\omega=\frac{45(v-\bar{v})(2-\bar{v})}{16\left(1-(\bar{v})^{2}\right)(10 v-\bar{v}(1+3 v))}.
\tag{3.7}
$$

Here $v$ is Poisson's ratio of the undamaged material and, for eutectic tin-lead solder, is equal to 0.37 (Cutiongco, 1991). It follows that for a large specimen with the critical microcrack density at the percolation threshold $\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{c}}=0.093$, the effective elastic and shear moduli are $\bar{E}=0.845 E$ and $\bar{\mu}=0.879 \mu$, and the effective Poisson's ratio is $\bar{v}=0.318$.

## 4. Microcrack nucleation process in fatigue and its self-similarity

When the applied stress is insufficient to nucleate a crack, due to thermal energy the probability that a fatigue crack will nucleate at a given site is $p_{\mathrm{n}} \propto \exp \left[-\left(Q_{\mathrm{n}}-V \tau\right) / R T\right]$, where $Q_{\mathrm{n}}$ is the activation energy for nucleation of a fatigue crack at a particular site along a grain or a phase boundary, $V$ is the activation volume, $\tau$ is the appropriate shear stress, $R$ is the universal gas constant and $T$ is the absolute temperature. The shear microstress $\tau$ can be decomposed into two components (Mura, 1994) $\tau^{\text {appl }}$ and $\tau^{\text {disl }}$, i.e. $\tau=\tau^{\text {appl }}+\tau^{\text {disl }}$, where $\tau^{\text {appl }}$ is caused by the loads applied along the boundaries of a solid and $\tau^{\text {disl }}$ is the dislocation stress. With cycling and accompanied plastic deformation the dislocation density will tend to grow if the homologous temperature is low, and, as a result, $\tau^{\text {disl }}$ and $p_{\mathrm{n}}$ will increase. This process is reduced by recovery; and the dislocation structure is changed by local rearrangement to a lower energy form. Some materials, such as tin-lead eutectic solder, exhibit only a moderate increase in dislocation density with plastic deformation. At high homologous temperatures, the intensity of recovery is high enough to keep the dislocation density and $\tau^{\text {disl }}$ at an almost constant level, and, as a result, a solid exhibits no isotropic hardening.

The state of a material in the given microvolume is not affected by the presence of a microcrack in the neighboring microvolume if the microstructure is stable except for microcracking and an average distance between nucleated microcracks is sufficiently large. These conditions are similar to the so-called 'loose bundle' model, when a material is approximated by a bundle of parallel springs or rods. It is assumed that the springs do not touch each other, and when one of them is cut or removed, the load produced by the bundle will drop, but the total strain and the stress in the intact springs remain unchanged. Then, as a result, $\tau^{\text {appl }}$, corresponding to the same applied strain, will not be changed from cycle to cycle

in strain controlled experiments. Thus, under these conditions it is reasonable to expect that for tin-lead eutectic solder both shear microstress $\tau=\tau^{\text {appl }}+\tau^{\text {disl }}$ and probability of fatigue crack nucleation, $p_{\mathrm{n}}$, are constant with cycling. It is assumed here, that until the microcrack density reaches its critical value given in (2.4), there is no strong correlation between nucleated microcracks and, for a tin-lead eutectic solder, $p_{\mathrm{n}}$ is constant.

The rate of growth of microcrack density per cycle, $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle }}=\mathrm{d} \omega / \mathrm{d} N$, depends on the probability that a crack will nucleate at a given site, $p_{\mathrm{n}}$, and an average number of sites available for nucleation during a particular cycle, $N_{\mathrm{s}}$. Since the total number of grain and interphase boundary segments is very large compared to the number of cracked boundary segments in the Sn-Pb eutectic solder, its decrease with cycling is insignificant. It is reasonable in this case to assume that $N_{\mathrm{s}} \approx$ const. Thus, if $p_{\mathrm{n}}$, for a given material, is constant as well, then $\mathrm{d} \omega /$ $\mathrm{d} N \approx$ const, and nucleation governed damage will exhibit an approximate steady state behavior as observed.

Mura (1994) has shown that the number of cycles needed for microcrack nucleation is a function of shear stress range per cycle along a slip plane, $\Delta \tau^{\text {cycle }}$. Thus, an increase in microcrack density per cycle, $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle }}$, is a function of $\Delta \tau^{\text {cycle }}$ as well, i.e. $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle }}=f\left(\Delta \tau^{\text {cycle }}\right)$. Since $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle }}$ is a dimensionless parameter, it is convenient to replace $\Delta \tau^{\text {cycle }}$ with dimensionless microstrain range, $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}=\Delta \tau^{\text {cycle }} / \mu$, where $\mu$ is the shear modulus. An increase in microcrack density per cycle is a function of shear strain range, i.e.

$$
\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle }}=g\left(\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}\right).\tag{4.1}
$$

Barenblatt and Botvina (1982) argued that microcrack nucleation governed damage process could be considered self-similar. Self-similarity means, that if we compare two microcrack density increments $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle1 }}$ and $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle2 }}$, corresponding to two different amplitudes of shear strain $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle1 }}$ and $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle2 }}$, then the ratio of $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle1 }}$ over $\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle2 }}$ is a function of the ratio of $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle1 }}$ over $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle2 }}$, i.e.

$$
\frac{\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle1 }}}{\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle2 }}}=g\left(\frac{\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle1 }}}{\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle2 }}}\right).\tag{4.2}
$$

It can be shown, similar to Barenblatt and Botvina (1982), that from (4.2) an increase in microcrack density per cycle is equal to

$$
\Delta \omega^{\text {cycle }}=\frac{\mathrm{d} \omega}{\mathrm{d} N}=\left(\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}\right)^{\chi},\tag{4.3}
$$

where $\chi$ is a material constant $N$ is the cycle number. If at $N=0$ the microcrack density $\omega=0$ and $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}$ is constant in every cycle, then the microcrack density for the cycle number $N$ is equal to

$$
\omega=\left(\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}\right)^{\chi} N.\tag{4.4}
$$

The value of $\omega$ can be substituted into (3.6) in order to find the effective properties of the damaged material in each cycle. The number of cycles to failure is given by

$$
N_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{c}}}{\left(\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}\right)^{\chi}},
\tag{4.5}
$$

where the critical microcrack density, $\bar{\omega}_{c}$, is given in (2.4). It needs to be pointed out, that $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ is not the cycle of the final structural failure, but instead, is the cycle where the damage evolution will be changed from microcrack nucleation to a macrocrack growth governed process. If the value of $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ can be experimentally determined with an acceptable accuracy, then the constant $\eta$ in (2.4) can be found by comparing results of two sets of experiments with identical $\Delta \gamma^{\text {cycle }}$ but different specimen lengths: the first set with non-dimensional specimen length $\lambda_{1}$ and the second one with non-dimensional specimen length $\lambda_{2}$. If $N_{\mathrm{f} 1}$ and $N_{\mathrm{f} 2}$ are the statistically average numbers of cycles to failure in the first and second sets, then it follows from (2.4) and (4.5) that

$$
\eta=0.093 \frac{N_{\mathrm{f} 2}-N_{\mathrm{f} 1}}{N_{\mathrm{f} 1} \lambda_{2}^{-1.14}-N_{\mathrm{f} 2} \lambda_{1}^{-1.14}}.
\tag{4.6}
$$

## 5. Fatigue of 63Sn-37Pb solder

Cutiongco (1991), who gave a detailed description of the specimen preparation and experiments parameters, collected most of the experimental data analyzed and modeled here. The specimens were dog-bone shaped, 6.4 mm thick with the gage section of $12 \times 12.4$ mm. Uniaxial total strain controlled fatigue tests were done in air on the MTS machine under tension-tension conditions, i.e. the strains were varied from zero to maximum values. Two types of tests were modeled: continuous tests, where the strain was immediately reversed after reaching its maximum or minimum values, and hold time tests, where after reaching its maximum or minimum values the strains were held constant for some time. The temperatures in the modeled tests were constant at 25, 50, 65, 80 or $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The strain range was from $0.3-1 \%$, the ramp time from $0.5-120 \mathrm{~s}$ and the hold time from $60-300 \mathrm{~s}$. All specimens were heat treated for 2 h and aged six to ten days prior to fatigue testing to give constant starting microstructure. Several experiments with shorter and longer aging times were also performed and modeled.

Peak stress decline curves, as the one shown in Fig. 1, can be normalized by dividing $\bar{\sigma}$ by $\sigma$, where $\sigma$ is a peak stress in the first stable cycle (beginning of steady state fatigue). For simplicity, it is assumed that material at the beginning of steady state fatigue has no microcracks. The ratio of $\bar{\sigma} / \sigma$ is a function of microcrack density, which can be seen from Eqs. (3.4), (3.6) and (3.7). Microcrack density itself is a function of an average shear strain range at a slip plane along a

grain or phase boundary per cycle, $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$, and the number of cycles, $N$. Thus, the ratio of $\bar{\sigma}/\sigma$ is also a function of $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$ and $N$.

There were five parameters that could have different values in the performed experiments: (1) strain range; (2) ramp time; (3) hold time; (4) temperature; and (5) initial microstructure (varied by aging time). It was reasonable to expect that $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$ would be affected by the strain range and hold time. In order to find its dependence on ramp time, temperature or initial microstructure, the experimental data for the ratio of $\bar{\sigma}/\sigma$ as a function of number of cycles, $N$, was analyzed when the strain range was constant and hold time was absent. It can be seen from Figs. 3-5 that, during nucleation governed damage evolution, the ratio of $\bar{\sigma}/\sigma$ is only slightly affected by ramp time, temperature or initial microstructure, i.e. $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$ is approximately constant with constant strain range $\Delta\varepsilon$. Based on that observation, it was assumed that for continuous fatigue loading $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}=k\Delta\varepsilon$, where $k$ is the proportionality constant.

When a continuous cycle is interrupted and an applied strain is held constant for some period of time, the microstructure will be modified by creep carried in part through grain boundary sliding. Thus, it is reasonable to expect a substantial increase in the value of shear strain, $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$, compared to the continuous fatigue tests with the same strain range. This increase is proportional to the amount of stress relaxation that takes place during hold and approaches asymptotically 90% of the $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$ in the experiments with no hold. It is noted that most of the experiments with hold time were performed at 0.6% strain range and further work is required to verify if 90% increase in $\Delta\gamma^{\text{cycle}}$ is applicable at different straining ranges.

Since all of the experiments were strain controlled, the peak stress was achieved at the same level of measured total strain in each cycle of an experiment. Making an assumption that an elastic strain at peak stress was not affected by the damage, we can use expression (3.4) together with (3.6) and (4.4) to find the peak stress, $\bar{\sigma}$, in the cycle number $N$

$$
\bar{\sigma}=\sigma\left[1-\frac{16(1-(\bar{v})^{2})(10-3 \bar{v})}{45(2-\bar{v})} A \Delta \varepsilon^{\chi} N\right],
\tag{5.1}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the peak stress in the first stable cycle, and $A=k^{\chi}$ is a constant independent of the test parameters. The effective Poisson's ratio, $\bar{v}$, can be found from (3.7), or its approximate value (if $v$=0.37) can be obtained from $\bar{v} \approx-0.61 \omega+0.37=-0.61 A \Delta \varepsilon^{\chi} N+0.37$.

Expression (5.1) was used to model fatigue in tin-lead eutectic solder as shown in Fig. 6. By fitting it to the experimental data it was determined that $\chi$=3.0 and $A$ was equal to 132.86 for continuous loading and to 911.29 for hold time experiments.

At the percolation threshold, that is assumed here to coincide with the final cycle of stable damage growth, expression in (3.6) gives $\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{f}}=0.845 \sigma$. The experimentally determined values of $\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}$ (Fig. 7) were distributed between $\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{f}}=$ $0.63 \sigma$ and $\bar{\sigma}=0.89 \sigma$. Despite the scatter of the experimental data, due to the

For relatively large specimens under continuous uniaxial cyclic loading the number of cycles to failure follows from (4.5) and is given by

$$
N_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{c}}}{A_{\mathrm{c}} \Delta \varepsilon^{\chi}} \approx \frac{0.093}{132.86 \Delta \varepsilon^{3}}=\frac{0.0007}{\Delta \varepsilon^{3}}. \tag{5.2}
$$

If the hold time is present, then

$$
N_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{c}}}{A_{\mathrm{h}} \Delta \varepsilon^{\chi}} \approx \frac{0.093}{911.29 \Delta \varepsilon^{3}}=\frac{0.0001}{\Delta \varepsilon^{3}}. \tag{5.3}
$$

Experimental data from continuous tests and the curve generated by (5.2) are plotted on Fig. 8, where the coefficient of determination $R^{2}=0.86$. The fit between experimental data and model, although not perfect, shows that the trend in the experimental data is reproduced well. This is remarkable due to the statistical aspects of the phenomenon. In all of the cases modeled here the lifetime estimations by the model were more conservative than the total number of cycles run during the experiments (the experiments were ended when the peak load dropped 50%), which proves that the proposed model is a good choice in a safe design practice.

## 6. Discussion

In the experiments modeled here damage evolution in 63Sn-37Pb solder shows a power law dependence on the range of the applied strain. The assumption of microcrack nucleation governed damage and its self-similarity appears appropriate. For the material investigated it appears that the roles of strain rate,

temperature or initial microstructure are marginal over the ranges examined. The lattice approximation of a solid used with its percolation threshold leading to critical microcrack density is useful to provide a conservative life-to-failure prediction.

Some of the experimental data collected by Cortez (1992) suggest that for the mixed-mode loading the maximum principal strain may be a good candidate for replacing uniaxial strain range in Eq. (5.2), but only through the testing and simulation of actual solder joints can both Eqs. (5.2) and (5.3) be modified for multiaxial loading conditions.

The link between percolation threshold and macrocrack initiation requires further investigation. Although leading to a simple physically based failure criterion, it neglects other effects, such as stress risers and environment. The resultant critical microcrack density gives approximately 15% load drop at macrocrack initiation in 63Sn-37Pb solder, which correlates fairly well with the presented experimental results. Cooper and Fine (1983) reported 25% reduction in load bearing cross-sectional area of fatigued iron plates. It will be of substantial interest to verify whether the conductivity percolation can be used to model a transition in damage evolution in materials other than 63Sn-37Pb solder.
