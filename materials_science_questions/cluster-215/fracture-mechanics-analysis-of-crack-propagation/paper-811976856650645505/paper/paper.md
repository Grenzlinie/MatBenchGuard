# Internal Hydrogen Supersaturation Produced by Dislocation Transport

H. H. JOHNSON AND J. P. HIRTH

A quantitative model is presented for the generation of internal supersaturations of hydrogen by kinetic rather than thermodynamic means. The maximum brittleness and slow strain rate limit is treated. The process invoked assumes hydrogen transport to deposition sites by dislocation drag, and reemission of the hydrogen by diffusion. The kinetic supersaturation is calculated from a balance between hydrogen arrival and departure rates, for internal and external hydrogen sources, and for smooth section and crack geometries. When applied to ferritic and austenitic steels, the model predicts that the kinetic supersaturations are extremely small in all realistic situations.

THE study of hydrogen embrittlement in steel is historically intertwined with the concept that brittleness is caused by hydrogen gas under pressure in voids. In this view hydrogen does not influence material properties; rather brittleness occurs because the elevated local stress fields associated with the high pressure hydrogen pockets allow a critical fracture event to occur at a much lower applied stress than in the absence of hydrogen.

Pressure models may be classified as either thermodynamic or kinetic, depending on the process whereby the pressure is presumed to develop. In thermodynamic models, which have a most extensive history, hydrogen gas precipitation and pressure build-up are just thermodynamic consequences of a lattice highly super-saturated with hydrogen. In the embrittlement range of temperatures, substantial pressures are required to equilibrate very small lattice concentrations, since the Sievert's law coefficient for hydrogen in iron is quite small. These concentrations may be easily produced by electrochemical or chemical charging at temperature or, since hydrogen is an endothermic solute in iron, by exposure to hydrogen at elevated temperatures and perhaps pressure, followed by rapid cooling.

Although the thermodynamics are straightforward, the concept that an associated pressure causes brittleness has been controversial. Under severe charging conditions irreversible deterioration of the structure may occur, as is shown in different alloys by the appearance of blisters and internal fissures, voids, and cracks. Although occasional experiments have indicated the presence of hydrogen gas under pressure in these macroscopic defects, especially blisters, there is little detailed understanding of the role of pressure in defect nucleation and growth.

Reversible embrittlement is observed under less severe charging conditions. The detailed investigations of Troiano¹ established that the behavior patterns of reversible and internal hydrogen embrittlement in high strength steels were not reconciliable with thermodynamic pressure models. Perhaps the most direct experimental evidence was the realization²,³ that hydrogen-induced crack propagation in high strength steels could occur in external hydrogen environments at pressures well below one atmosphere, which surely precludes a thermodynamic pressure mechanism. If the embrittlement site is ahead of the crack tip and in the bulk, then just as water does not spontaneously run uphill, neither should hydrogen from an external source build up a higher pressure in an internal void than the source pressure.

Kinetic models postulate hydrogen delivery to voids or other disjunctions by dislocation transport. With respect to internal hydrogen brittleness Bastien and Azou⁴,⁵ first suggested that moving dislocations might transport significant quantities of hydrogen, and that when the moving dislocations annihilated or intersected a void, substantial local hydrogen concentrations might be developed. This suggestion lay more or less dormant for many years, but has recently been revived⁶⁻⁹ as a result of new experimental evidence based upon tritium and autoradiographic techniques. It was shown that the onset of plastic deformation is accompanied by substantial tritium release,⁶ presumably because of dislocation transport of tritium to the surface. From an external hydrogen brittleness study it has also been reported⁷ that, under otherwise equivalent conditions, tritium penetrates much more deeply into stainless steel undergoing simultaneous plastic deformation than when the deformation is purely elastic. The interpretation is that hydrogen is transported by moving dislocations, and the speculation is that this hydrogen may precipitate at discontinuities, leading to substantial pressures for purely kinetic reasons. It is also probable that lattice hydrogen uptake is enhanced by hydrogen entry through the formation of clean surface steps upon plastic deformation.¹⁰,¹¹

Direct experimental evaluation of the kinetic pressure concept does not seem feasible, since there is no way of measuring the postulated internal pressures. The best that can be done at present is to develop quantitative models for the kinetic embrittlement

One such model is presented in this paper; it is based on general assumptions and contains parameters for which reasonable estimates of magnitude can be made. The model is applied to three cases which encompass both internal and external hydrogen brittleness, i) external hydrogen entering a smooth tensile specimen, ii) external hydrogen entering a crack tip region, and iii) internal hydrogen of uniform initial distribution.

## DESCRIPTION OF MODEL

The model envisions transport of hydrogen by dislocations, and release of the hydrogen, with a consequent local concentration increase, when dislocations annihilate or intercept a void. During plastic deformation hydrogen-laden dislocations will continually arrive and discharge at the deposition sites. The localized concentrations so produced will not grow indefinitely, since hydrogen will tend to diffuse away. A steady state will ensue in which the hydrogen arrival rate by dislocations will be balanced by hydrogen diffusion away from the deposition site. The supersaturation or void pressure which results from this kinetic model will be higher than the equilibrium pressure, and can be calculated by equating the arrival and departure rates.

The rate $I$ of hydrogen arrival via dislocation transport will be directly related to the macroscopic strain rate $\dot{\epsilon}$. This relation will be developed for the square, two-dimensional array of dislocation sources and annihilation sites depicted schematically in Fig. 1. This geometry is adopted both for simplicity of calculation and because it should give a near upper bound of the rate $I$. Use of a three dimensional geometry instead of the two dimensional one would only change the following results by a geometric factor of about $2 .{ }^{12,13}$ More importantly, in a more complicated model the sources and sinks would be offset, as in Weertman's creep model $^{14}$ or its grain boundary modification, ${ }^{15}$ with a viscous constraint to annihilation which would slow the rate $I$ relative to the present calculation. Hence the present upper bound model gives the most favorable case for the kinetic hydrogen supersaturation concept. For each dislocation pair annihilated the strain increment is $b / \lambda$, and for $n$ pairs $n b / \lambda$. The relation between strain rate $\dot{\epsilon}$ and the average dislocation pair annihilation rate $\dot{n}$ is then

$$
\dot{\epsilon}=\dot{n} b / \lambda. \tag{1}
$$

## External Hydrogen-Smooth Surface

The case where hydrogen enters a smooth surface undergoing plastic strain, as in many experiments with high pressure hydrogen or electrochemical charging, is depicted in Fig. 2. An equilibrium hydrogen concentration $c_{0}$ is established at the surface by the external environment. The hydrogen is transported into the interior by dislocations which originate at the surface. As the dislocations annihilate at $L$ in the interior, they deposit their absorbed hydrogen, creating a region of hydrogen supersaturation in otherwise perfect crystal. The region will be a small cylinder of radius $r_{0}$ of the order of dislocation core dimensions. If a void nucleated in this region it would both deplete the hydrogen, reducing the supersaturation, and act as a sink for hydrogen. Hence, again

to calculate the upper bound for supersaturation effects, it is assumed that no void forms but that hydrogen is continuously transported into the cylindrical sink by dislocations and deposited there upon dislocation annihilation. If a void were to form, however, the instantaneous pressure in the void would be that in local equilibrium with the maximum supersaturation established in the above manner. The latter conclusion would be independent of the void shape, so again the two dimensional model is not restrictive.

If the sink for dislocation-transported atomic hydrogen is a void, then recombination to the molecular state would be expected upon discharge. Hydrogen emission and diffusion away from the void must then be preceded by molecular dissociation. It is assumed here that both the recombination and dissociation rates are sufficiently high that local equilibrium is established, and it is believed that this is the most probable situation.

There are, nevertheless, other possibilities. If the dissociation reaction were selectively impeded, then partial interface control of the departure process would obtain, and the pressure in the void would be higher than estimated here. Alternatively, the recombination process might be poisoned, and it has been suggested¹⁶ that this could occur at grain boundaries where Group V elements, typical poisons, tend to segregate. In this instance the pressure would be lower than calculated here.

It should be noted that dislocation annihilation is a requisite for the establishment of a significant local supersaturation. If dislocations merely intersected a void they should not enhance the supersaturation there to an appreciable degree. Firstly, most evidence suggests that dislocations act as trapping lines and hinder the diffusive motion of hydrogen, so favorable pipe diffusion along the dislocation would not be expected. Secondly, since the hydrogen adsorbed at the dislocations is in local equilibrium with the region in which the dislocation motion originated there will be little or no chemical potential difference between the dislocation and the void or the bulk region adjacent thereto even though a large concentration difference exists. The maximum chemical potential gradient would be established by the difference between the chemical potential at the free surface and that at the current site of the dislocation. Only when the dislocations annihilate is there a change in the chemical potential of the hydrogen which would drive a diffusive flow.

The hydrogen thus deposited must be expected to reenter the bulk and diffuse away from the discontinuity. In the steady state the rate of hydrogen arrival via dislocation transport will just equal the rate of hydrogen departure via diffusion. The bulk concentration $c_1$ at the discontinuity will exceed the surface concentration $c_0$ for kinetic reasons; assuming local equilibrium at the discontinuity one can calculate the supersaturation there as well as the local equilibrium hydrogen pressure for any void found at the discontinuity.

In modeling the process as a steady state problem discrete annihilation events are in effect smoothed to an average rate via Eq. [1]. This is equivalent to the replacement of discrete slip dislocations by a continuous distribution of infinitesimal dislocations.

In this continuum approximation the basic diffusion laws guarantee that the steady state supersaturation will be a maximum relative to any transient effects. However, the instantaneous supersaturation which will occur because of discrete annihilation events may well be larger than estimated by this continuum model. Present experimental knowledge is not sufficient to allow realistic modeling of the instantaneous supersaturation. However, all other approximations employed in this analysis, as will be seen in the following, are deliberately conservative, and should compensate for any underestimate caused by the continuum approximation.

The amount of hydrogen deposited in the discontinuity per unit time, $I$, is given by

$$
I=\dot{m} / a \tag{2}
$$

where
$a$ = atom spacing along dislocation and
$\eta$ = number of H atoms/atom site along dislocation.

From the diffusion point of view, the discontinuity acts as a line source of hydrogen which emits $I$ atoms of hydrogen per unit time into the surrounding material with the planar surface acting as the diffusion sink. There is an exact analogy to a corresponding problem in heat flow which is discussed in detail by Carslaw and Jaeger.¹⁷ The solution to the diffusion problem is

$$
\frac{c_{1}-c_{0}}{I}=\frac{1}{2 \pi D} \cosh ^{-1} \frac{L}{r_{0}} \tag{3}
$$

and it may be noted that $\cosh^{-1}(L/r_0)$ is a slowly increasing function of the argument $L/r_0$. Since $L/r_0$ will be very large in all cases of interest, Eq. [3] may be written in its asymptotic form as

$$
\frac{c_{1}-c_{0}}{I}=\frac{1}{2 \pi D} \ln \frac{2 L}{r_{0}} \cdot \tag{4}
$$

The final form in terms of measurable or estimable parameters is obtained by substitution of Eqs. [1] and [2] into [4], and the maximum supersaturation $S$ then becomes

$$
S=\frac{c_{1}}{c_{0}}-1=\frac{\lambda \dot{\epsilon} \eta}{2 \pi a b D c_{0}} \ln \frac{2 L}{r_{0}} \cdot \tag{5}
$$

Before proceeding to numerical estimates, it should be noted that Eq. [5] predicts that the supersaturation is directly proportional to the strain rate. This will be true for any kinetic supersaturation model, continuum or discrete, which treats $\eta$ as a constant. However, it is well documented that hydrogen embrittlement increases as the strain rate decreases, suggesting on quite general grounds that hydrogen embrittlement and kinetic supersaturation are not related.

To obtain numerical estimates the following parameters are taken for iron.

$\eta$ = 2: much larger estimates of $\eta$ are sometimes obtained by calculation but experimental results from internal friction and permeation measurements¹⁹,²⁰ suggests 2 as an upper limit and recent computations in the Fermi-Dirac model of the adsorption field of hydrogen at dislocations support this value,²¹

$L = \lambda = 10^{-7}$ m, typical of the cell structure size in iron, $^{22}$

$ab = 5.81 \times 10^{-20}$ m² for a (110) plane in Fe,

$D = 10^{-9}$ m²/s for H in annealed Fe at $300$ K, $^{20}$

$c_0 = 3.4 \times 10^{21}$ atoms H/m³ Fe-0.1 MN/m² external pressure at $300$ K, $^{23}$

$r_0 = 10^{-9}$ m,

$\dot{\epsilon} = 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹, typical for tensile tests involving hydrogen embrittlement.

For these values the calculated supersaturation $S$ is approximately $8.5 \times 10^{-6}$, which of course means that there is no kinetic pressure build-up in the cavity. This conclusion will remain unchanged for virtually any set of values of $L$ and $r_0$, because of the slowly varying nature of the $\ln(2L/r_0)$ function. A significant value of $S$ would require the other estimated parameters to be in error by many orders of magnitude, which seems wholly improbable. The only parameter in the above analysis which could be larger is the strain rate which could be as large as $10^{-3}$ s⁻¹ for the type of study in question here, but this would only give $S = 8.5 \times 10^{-4}$.

With regard to the permeability product $Dc_0$, values for cold worked iron or for steels with high densities of various defects can be lower by a factor of as much as $10^3$. However, this effect arises from hydrogen trapping which would be competitive with the process considered here, so it would be inconsistent to use these lower values. Thus, it may be concluded that a kinetic pressure mechanism is a most unlikely explanation for hydrogen embrittlement observed upon mechanical straining of iron in an external hydrogen environment.

The picture just given is valid in the low strain rate and maximum embrittlement limit. To incorporate the well known effect of strain rate on hydrogen embrittlement into the analysis would require introduction of a model for the dependence of $\eta$ on strain rate. This does not seem necessary at the present time.

Austenitic stainless steel exposed to high pressure hydrogen, at say $10$ MN/m², is a second example. The factors which differ appreciably from those for iron are those in the product $Dc_0$, for which a reasonable value is $^{24}$ $2 \times 10^{10}$ atoms/m-s in contrast to the value $3.4 \times 10^{12}$ atoms/m-s for iron. With $ab$ now $5.77 \times 10^{-20}$ m² for a (111) plane and the other factors the same as in the iron case, the calculated value of $S$ is $1.4 \times 10^{-3}$. Hence, for this case the kinetic pressure mechanism is also improbable.

### External Hydrogen-Crack Growth

The analysis may also be adapted to crack growth experiments with an external hydrogen environment, which is a common experimental approach for high strength steels. If the crack is infinitely sharp, then the analysis summarized in Appendix 1 shows that the concentration difference between the discontinuity and the crack tip surface is

$$
\frac{c_1 - c_0}{I} = \frac{1}{2\pi D} \ln \frac{4L}{r_0}, \tag{6}
$$

where the discontinuity of radius $r_0$ is located a distance $L$ ahead of the crack tip. The only effect of the change in geometry from a smooth surface to a crack is to double the argument of the ln function. Cracks with a flank angle other than zero could be treated by an extension of the analysis in Appendix 1 but the results would be intermediate between the extreme cases of a smooth surface and zero flank angle. The supersaturation for zero flank angle is then

$$
S = \frac{\lambda \dot{\epsilon} \eta}{2\pi ab D c_0} \ln \frac{4L}{r_0}. \tag{7}
$$

For the propagating crack the strain rate at the crack tip will be much larger than the average or macroscopic strain rate, although the other parameters in Eq. [7] may be treated as in the previous example. The calculation of the exact strain rate at the tip of a crack is an unsolved problem of fracture micromechanics. However, for the usual case of some plastic flow at the crack tip the strain rate can be roughly estimated on the basis of work by Rice and Johnson $^{25}$ and Hahn and Rosenfield. $^{26}$ A ligament is imagined of length $2R$, where $R$ is the plastic zone size, and width $\delta a$, positioned perpendicular to the crack plane and about to be penetrated by the crack. The ligament will undergo a displacement $U \cong 2\rho$ where $\rho$ is the radius of curvature of the crack tip. The crack velocity is $V = \delta a/\delta t$, and the strain of the ligament is $U/2R$, so

$$
\dot{\epsilon} \simeq \frac{U}{2R} \frac{v}{\delta a} \simeq \frac{v}{2R} \tag{8}
$$

where $v$ may be determined experimentally and $R$ estimated from standard formulae. The final equation for the supersaturation is then

$$
S = \frac{\lambda \eta v}{4\pi ab D c_0 R} \ln \frac{4L}{r_0}. \tag{9}
$$

It is of interest that the crack depth does not appear explicitly. This is because the analysis assumes that the void position is very close to the crack tip, *i.e.*, $L$ is much less than the crack depth. The void then receives hydrogen only from the crack tip vicinity and the length of crack becomes irrelevant. With the same parameters as before for iron, a typical value $^{2,3}$ of the crack velocity of $10^{-5}$ m/s, and a plastic zone size of $5 \times 10^{-5}$ m computed for a stress intensity factor of $21$ (MN/m²) m¹/² with a yield strength of $1.3 \times 10^{3}$ MN/m², the supersaturation becomes

$$
S \simeq 10^{-1}, \tag{10}
$$

which again implies a negligible pressure build-up for kinetic reasons. The parameter of greatest uncertainty is probably the strain rate, but the estimate given here, $10^{-1}$ s⁻¹, seems quite generous for slow crack growth.

Since slow and hydrogen-induced crack growth has not been established in austenitic stainless steels, values of crack velocity $v$ are not available, and Eq. [9] cannot be applied to these alloys. This implies that any crack growth rates eventually measured for austenitic stainlesses will be much smaller than the value given previously for high strength steels, which would in turn imply even smaller supersaturations for the austenitic stainlesses.

## Internal Hydrogen-Homogeneous Initial Distribution

An estimate may also be developed for the situation where the hydrogen is initially homogeneously distributed in the bulk phase as a result of thermal or electrochemical charging. During plastic deformation each moving dislocation will carry an equilibrium atmosphere of hydrogen. Each cylindrical discontinuity may then be considered as receiving hydrogen from dislocations and reemitting hydrogen in a two-dimensional diffusion field. Suppose, Fig. 1, that the sinks are lines (the dislocation sources in the figure) arranged in a square grid of spacing $L$ around the source. There is then an exact electrostatic analog of the diffusion problem. $^{27}$ The result in this case is

$$
S=\frac{\lambda \dot{\epsilon} \eta}{2 \pi a b D c_{0}} \ln \frac{L}{r_{0}}. \tag{11}
$$

From the numerical examples given earlier, it is clear that the supersaturation will also be negligible in this instance, about the same as for the surface source calculations.

## SUMMARY

To explore the often suggested relation between dislocation-transported hydrogen and hydrogen embrittlement, a continuum model has been developed to estimate the local supersaturation created when hydrogen is transported by dislocations and deposited at sinks. The kinetic supersaturation is found to be directly proportional to the strain rate, a result difficult to reconcile with the known inverse dependence of hydrogen embrittlement upon strain rate.

For ferritic and austenitic steels numerical estimates of the kinetic supersaturation were developed for internal and external hydrogen sources, and for smooth and crack geometrics. The kinetic supersaturation was small in all cases, suggesting that hydrogen embrittlement in these materials is not related to kinetic supersaturation.

## APPENDIX I

The diffusion solution for the crack geometry may be determined from the corresponding solution for a smooth surface, Eq. [3], by a Schwarz-Christoffel transformation as outlined in Fig. 3. The complex $z$ plane represents the crack geometry, a crack of depth a and zero flank angle, while the complex w plane depicts the smooth surface geometry. The Schwarz-Christoffel mapping function is

$$
z=\left(w^{2}-a^{2}\right)^{1 / 2}, \quad w=\left(z^{2}+a^{2}\right)^{1 / 2} \tag{12}
$$

A small circle of radius $\delta$ located a distance $p$ from the smooth surface, will map into a circle of radius $\xi$ at a distance $d$ ahead of the crack top provided only that

$$
\xi \ll a+p, \text { and } \delta \ll d \tag{13}
$$

Both inequalities are physically reasonable. Further relations between the voids in the two complex planes are

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \xi=\frac{p \delta}{\left(a^{2}+p^{2}\right)^{1 / 2}} \simeq \frac{p \delta}{-a}, \quad(a+d)^{2}=a^{2}+p^{2}, \\
& p \simeq(2 a d)^{1 / 2}
\end{aligned} \tag{14}
$$

The diffusion resistance in the $z$ crack geometry is then obtained by transformation of the parameters in Eq. [5], yielding

$$
\frac{c_{1}-c_{0}}{I}=\frac{1}{2 \pi d} \ln \frac{4 d}{\xi} \tag{15}
$$

which, with a change in notation, is just Eq. [6]. The above Eq. [15] may also be verified by direct calculation.
