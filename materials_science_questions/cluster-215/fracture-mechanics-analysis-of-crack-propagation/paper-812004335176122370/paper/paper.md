# The Theory of Brittle Fracture Initiation under Triaxial Stress Conditions—I

S. A. F. Murrell and P. J. Digby

(Received 1969 December 15)

## Summary
A calculation has been carried out of the conditions under which crack propagation is initiated in an isotropic, brittle solid of the Griffith type when the solid is subjected to a homogeneous, uniform but otherwise arbitrary triaxial system of stresses at infinity. The cracks have been taken to be flattened ellipsoidal cavities. The results are in broad agree- ment with results obtained in the two-dimensional treatment of the problem, and with experimental observations. Tensile and shear fracture is possible and the Mohr envelope is of parabolic form. Fracture initia- tion is shown to be independent of the intermediate principal stress.

## 1. Introduction
We are studying the fracture of rocks and the phenomena of faulting (and the associated earthquakes) in the crust and mantle of the Earth. For this reason we are interested in the problem of brittle fracture under triaxial stress conditions.

The foundation of the theory of brittle fracture is due to Griffith (Griffith 1920), according to whom brittle solids inherently contain flaws which weaken them; the flaws having the form of microscopic, flat shaped and very sharp cracks at whose edges stresses, strains and strain energy are highly concentrated. Brittle fracture is characterized by small, mainly elastic, deformations prior to fracture and by rela- tively smooth tensile fracture surfaces (for example, conchoidal or cleavage fractures) showing evidence of only a relatively small proportion of localized inelastic deforma- tion. It is therefore legitimate to develop the theory of brittle fracture in the first approximation on the assumption that all strains are elastic up to the point of fracture. Griffith did this and so do we in the present paper.

The nature of real Griffith cracks is still not well understood. There are two problems involved here: one is how the cracks originate, and the other is how they remain open. Murrell (1964a) has discussed these questions previously in some detail, and they will not therefore be discussed in the present paper. We proceed from his conclusion that the Griffith model of a crack is the most satisfactory one available at present, both mathematically and physically.

According to Griffith's theory, brittle fracture occurs when the magnified tensile stress close to the ends of the major axis of a crack reaches a value sufficient to rupture atomic bonds there in tension (that is, a value equal to the cohesive strength

of the body;* Kelly, Tyson & Cottrell (1967) have also considered the problem of rupture of atomic bonds by shear, to form lattice dislocations). The solid is assumed, as usual in calculations of this type, to be linearly elastic up to its rupture stress. Griffith (1924) and Murrell (1964a, b) found that according to the Griffith model the strength of brittle solids (that is, the maximum stress difference that the solid will withstand) depends markedly on the mean applied stress (that is, the first stress invariant or what Hill 1950 calls the hydrostatic component of stress). In the two- dimensional or biaxial case (plane stress or strain) it is found that:

$$
(P-R)=\left(8 K^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}[P+R]^{\frac{1}{2}} \tag{1}
$$

where $P$ and $R$ are the major and minor principal stresses respectively, and $K'$ is the uniaxial tensile strength (we take tensile stresses to be positive). In terms of the shear stress $(\tau)$ and the normal stress $(\sigma)$ acting on the plane containing the major axis of the crack which nucleates the fracture (which is approximately the same as the fracture surface) equation (1) can be written:

$$
\tau=2 K^{\prime \frac{1}{2}}\left[K^{\prime}-\sigma\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}. \tag{2}
$$

This is broadly in accordance with experimental observations on brittle solids (see Murrell 1966), although it is found experimentally that the relationship between $\tau$ and $\sigma$ is not quite a quadratic one, but rather, for $-\sigma \gg K'$, of the form:

$$
\tau \propto(-\sigma)^{n} \tag{3}
$$

where $n \simeq 0.6$ (see Murrell 1965; and also Hoek 1968).

However, equation (1) is based on a two-dimensional model of the fracture process (that is, on plane elasticity theory) and is not therefore capable of being directly tested experimentally. In particular the two-dimensional theory can provide no information about the influence of the intermediate principal stress on fracture or about the orientation of a fracture surface in a triaxial stress field. It is therefore of considerable interest to develop a three-dimensional model, and that is the purpose of the present paper. To do this we make use of results obtained by Eshelby (1957), who has determined the elastic field of an ellipsoidal inclusion. In our case the inclusion is taken in Part I of the paper as a traction-free ellipsoidal cavity. Results are obtained in a second paper (Part II) for cracks containing a fluid under pressure and for cracks which are closed and subject to frictional surface tractions. The minimum energy criterion for crack propagation is considered in a third paper.

## 2. Model of a brittle solid

We consider bodies consisting of an isotropic, homogeneous, elastic matrix (denoted $M$) which is uniformly filled with a large number of randomly oriented but otherwise identical ellipsoidal cavities (with principal axes $a, b, c$ where $a>b>c$). The cavities are spaced sufficiently far apart to ensure that interaction of their elastic fields is negligible. The calculation of the stress required to initiate the propagation of a crack and hence the fracture of the body is then carried out in two stages:

Stage I. We calculate the maximum tensile stress in the surface of a particular cavity whose orientation is fixed with respect to the principal stress axes at sufficiently great (infinite) distances from the cavity;

* It has been pointed out to us that the cohesive strength will in fact be stress dependent since it depends on the overall atomic structure of the solid, which will be distorted by all three principal stresses. This factor is ignored in the present work, but it may well merit further study. However, we believe that this effect is likely to be small, since the analogous effect in crystal plasticity, that is the effect of hydrostatic pressure on the critical resolved shear stress, is a second-order one (see Haasen & Lawson 1958, and Paterson 1967).

Stage II. We then calculate the orientation with respect to the principal stress axes at infinity of the cavity for which the maximum tensile stress, obtained in Stage I above, itself reaches a maximum value, and set the maximum tensile stress thus obtained equal to the atomic cohesive strength of the matrix (denoted $T$).

### 3. Elastic field of an ellipsoidal cavity

We follow, as far as possible, the notation, as well as the method of Eshelby (1957) for ease of reference. In particular suffix notation is used, in which suffixes take the values 1, 2, 3 and repeated suffixes denote summation over the three values.

The components of position and displacement vectors are written $x_i$ and $u_i$ respectively, and those of stress and strain tensors are written $p_{ij}$ and $e_{ij}$, with

$$
e_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i}) \tag{4}
$$

$$
p_{ij}=\lambda e_{mm}\delta_{ij}+2\mu e_{ij} \tag{5}
$$

for an isotropic medium with Lamé elastic constants $\lambda$ and $\mu$. The $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta function.

It is convenient to divide a second-order tensor $f_{ij}$ into a scalar part (written $f=f_{mm}$) and a deviatoric part (written $'f_{ij}$), where

$$
'f_{ij}=f_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}f\delta_{ij}.
$$

Thus, equation (5) may be written

$$
p=3\kappa e
$$

$$
'p_{ij}=2\mu 'e_{ij}
$$

where $\kappa=\lambda+2\mu/3$.

In the present work it was found convenient to use two different systems of Cartesian co-ordinate axes, of variable orientation, in connection with the different phases of the calculation.

In the first phase we define a right-handed set of orthogonal unit vectors $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3)$ on the surface of any given cavity, with $\alpha_1$ normal to the surface. Our $\alpha_1$ is therefore equivalent to the vector $\mathbf{n}$ used by Eshelby (1957). Components of vectors and tensors referred to the corresponding axes of a given set of $\alpha_i$ vectors are distinguished by a bracketed $\alpha$, as for example $p(\alpha)_{ij}$.

In the second phase we consider cavities of any (variable) orientation with respect to the applied principal stress directions at a sufficiently great distance from any cavity ('at infinity'; the corresponding principal stress are denoted by $K_1$, $K_2$, and $K_3$ with $K_1>K_2>K_3$), and we therefore make use of a co-ordinate system coinciding with the principal axes of a given ellipsoidal cavity. The corresponding right-handed set of orthogonal unit vectors directed along the principal axes $a$, $b$ or $c$ of a given cavity are denoted $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ and $\theta_3$ respectively. Components of vectors and tensors referred to this co-ordinate system have no distinguishing bracket.

$S$ denotes the ellipsoidal surface separating the matrix $M$ and the cavity (or the 'inclusion' or 'inhomogeneity' in Eshelby's work).

To perform the first stage of the calculation an expression is required for the elastic field of an ellipsoidal cavity in a stressed solid. The elastic field of an ellipsoidal cavity is just a particular case of the Inclusion, Inhomogeneity and related problems considered in the work of Eshelby (1957) who considered the following two basic problems.

#### (A) The elastic field of a transformed inclusion

An arbitrarily shaped region (the 'Inclusion') in the matrix $M$ undergoes a prescribed change of size and shape which in the absence of $M$ would be an arbitrary

homogeneous strain $e_{ij}{}^T$ say (called by Eshelby the 'stress-free' or 'transformation' strain). It is required to find the elastic field produced in $M$ and in the inclusion when the latter is embedded in, and is therefore constrained by, $M$. The components of displacement, strain, and stress of this field are distinguished by an affix $c$ ($u_{ij}{}^C$, $e_{ij}{}^C$, $p_{ij}{}^C$) in this case.

In the first instance the inclusion is taken to have the same elastic constants as the matrix. Eshelby established a general relationship between the constrained strains $e_{ij}{}^C$ at closely adjacent points inside and outside the interface $(S)$ between inclusion and matrix and the transformation strains $e_{ij}{}^T$. In the case of an ellipsoidal inclusion Eshelby found explicit expressions for the coefficients $S_{ilmn}$ relating the constrained and the stress-free (or 'transformation') strains in the inclusion. We have

$$
e_{il}{}^C \text{ (in)} = S_{ilmn} e_{mn}{}^T \tag{6}
$$

where the coefficients $S_{ilmn}$ are functions of the geometry of the ellipsoid and the Poisson's ration of the inclusion material. From these coefficients and the prescribed $e_{ij}{}^T$ it is possible to find the strains ($e_{ij}{}^C$ (in)) just inside and just outside ($e_{ij}{}^C$ (out)) an inclusion.

The stress in the matrix can be found from $e_{ij}{}^C$ (out) by using equation (5). On the other hand part of the $e_{ij}{}^C$ (in) is due to the (stress-free) transformation, so in this case we have

$$
p_{ij} \text{ (in)} = \lambda [e_{mm}{}^C \text{ (in)}-e_{mm}{}^T] \delta_{ij} + 2\mu [e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (in)}-e_{ij}{}^T]. \tag{7}
$$

If the strain field $e_{ij}{}^A$, which would be uniform in the absence of perturbation by the inclusions present, is now applied the strain and stress inside our inclusion with arbitrary (but homogeneous) $e_{ij}{}^T$ are respectively:

$$
e_{ij} \text{ (in)} = e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (in)} + e_{ij}{}^A \tag{8}
$$

and

$$
p_{ij} \text{ (in)} = \lambda [e_{mm}{}^C \text{ (in)}+e_{mm}{}^A - e_{mm}{}^T] \delta_{ij} + 2\mu [e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (in)}+e_{ij}{}^A - e_{ij}{}^T]. \tag{9}
$$

On the other hand the strain and stress in the matrix are respectively:

$$
e_{ij} \text{ (out)} = e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (out)} + e_{ij}{}^A \tag{10}
$$

and

$$
p_{ij} \text{ (out)} = \lambda [e_{mm}{}^C \text{ (out)}+e_{mm}{}^A] \delta_{ij} + 2\mu [e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (out)}+e_{ij}{}^A]. \tag{11}
$$

### (B) The elastic field of an ellipsoidal inhomogeneity
An ellipsoidal inhomogeneity has elastic constants $\lambda_1$, $\mu_1$, $\kappa_1$ which differ from those of the matrix (which are $\lambda$, $\mu$, $\kappa$). The body containing the inhomogeneity is subjected to a strain field $e_{ij}{}^A$ which would be uniform in the absence of perturbation by the inhomogeneity.

Eshelby solved the problem of finding the elastic field of such an inhomogeneity by replacing the inhomogeneity by an equivalent transformed inclusion (remembering that the inclusion in problem A above had the same elastic constants as the matrix). Equivalence is established by requiring that the displacements and tractions at the interface $S$ are the same in the two cases, and that continuity is maintained across $S$. The strains due to the mutual constraint of inhomogeneity and matrix, denoted by $e_{ij}{}^C$, can be found in terms of the uniform applied strain field, the elastic constants, and the geometry of the inhomogeneity.

In the present case we have:

$$
\begin{align}
e \text{ (in)} = e^C \text{ (in)} + e^A, \quad 'e_{ij} \text{ (in)} = 'e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (in)} + 'e_{ij}{}^A \tag{12}
\end{align}
$$

$$
\begin{align}
p \text{ (in)} = 3\kappa_1 [e^C \text{ (in)} + e^A], \quad 'p_{ij} \text{ (in)} = 2\mu_1 ['e_{ij}{}^C \text{ (in)} + e_{ij}{}^A]. \tag{13}
\end{align}
$$

The equations from which the $e_{ij}{}^C$ (in) can be determined are:
$$\kappa_{1}\left[e^{C}(\text{in})+e^{A}\right]=\kappa\left[e^{C}(\text{in})+e^{A}-e^{T}\right]\tag{14}$$
and
$$\mu_{1}\left[{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{C}(\text{in})+{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{A}\right]=\mu\left[{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{C}(\text{in})+{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{A}-{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{T}\right]\tag{15}$$
where the $e_{ij}{}^T$ are related to the $e_{ij}{}^C$ (in) by equation (6). In this way we find that the stress components just outside the inhomogeneity, relative to $a, b, c$ (principal axes of the ellipsoid) are given by the equations,
$$\begin{aligned}
p & =3 \kappa\left[e^{A}+e^{C}(\text{out})\right] \\
& =3 \kappa e^{A}+3 \kappa\left\{e^{C}(\text{in})-\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1+v}{1-v}\right) e^{T}-\alpha_{1 i} \alpha_{1 j}\left(\frac{1-2 v}{1-v}\right){ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{T}\right\}
\end{aligned}\tag{16}$$
and,
$$\left.\begin{aligned}
{ }^{\prime} p_{i l}=2 \mu\left[{ }^{\prime} e_{i l}{ }^{A}+{ }^{\prime} e_{i l}{ }^{C}(\text{out})\right] \\
=2 \mu{ }^{\prime} e_{i l}{ }^{A}-2 \mu{ }^{\prime} e_{i k}{ }^{T} \alpha_{1 k} \alpha_{1 l} \\
\quad+2 \mu\left\{{ }^{\prime} e_{i l}{ }^{C}(\text{in})+e_{j k}{ }^{T}\left(\frac{1}{1-v}\right) \alpha_{1 i} \alpha_{1 l} \alpha_{1 j} \alpha_{1 k}\right. \\
\left.\quad+\frac{{ }^{\prime} e_{j k}{ }^{T}}{3}\left(\frac{1-2 v}{1-v}\right) \delta_{i l} \alpha_{1 j} \alpha_{1 k}-\frac{e^{T}}{3}\left(\frac{1+v}{1-v}\right)\left[a_{1 i} \alpha_{1 l}-\frac{\delta_{i l}}{3}\right]\right\}
\end{aligned}\right\}\tag{17}$$
in which the $e_{ij}{}^C$ are related to the $e_{im}{}^T$ by equation (6).

For the particular case of the ellipsoidal cavity. in which we are interested, we have,
$$\lambda_{1}=\mu_{1}=0,$$
so that
$$e_{ij}{}^T=e_{ij}{}^C+e_{ij}{}^A.$$
Then
$$e_{i j}{ }^{A}=e_{i j}{ }^{T}-S_{i j m n} e_{m n}{ }^{T}.\tag{18}$$

Thus using (18), $p$ and $'p_{ij}$ in (16) and (17) can be found in terms of $e_{ij}{}^A$. We are now in a position to discuss Stage I of the calculation referred to in Section 2.

### 4. Stationary values of the tensile or compressive stress in the surface of an ellipsoidal cavity of fixed orientation
The vectors $\alpha_{r}$ have components,
$$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}=\left(\alpha_{r 1}, \alpha_{r 2}, \alpha_{r 3}\right) \quad(r=1,2,3)$$
relative to $a, b, c$. Using this notation, we then have that the traction vector $\boldsymbol{R}(\alpha)_{j}$ acting on the surface of a cavity at a point with unit normal $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}$ is defined by
$$\boldsymbol{R}(\alpha)_{j}=p(\alpha)_{j r} \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}.\tag{19}$$

As we have initially taken the surface of the cavity to be traction free we have
$$\boldsymbol{R}(\alpha)_{1}=0\tag{20}$$
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}$ can take any direction in space.

We require to find the position on the surface of a cavity of any given fixed orientation at which the normal stress achieves its maximum value. We do this by considering the variation of the stress with respect to $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}$. By using the transformation,
$$p(\alpha)_{r s}=\alpha_{r m} \alpha_{s n} p_{m n}\tag{21}$$

together with (16) and (17), it is found that the stress components $p(\alpha)_{r s}$ relative to the set of axes $\alpha_{1}, \alpha_{2}, \alpha_{3}$ are given by

$$
p(\alpha)_{r s}=\frac{E}{1+v}{ }^{\prime} e_{m n}{ }^{T}\left\{\left(\alpha_{r m} \alpha_{s n}\right)-\left(\frac{v}{1-v}\right) \delta_{r s} \alpha_{1 m} \alpha_{1 n}\right\}+B \delta_{r s}
\tag{22}
$$

for $r, s=1,2,3$ where

$$
B=\frac{E e^{T}}{3(1-v)}
\tag{23}
$$

and $E, v$ are Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio respectively of the matrix $M$. The $' e_{i j}{ }^{T}$ in equation (22) $(i, j=1,2,3)$ do not depend on the $\alpha_{i j}$ and can be determined explicitly in terms of the $e_{i j}{ }^{A}$ when required by referring to equation (18). In this paper we are only concerned with the stationary values of the tensile or compressive stresses. Hence we only consider stationary values of equation (22) above for $r=s=2,3$. Thus Stage I of the calculation referred to in Section 2 has been reduced to the problem of finding the stationary values of the expression (22), for $r=s=2,3$ with respect to the $\alpha_{i j}$, subject to certain restrictions on the $\alpha_{i j}$. To solve this problem we use the method of Lagrange Multipliers. The restrictions on the $\alpha_{i j}$ can be expressed in the form:

$$
\alpha_{i r} \alpha_{j r}=\delta_{i j}\left(\lambda_{i j}\right) \quad(i, j=1,2,3)
\tag{24}
$$

where the $\lambda_{i j}$ are the Lagrange undetermined multipliers. On performing the necessary differentiations on the expression $p(\alpha)_{r s}$ in equation (22) for the purpose of finding the extrema, we find:

$$
\frac{\partial p(\alpha)_{r s}}{\partial \alpha_{p q}}-\lambda_{i j} \frac{\partial\left(\alpha_{i r} \alpha_{j r}\right)}{\partial \alpha_{p q}}=0 ; \quad \begin{aligned}
& p, q=1,2,3 \\
& r=s=2,3.
\end{aligned}
\tag{25}
$$

From equations (22), (25) it is found that, with $p=1, q=1,2,3$

$$
\alpha_{i j}\left(\frac{2 v}{1-v}\right){ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{T}+2 \lambda_{i j} \alpha_{j l}=0 ; \quad i, j=1,2,3.
\tag{26}
$$

The following results are then obtained:

1. There are six points on the surface of any given cavity at which the normal stress components tangential to the crack surface $(p(\alpha)_{22}$ and $p(\alpha)_{33})$ take stationary values. However, because of symmetry only three of the stationary points are independent and distinct, and we denote these by a bracketed $\chi_{i}(i=1,2,3)$.

2. At each stationary point both $p(\alpha)_{22}$ and $p(\alpha)_{33}$ take stationary values, denoted by $p(\alpha)_{22}(\chi_{i})$ and $p(\alpha)_{33}(\chi_{i})$.

3. The unit normals at the stationary points are mutually orthogonal.

4. The stationary values are as follows:

$$
\left.\begin{array}{l}
p(\alpha)_{22}\left(\chi_{1}\right)=\frac{E}{1+v}\left\{N-\frac{v L}{1-v}\right\}+B ; \quad p(\alpha)_{33}\left(\chi_{1}\right)=\frac{E}{1+v}\left\{M-\frac{v L}{1-v}\right\}+B \\
p(\alpha)_{22}\left(\chi_{2}\right)=\frac{E}{1+v}\left\{L-\frac{v M}{1-v}\right\}+B ; \quad p(\alpha)_{33}\left(\chi_{2}\right)=\frac{E}{1+v}\left\{N-\frac{v M}{1-v}\right\}+B \\
p(\alpha)_{22}\left(\chi_{3}\right)=\frac{E}{1+v}\left\{M-\frac{v N}{1-v}\right\}+B ; \quad p(\alpha)_{33}\left(\chi_{3}\right)=\frac{E}{1+v}\left\{L-\frac{v N}{1-v}\right\}+B
\end{array}\right\} (27)
$$

where $L$, $M$, $N$ are the roots of the equation,

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
x^{3}+' I_{2}{ }^{T} x+' I_{3}{ }^{T} & =0 \\
\text { where } \quad ' I_{2}{ }^{T} & =-\frac{1}{2}{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{T}{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{T} \\
'I_{3}{ }^{T} & =-\frac{1}{3}{ }^{\prime} e_{i j}{ }^{T}{ }^{\prime} e_{j k}{ }^{T}{ }^{\prime} e_{k i}{ }^{T}
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\tag{28}
$$

(remembering that $B$ is defined in equation (23)).

We have now completed Stage I of the calculations.

### 7. Tensile strength of a brittle solid
By setting the value of the maximum tensile stress given in equation (56) equal to $T$ (the interatomic cohesive strength) we obtain the following expression for the tensile strength $(K_{1}^{*})$ in terms of the physical properties of the solid matrix, and of the crack geometry:
$$
K_{1}^{*}=\Delta_{1} \frac{(1-v)^{2}}{(1-2 v)}\left(\frac{c}{b}\right) T
\tag{66}
$$
which, for the particular case of a penny-shaped crack (equation (61)), simplifies to:
$$
K_{1}^{*}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{c}{b}\right) T.
\tag{67}
$$

Murrell (1964a) discussed the problem of assigning a value to $T$, and a much more extensive discussion has since been given by Kelly (1966). On the whole the expression given by Elliot (1947) is still as good as any other which is available. Elliot gives:
$$
T=\left(\frac{1 \cdot 09 \gamma E}{a_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
\tag{68}
$$
for an isotropic solid having a Poisson's ratio $v=0 \cdot 25$. In this expression $\gamma$ is the specific surface energy of the cleavage surface and $a_{0}$ is the interatomic spacing across the cleavage surface. Putting this value of $T$ into equation (67) we obtain:
$$
K_{1}^{*}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{c}{b}\right)\left(\frac{1 \cdot 09 \gamma E}{a_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.
\tag{69}
$$

It is of some interest to eliminate $c$ from this equation and to express it in terms of crack length $l(\bumpeq 2 a)$ and crack tip radius $\rho\left(\bumpeq c^{2} / a\right.$ at the end of the $a$-axis), thus obtaining the expression:
$$
K_{1}^{*}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)\left(\frac{2 \cdot 18 \gamma E \rho}{a_{0} l}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
\tag{70}
$$

compare equation (37) in Murrell (1964a), which gives for the two-dimensional case

$$
\left.K_{1}^{*}=\left(\frac{2 \cdot 18}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma E \rho}{a_{0} l}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].
$$

## 10. Discussion
In this paper we have calculated the conditions under which fracture propagation is initiated in a brittle solid of the Griffith type when the solid is subjected to a homo- geneous, uniform but otherwise arbitrary triaxial system of stresses at points which are sufficiently far from any crack that the stress field is not there perturbed by the presence of cracks. The cracks have been taken to be flattened ellipsoidal cavities.

The initiation of crack propagation is not, in general, synonymous with the complete (macroscopic) fracture of a brittle body, and this we shall discuss later, but it is clearly the first stage of the fracture process, and must therefore bear a relation- ship to the fracture strength of the body.

It has been pointed out previously by Griffith (1924), Orowan (1948-49), and Murrell (1958, 1964a, 1965, 1966) that the two-dimensional Griffith theory explains in a general way the dependence on the stress system of the strength and the fracture type of very brittle solids such as glass, rocks, concrete and cast iron. Murrell (1964a) outlined the laws observed experimentally to govern brittle fracture underconditions of triaxial stress, as follows:

(i) Fracture is tensile and occurs at a fixed value $(K_{1}^{*})$ of the tensile stress normal to the fracture surface as long as the magnitude of the other principal stresses does not exceed some multiple of $K_{1}^{*}$.

(ii) Once one of the principal stresses has a compressive value of magnitude greater than the fixed multiple referred to in (i) then there is a transition from tensile to shear fracture.

(iii) Regardless of whether fracture is of the tensile or shear type the maximum shear stress in the body at fracture increases monotonically as the hydrostatic stress is increased in a compressive sense. This explains why the strength of brittle bodies is increased by external hydrostatic pressure, and why eventually brittle fracture is superseded, first by cataclastic flow (see Murrell 1965), and eventually by plastic flow when the pressure becomes great enough.

(iv) Once the critical compressive stress referred to in (ii) is exceeded the orienta- tion of the cracks which propagate, and of the macroscopic fracture surface, moves progressively away from a direction normal to the direction of the applied major principal stress $K_{1}$ (at infinity).

The results given in the present paper confirm that the Griffith theory provides the basis of an explanation of the laws governing brittle fracture. In addition, they provide information not previously available concerning the effect of the crack shape and of the elastic constants of the matrix on the strength of brittle solids.

More importantly the results given here provide some information concerning the effect of the intermediate principal stress on brittle fracture. The results show that the initiation of crack propagation does not depend upon the intermediate principal stress $K_{2}$.

Mohr (1900) put forward the hypothesis that the strength solids was independent of $K_{2}$, and Anderson (1942) used this in his theory of faulting in the Earth's crust.

However, Murrell (1963, 1965) and Mogi (1967) have obtained experimental evidence which contradicts Mohr's hypothesis. This evidence, together with the theoretical work presented here, suggests that the later stages of crack and fracture propagation involve some physical feature which is missing from the analysis given here. In this paper we have considered the initiation of crack propagation at isolated points on a crack surface. However, it is clearly necessary for the initiation of propagation to spread around the entire periphery of a microscopic crack if the crack is to grow into a macroscopic fracture (this problem does not arise in the two-dimensional case nor in the three-dimensional tensile fracture case). We shall present an analysis of this problem in a later paper, but we suggest that this may be a feature of the later stages of crack propagation which depends on the intermediate principal stress.

Another aspect of the later stages of crack and fracture growth for which a satisfactory physical explanation has still not been obtained is the conflict between the apparent stabilization of cracks after their initial growth by shear and the final growth of these cracks into macroscopic fractures. Griffith cracks which grow through the application of applied shear stresses propagate initially, not at the crack tip (which would imply that they would retain their original shape, see Section 6 of this paper), but in such a way that the newly-formed segments of crack tend to lie along an applied principal stress direction (Brace & Bombalakis 1963; Hoek 1968). Such cracks then become stabilized because the stresses at their tips are small, and even compressive, when the applied principal stresses are compressive. The concomitant of this is that the initiation of crack propagation is observed to occur at stress differ- ences of the order of a half the final stress difference at which fracture occurs. As the stress difference is raised above this value further crack growth occurs, at a greater intensity, and is accompanied eventually by an increase in volume of the body (dilatancy, Brace, Paulding & Scholz 1966; Coffin 1950) associated with the crack growth and finally with the development of a macroscopic fracture.

It would seem, therefore, that the development of a macroscopic fracture is probably a co-operative process, in which the involvement and interaction of many cracks is an important feature. Just how much importance should be attached to the 'co-operative' aspect of the fracture process is difficult to estimate at present. Murrell (1965) has shown experimentally that reasonably close agreement with the formal Griffith theory is possible, which suggests that the 'co-operative' aspect of fracture may be merely a theoretical refinement. In order to explain this it has been suggested (Murrell 1969) that cracks only become temporarily stabilized after the initial stages of growth.

Nevertheless, whatever importance is attached to co-operative processes in fracture, it is clear that the simple original Griffith concept of cracks which act independently of each other is capable of explaining the main features of the laws governing the effect of triaxial stresses on brittle fracture.

### 11. Conclusions
In this paper we have taken the classical model of the brittle fracture process as developed by Griffith (1924) and have carried out the calculations for the general, three-dimensional case, taking the Griffith crack as a flattened ellipsoidal cavity and considering a body subjected to an arbitrary but uniform and homogeneous triaxial stress system at infinity. The solution was facilitated by Eshelby's (1957) calculation of the elastic field of an ellipsoidal inhomogeneity. In Part I of the paper we take the crack surface to be traction free, as in the original work of Griffith.

We show that there are six points on the surface of any given crack (cavity) at which the normal stress components tangential to the crack surface (written $p(\alpha)_{22}$ and $p(\alpha)_{33}$) take stationary values. At these points the two orthogonal normal

stress components both take stationary values. Because of symmetry only three of the stationary points are independent. The unit normals at the stationary points are mutually orthogonal.

Of the stationary values of $p(\alpha)_{22}$ and $p(\alpha)_{33}$ some are compressive (minima) and some tensile (maxima), and their magnitude and sign depend on the magnitude of the applied stresses and their orientation relative to the crack (cavity) axes. We have calculated the crack orientation for which the stationary values of $p(\alpha)_{i i}$ ($i=2,3$) are a maximum (these being tensions) for the case in which the $c$-axis of the crack is much smaller than the $a$- and $b$-axis. We show that the $p(\alpha)_{i i}(\chi_{j})$ ($i=2,3, j=1,2,3$) take stationary values when one of the crack (cavity) axes coincides with one of the axes of principal stress at infinity. The maximum tensions occur when either:

(i) the $c$-axis of the cavity is parallel to the axis of major principal stress ($K_1$) at infinity, and $K_1$ is tensile; or

(ii) the $b$ axis of the cavity is parallel to the axis of intermediate principal stress ($K_2$) at infinity.

Case (i) gives rise to tensile fracture for sufficiently great $K_1$, and Case (ii) gives rise to shear fracture for sufficiently great $(K_1-K_3)$.

The results depend in a complex way upon the ratio of the $a$ and $b$ axes of the cavity and on the Poisson's ratio of the solid, but for the case of a penny-shaped crack ($a=b$) the following results are obtained:

Case (i):
$$
p(\alpha)_{22}(\chi_{3})=\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\frac{b}{c}\right) K_{1}
$$
and a maximum tension exists at the extremity of every $a$-radius of the crack (cavity).

Case (ii):
$$
p(\alpha)_{22}(\chi_{3})=\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\frac{b}{c}\right) \frac{1}{v(4-v)}\left[\left\{\left(K_{1}+K_{3}\right)^{2}+\frac{v(4-v)}{(2-v)^{2}}\left(K_{1}-K_{3}\right)^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(K_{1}+K_{3}\right)\right]
$$
with the cavity oriented so that its $a$-axis makes an angle $\theta$ with the $K_1$ axis, where
$$
\cos 2 \theta=\frac{(2-v)^{2}}{v(4-v)}\left[\left(\frac{K_{1}+K_{3}}{K_{1}-K_{3}}\right)+\frac{\left|\left\{\frac{v(4-v)}{(2-v)^{2}}\left(K_{1}-K_{3}\right)^{2}+\left(K_{1}+K_{3}\right)^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right|}{\left(K_{1}-K_{3}\right)}\right]
$$

This maximum tension occurs in the $a-c$ plane and the tangent at $\chi_{3}$ makes an angle $\phi$ with the $a$-axis, where
$$
\tan \phi= \pm \frac{2}{(2-v)} \cot 2 \theta.
$$

Crack propagation is initiated when $p(\alpha)_{22}(\chi_{3})=T$ where $T$ is the interatomic cohesive strength of the solid. Writing the uniaxial tensile strength as $K_1^*$ we can obtain a criterion for the initiation of crack propagation in the form:
$$
\left(K_{1}-K_{3}\right)^{2}=(2-v)^{2} K_{1} *\left[K_{1} * v(4-v)-2\left(K_{1}+K_{3}\right)\right]
$$
where
$$
K_{1}^{*}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{c}{b}\right) T
$$

for the case of a penny-shaped crack. The envelope of the corresponding stress circles in the Mohr diagram (the Mohr envelope) is:

$$
\left(\frac{\tau}{K_{1}{ }^{*}}\right)^{2}=(2-v)^{2}\left\{1-\frac{\sigma}{K_{1}{ }^{*}}\right\}
$$

where $\tau$ and $\sigma$ are respectively the shear stress and normal stress acting at infinity on a plane parallel to the $a-b$ plane of the crack.

These results are all very similar in many respects to the results for the two- dimensional Griffith crack (see Murrell 1964a), and we have shown that the latter results can be derived from ours.

The important thing here is that we have shown that the initiation of crack propagation at least, is independent of the intermediate principal stress. (This conclusion is subject to correction to take account of any dependence of the cohesive strength upon stress. We believe, however, that any correction is likely to be small.) However, investigation of the subsequent stages of crack growth is now required, since there is experimental evidence to show that the brittle fracture strength is not independent of the intermediate principal stress (Murrell 1963, 1965; Mogi 1967).

The theoretical problem considered here is of considerable interest in geophysics and geology in connection with the mechanics of faulting and especially in con- nection with earthquake mechanics. The increased precision of seismometry in recent years has opened up the possibility of determining the fault planes of earth- quakes with some precision. Theoretical and experimental work on fracture under triaxial stress conditions may enable the observed fault planes to be related to the elastic stress field in the crust and mantle of the Earth.
