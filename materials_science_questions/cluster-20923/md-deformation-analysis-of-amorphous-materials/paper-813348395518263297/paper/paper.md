# Nanoscale amorphization as a special deformation mode in nanowires

I.A. Ovid'ko*

Institute of Problems of Mechanical Engineering (Russian Academy of Sciences), Bolshoj 61, Vas. Ostrov,
St. Petersburg 199178, Russia
Department of Mathematics and Mechanics at St. Petersburg State University, Universitetskii pr., 28,
Staryi Petergof, St. Petersburg 198504, Russia

Received 30 September 2011; revised 26 November 2011; accepted 1 December 2011
Available online 10 December 2011

Crystals are conventionally treated to be deformed through generation and evolution of crystal lattice defects. In this paper, nanoscale amorphization (NA; formation of nanoscale amorphous regions in crystals under mechanical load) is suggested and theoretically described as a special/alternative deformation mode operating in crystalline solids. It is shown that NA can contribute effectively to plastic flow in crystalline Ni and Si nanowires.

Keywords: Plastic deformation; Phase transformations; Nanostructured materials; Amorphization

Nanoscale deformation and amorphization processes in crystalline materials represent a wide class of phenomena, being of large significance from both fundamental and applied viewpoints; see, for example, Refs. [1–15]. Of particular interest is nanoscale amorphization (NA; the formation of nanoscale amorphous regions) in crystalline nanowires and polycrystals under mechanical load [1,2,5,10,11,16–18]. This process dramatically changes the local structure and thereby the mechanical and functional properties of materials. Conventionally, two basic mechanisms for (nano)amorphization in deformed crystals are considered. The first mechanism represents the amorphization realizing as a conventional phase transition due to accumulation of defects in the crystalline phase [5,19]. In doing so, plastic deformation serves as a prerequisite for the amorphization occurring through formation and growth of amorphous nuclei in a deformed crystal, when the free energy $(F_{\text{cr}}+F_{\text{def}})$ of the crystal with deformation-produced defects exceeds that $(F_{\text{am}})$ of the amorphous state: $F_{\text{cr}}+F_{\text{def}}>F_{\text{am}}$ [5,19]. In particular, this approach has been exploited effectively in a description of amorphization in powders by mechanical milling [19]. At the same time, it meets a fundamental question concerning the applicability of equilibrium thermodynamics (operating with balance of free energies) to such highly non-equilibrium systems as crystals under mechanical load. The second mechanism is relevant to amorphization in crystals at extremely high stresses and strain rates. This mechanism relates the amorphization to an elastic instability—loss of shear rigidity—at very high stresses [1,2,5,16,17]. In other terms, stress-induced melting occurs in a solid which thereby exhibits both disorder in the atomic structure and near vanishing elastic shear constant(s) [1,2,5,16,17]. In this case, as noted by Ma [5], plastic deformation does not serve as a prerequisite for the amorphization, but plays the role of a competitive dissipating process. Thus, according to contemporary representations on amorphization in deformed crystals, plastic deformation either is a prerequisite for the amorphization process (at moderate and high stresses) [5,19] or competes with it (at extremely high stresses) [1,2,5,16,17]. The main aim of this paper is to suggest and theoretically describe the third mechanism, in which case NA serves as a special deformation mode in initially crystalline solids in a wide range of applied stresses. That is, NA and plastic deformation represent the same process. This mechanism is examined in the scientifically interesting and technologically important situation with crystalline nanowires.

Within the present approach, NA represents a special mode of plastic deformation realized through spatially inhomogeneous multiplane shear (SIMS) in a nanoscale volume during a very short time interval (Fig. 1). SIMS is defined here as a spatially inhomogeneous plastic shear occurring simultaneously along $n$ neighboring crystal planes (Fig. 1b and c). It is characterized by a shear vector function $\boldsymbol{s}'(x,y)$ dependent on the coordinates $x$ and $y$ of atoms within the nanoscale region ABCD subjected to SIMS (Fig. 1b and c). The notion of SIMS is a generalization of that of "conventional" (spatially homogeneous) multiplane shear in ideal crystals of infinite sizes [20] and nanocrystals [21]. Such "conventional" shear occurs simultaneously in several neighboring crystal planes [20,21] and can produce twins in nanowires and nanomaterials [21]. In contrast to "conventional" multiplane shear, SIMS produces disorder of the initially crystalline structure in the shear direction. In other words, SIMS creates a kind of two-dimensional (2D) amorphous state in $n$ periodically spaced planes (Fig. 1c). That is, a disorder in atomic arrangement in each plane is created, but there is a periodic arrangement of the planes themselves (Fig. 1c). The transformation from the crystalline state to the 2D amorphous state is driven by the shear stresses and occurs as a plastic deformation mode. Then, an elastic instability of the plastically sheared region occurs which transforms the 2D amorphous state (Fig. 1c) into the conventional three-dimensional (3D) amorphous state (Fig. 1d). This elastic instability is driven by relaxation of the elastic energy (with contributions related to all the components of the stress tensor) of the plastically sheared region.

Consider the crystallography, energy and stress characteristics of NA realizing through SIMS in a single crystalline face-centered cubic (fcc) nanowire under tensile load (Fig. 1a-d). The SIMS occurs in $n$ $\{111\}$ crystallographic planes, making a $45^{\circ}$ angle with the tension direction (Fig. 1b and c). The distance between neighboring $\{111\}$ crystallographic planes is related to the crystal lattice parameter $a$ as $p=a/\sqrt{3}$. At any moment of deformation, SIMS is specified by an averaged (over the region ABCD) value $s$ of the shear magnitude $\boldsymbol{s}'(x,y)$:$s=\langle \boldsymbol{s}'(x,y)\rangle$.

The NA through SIMS (Fig. 1) is characterized by the energy change $\Delta W=W_{\text{S}}-W_{0}$, where $W_{\text{S}}$ is the energy of the nanowire where SIMS occurs, and $W_{0}$ is the energy of the nanowire in its initial state. $\Delta W$ is a function of $s$ as well as the sizes $L$ and $H$ $(=np)$ of the new amorphous region. The energy change $\Delta W$ (per one shear line; Fig. 1b and c) has the four basic terms:

$$
\Delta W(s,L,H)=W_{\text{interior}}+W_{\text{AC-BD}}-A+W_{\text{step}} \tag{1}
$$

Here, $W_{\text{interior}}$ denotes the energy of the interior area of the plastically sheared region ABCD; $W_{\text{AC-BD}}$ is the energy of the interfaces, AC and BD, between the sheared region and the neighboring material; $A$ is the work of the external shear stress $\tau$, spent to the plastic shear; and $W_{\text{step}}$ is the energy of free surface steps generated due to SIMS (Fig. 1b-d).

To calculate the terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (1), first consider the situation where the amorphization homogeneously occurs within all the nanowire volume. This situation corresponds to the results of simulations [1,2], showing that the amorphization in Ni and NiCu alloy nanowires occurs homogeneously. In this situation, $W_{\text{AC-BD}}=0$.

The exact expression for the energy $W_{\text{interior}}$ is unknown. In this case, $W_{\text{interior}}$ is taken approximately as a function of $s$ on the basis of the following statements and assumptions: (i) $W_{\text{interior}}$ is sensitive to the standard characteristic $\gamma_{\text{GSF}}(s)$ (the energy density of the generalized stacking fault) of the ideal plastic shear in the crystalline phase; (ii) SIMS has an extra flexibility (due to its spatial non-homogeneity) and is thereby enhanced, compared with the ideal, spatially homogeneous plastic shear (producing generalized stacking faults) in crystals; (iii) amorphization is complete when $s$ reaches a value of around $b$, the Burgers vector magnitude of a partial dislocation; and (iv) in the final amorphous state $(s=b)$, the energy $W_{\text{interior}}(s=b)=LpW_{\text{A}}$, where the product $Lp$ is the volume of the amorphous phase per one sheared plane, and $W_{\text{A}}$ denotes the excess energy density of the amorphous phase, compared with its crystalline counterpart. With points (i)-(iv), the function $W_{\text{interior}}(s)$ is approximated as follows:

$$
W_{\text{interior}}(s)=L\gamma_{\text{GSF}}(s)\lambda+(s/b)L(pW_{\text{A}}-\lambda\gamma_{0}) \tag{2}
$$

where $\lambda<1$ is the factor taking into account the enhancement of SIMS, compared with the ideal shear.

The energy density dependence $\gamma_{\text{GSF}}(s)$ for the generalized stacking faults in Ni and Si (materials under further consideration) was simulated in Refs. [22,23], respectively. With the results in Refs. [22,23], this dependence in Ni and Si is well approximated by the following formula:

$$
\gamma_{GSF}(s/b)=
\begin{cases}
\frac{\gamma_{m}}{2}(1-\cos\frac{2\pi s}{b}),& 0\leq s/b<1/2, \\
\frac{\gamma_{m}+\gamma_{0}}{2}-\frac{\gamma_{m}-\gamma_{0}}{2}\cos\frac{2\pi s}{b},& 1/2\leq s/b<1.
\end{cases} \tag{3}
$$

Here $\gamma_{m}$ and $\gamma_{0}$ are the maximum and minimum values of $\gamma_{GSF}(s)$, corresponding to unstable and stable configurations of the generalized stacking fault, respectively.

The work $A$ (per one shear plane) of the external shear stress $\tau$ and the energy $W_{\text{step}}$ of $n$ free surface steps (per one step, each having height $s$) are given in the standard way as

$$
A=\tau sL \text{ and } W_{\text{step}}=s\gamma_{\text{s}} \tag{4}
$$

respectively. Here, $\gamma_{\text{s}}$ is the specific energy of the free surface (per its unit area).

With the expression $W_{\text{AC-BD}}=0$ and Eqs. (1)-(4), the dependences $\Delta W(s)$ were calculated in the case of Ni nanowire, for the following typical values of parameters: $a=0.35$ nm, $b=0.14$ nm, $\gamma_{\text{m}}=0.17$ J m$^{-2}$ [22], $\gamma_{0}=0.12$ J m$^{-2}$ [22], $\gamma_{\text{s}}=0.12$ J m$^{-2}$, $\lambda=0.6$ and $W_{\text{A}}\approx G/65$, where $G=76$ GPa is the shear modulus. (The latter estimate of $W_{\text{A}}\approx G/65$ is taken as the maximum value from the range, $W_{\text{A}}\approx G/83$-$G/65$, given by Morris [24] for experimentally measured values of $W_{\text{A}}$ in metallic materials.) The critical stress $\tau=\tau_{c}$ is defined as the lowest stress at which the NA occurs in the non-barrier way. In mathematical terms, $\tau_{c}$ is the lowest stress corresponding to the condition $\partial(\Delta W)/\partial s<0$ in the range of $s$ from 0 to $b$. With this condition, the dependences of $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ on the nanowire width $L'=L/2^{1/2}$ were calculated at $\lambda=0.3,0.6$ and 0.9 (see Fig. 2b). The calculated values of the critical stress $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ at $\lambda=0.6$ are well consistent with those for the stress at which the homogeneous amorphization of Ni nanowires occurs in simulations [1,2].

Now consider the situation where NA occurs within a nanoscale band ABCD consisting of $n$ sheared planes in a nanowire (Fig. 1). This situation corresponds approximately to the results of the experiment [10] and simulations [25], both showing that the amorphization occurs non-homogeneously in nanoscale regions of deformed Si nanowires. In this situation, the interfaces AC and BD (Fig. 1) contribute to the energy change $\Delta W(s,L,H)$ (see Eq. (1)), and the expression for the energy $W_{\text{interior}}(s)$ is slightly modified. In a first approximation, the energy $W_{\text{AC-BD}}$ of the interfaces AC and BD (Fig. 1) per one sheared plane is taken as the following linear function of $s$:

$$
W_{\text{AC-BD}}=(s/b)L(2/n)W_{\text{cr-glass}} \tag{5}
$$

where $W_{\text{cr-glass}}$ is the energy density (per unit area) of a crystal/glass interface. The factor $(2/n)$ in Eq. (5) takes into account the fact that the interfaces AC and BD are two of $n$ sheared planes (Fig. 1). The same fact is reflected in the factor $((n-2)/n)$, which appears in the following formula for the energy $W_{\text{interior}}(s)$ of the interior area of the region ABCD per one sheared plane:

$$
W_{\text{interior}}(s)=L\gamma_{\text{GSF}}(s)\lambda+(s/b)L((n-2)/n)(pW_{A}-\lambda\gamma_{0}) \tag{6}
$$

With Eqs. (1), (3), (4), (5), and (6), the dependences $\Delta W(s)$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}(L')$ were calculated in the case of Si nanowire for the following typical values of parameters: $a=0.54$ nm, $b=0.22$ nm, $\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}=1.67$ J m$^{-2}$ [23], $\gamma_{0}=0.075$ J m$^{-2}$ [23], $\gamma_{\mathrm{s}}=1.5$ J m$^{-2}$, $W_{\mathrm{A}}=8.13\times10^{8}$ J m$^{-3}$, $W_{\text{cr-glass}}=0.23$ J m$^{-2}$, $n=H/p$, and $\lambda=0.1$, 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9. In general, they are qualitatively similar to the corresponding dependences shown in Figure 2a and b, respectively, for Ni nanowires. At the same time, the critical stress level for NA in Si nanowires is much higher than that in Ni nanowires. In the case of Si nanowires with $n=5$ (this value corresponds approximately to the data [10,25]), the dependences $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}(L')$ are presented in Figure 2c. Typical values of $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ at $\lambda=0.1$ (Fig. 2c) are consistent with simulated [25] values of the stress at which NA occurs in nanocrystalline Si nanowires containing amorphous grain boundaries (which introduce a local structural disorder in the crystalline state and increase its energy).

Also, note that dislocations were experimentally [10] observed in a Si nanowire before NA. They were produced at the initial stage of plastic deformation [10]. Nevertheless, within the present approach, the experimentally observed [10] NA in the Si nanowire is not a conventional phase transition driven by the dislocation energy only. In the spirit of the present model (Fig. 1), the NA and plastic deformation at its second stage is the same process, and it is just enhanced (but not driven exclusively) by the pre-produced dislocations that create stress fields in the crystalline state and increase its energy.
