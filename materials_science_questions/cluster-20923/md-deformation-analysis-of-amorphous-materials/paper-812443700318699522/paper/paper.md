# High pressure research at CHESS

Keith Brister

Cornell High Energy Synchrotron Source, Wilson Laboratory, Cornell University, Ithaca, NY 14853, USA

Since February 1990 there has been a dedicated high pressure line at the Cornell High Energy Synchrotron Source (CHESS). This facility provides X-ray instrumentation for energy dispersive X-ray diffraction and Laue diffraction using diamond anvil cells. Both hard-bend magnet and wiggler radiation are available as well as focused monochromatic radiation. In addition, support instrumentation is also available: a ruby system, laser heating, sample loading, and data analysis software. Experienced users need only to bring there diamond anvil cells and samples and can leave with the initial data analysis finished. Research using diamond anvil cells will be introduced and the facility will be described. Some of the diamond anvil cell research done at CHESS will be reviewed, including crystalline to amorphous transitions (R.R. Winters et al., J. Chem. Phys. in press), properties of $C_{60}$ under stress (S.J. Duclos et al., Nature 351 (1991) 380), deep earthquakes (T.C. Wu et al., submitted to J. Geophys. Res.), and reaching pressures of the center of Earth (A.L. Ruoff et al., Rev. Sci. Instr. 61 (1990) 3830).

## 1. Introduction

About two years ago CHESS opened a National High Pressure Facility using mainly the bending mag- net station B1. The funds were provided by the Na- tional Science Foundation [1] for the purpose of in- stalling equipment for high pressure diamond anvil cell experiments [2]. The equipment available to the users of this facility include two energy dispersive X-ray diffraction setups, an in situ ruby fluorescence system for pressure measurements, a high quality microscope with a long working distance for manipulating samples in a diamond anvil cell, and a laser heating system for high temperature experiments.

Fig. 1 shows a schematic of a diamond anvil cell. The sample is placed between two diamonds, in a hole drilled in a metal gasket. To apply pressure to the sample, a uniaxial force is applied to the diamonds. For experiments that need to be done under hydro- static conditions, a pressure transmitting medium is also placed in the sample chamber. For experiments below about 15 GPa, a 4:1 mixture of methan- ol:ethanol is used. At higher pressures, rare gases have been used. Above 50 GPa, even the rare gases become quite hard and can support a shear stress so experiments above this pressure are usually done with- out a pressure transmitting medium.

Measurement of the pressure in the diamond anvil cell can be done in two ways. An X-ray marker such as NaCl, Au, or Pt can be used. The lattice parameter of the marker is monitored during the experiment and from this information the pressure can be inferred from the isothermal equation of state of the marker. An energy dispersive diffraction setup is shown schematically in fig. 2. For these experiments, Bragg's law is written

$$
E d=\frac{h c}{2 \sin \theta}, \tag{1}

where $E$ is the energy of a diffracted X-ray, $d$ is the Bragg spacing of the diffracting plane, $h$ is Planck's constant, $c$ is the speed of light, and $\theta$ is the diffraction angle. Here the diffraction angle $\theta$ is kept fixed and the energy $E$ is measured using a solid state detector. A typical spectrum is shown in fig. 3. In general one sees diffraction lines from the sample and from the pressure marker as well as fluorescence lines. In some experiments diffraction from the metal gasket can also be seen. Although the technique is limited by the reso- lution of the solid state detector (135 eV at the $\mathrm{Mn} \mathrm{K}_{\alpha}$ line for the intrinsic Ge detector used by this facility), it has the advantage that data can be taken, displayed, and analyzed quickly. For cubic, hexagonal close packed, and many tetragonal and orthorhombic struc- tures, the detector resolution does not prevent the analysis of the data or the determination of the struc- ture. It is only when a structure gives several overlap- ping peaks that problems occur. For these situations an angle dispersive mode is available.

At CHESS, a diamond anvil cell user brings a diamond anvil cell and a sample and leaves with the energy dispersive data mostly analyzed. User groups with no previous X-ray experience have been able to start taking data within a few hours of arriving at CHESS.

### 2. Ultrahigh pressures

Recent experiments done at the CHESS National High Pressure Facility by Ruoff and his research group have reached pressures of 416 GPa [3]. For compari- son, the pressure at the center of Earth is about 360 GPa. These experiments represent the highest cali- brated pressures reached in a static experiment and show that systematic studies of phase transitions and equations of states can be carried out under such extreme conditions.

To reach such pressures, specially shaped diamonds are used [4]. The tip of the diamond is beveled as shown in fig. 4. The flat region is typically 12 to $50 \mu \mathrm{m}$ in diameter while the diameter of the entire tip is typically $300 \mu \mathrm{m}$. At high pressures, the diamonds tend to cup and without the bevel, the diamonds fail when

the edge of one diamond punches through the gasket and touches the other anvil. The bevel is used to compensate for the cupping of the diamonds. A typical pressure distribution in a diamond anvil cell at ultrahigh pressures is shown in fig. 5.

At such extreme pressures, the state of stress of the sample is certainly not hydrostatic. The pressure is applied uniaxially, along the diamond axis, and no pressure transmitting medium is used (there is no known medium that is hydrostatic at these pressures).

![](./images/812443700318699522_5.jpg)

Fig. 5. A typical pressure profile in a diamond anvil cell at ultrahigh pressure.

The actual state of stress is shown in eq. (2):

$$
(\sigma_{ij}) = \begin{pmatrix}
P + \frac{2\sigma}{3} & & \\
& P - \frac{\sigma}{3} & \\
& & P - \frac{\sigma}{3}
\end{pmatrix}, \tag{2}
$$

where $\sigma_{ij}$ is a component of the stress tensor, $P$ is the hydrostatic pressure, and $\sigma$ is the flow stress.

If the state of stress is not taken into account, the pressure that one then measures is a function of the diffraction angle. The diffracting plane normals are almost perpendicular to the applied stress for most diamond anvil cells so an uncompensated measurement will yield a pressure smaller than that applied. Although the best way to find the actual state of stress is to make measurements at a number of different diffraction angles, the limited angular X-ray aperture offered by ultrahigh pressure diamond anvil cells reduces the usefulness of this approach. What Ruoff's group does instead is to estimate the nonhydrostatic component using a scaling relation. The result is eq. (3) and allows one to calculate the hydrostatic lattice parameter $a_P$ as a function of the measured lattice parameter $a_{P+\sigma}$ knowing the Bragg angle $\theta$, the zero pressure compressive flow stress $\sigma_0$, the zero pressure polycrystalline shear modulus, $G_0$ and the Poisson ratio at both zero and high pressure, $\nu_0$ and $\nu$, respectively.

$$
\frac{a_P}{a_0} = \frac{a_{P+\sigma}}{a_0} \left(1 - \frac{\sigma_0(1 - \nu_0)}{6G_0(1 - \nu)} (1 - 3\sin^2\theta)\right). \tag{3}
$$

One of the driving forces behind achieving such high pressures is to study phase transitions in metals. For example, the nonmagnetic transition metals have the structural sequence hcp → bcc → hcp → fcc as the d-band fills up as can be seen from fig. 7. Ruoff's group has shown that electrons can be transferred to the d-band from the s-band and initiate this sequence of transitions. In zirconium [5], increasing the pressure to 30 GPa at ambient temperatures results in a structural phase transition from a hexagonal $\omega$ phase to a body-centered-cubic phase. The significance of this is that zirconium, a group IV metal, is taking on the structure of niobium, a group V metal. Likewise, hafnium [6] undergoes a similar transition at 71 GPa while titanium undergoes the first part of the transformation, hcp to $\omega$, but the $\omega$ phase is stable to the highest pressures reached in those experiments, 87 GPa.

### 3. Shear induced phase transitions

A combination of high pressures and high temperatures is necessary to study processes which occur deep within the Earth. Although the combination that would allow a look at the center of Earth has not yet been achieved, portions of the mantle can be explored with diamond anvil cell techniques. Such experiments are being done by Bassett and his co-workers [7,8].

One question which has gone unanswered for many years is "what causes deep focus earthquakes?" These earthquakes originate in the subduction zone where two plates collide and force material from the cooler upper mantle to descend into the deeper, hotter part of the mantle. See fig. 8. The main constituent of the upper mantle is olivine, $(Fe, Mg)_2SiO_4$, which has a hexagonal structure (the $\alpha$ phase) while at higher temperatures and pressures it has a cubic structure (the $\gamma$ phase).

Recent work by Wu et al. [8] has shown that the probable cause of the deep earthquakes is a shear induced $\alpha$-$\gamma$ transition in olivine. As olivine is pulled down from the upper mantle, it stays in the $\alpha$ phase. The temperature of this material is still low ($600^\circ$C at 400 km) compared to the ordinary mantle temperatures at this depth ($1400^\circ$C). At depths between about 525 and 650 km, the stable phase is the $\gamma$ phase. The cooler subducting material can transform from the $\alpha$ phase to the $\gamma$ phase at pressures where the $\gamma$ phase is stable with the application of shear stress. When a region undergoes this transformation, it loses its ability to support a shear stress which places more stress on neighboring material, causing it to transform. This unstable process can proceed quickly and release a large amount of energy to produce an earthquake.

A diamond anvil cell equipped with a brass gasket and a resistive heater was used for these experiments. The soft gasket deformed very easily and allowed the sample to extrude from the anvils. During the extrusion the friction between the diamond and the sample plays a crucial role in determining the state of stress in the sample. It is this friction that keeps the sample from just squirting out from between the diamonds. During an experiment with a very soft gasket or no gasket at all, a dynamic equilibrium is achieved between the flow rate of the sample and the stress which supports the load on the sample [9,10]. At this point in the experiment, when more load is applied to the cell, the material in the center does not flow since it is supported both by the diamond-sample friction and the material further from the center. At the edge of the anvils, the material does flow since the diamond-sample friction alone cannot prevent it and there is no material further away from the center to support it. The boundary of the flow-no flow regions is the zone of maximum shear strain.

## 4. Reversible amorphous transitions

Materials can be classified by the types of bonding that hold them together. For the purposes of studying reversible amorphous transitions, it is appropriate to consider materials which have nondirectional bonding between structural units. In these solids it is possible to have short and mid-range structure order without any

long range order. For example, $AlPO_4$, studied by Kruger and Jeanloz [11] (on a rotating anode, not at CHESS), undergoes a crystalline to amorphous transi- tion which is not only reversible, but the final crystal orientation is the same as the original orientation. This can only be explained by the atoms being distorted from their original positions only slightly.

Hammack and his group from Carnegie Mellon have found the first reversible crystalline-amorphous transition in a material which can be prepared in either state under ambient pressure [12,13]. The fact that the material can be prepared in either state at ambient pressure allows the investigation of the relation be- tween melt-quenched and pressure-induced amor- phous states and in general to investigate noncrys- talline materials which exhibit medium range order on the scale of 5-20 $\mathring{A}$.

Hammack and his students have used a combina- tion of X-ray diffraction and Raman spectroscopy to infer medium range order by applying pressure (up to10 GPa and room temperature), observing the forma- tion of an amorphous state, and releasing the pressure to observe the return of the original crystal structure. X-ray diffraction probes the long range order in a material while Raman spectroscopy probes the short range order. In the amorphous states observed by Hammack, the X-ray signal disappears but not the Raman signal. This shows the material still has short range order and the fact that the original crystal struc- ture returns allows one to infer the presence of medium range order. The X-ray spectra from one experimental run is shown in fig. 10.

The sample they used was a mixture of $Ca(NO_3)_2$ and $NaNO_3$ where the calcium in $Ca(NO_3)_2$ is sur- rounded by a slightly distorted cuboctahedron of oxy- gen atoms and the sodium in $NaNO_3$ is surrounded by an octahedron of oxygen. This combination of cubocta- hedra and octahedra is able to fill space. What they believe is happening is that the cuboctahedra are dis- torting to a more stable but noncrystallographic icosa- hedron. This is shown in fig. 11. Starting with a cuboc- tahedron, an icosahedron can be formed by a simple rotation of the triangular faces. Another way of achiev- ing the same thing is to decrease the distance between the center of the cuboctahedron while keeping the edge distance the same. The resulting solid has a smaller volume, as one would expect under compres- sion. Since the atomic positions are very close to what they were before the application of pressure, the tran- sition is reversible. Note that since the icosahedron- octahedron combination cannot fill space, defects must be created. The defects cannot be expected to go away after the reverse phase transition.

This model for reversible crystalline-amorphous transitions can only be applied to solids which have nondirectional bonding between polytetrahedral units.

![](./images/812443700318699522_11.jpg)

Fig. 11. The transformation of a cuboctahedron into an icosa- hedron. The vertices of the cuboctahedron move in the direc- tions indicated by the arrows, effectively rotating the triangu- lar faces.

There must also be an icosahedral packing which re- sults in a higher density. For example, many elemental metals have face-centered-cubic and hexagonal close packed lattices which have cuboctahedral atomic ar- rangements. Since these are already close packed lat- tices, there is no higher density packing of icosahedra to which the material can transform. In fact, such a transformation has never been observed.

### 5. $C_{60}$ experiments

Ever since the discovery of a way to make "large" quantities of fullerines [14] there has been a lot of work done on $C_{60}$ [15]. The molecules form triacontadihedra (truncated icosahedra) which, through van der Waals bonds, form a face-centered-cubic lattice. The isother- mal equation of state of the solid fullerite has been measured at CHESS by Duclos et al. [16]. The X-ray diffraction patterns from these studies show some in- teresting effects due to the symmetry of the moleculeswhich make up the solid. At low pressures the (200) line is missing, but at higher pressures the (200) line increases in intensity while the (111) line disappears. The explanation for this, due to Fischer et al. [17], is as follows: Model the molecule as a hollow sphere of charge at the radius $r_c$ of fullerine molecule,
$$\rho(r)=\rho_{0} \delta\left(r-r_{c}\right).\qquad(4)$$

Diffracted X-rays will satisfy Bragg's law, eq. (1), so the form factor can be written as
$$\begin{aligned}
f(E) & =\int_{0}^{\infty} \rho \delta\left(r-r_{c}\right) \frac{\sin (2 \pi r / d)}{(2 \pi r / d)} \mathrm{d} r \\
& =\frac{\rho d \sin \left(2 \pi r_{c} / d\right)}{2 \pi r_{c}},
\end{aligned}\qquad(5)$$
which has zeros at
$$2 r_{\mathrm{c}} / d=n, \qquad(6)$$

## 6. Conclusions

The High Pressure Facility at CHESS has had a
very successful start. For experimenters who have al-
ready had experience using synchrotron radiation with
diamond anvil cells, it has largely reduced the setup
time and equipment maintenance costs while allowing
the experimenters to concentrate on the science rather
than the equipment. For those who have had no previ-
ous experience, it has provided an extremely low cost
way to try out the techniques without making any
commitments.
