# Magnetoelastic Model of the Temperature-Induced Metamagnetism for Lattices with Axial Symmetry²)

By
P. PAWLICKI

A new model of the temperature-induced metamagnetic phase transitions is considered for the class of the two-sublattice magnets with axial symmetry. The model Hamiltonian contains the usual Ising term as well as the coupling of the uniform strain with both exchange interaction and single ion anisotropy. Within the MFA all these couplings are found to be necessary to produce the metamagnetic phase change. The results are compared with experiment.

Рассматривается новая модель метамагнитного, температурного фазового превращения для класса двухподрешеточных магнетиков с аксиальной симметрией. Наряду с обычным изинговским членом модельный гамильтонян содержит магни-тоупругие взаимодействия однородной деформации с обменной и с одноионной анизотропией. Нами найдено в приближении МФА что все эти взаимодействия необходимы для получения метамагнитного преващения. Результаты сравнено с экспериментом.

## 1. Introduction

Several compounds like FeRh [1], $\mathrm{Mn}_{3} \mathrm{GaC}$ [2], or $\mathrm{Mn}_{2-x} \mathrm{Cr}_{x} \mathrm{Sb}$ [3] exhibit a spontaneous phase transition from the low-temperature antiferromagnetic (A) phase to the high-temperature ferromagnetic (F) one. It was established in 1960 by Kittel [4] that this kind of phase change may be explained within the simple phenomenological model including the magnetoelastic interactions (exchange inversion hypothesis). This idea was then developed by many authors [e.g. 5 to 7] but the spin models appeared only within the last ten years. Onyszkiewicz in 1972 was the first to apply the four-spin short-range interactions [8 to 10] which may be considered [11] under some assumptions as equivalent to the magnetoelastic ones. Tuszyński [12, 13] introduced a refined model of the exchange inversion where the contributions from the lattice oscillations are included.

In our previous works [14, 15] we proposed the magnetoelastic Hamiltonian for the case of $\mathrm{Mn}_{3} \mathrm{GaC}$. The coupling of the uniform axial and volume strains with the spin interactions was taken into account in the model. However, because of the complicated magnetic structure of this compound the construction of the Hamiltonian is specific for this type of ordering and therefore this case is inconvenient for further theoretical treatment. We have found a simpler and more general model of the $\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}$ phase transition and in the present work we intend to give its molecular field analysis.

We shall consider the following magnetoelastic Hamiltonian which may be applied
for the two-sublattice magnet with axial symmetry:

$$
H=-(I+K \varepsilon) \sum_{f, g} S_{f}^{z} S_{g}^{z}-M \sum_{f, f^{\prime}} S_{f}^{z} S_{f^{\prime}}^{z}-L \varepsilon \sum_{f}\left(S_{f}^{z}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2} C \varepsilon^{2}. \tag{1}
$$

The above Hamiltonian includes the intrasublattice interaction $M$, the intersublattice
interaction $I+K \varepsilon$ coupled linearly with the uniform strain $\varepsilon$, and the single-ion aniso-
tropy $L$ coupled also with the strain. In order to simplify the model we omitted here the
possible coupling of the strain with intrasublattice interaction as well as the uncoupled
single-ion anisotropy. The quantity $\varepsilon$ may be considered as the axial $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{z z}$ component
of the strain tensor.

Hamiltonians of similar construction are used in Callens' theory of magnetostriction
[16]. Let us point to a difference between their approach and ours. It is the fact that
we use $z^{2}$ instead of $z^{2}-\frac{1}{3} r^{2}$ as the axial invariant. This choice follows from the fact
that in the theory of phase transitions the exchange anisotropy is used rather in the
form of $S_{f}^{z} S_{g}^{z}$ instead of $S_{f}^{z} S_{g}^{z}-\frac{1}{3} S_{f} \cdot S_{g}$.

In the subsequent parts of the present work we intend to show that the Hamiltonian
(1) gives the $A \rightarrow F$ phase transition at least within the MFA.

### 2. Molecular Field Approximation (MFA)

In order to obtain the thermodynamical properties of the model (1) we resort to the
MFA, starting with the variation principle for the free energy,

$$
F \leqq F_{0}=-\frac{1}{\beta} \ln \operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp \left(-\beta H_{0}\right)\right]+\left\langle H-H_{0}\right\rangle_{0}, \tag{2}
$$

see, e.g. Tyablikov [17], where

$$
\langle\ldots\rangle_{0}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\ldots \varrho_{0}\right], \tag{3}
$$

$$
\varrho_{0}=\exp \left(-\beta H_{0}\right)\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp \left(-\beta H_{0}\right)\right]\right\}^{-1}, \tag{4}
$$

and $H_{0}$ denotes a trial Hamiltonian which we specify as follows:

$$
H_{0}=-y_{1} \sum_{f} S_{f}^{z}-y_{2} \sum_{f}\left(S_{f}^{z}\right)^{2}-y_{3} \sum_{g} S_{g}^{z}-y_{4} \sum_{g}\left(S_{g}^{z}\right)^{2}. \tag{5}
$$

The indices $f$ and $g$ correspond to the sites belonging to two different sublattices. The
Hamiltonian $H_{0}$ depends on the variational parameters $y_{1}, y_{2}, y_{3}$, and $y_{4}$ which can be
determined from the minimum conditions for the function $F_{0}$,

$$
\frac{\partial F_{0}}{\partial y_{1}}=\frac{\partial F_{0}}{\partial y_{2}}=\frac{\partial F_{0}}{\partial y_{3}}=\frac{\partial F_{0}}{\partial y_{4}}=0. \tag{6}
$$

For $S=1$, the free energy is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
f= & \frac{2 F_{0}}{N z_{1} I}=-t\left\{\ln \left[2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{2} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{t}\right)+1\right]+\ln \left[2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{4} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(\frac{y_{3}}{t}\right)+1\right]\right\}- \\
& -\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}(1+k \varepsilon)-l \varepsilon\left(Q_{1}+Q_{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2} m\left(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\right)+y_{1} \sigma_{1}+y_{3} \sigma_{2}+ \\
& +y_{2} Q_{1}+y_{4} Q_{2}+c \varepsilon^{2}, \tag{7}
\end{aligned}
$$


where
$$y_{1}=\sigma_{2}(1+k \varepsilon)+\sigma_{1} m,\qquad(8)$$

$$y_{2}=y_{4}=l \varepsilon,\qquad(9)$$

$$y_{3}=\sigma_{1}(1+k \varepsilon)+\sigma_{2} m,\qquad(10)$$

$$\sigma_{1}=\left\langle S_{j}^{z}\right\rangle_{0}, \quad \sigma_{2}=\left\langle S_{g}^{z}\right\rangle_{0}, \quad Q_{1}=\left\langle\left(S_{j}^{z}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{0}, \quad Q_{2}=\left\langle\left(S_{g}^{z}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{0},\qquad(11)$$

$$t=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{z_{1} I}, \quad l=\frac{L}{z_{1} I}, \quad k=\frac{K}{I}, \quad c=\frac{C}{N z_{1} I}, \quad m=\frac{M z_{2}}{z_{1} I},\qquad(12)$$
and $z_{1,2}$ denotes the number of first and second neighbours. The self-consistent equations for the magnitudes $\sigma_{1}, \sigma_{2}, Q_{1}, Q_{2}$ , and the equilibrium strain $\varepsilon$ can be obtained from the conditions
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma_{1}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma_{2}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial Q_{1}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial Q_{2}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial \varepsilon}=0,\qquad(13)$$
which yield
$$\sigma_{1}=\frac{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{2} / t} \operatorname{sh}\left(y_{1} / t\right)}{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{2} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(y_{1} / t\right)+1}, \quad \sigma_{2}=\frac{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{4} / t} \operatorname{sh}\left(y_{3} / t\right)}{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{4} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(y_{3} / t\right)+1},\qquad(14)$$

$$Q_{1}=\frac{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{2} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(y_{1} / t\right)}{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{2} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(y_{1} / t\right)+1}, \quad Q_{2}=\frac{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{4} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(y_{3} / t\right)}{2 \mathrm{e}^{y_{4} / t} \operatorname{ch}\left(y_{3} / t\right)+1},\qquad(15)$$

$$\varepsilon=\frac{l\left(Q_{1}+Q_{2}\right)+\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2} k}{2 c}.\qquad(16)$$

### 3. The Formulae for A and F Phases
In the A phase we have $\sigma_{1}=-\sigma_{2}=\sigma$ and therefore from (14) to (16) we obtain
$$\sigma=\frac{2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{sh}[\sigma(m-(1+k \varepsilon)) / t]}{2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{ch}[\sigma(m-(1+k \varepsilon)) / t]+1},\qquad(17)$$

$$\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2 c}\left\{\frac{4 l \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{ch}[\sigma(m-(1+k \varepsilon)) / t]}{2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{ch}[\sigma(m-(1+k \varepsilon)) / t]+1}-\sigma^{2} k\right\}.\qquad(18)$$

As can be seen we have only two coupled equations instead of four. The quantities $Q_{1}$ and $Q_{2}$ can be calulated separately from (15) after solving (17) and (18). For the F phase we have $\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=\sigma$ which gives
$$\sigma=\frac{2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{sh}[\sigma(m+1+k \varepsilon) / t]}{2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{ch}[\sigma(m+1+k \varepsilon) / t]+1},\qquad(19)$$

$$\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2 c}\left\{\frac{4 l \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{ch}[\sigma(m+1+k \varepsilon) / t]}{2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t} \operatorname{ch}[\sigma(m+1+k \varepsilon) / t]+1}+\sigma^{2} k\right\}.\qquad(20)$$

In the paramagnetic (P) phase the magnetization $\sigma$ vanishes, but the equation for the equilibrium strain still remains involved,
$$\varepsilon=\frac{2 l \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t}}{c\left(2 \mathrm{e}^{l \varepsilon / t}+1\right)}.\qquad(21)$$

We should note that the self-consistency of the equations for the equilibrium strain results from the coupling of the single-ion anisotropy with the strain.

## 4. Condition for the $\mathbf{A \to F}$ Phase Transition

The general condition for the $\text{A} \to \text{F}$ phase transition consists of two parts [14]. The first one is the inequality for the free energies of A and F phases at zero temperature,
$$
f_{\mathrm{A}}<f_{\mathrm{F}}. \tag{22}
$$

In our case we have for $T=0$
$$
f_{\mathrm{A}}=1-m-\frac{(2 l-k)^{2}}{4 c}, \tag{23}
$$

$$
f_{\mathrm{F}}=-1-m-\frac{(2 l+k)^{2}}{4 c}, \tag{24}
$$
and inequality (22) takes the form
$$
1+\frac{k l}{c}<0. \tag{25}
$$

If we calculate the energy of the intersublattice coupling $1 + k\varepsilon$ for $T=0$, we obtain $1 + kl/c - k^{2}/(2c)$ for the A phase and $1 + kl/c + k^{2}/(2c)$ for the F phase. Thus, it can be seen that condition (25) is equivalent to the statement that the modulus of the exchange coupling $1 + k\ \varepsilon$ is greater for the A phase than for the F phase.

The second part of the condition may be expressed in terms of the Néel temperature of the A phase and the Curie temperature of the F phase,
$$
T_{\mathrm{N}}<T_{\mathrm{C}}. \tag{26}
$$

This inequality ensures the ferromagnetic ordering at least within some temperature interval below $T_{\mathrm{C}}$. Therefore, both parts ((22) and (26)) of the condition allow us to expect the $\text{A} \to \text{F}$ phase transition somewhere between zero and the critical temperature.

## 5. Results

The mechanism of the $\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}$ phase transition in the model considered here may be described as follows.

The strain $\varepsilon$ may be expressed independent of any approximation if we use the following identity [17]:
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial \varepsilon}=\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial \varepsilon}\right\rangle, \tag{33}
$$
where $F$ is the free energy and $H$ denotes the Hamiltonian. From the condition $\partial F / \partial \varepsilon=0$ we obtain
$$
C \varepsilon=\frac{K\left\langle S_{j}^{z} S_{g}^{z}\right\rangle N z_{1}}{2}+L N\left\langle\left(S^{z}\right)\right\rangle^{2}, \tag{34}
$$
where the brackets \langle\rangle denote now the correlation functions of spins. It is obvious that the correlation functions should diminish their moduli with the rise of temperature. Therefore, the magnetoelastic interactions $K \varepsilon$ and $L \varepsilon$ may become smaller than the interaction $I$ which is constant. If the parameters are chosen in such a way that the terms $K \varepsilon$ and $L \varepsilon$ establish antiferromagnetic ordering, while $I$ prefers ferromagnetic one, we obtain the $\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{F}$ phase transition.

An example of such a behaviour in our model is shown in Fig. 5, where we plotted magnetization, free energy, and strain as functions of temperature. The curves in Fig. 5 are plotted for $k=-1.1, c=1, l=1$, and $m=2$. For these values we obtained the strain $\varepsilon$ of the order of unity. After the scaling $k \rightarrow n k, l \rightarrow n l$, and $c \rightarrow n^{2} c$ we can diminish the order of $\varepsilon$ by $n$ times without any other changes, which may be easily seen from (17) to (20) and (7). Therefore, for instance, if $n=10^{3}$, we obtain $\varepsilon$ of the order of $10^{-3}$ which is physically more sensible.

In Fig. 5 we have $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}>0$ which is really the case of $\mathrm{Mn}_{3} \mathrm{GaC}$ [2]. For FeRh [1] and $\mathrm{Mn}_{2-x} \mathrm{Cr}_{x} \mathrm{Sb}$ [3] this sign should be reversed. This may be easily done if we only change the sign of the parameters $k$ and $l$. As follows from (7), (14) to (16) the only result is the change of the sign of both quantities $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}$ while the magnetization and the free energy remain unchanged.

### 6. Concluding Remarks

It was our purpose to search for the simplest magnetoelastic model which would account for the $\text{A} \rightarrow \text{F}$ spontaneous phase transition. Several other versions of the magnetoelastic Hamiltonians were analyzed by us elsewhere [14, 15] and we see that the model presented here is the most promising one. It is applicable to the two-sub-lattice magnet with axial symmetry. By virtue of the arguments presented in Section 4 we suppose that the model (1) really exhibits this kind of behaviour, not only within the MFA. Therefore, the model could be useful for our future calculations of the nu-cleation and kinematic phenomena in the vicinity of the first-order phase change.

Moreover, the model well describes the behaviour of the Cr-modified $\text{Mn}_2\text{Sb}$. It was reported earlier [18] that this compound exhibits a strong $c$-dependence of the ex-change interaction and only the lattice dimension $c$ undergoes a contraction during the phase transition exactly as in our model.

On the other hand, the $\text{A} \rightarrow \text{F}$ phase change in FeRh is known to be accompanied by an isotropic contraction [1] and because of the cubic symmetry of this compound it cannot be described by the axial Hamiltonian (1). We suppose, however, that the model adequate for FeRh may be easily obtained from Hamiltonian (1) if we only replace the Ising terms by Heisenberg ones and the single-ion anisotropy by the cubic one. In such a case the strain $\varepsilon$ must be isotropic ($\varepsilon = \varepsilon_{xx} + \varepsilon_{yy} + \varepsilon_{zz}$). The reinterpretation of the metamagnetic properties of FeRh in terms of the localized spins may be a matter of interest because the explanation based on the Hubbard Hamiltonian [19] cannot be true as has been experimentally shown [20].
