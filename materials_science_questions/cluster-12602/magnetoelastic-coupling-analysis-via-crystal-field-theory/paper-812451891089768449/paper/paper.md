# Effect of roughness on the surface anisotropy
and magnetostriction of magnetic multilayers

H. Szymczak, M. Rewieński, R. Żuberek *

Institute of Physics of the Polish Academy of Sciences, Al. Lotników 32 / 46, 02-668 Warsaw, Poland

Received 6 April 1994; in revised form 29 June 1994

## Abstract
The effect of interfacial roughness on the effective anisotropy and magnetostriction of the magnetic multilayers has been calculated in frames of dipolar and single-ion approximations. The results indicate that the surface contribution to the effective anisotropy and magnetostriction could become, in some cases, very important.

## 1. Introduction
The discovery of perpendicular anisotropy in mul- tilayered magnetic materials opened new possibilities for memory applications, particularly for erasable magneto-optic and perpendicular recording applica- tions. The perpendicular anisotropy is attributed to the interfacial surface anisotropy which arises due to a different environment of the surface atoms as compared to the bulk ones. Strong experimental evidence for the surface anisotropy was found by Gradman [1]. The discovery of surface anisotropy in thin films and multilayers has generated interest in the relationship between this effect and surface mag- netostriction. The surface magnetostriction has been observed experimentally in multilayers by means of the strain modulated ferromagnetic resonance [2] (see also review papers [3-5] and references therein) and in amorphous alloys [6] by means of the spin polar- ization studies of secondary electrons emitted from the surface of the samples. At present it is not clear which mechanism is responsible for surface magne- tostriction. In the case of the surface anisotropy it is well established that, at least, three mechanisms give significant contribution to the resulting anisotropy. These are: single-ion mechanism, dipole-dipole mechanism and the effect of surface roughness. The importance of the interfacial roughness in the result- ing magnetic anisotropy was theoretically suggested by Bruno [7].

Up to now detailed theoretical calculations of the surface magnetostriction have been performed only in frames of the dipolar model [8]. The purpose of this paper is to explore the consequence of the commonly accepted fact that magnetostriction and magnetic anisotropy have the same origin. We will calculate the effect of the interface roughness on the magnetostriction of the magnetic multilayers and particularly on the surface magnetostriction.

In recent years, a number of rare-earth-transi- tion-metal compositionally modulated films have been studied ([9] and references in review paper [10]). It has been shown that in the case of rare-earth ions with orbital angular momentum the major con-

## 2. Model of the surface roughness

In the presented calculations the model of the interface roughness proposed by Bruno [7] is adopted for a film with a finite thickness, $t$, while in [7] the thickness of the film is, in fact, not taken into account. The roughness is characterized by two sta- tistical parameters $\sigma$ and $\xi$ (see Fig. 1), where $\sigma$ is the average deviation from the reference plane and $\xi$ is the correlation length (the average lateral size of the flat areas on the surface). In this model the roughness on the top of the film is 'in phase' with the roughness on the bottom. It can be shown that both surface anisotropy and surface magnetostriction are not dependent on the roughness 'phase shift' between top and the bottom. Calculation, although simple, are too long to be reproduced here.

## 3. Calculation of the magnetostatic energy

### 3.1. Magnetization normal to the film

The magnetostatic energy of the film with magne- tization vector perpendicular to the surface has been calculated using formalism developed in the theory of domain structure adopted to study the demagnetiz- ing energy in imperfect films by Yafet et al. [12] and by Bruno [7]. Following the method developed by Bruno [7] it is easy to show that the density of magnetostatic energy of the film with the magnetiza- tion normal to the surface has the following form

$$E_{\text {mag }}^{\perp}=2 \pi M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(1-(1-f) \sigma / t),\qquad(1)$$

with

$$
\begin{aligned}
f= & \frac{4^{2}}{\pi^{4} \sigma} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left\{1-\exp \left(-P_{k l} t\right)\right. \\
& \left.+\exp \left(-P_{k l}(t-2 \sigma)\right)-\exp \left(-P_{k l} 2 \sigma\right)\right\} \\
& /\left\{P_{k l}(2 k+1)^{2}(2 l+1)^{2}\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$P_{k l}=(\pi / \xi)\left[(2 k+1)^{2}+(2 l+1)^{2}\right]^{0.5}.\qquad(3)$$

The lowest-order term of the density of magnetoelas- tic energy has the form

$$E_{\mathrm{me}}=B_{i j k l} \alpha_{i} \alpha_{j} \varepsilon_{k l},\qquad(4)$$

where $B_{i j k l}$ are magnetoelastic tensor components, $\alpha_{i}$ is the direction cosines of magnetization $M$ and $\varepsilon_{k l}$ are components of strain tensor.

![](./images/812451891089768449_3.jpg)

Fig. 1. Schematic illustration of surface roughness.

Using expression (1) the components of the magnetoelastic tensor are given by:

$$
B_{3333}=\frac{\partial E_{\text {mag }}^{\perp}}{\partial \varepsilon_{33}}=-2 \pi M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(1-g) \sigma / t, \quad(5)
$$

$$
B_{3311}=\frac{\partial E_{\mathrm{mag}}^{\perp}}{\partial \varepsilon_{11}}=-2 \pi M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(1-2 f+g) \sigma / t, \quad(6)
$$

where
$$
\begin{aligned}
g= & \frac{4^{2}}{\pi^{4} \sigma} \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\left\{t \exp \left(-P_{k l} t\right)\right. \\
& -(t-2 \sigma) \exp \left(-P_{k l}(t-2 \sigma)\right) \\
& \left.+2 \sigma \exp \left(-P_{k l} 2 \sigma\right)\right\} \\
& /\left\{(2 k+1)^{2}(2 l+1)^{2}\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In order to compare the results of the calculations with the experiment it is necessary to express the magnetoelastic energy density, $E_{\mathrm{me}}$, in relation to the components of the uniaxial stress $\sigma_{i j}$:

$$
E_{\mathrm{me}}=M_{i j k l} \alpha_{i} \alpha_{j} \sigma_{k l}. \quad(8)
$$

In the case of components $M_{3333}$ and $M_{3311}$ the following relations with $B_{3333}$ and $B_{3311}$ exist:

Fig. 3. Component of the magnetoelastic tensor $B_{3333}^{\mathrm{s}} / 2 \pi M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}$ for $\xi=10$ and $30 \AA, t=10$ and $30 \AA$.

$$
M_{3333}=\frac{\left(c_{1111}+c_{1122}\right) B_{3333}-2 c_{1122} B_{3311}}{\left(c_{1111}-c_{1122}\right)\left(c_{1111}+2 c_{1122}\right)}, \quad(9)

$$
M_{3311}=\frac{\left(c_{1111}+c_{1122}\right) B_{3311}+c_{1122} B_{3333}}{\left(c_{1111}-c_{1122}\right)\left(c_{1111}+2 c_{1122}\right)}, \quad(10)
$$

where $c_{i j k l}$ are components of the elastic tensor.

Usually the effective magnetoelastic tensor $B_{i j k l}$ is presented in the following form:

$$
B_{i j k l}=B_{i j k l}^{\mathrm{v}}+\frac{2}{t} B_{i j k l}^{\mathrm{s}}, \quad(11)
$$

where the first term describes the bulk magnetoelastic interactions and the second the surface magnetoelastic interactions.

It results from Eqs. (5)-(7) that in frames of the presented model

$$
\lim _{t \rightarrow \infty} B_{3333}(t)=0, \quad(12)
$$

$$
\lim _{t \rightarrow \infty} B_{3311}(t)=0. \quad(13)
$$

It means that expressions (5) and (6) describe the pure surface contribution to $B_{i j k l}$ due to the surface roughness. Taking into account Eq. (11) one should present the surface magnetoelastic tensor $B_{i j k l}^{\mathrm{s}}$ in the form:

$$
B_{i j k l}^{\mathrm{s}}=B_{i j k l} t / 2. \quad(14)
$$

The surface anisotropy constant due to the roughness

### 3.2. Magnetization in the plane of the film

The easiest way to estimate a stray field energy
for the geometry of the film given in Fig. 1 with in
plane magnetization is to replace the regions simulat-
ing the roughness with suitable ellipsoids. The de-
magnetizing energy of the general ellipsoid of vol-
ume $V$ is given by:

$$
E_{\mathrm{mag}}=1 / 2 V M N M \tag{17}
$$

where $N$ is the demagnetizing factor tensor [14]. The
values of $N$ have been calculated assuming that
$\sigma \ll \xi$ and neglecting the interactions between the
ellipsoids. In this approximation the density of mag-
netostatic energy for magnetization parallel to the
plane $(M \parallel x)$ has the following form:

$$
E_{\mathrm{mag}}^{\| \prime}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2} M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2} \frac{\xi_{y} \sigma}{\xi_{x}^{2}}, \tag{18}
$$

$\xi_{x}$ and $\xi_{y}$ are the $\xi$ values in the $x$ and $y$ direction,
respectively.

Consequently the components of the surface mag-
netoelastic tensor $B_{i j k l}$ have the form $(\xi_{x}=\xi_{y}=\xi)$:

$$
B_{1111}^{\mathrm{s}}=-\pi^{2} M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2} \frac{\sigma}{\xi} \frac{t}{2}, \tag{19}
$$

$$
B_{1122}^{\mathrm{s}}=B_{1133}^{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2} M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2} \frac{\sigma}{\xi} \frac{t}{2}. \tag{20}
$$

$M_{1111}^{\mathrm{s}}$

The calculated values of the component $M_{1111}^{\mathrm{s}}$ for polycrystalline iron and nickel for various $\sigma$ and $\xi$ are given in Figs. 5 and 6. Experimental values of $M_{1111}^{\mathrm{s}}$ had been presented in previous papers [3,4,13]; they are equal to:

Therefore contributions to the surface magnetostric- tion due to the surface roughness, described by the component $M_{1111}^{\mathrm{s}}$ should not be neglected, especially for Fe-based multilayers. Also the interfacial rough- ness contribution to the surface anisotropy is the highest for Fe-based multilayers [7].

### 4. Crystal field effects
Detailed studies of the rare-earth-transition metal amorphous compositionally modulated films [9] have shown that the single-ion anisotropy arising from the crystal field effects is the major contribution to the perpendicular anisotropy for this class of materials. The perpendicular anisotropy for the compositionally modulated amorphous structure is related to the anisotropy of the distribution function describing local directions of the crystal field acting on rare-earth ions. The anisotropy of the distribution function can also arise from the presence of the surface.

We are going to explore this idea to the surface magnetostriction. It will be assumed that the crystal has regular symmetry. The presence of the surface is equivalent to the cutting of some of the ligands (creating the crystal field acting on rare-earth ions) and thus to a lowering of the crystal field from regular to axial symmetry. The single-ion anisotropy due to lowered symmetry at the surface can be expressed, in lowest approximation, by the second order spherical harmonic
$$
E_{\mathrm{an}}=B_{2}^{0} Y_{2}^{0}(\theta, \phi). \quad (22)
$$

To calculate the effect of deformation on the $E_{\mathrm{an}}$, the Orbach approximation [15] will be used:
$$
\left|\frac{\partial B_{n}^{m}(r)}{\partial r}\right| \approx\left|B_{n}^{m}(r) \cdot r\right|. \quad (23)
$$
where $r$ denotes the position of ligand ions. This approximation was used previously [15] to calculate the spin-lattice relaxation times for rare-earth ions

In frames of this approximation it is easy to obtain following estimation
$$\left|B_{3333}^{\mathrm{s}}\right| \approx\left|K_{\mathrm{u}}\right| a, \tag{24}$$
$$B_{1111}^{\mathrm{s}} \approx \frac{\sigma}{\xi} B_{3333}^{\mathrm{s}}, \tag{25}$$
where $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ is the uniaxial anisotropy for rare-earth ions on the surface and $a$ is the lattice constant (or distance between the rare-earth ions).

Taking for $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ value observed in uniaxial rare-earth compounds
$$K_{\mathrm{u}} \sim 10^{8} \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{3}, \tag{26}$$
one can estimate
$$B_{3333}^{\mathrm{s}} \sim 10^{2}-10^{3} \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}. \tag{27}$$

The presented above rough estimations of the component of the surface magnetoelastic tensor indicate that for rare-earth multilayers one should expect to observe a rather high value of surface magnetostriction. Unfortunately, there is no experimental data to compare with above predictions. Nevertheless it seems to be unquestionable that single-ion mechanism of surface magnetostriction (and anisotropy) should be taken into account when considering rare-earth-based multilayers.

## 5. Conclusions

We have shown that the interfacial roughness should be considered as possible source of surface anisotropy and magnetostriction. The effect of roughness seems to be especially important for rare-earth-based multilayers. For such materials this effect has the single-ion origin. It has been evidenced that for Fe-based multilayers the effect of roughness due to the dipolar fields is important and cannot be neglected.
