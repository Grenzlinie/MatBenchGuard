# Acoustic paramagnetic resonance of $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ in potassium magnesium fluoride

By H. M. ROSENBERG AND J. K. WIGMORE*

The Clarendon Laboratory, University of Oxford

(Communicated by B. Bleaney, F.R.S.-Received 29 April 1967)

We have observed changes in both the real and imaginary parts of the propagation factor for an acoustic wave travelling in the paramagnetic crystal, $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}: 0.005 \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$, near resonance. The measured values of these two quantities are found to be related in the manner predicted by Kramers-Kronig theory. From the observed absorption and dispersion, we have been able to deduce the coefficients of the quadrupolar magnetoelastic tensor, $\left|G_{11}\right|$ and $\left|G_{44}\right|$, to be 58 and $39 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ respectively. The experimental figures are in good agreement with values calculated microscopically using the point charge model of the crystal field.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

This paper describes acoustic paramagnetic resonance experiments performed on single crystals of $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}: \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$, with a view to determining the spin-phonon coupling constants of the $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ ion in a cubic crystal field.

The formalism of acoustic paramagnetic resonance (hereafter abbreviated to a.p.r.) is similar to that of electron paramagnetic resonance (e.p.r.) in that both techniques detect transitions between the lowest levels of the ion in its crystalline environment. Instead of an electromagnetic probe, however, a.p.r. utilizes a train of monochromatic, polarized elastic waves to excite the resonances. From the magnitude of the resonance absorption it is possible to measure the strength of the interaction between the spin system and a phonon of specified frequency, polarization and wave vector. This information is of particular interest for the quantitative interpretation of spin-lattice relaxation times, which contain an average of the interaction over all the phonon modes of the crystal. A second technique that may measure the individual coefficients of the spin-phonon coupling, is that of e.p.r. under stress (Feher 1964). The values obtained by this method, however, are not always consistent between different specimens of the same material (Tucker 1966), presumably because their derivation requires a knowledge of the strain field in the region of the paramagnetic ion, and this may be affected greatly by dislocations and other defects. A.p.r. does not suffer from this disadvantage since the absorption coefficient of the elastic waves is independent of their amplitude.

The environment of the $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ ion in $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}$ has point group $O_{h} . \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ in a field of this symmetry has previously been investigated by a.p.r. using $\mathrm{MgO}$ as the host crystal (Shiren $1962 a$ ), but $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}$ is a superior material for this work for several reasons. First, it melts at $1070{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, compared with $2700{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for $\mathrm{MgO}$, and it can therefore be readily annealed. Secondly, and again because of its relatively low melting point, $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}$ can be prepared in small quantities, of the order of a few grams. It is then economical to use very pure starting materials so that the doping of the final

### 2. THEORY
The a.p.r. technique consists of measuring the change in propagation factor, $\exp \mathrm{i}\{\omega t-(\alpha-\mathrm{i}\beta)x\}$, for the amplitude of an acoustic wave travelling in a paramagnetic crystal, as the splitting of the low-lying paramagnetic energy levels is varied with an external magnetic field through the region where it is resonant with the energy $\hbar\omega$ of the ultrasonic phonons. $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the wave, $\beta$ is the absorption coefficient and $\alpha$ is related to the phase velocity $v$, through the relation $v=\omega/\alpha$. Both $\beta$ and $v$ are observed experimentally. The absorption coefficient must be rewritten
$$\beta=\beta_0+\beta(H), \tag{1}$$
where $\beta_0$ is the field independent background attenuation due for example to phonon-phonon interactions and to phase effects produced by non-parallelism of the end surfaces, and $\beta(H)$ is the contribution from the a.p.r. Similarly $\alpha$ is of the form
$$\alpha=\alpha_0+\alpha(H). \tag{2}$$

If the spin system is not saturated, $\alpha(H)$ and $\beta(H)$ are connected by the Kramers-Kronig theory (see, for example, Abragam 1961). A.p.r. is a phenomenon to which this theory has not previously been applied. The Kramers-Kronig relations are not in general analytical, but we note that for the Lorentzian lineshape (which is the lineshape applicable to the resonances of $KMgF_3:Ni^{2+}$ (see figure 2)) $\alpha(H)$ and $\beta(H)$ are of the form
$$\alpha(H)=A\frac{(H-H_0)/\delta}{1+(H-H_0)^2/\delta^2}, \tag{3}$$

$$\beta(H)=A\frac{1}{1+(H-H_0)^2/\delta^2}, \tag{4}$$
where $\delta$ is halfwidth at half maximum of $\beta(H)$, $H_0$ is the magnetic field at resonance, and $A$ is a constant which defines the maximum of the absorption function and which may be directly related to the strength of the spin-phonon coupling for the spin system.

The e.p.r. of $KMgF_3:Ni^{2+}$ is represented by the effective spin Hamiltonian
$$\mathscr{H}_s=g\beta_m\mathbf{H}.\mathbf{S}+\sum_{n}\{A_{\parallel}I_zS_z+A_{\perp}(I_xS_x+I_yS_y)\} \tag{5}$$
with $\mathbf{S}=1$, where $\beta_m$ is the Bohr magneton, $\mathbf{H}$ the mgnetic field and $g$ is the $g$-factor of the resonance, equal to 2·2797 (Hall, Hayes, Stevenson & Wilkens 1963)

and isotropic because of the cubic environment of the $Ni^{2+}$ ion. The second term describes the transferred hyperfine structure due to the $^{19}F$ nuclei of the six nearest neighbour fluorine ions, over which the summation is taken. Hall *et al.* (1963) give $A_{\parallel }=65\cdot 9\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, and $A_{\perp}=25\cdot 8\times 10^{-4}\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. In our experiments this hyperfine structure was barely detectable because of the large inhomogeneous broadening of the a.p.r. lines. We shall therefore take the hyperfine term to be included in the phenomenological lineshape.

The effect of applying a static strain to the crystal is described by the spin-lattice Hamiltonian, for an effective spin S,
$$
\mathscr{H}_{S L}=\beta_{m} \mathbf{H} \cdot \delta g \cdot \mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S} \cdot d \cdot \mathbf{S}
\tag{6}
$$
$\delta g$ is the small change in the $g$-factor due to a strain $\epsilon$, and these two quantities, both second rank tensors, are related through a fourth rank magnetoelastic tensor. In the Voigt notation (subscripts 1,2,3,4,5,6 represent $xx, yy, zz, yz, zx, xy$ respectively)
$$
\delta g_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{6} F_{i j} \epsilon_{j}.
\tag{7}
$$
Similarly $d$, representing a small change in zero-field splitting, is connected to $\epsilon$ by
$$
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{6} G_{i j} \epsilon_{j}.
\tag{8}
$$

Group theoretical arguments (Shulman, Wyluda & Anderson, 1957) show that when the local crystal field has cubic symmetry in the absence of strain, the number of independent coefficients of $F$ and $G$ reduce to 3 and 2 respectively. The resultant matrices are
$$
\left(\begin{array}{cccccc}
F_{11} & F_{12} & F_{12.} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
F_{12} & F_{11} & F_{12} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
F_{12} & F_{12} & F_{11} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & F_{44} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & F_{44} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & F_{44}
\end{array}\right)
$$
and
$$
\left(\begin{array}{cccccc}
G_{11} & -\frac{1}{2} G_{11} & -\frac{1}{2} G_{11} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
-\frac{1}{2} G_{11} & G_{11} & -\frac{1}{2} G_{11} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
-\frac{1}{2} G_{11} & -\frac{1}{2} G_{11} & G_{11} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & G_{44} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & G_{44} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & G_{44}
\end{array}\right)
$$

The quadrupolar term $\mathbf{S}.d.\mathbf{S}$ in the spin-lattice Hamiltonian (6) dominates the dipolar expression $\mathbf{H}.\delta g.\mathbf{S}$ by approximately two orders of magnitude (Mattuck & Strandberg 1960). Thus the dipolar interaction is evident only for spin systems with $\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{2}$, where the matrix elements of $\mathbf{S}.d.\mathbf{S}$ are identically zero. For the present case of $KMgF_{3}:Ni^{2+}$ with $\mathbf{S}=1$, the quadrupolar term accounts for $99\%$ of the interaction and our measurements were insufficiently sensitive for us to detect any possible dipolar contribution.

We now wish to express the constant $A$, of equations (3) and (4), in terms of $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$.

The propagation of the acoustic energy flux $E$, will be given by the expression
$$E=E_{0} \exp 2 \mathrm{i}\{\omega t-(\alpha-\mathrm{i} \beta) x\}$$
and on differentiation this yields
$$\Delta E / E=2 \beta(H) \Delta x.\tag{9}$$

We may obtain a further expression for $\Delta E / E$, as follows. If $n$ is the net number of spins per unit volume that are absorbing energy, and $U$ is the transition probability of a single excitation, then $\Delta E$ is given by $n \hbar \omega U \Delta x$. $\Delta E$ is the energy absorbed per unit cross section as the acoustic wave travels a distance $\Delta x$. The total energy flux is $\frac{1}{2} \epsilon^{2} \rho v^{3}$, where $\epsilon$ is the acoustic strain amplitude, $\rho$ the density of the dielectric, and $v$ the appropriate velocity of sound for the phonon mode used, and hence
$$\Delta E / E=\frac{2 n \hbar \omega U}{\epsilon^{2} \rho v^{3}} \Delta x.\tag{10}$$

Comparing (9) and (10) we see that
$$\beta(H)=n \hbar \omega U /\left(\epsilon^{2} \rho v^{3}\right).\tag{11}$$

It is assumed (and experimentally ensured) that there is no saturation of the spin system. The transition probability $U$, is obtained from the time-dependent perturbation expression$\dagger$
$$U=\frac{\pi}{2 g \beta_{m} \hbar}\left|\left\langle\left|\mathscr{H}_{S L}\right|\right\rangle\right|^{2} f(H)\tag{12}$$
where the perturbation is $\mathscr{H}_{S L} \cos \omega t$ acting between the states of the effective spin Hamiltonian (5). We recall that $\mathscr{H}_{S L}$ refers to a static straining of the crystal.

$f(H)$ is the lineshape of the resonance, which for a Lorentzian line is normalized at its maximum to a value $1 /(\pi \delta)$, where $\delta$ is the half-width at half height of the resonance line, in Oe.

The condition for the validity of this perturbation procedure is that the spin states $M_{Z}=|+1\rangle,|0\rangle,|-1\rangle$ must not be appreciably mixed by the acoustic interaction i.e.
$$G_{i j} \epsilon \leqslant g \beta_{m} H.\tag{13}$$

This criterion is satisfied in the system $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}: \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$.

The final expression for $\beta(H)$, using (11) and (12) is
$$\beta(H)=\frac{\pi n \omega\left|\left\langle\left|\mathscr{H}_{S L}\right|\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{2 g \beta_{m} \epsilon^{2} \rho v^{3}} f(H).\tag{14}$$

We have chosen to derive the absorption function $\beta(H)$, rather than the dispersion $\alpha(H)$, from the spin-lattice Hamiltonian, regarding the latter as obtainable through the Kramers-Kronig relations. It is also possible, at least in principle to calculate the dispersion function directly. Unfortunately, however, the mathematical complexity is so great that only the simplest case has so far been treated, that of longitudinal acoustic propagation along a chain of identical atoms, by Jacobson & Stevens (1963).

$\dagger$ Expression (12) is easily obtained from the more common form in terms of the frequency $\nu: U=\frac{1}{4} \hbar^{2}|\langle|\mathscr{H}|\rangle|^{2} g(\nu)$, derived, for example, by Pake (1962).

Their theory does indeed give the characteristic dispersion lineshape with one maximum and one minimum, although it is difficult to extend into a model having arbitrary phonon direction and polarization in a three-dimensional lattice.

In order to use (14) we must calculate $\langle|\mathscr{H}_{SL}|\rangle$. Since only certain symmetry axes of a crystal are acoustic flux axes, it is simpler to modify the original spin-lattice Hamiltonian (6) for the particular acoustic mode and magnetic field geometry of interest, than it is to transform a general matrix element expressed in terms of the Euler angles and an arbitrary strain direction. We shall illustrate the method by evaluating $\langle|\mathscr{H}_{SL}|\rangle$ for the acoustic strain directions $\langle 001 \rangle$ and $\langle 101 \rangle$ with the magnetic field in the $xz$ plane and at an angle $\theta$ with the $\langle 001 \rangle$ direction.

For the case of $\epsilon_{\|}\langle 001 \rangle$, $\epsilon_{zz} = \epsilon$; $\epsilon_{xx} = \epsilon_{xy} = \dots = 0$. Then the spin-lattice Hamiltonian becomes
$$
\mathscr{H}_{SL} = G_{11} \epsilon \{ (S_z^c)^2 - \frac{1}{2}[(S_x^c)^2 + (S_y^c)^2] \}, \tag{15}
$$
where the superfix ' $c$ ' indicates that the operators are defined with respect to the symmetry axes of the crystal. The Hamiltonian must be transformed into the coordinate system in which $\mathbf{H}$ is parallel to $z$, by a rotation of $\theta$, so that
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
S_z^c &= S_z \cos \theta + S_x \sin \theta, \\
S_x^c &= S_x \cos \theta - S_z \sin \theta, \\
S_y^c &= S_y,
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{16}
$$
where $S_x, S_y, S_z$ are the spin operators in the new coordinate system. $\mathscr{H}_{SL}$ transformed becomes
$G_{11} \epsilon \{ S_z^2 (\cos^2 \theta - \frac{1}{2} \sin^2 \theta) + S_x^2 (\sin^2 \theta - \frac{1}{2} \cos^2 \theta) - \frac{1}{2} S_y^2 + \frac{3}{2} (S_x S_z + S_z S_x) \sin \theta \cos \theta \}$
and the interaction matrix $\langle|\mathscr{H}_{SL}|\rangle$ is
$$
G_{11} \epsilon \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{3}{4} \cos^2 \theta - \frac{1}{4} & \frac{3}{2 \sqrt{2}} \sin \theta \cos \theta & \frac{3}{4} \sin^2 \theta \\
\frac{3}{2 \sqrt{2}} \sin \theta \cos \theta & \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{2} \cos^2 \theta & - \frac{3}{2 \sqrt{2}} \sin \theta \cos \theta \\
\frac{3}{4} \sin^2 \theta & - \frac{3}{2 \sqrt{2}} \sin \theta \cos \theta & \frac{3}{4} \cos^2 \theta - \frac{1}{4}
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{17}
$$

For an acoustic strain parallel to the $\langle 101 \rangle$ axis,
$$
\epsilon_{xz} = 2 \epsilon_{xx} = 2 \epsilon_{zz} = \epsilon; \quad \epsilon_{yy} = \epsilon_{yz} = \epsilon_{yx} = 0.
$$
$\mathscr{H}_{SL}$ is now
$$
\frac{1}{4} G_{11} \epsilon \{ (S_x^c)^2 + (S_z^c)^2 - 2 (S_y^c)^2 \} + G_{44} \epsilon \{ S_x^c S_z^c + S_z^c S_x^c \} \tag{18}
$$
and applying the angular transformations as above, yields the new interaction matrix:
$$
\epsilon \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{8} G_{11} - G_{44} \sin \theta \cos \theta & G_{44} \frac{\cos^2 \theta - \sin^2 \theta}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{3}{8} G_{11} + G_{44} \sin \theta \cos \theta \\
G_{44} \frac{\cos^2 \theta - \sin^2 \theta}{\sqrt{2}} & - \frac{1}{4} G_{11} + 2 G_{44} \sin \theta \cos \theta & G_{44} \frac{\sin^2 \theta - \cos^2 \theta}{\sqrt{2}} \\
\frac{3}{8} G_{11} + G_{44} \sin \theta \cos \theta & G_{44} \frac{\sin^2 \theta - \cos^2 \theta}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{8} G_{11} - G_{44} \sin \theta \cos \theta
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{19}
$$

It should be remarked that in a system which has an isotropic \( g \) factor, the dipolar term \( \beta \mathbf{H}.\delta \boldsymbol{g}.\mathbf{S} \) gives identically the same angular dependence for the \( \Delta M = \pm 1 \) transitions as does the quadrupolar expression, whatever the axis of acoustic strain. The reason for this can be seen most simply by writing out the general rotation of coordinates, of which (16) is a special case:

$$
S_x^c = aS_x + bS_y + cS_z,
$$

$$
S_y^c = dS_x + eS_y + fS_z
$$

$$
S_z^c = hS_x + jS_y + kS_z.
$$

If the \( g \)-factor is isotropic, and only when this is so,

$$
H_x^c = cH_z,
$$

$$
H_y^c = fH_z
$$

$$
H_z^c = kH_z;
$$

\( a,b...k \) are the transformation coefficients, and \( H_z \) is the applied magnetic field. Then the dipolar matrix elements of the \( \Delta M = \pm 1 \) transitions are given by operators like \( S_xH_z \) and \( S_yH_z \), and it is clear that these terms have exactly the same coefficients \( (a...k) \times (a...k) \) as do \( S_xS_z \), and \( S_yS_z \) which are responsible for the quadrupolar \( \Delta M = \pm 1 \) matrix elements. It is therefore impossible in a cubic material to distinguish between the dipolar and quadrupolar mechanisms from the angular dependence of the \( \Delta M = \pm 1 \) absorption.

It is worth noting that the off-diagonal elements of the interaction matrices (17) and (19) are measured by the a.p.r. technique, whereas the strain e.p.r. experiments (Feher 1964) observe the magnetic field shifts given by the diagonal elements.

### 3. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

Our measurements were taken at the three temperatures \( 20 \cdot 4, 4 \cdot 2 \) and \( 1 \cdot 7 ^\circ \text{K} \), and at a frequency of \( 9 \cdot 4 \, \text{Gc/s} \). These low temperatures were necessary because at the high frequency used, \( \beta_0 \) increases rapidly with increasing temperature and above about \( 40 ^\circ \text{K} \) it becomes sufficiently large to prevent completely the observation of hypersonic waves in \( \text{KMgF}_3 \). The monochromatic phonons are generated by a quartz rod transducer sited in the electric field of a microwave coaxial re-entrant cavity. They are introduced into the \( \text{KMgF}_3 \) specimen through an acoustic 'bond' of silicone fluid MS 200/500.

The whole a.p.r. spectrometer is shown schematically in figure 1. It is a conventional pulse echo apparatus (Dobrov 1964) but with the addition of a phase-sensitive bridge consisting of a precision attenuator, calibrated phase shifter, and second microwave cavity containing a second quartz transducer which acts as a reference rod. (Guermeur, Joffrin, Levelut & Penné 1964). A microwave pulse is split by the magic tee, half going to the cavity containing the transducer for the \( \text{KMgF}_3 \), and half to the reference cavity. The length of the three crystals, quartz transducer, quartz reference, and \( \text{KMgF}_3 \) specimens, are very carefully cut so that the time

The peak voltage of a $KMgF_3$ echo is selected by means of delay circuits and an integrating network, and is displayed on an $X-Y$ recorder. The system also has the advantage that the integrating network improves the signal-to-noise ratio of the resonance by a factor of approximately ten. The sensitivity is such that a $\beta(H)$ of $10^{-2}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ can just be detected and this corresponds to a relative change in phase velocity $\Delta v/v$, of $5\times10^{-8}$.

Careful preparations of specimens is an important aspect of a.p.r. experiments. First, the material must be an annealed single crystal as free as possible from point defects and dislocations; hypersound is strongly scattered by any such structure. It is only recently that crystals of $KMgF_3$ large enough to permit acoustic investigations have been prepared (Hukin 1966) in this laboratory. In addition, the specimens must be oriented along one of the main symmetry axes of the crystal to better than a degree, and they must be polished flat and parallel to about $\frac{1}{2}\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is the acoustic wavelength which is approximately $4000\,\text{\AA}$ at $9.4\,\text{Gc/s}$. This is accomplished by waxing the specimen into a hole in the centre of a steel disk with parallel faces, and lapping down the relatively soft $KMgF_3$ until continuous interference fringes can be seen across the steel and $KMgF_3$ surface. Finally the specimen is bonded on to a quartz transducer. The crystals are held in a jig, and a weight of approximately 1 kg is applied through steel ball bearings to press the faces together.

### 4. ACOUSTIC PARAMAGNETIC RESONANCE SPECTRA

A.p.r. has the advantage over e.p.r. in that the selection rules permit both lines to be observed since the interaction of the acoustic probe with the spins has a quadrupolar form. In e.p.r. the $\Delta M=\pm2$ is a forbidden transition.

During one experiment of exceptionally high sensitivity, we were able to detect a change in signal velocity on resonance, that is, a change in the arrival time of an acoustic pulse that had propagated through the $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}$. The relation between phase and signal velocity in a strongly absorbing medium cannot be determined analytically for the general case (Brillouin 1960). It has been evaluated however by Baerwald (1930) in the limit

$$
x \gg 1 / \beta(H),
$$

where $x$ is the path length of the wave in the medium. This condition was roughly satisfied in our experiment with $\beta(H)_{\text {max. }}=2 \cdot 3 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}, x=0 \cdot 78 \mathrm{~cm}$.

Baerwald found

$$
t-t_{0}=\frac{x}{2 \pi \delta} \beta(H), \tag{21}
$$

where $t-t_{0}$ is the change in arrival time of the pulse and $\delta$ (Mc/s) is the linewidth of the transition for an isolated ion, that is, equal to the observed linewidth if the resonance is homogeneously broadened.

In our experiments we have measured the change in arrival time to be $(5 \pm 1) \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{~s}$. The observed resonance lines of $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}: \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ are strain broadened, and therefore in order to make a comparison between our measured result for $t-t_{0}$ and Baerwald's theory, we were forced to calculate a value for $\delta$. We used the expression of Loudon (1960), modified by Shiren (1962b)

$$
\delta=1 \cdot 16 \gamma^{2} \hbar N \mathrm{Mc} / \mathrm{s} \tag{22}
$$

where $N$ is the concentration of $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$, and $\gamma$ is equal to $19 \cdot 2 \mathrm{Mc} / \mathrm{s}$ Oe for this ion. Under our conditions this gives $\delta=9 \times 10^{6} \mathrm{Mc} / \mathrm{s}$. If this figure is substituted into

80
H. M. Rosenberg and J. K. Wigmore

(21), a value of $4 \cdot 2 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{~s}$ is obtained for $t-t_{0}$. In addition to this satisfactory quantitative agreement with Baerwald's theory, we were able to confirm that the lineshape of the changes in signal velocity did indeed follow that of the absorption function, rather than of the phase or group velocity.

## 5. The magnetoelastic coefficients $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$

The results for $\beta(H)$ in table 1 enable us to evaluate with considerable overdetermination, the moduli of $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$ and their relative sign. But because the absorption coefficient contains the square of a matrix element, it is not possible to determine absolute signs from a.p.r. measurements. Values of $|G_{11}|$ can be inferred independently from the $\Delta M= \pm 1$ and $\Delta M= \pm 2$ absorption lines with $\epsilon$ parallel to $\langle 001\rangle$. We obtain figures of $59 \pm 5 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ and $56 \pm 5 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ respectively. From the $\Delta M= \pm 1$ transition with $\epsilon$ along the $\langle 101\rangle$ axis, we find the value of $G_{44}$ to be $39 \pm 5 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$.

Because the measurements of $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$ were necessarily made using different specimens of $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}$, it was important to have an independent check on the ratio $G_{11}/G_{44}$. This was obtained from the $\Delta M= \pm 2$ absorption with $\epsilon$ parallel to $\langle 101\rangle$. The angular dependence of this line has been shown in figure $5b$. It is possible to determine the ratio of $G_{11}/G_{44}$ with great accuracy from these results because the value of the absorption accidentally becomes zero at $\theta=-45^{\circ}$. Then from (19)

$$
\frac{9}{64} G_{11}^{2}+\frac{6}{8} G_{11} G_{44} \sin \theta \cos \theta+G_{44}^{2} \sin ^{2} \theta \cos ^{2} \theta=0, \tag{23}
$$

whence $G_{11}/G_{44}=1 \cdot 33 \pm 0 \cdot 05$. This is to be compared with $1 \cdot 49 \pm 0 \cdot 3$ from the individual measurements of $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$. The figures are in very reasonable agreement, suggesting that the a.p.r. measurements are consistent between different specimens.

Observations on this $\Delta M= \pm 2$ transition also yield the relative sign of $G_{11}G_{44}$. The l.h.s. of (23) is clearly different for positive and negative values of a given angle $\alpha$, the maximum occurring at $\theta \sim +45^{\circ}$ or $\theta \sim -45^{\circ}$ depending on the sign of $G_{11}G_{44}$. From figure 5, the maximum is at $\theta=+45^{\circ}$ and $G_{11}G_{44}$ therefore has positive sign.

For comparison with these results, we have calculated $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$ on the basis of the point charge crystal field model. Since $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ in a cubic field has an orbital singlet lying lowest (representation $A_{2}$), the effective spin is equal to the real spin, and we were able to follow the method of Pryce (1950) to derive the spin-lattice Hamiltonian. This is a formal perturbation treatment but with the spin operators $S_{x}, S_{y}, S_{z}$ regarded as non-commuting algebraic variables. The unperturbed Hamiltonian of the system is

$$
\mathscr{H}_{0}=\mathscr{H}_{F}+\mathscr{H}_{C}+\mathscr{H}_{LL}+\mathscr{H}_{SS} \tag{24}
$$

where the suffixes, $F$, $C$, $LL$, and $SS$ refer to free ion, crystal field, orbit-orbit, and spin-spin interactions respectively, and the perturbation is taken as

$$
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}=\beta_{m} \mathbf{H}.(\mathbf{L}+2 \mathbf{S})+\lambda \mathbf{L}. \mathbf{S}+\mathscr{H}_{OL} \tag{25}
$$

representing the magnetic field, spin-orbit, and orbit-lattice interactions respectively. Then to third-order, the energy perturbation, $\Delta E$, of the ground term is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E=\left\langle 0\left|\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right| 0\right\rangle+\sum_{n \neq 0} \frac{\left\langle 0\left|\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right| n\right\rangle\left\langle n\left|\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right| 0\right\rangle}{E_{0}-E_{n}} & \\
& +\sum_{\alpha, \beta \neq 0} \frac{\left\langle 0\left|\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right| \alpha\right\rangle\left\langle\alpha\left|\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right| \beta\right\rangle\left\langle\beta\left|\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right| 0\right\rangle}{\left(E_{0}-E_{\alpha}\right)\left(E_{0}-E_{\beta}\right)}, \quad(26)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $0, \alpha, \beta$, and $n$ refer to orbital states. It is necessary to take the procedure to third order, since the first order expression makes zero contribution to the interaction and the second order term is greatly reduced in magnitude because of a cancellation similar to that first demonstrated by Van Vleck (1940). This arises because the matrix elements of $\mathscr{H}^{\prime}$ of the type $\left\langle\alpha\left|\beta_{m} \mathbf{H} .(\mathbf{L}+2 \mathbf{S})+\lambda \mathbf{L} . \mathbf{S}\right| \beta\right\rangle$ change sign on interchange of $\alpha$ and $\beta$, whereas $\left\langle\alpha\left|\mathscr{H}_{O L}\right| \beta\right\rangle$ are unaffected by interchange. The matrix elements of $\mathscr{H}_{O L}$ between the various orbital states were derived by the normal mode analysis of Van Vleck (1939) and evaluated by the method of operator equivalents (Stevens 1952). Only terms linear in the acoustic strain were included, corresponding to the direct, or 'one-phonon', process. $\Delta E$ was reduced by this procedure into the spinlattice Hamiltonian for $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}: \mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ with the constants (Wigmore 1966)

$$
G_{11}=-\frac{100}{9} \frac{\lambda^{2} e e^{\prime}}{\left(E_{T_{2}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)^{2}} \frac{\left\langle r_{j}^{4}\right\rangle}{R^{5}},
$$

$$
G_{44}=-\frac{4}{7} \lambda^{2} e e^{\prime}\left\{\frac{3\left\langle r_{j}^{2}\right\rangle R^{-3}+(5 / 3)\left\langle r_{j}^{4}\right\rangle R^{-5}}{\left(E_{T_{2}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)^{2}}+\frac{6\left\langle r_{j}^{2}\right\rangle R^{-3}+(5 / 2)\left\langle r_{j}^{2}\right\rangle R^{-5}}{\left(E_{T_{2}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)\left(E_{T_{1}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)}\right\}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the spin-orbit coupling constant, $e$ and $e^{\prime}$ are respectively the charge on the electron and the effective charge on a neighbouring ligand $\mathrm{F}$ ion, $\left\langle r_{j}^{2}\right\rangle$ and $\left\langle r_{j}^{4}\right\rangle$ are the quantum mechanical expectation values of $r^{2}$ and $r^{4}$ summed over all the $3 d$ electrons of the $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ ion, $R$ is the lattice parameter of the $\mathrm{KMgF}_{3}$, and $\left(E_{T_{2}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)$ and $\left(E_{T_{1}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)$ are the energy splittings to the first and second excited orbital states, respectively. These quantities were all accurately known, with the exception of $e e^{\prime}$. We estimated this from the measured value of $\left(E_{T_{2}}-E_{A_{2}}\right)$, using (Van Vleck 1939)

$$
\frac{e e^{\prime}\left\langle r_{j}^{4}\right\rangle}{R^{5}}=6 D q=-4560 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1} .
$$

The figure was taken from Knox, Shulman \& Sugano (1963).

We finally obtained
$$
G_{11}=+54 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1} \text { unit strain }^{-1},
$$

$$
G_{44}=+43 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1} \text { unit strain }^{-1} .
$$

In view of the considerable covalency and overlap that undoubtedly do exist between the $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ and the $F$ ligand ions, as witnessed for example by the presence of transferred hyperfine structure, this agreement with measured values is amazingly close. It is interesting that for $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ in $\mathrm{MgO}$ also, Shiren (1962a) found good agreement between experiment and point ion theory, although for other $3 d$ ions in $\mathrm{MgO}$ the figures may differ by as much as an order of magnitude. As yet, there have been no reported attempts to calculate spin-phonon coupling constants using more refined crystal field models, presumably because of the complexity of the problem.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

Our results verify that the absorption and dispersion functions for acoustic para- magnetic resonance, like those for electron paramagnetic resonance, are connected quantitatively through the Kramers-Kronig relations. In addition, the changes in acoustic signal velocity at resonance are accurately described by the theory of pulse propagation in a dispersive medium, taken in the Baerwald limit. From the measured a.p.r. absorption we have inferred the quadrupolar magnetoelastic coefficients $G_{11}$ and $G_{44}$ , to be $58 \pm 5 cm^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ , and $39 \pm 5 cm^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ , respectively. These figures agree rather better than could be expected with the values of $54 cm^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ and $43 cm^{-1}$ unit strain $^{-1}$ , calculated on the basis of the point ion crystal field model.
