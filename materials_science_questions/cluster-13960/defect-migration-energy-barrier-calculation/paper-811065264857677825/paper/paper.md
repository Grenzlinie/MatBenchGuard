## Abstract
We investigate the behaviors of rhenium (Re) and osmium (Os) and their interactions with point defects in tungsten (W) using a first-principles method. We show that Re atoms are energetically favorable to disperse separately in bulk W due to the Re-Re repulsive interaction. Despite the attractive interaction between Os atoms, there is still a large activation energy barrier of 1.10eV at the critical number of 10 for the formation of Os clusters in bulk W based on the results of the total nucleation free energy change. Interestingly, the presence of vacancy can significantly reduce the total nucleation free energy change of Re/Os clusters, suggesting that vacancy can facilitate the nucleation of Re/Os in W. Re/Os in turn has an effect on the stability of the vacancy clusters ($V_n$) in W, especially for small vacancy clusters. A single Re/Os atom can raise the total binding energies of $V_2$ and $V_3$ obviously, thus enhancing their formation. Further, we demonstrate that there is a strong attractive interaction between Re/Os and self-interstitial atoms (SIAs). Re/Os could increase the diffusion barrier of SIAs and decrease their rotation barrier, while the interstitial-mediated path may be the optimal diffusion path of Re/Os in W. Consequently, the synergistic effect between Re/Os and point defects plays a key role in Re/Os precipitation and the evolution of defects in irradiated W.

Keywords: tungsten, transmutation elements, point defects

## 1. Introduction

Nuclear fusion is considered to be a good way to lib- erate human beings from fossil fuel sources in the future. Nowadays, the development of key materials is particularly challenging for the final application of nuclear fusion energy, in which the choice of the plasma facing materials (PFMs) is one of the key issues. Tungsten (W) and W alloys are regarded as the most promising candidates for PFMs, owing to their high melting temperature, good thermal conductivi- ties, and low sputtering erosion [1–4]. However, as a PFM, W will be irradiated by high energy (14MeV) neutron, and undergo transmutation to its near-neighbors in the periodic table, such as rhenium (Re), osmium (Os), etc [2, 5–9]. It has been found that Re and Os will precipitate and form a new Re/Os-rich phase under irradiation, and further signifi- cantly degrade the performance of W-PFMs. Therefore, the effects of Re and Os on the microstructure and mechanical

properties of W are some of the key concerns for the design of future nuclear fusion reactors.

Currently, the concentration of transmutation elements (TEs) is mainly obtained by numerical simulation. The recent available data of TEs in W [2, 9] is obtained under the condition of the first wall in model B within the European Power Plant Conceptual Study (PPCS) [10]. Model B is based on a blanket made with alternate layers of lithium ortho-silicate as tritium-generating material and pebbles of beryllium as a neutron multiplier. Helium is used as the coolant, and the structural material is Eurofer. The average neutron wall load is 2 MW m⁻², while the maximum tolerable heat flux is 10 MW m⁻². Cottrell *et al* [2] reported that the concentration of Re and Os in W after five years of irradiation in the model B condition (with a flux of 1.04 × 10¹⁵ n cm⁻² s⁻¹) is 12 at.% and 13 at.%, respectively. Recently, Gilbert *et al* [9] amended these results by taking into account the self-shielding effect, which has proved to be of particular importance for absorption reaction rate calculations in a moderated neutron environment. It has been found that the concentration of Re and Os in W after the same service time under the same irradiation conditions is 3.80 at.% and 1.38 at.%, respectively. Although the concentration of TEs in W is low, the influence of TEs on W performance in nuclear fusion reactors cannot be disregarded.

It has been found that the thermal conductivity of W will decrease sharply ~50% with doping as little as 5 wt.% Re [11, 12]. The presence of Os in W will induce an increase in electrical resistivity [13]. Further, W–Re alloys show more obvious irradiation hardening with a high level of damage (above 33 dpa) than the pure W [14], because of the irradiation-induced precipitation of WRe (σ-phase) and WRe₃ (χ-phase) [15–21]. Moreover, W–Os alloys are observed to more easily induce irradiation hardening than W–Re even at a low level of damage [17–20]. In W–Re–Os alloy, Os will play a more important role in irradiation hardening than Re [19]. Os is found to be more easily clustered in comparison with Re in W–Re–Os alloy, which may be attributed to that the attractive interaction between Os and vacancy is stronger than that of Re and vacancy [22].

Interestingly, it is found that Re/Os can also restrain irradiation damage under a low level of irradiation [23–25]. For example, the density of the void in W will decrease ~50% with the addition of 3 wt.% Re under neutron irradiation to 0.15 dpa [23]. Further, the vacancy clusters could also act as nucleation sites for the Re/Os clusters, and mediate the diffusion of them, leading to the growth of Re/Os-rich clusters [22]. It is found that Re also has an effect on deuterium (D) in W [26–28]. The retention of D is much lower in W–Re than that in W [28], which may be attributed to that Re blocks the nucleation of vacancies by forming a Re–vacancy (Re–V) complex or enhances the recombination between vacancy and self-interstitial atoms (SIAs). The above experimental results indicate that TEs play an important role in the evolution of defects in W, such as vacancy and SIAs.

Now, it is feasible to employ the first-principles method based on the density functional theory (DFT) to determine the interaction between TEs and point defects in W. However, only a very limited amount of work has focused on this aspect.

The preliminary studies show that there is a strong attractive interaction between Re/Os and point defects [29, 30]. The interaction between TE atoms is very weak in W [29, 31]. Understanding the fundamental mechanisms of TEs and TEs–defect interaction is very important for comprehending the experimental phenomenon and developing a predictive model for W-PFM applications in fusion reactors. However, so far, the physical mechanism for the effects of TEs on the point defects in W is still unclear. In the present work, using Re and Os as an example, we try to explore the physical origin of the interaction between TEs and point defects using a first-principles method in combination with thermodynamic models.

## 2. Computational method

Our first-principles calculations were performed using the pseudopotential plane-wave method implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) code [32, 33] based on the DFT. The projector augmented wave potential (PAW) method [34] is used to describe the interaction between ions and electrons. In a form proposed by Perdew and Wang [35] within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), electronic exchange and correlation effects are evaluated. Considering the computational loads and the accuracy, the supercell containing 128 lattice sites is used in the present work (a detailed discussion of the supercell size, pseudopotential and exchange-correlation functional are given in the supplementary data stacks.iop.org/NF/57/046006/mmedia). The calculations were performed under 3D periodic boundary conditions. In all the calculations, the atomic positions are relaxed and the shape and size of the supercell are optimized. The cutoff energy of the plane wave and *k*-point density is obtained by the convergence tests for the system. Finally, the cutoff energy is set to be 350eV and a *k*-points grid density of 3 × 3 × 3 is employed [36]. The calculated equilibrium lattice constant is 3.17 Å for body-centered-cubic (bcc) W, which is consistent with the experiment value of 3.16 Å. The iteration concluded when forces on all atoms reached less than 10⁻³ eV Å⁻¹.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Stability of Re and Os in W

In order to investigate the stability of Re and Os in bulk W, we examine the solution energy of the potential sites for them. Here, only the substitutional site is considered, because the radius between Re/Os and W is comparable. The solution energy of Re and Os at the substitutional site is defined as

$$
E^{\text{sol}} = E_{(\text{N}-1)\text{W+X}} - \frac{\text{N}-1}{\text{N}}E_{\text{NW}} - E_{\text{X}}, \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{(\text{N}-1)\text{W+X}}$ is the total energy of the supercell with an substitutional X (X = Re or Os) atom and $E_{\text{NW}}$ is the total energy of the pure W. $E_{\text{X}}$ is the energy per atom of Re/Os in its ground state. It is found that the solution energy of Re and Os is 0.19eV and 0.76eV, respectively, which is consistent with previous studies [31, 37].

To investigate the interaction between Re/Os atoms, we calculate the binding energy of Re-Re, Os-Os, and Re-Os in bulk W. One Re/Os atom is set at a substitutional site while the other Re/Os atom occupies a different substitutional site. The binding energy ($E^\text{b}$) can be obtained by
$$
\begin{aligned}
E^{\mathrm{b}}= & a E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{Re}}+b E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{Os}}-E_{(\mathrm{N}-a-b) \mathrm{W}+a \mathrm{Re}+b \mathrm{Os}} \\
& -(a+b-1) E_{\mathrm{NW}},
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
$$
where $E_{(\mathrm{N}-a-b) \mathrm{W}+a \mathrm{Re}+b \mathrm{Os}}$ is the total energy of the supercell containing N lattice points, $(N-a-b)$ W atoms, $a$ Re atoms, and $b$ Os atoms. $E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{Re}}$ and $E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{Os}}$ have similar meanings. Positive values indicate attraction, while negative ones indicate repulsion. Here, the effect of supercell size on the stability of small size clusters has also been considered. We have calculated the binding energy as a function of the Re/Os-Re/Os distance in a 128-atom supercell and a 250-atom supercell, respectively (the calculated numbers of the binding energy are listed in table S1 in the supplementary data). Figure 1 shows that the supercell size has slight effects on the binding energy of Re/Os-Re/Os pair. Further, it can be found that the interaction of Re-Re and Re-Os is repulsive, while that of Os-Os is attractive. The interaction becomes increasingly weaker with the increase in the interatomic distance (except for the mutation of Os-Os pair at $\sim$4.5 Å). The strongest repulsive interaction of Re-Re and Re-Os occurs at the nearest neighbor configuration at binding energies of about $-0.07\mathrm{eV}$ and $-0.06\mathrm{eV}$, respectively. The binding energy of Os-Os at the most stable configuration is also only $0.04\mathrm{eV}$ with the equilibrium distance of $\sim$2.75 Å. These indicate that the interaction between Re/Os atoms in W is very weak, which is consistent with previous studies [29, 31].

We further investigate the interaction between multi Re/Os atoms by calculating their total binding energy. Figure 2 shows the total binding energy of $\mathrm{Re}_k/\mathrm{Os}_k$ as a function of the number of Re/Os atoms in a 128-atom supercell and a 250-atom supercell, respectively (the calculated numbers of the binding energy are shown in table S2 in the supplementary data). It can be found that the total binding energy of small Re/Os clusters is almost invariable in different supercells, indicating that the effect of supercell size on the stability of Re/Os clusters is slight. The total binding energy of Re clusters decreases with the increasing of the Re number, while that of the Os cluster increases with the increasing of the Os number. This suggests that Re is favorable to be separately in bulk W, while Os prefers aggregation. Interestingly, it should be noted that the increase in the total binding energy of the Os cluster is very sharp as shown in figure 2. For example, the binding energy of $\mathrm{Os}_2$ is only $0.04\mathrm{eV}$, and it rises to $\sim0.3\mathrm{eV}$ for $\mathrm{Os}_4$, suggesting that Os energetically prefers to aggregate together and form the Os clusters as large as possible.

Next, we calculate the total nucleation free energy change for the Os cluster formation in W using a novel approach proposed by Seko et al [38, 39]. In this treatment, the static contribution to the enthalpy change, the configurational entropy change and vibrational entropy change for cluster formation are accurately estimated. The total nucleation free energy change can be expressed as
$$
\Delta G=\left(\Delta H+H_{s}\right)-T \Delta S_{\mathrm{vib}}-T \Delta S_{\mathrm{config}},
\tag{3}
$$
where the first term $(\Delta H+H_{s})$ is the cluster energy change including both the enthalpy change for the cluster from isolated atoms and interface energy, which can be directly obtained by the first-principles calculation (the calculated numbers of formation energy are listed in table S3 in the supplementary data). The second term and the third term represent the vibrational entropy change and the configurational entropy change due to cluster formation, respectively. Considering the phonon contributions to the entropy and employing the high-temperature limit of the harmonic approximation for the lattice dynamics, $\Delta S_{\mathrm{vib}}$ is the temperature-independent constant. It is found that the vibrational entropy has a negative effect on Os clustering in W, but this influence is weak. For example, the $\Delta S_{\mathrm{vib}}$ is calculated to be $6.5 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{eV} \mathrm{K}^{-1}$ for $\mathrm{Os}_2$, and the contribution of the vibrational entropy change is only $0.002$–$0.013\mathrm{eV}$ at the temperature range of 300–2000 K. Therefore, the contribution of vibrational entropy is not considered for the total nucleation free energy change of Os clusters. Further, similar with a previous study [39], $\Delta S_{\text{config}}$ can be estimated within the Bragg–Williams approximation as

$$
\Delta S_{\mathrm{config}} / k_{\mathrm{B}}=(k-1) \ln c_{\mathrm{Os}}-k+\frac{3}{2} \ln k+\frac{1}{2} \ln (2 \pi), \tag{4}
$$

where $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ represents the Boltzmann constant, $k$ the number of atoms in the cluster, and $c_{\mathrm{Os}}$ the concentration of Os in W. The entropy change is estimated at the temperature of 800 K [40]. The concentration of Os has been set to 1.38 at.%, which corresponds to the Os content in the first wall after five years of reactor operation [9].

Figure 3 shows the total nucleation free energy change as a function of cluster size. It can be found that the variation of the total nucleation free energy change with the increasing of the Os number can be divided into two regions, namely 1-10 and 10-14 (the configurations and the methodology for the construction of multi-Os clusters are shown in the supplementary data). In the former region, the total nucleation free energy change will increase with the increasing of the Os number. In contrast, in the latter region, the total nucleation free energy change will decrease with the increasing of the Os number. Therefore, the activation energy barrier $\Delta G^{*}$ (top of the nucleation barrier) of Os nucleation in W is estimated to be 1.10 eV at critical number $k^{*}$ of 10. When the size of the Os clusters is smaller than 10, these clusters have a greater possibility of being dissolved than of continuing to grow in order to lower $\Delta G$ [41, 42]. As for the clusters with a size larger than 10, these clusters are likely to grow spontaneously to lower $\Delta G$ [41, 42]. The activation energy barrier $\Delta G^{*}$ is the energy required to form a critical nucleus of number $k^{*}$ [43, 44]. In classical nucleation theory (CNT) [45, 46], the nucleation rate and probability of forming a nucleus (equal to or exceeding the critical size) is proportional to $\exp (-\Delta G^{*} / k_{\mathrm{B}} T)$. So, if $\Delta G^{*}$ is large and positive, the nucleation rate will be slow and the probability will be low. Therefore, the nucleation of Os atoms in bulk W is also difficult due to the large and positive activation energy, though the Os-Os interaction is attractive.

### 3.2. Diffusion of Re and Os in W

The diffusion behaviors of impurities/alloying elements play a key role on the properties of materials. Previous studies show that the diffusion of impurities has an effect on the precipitate-coarsening and creep strength properties of materials [47–50], which influence the application of materials under high temperature. Here, we have investigated the diffusion coefficients of Re and Os in bulk W via the vacancy-mediated path, and compared them to the self-diffusion of W (the detailed calculation procedures of diffusion coefficients are given in the supplementary data).

Table 1 shows that the diffusion energy of vacancy in pure W is 1.65 eV, which is consistent with the previous studies [29, 51, 52]. When there is a Re/Os atom, the vacancy diffusion energy along the path 4 (6 or 8) (leaves away from Re/Os) is higher than that along the path 3 (5 or 7), which can be attributed to the strong attractive interaction between Re/Os and vacancy. Further, the diffusion energy of Re and Os via the mono-vacancy mechanism is 1.63 eV and 1.36 eV, respectively, which is slightly lower than that of the self-diffusion of W. It should be noted that the calculated results and the experimental data are not consistent (figure 4(b)), which could be attributed to the discrepancy of the activation energies. According to the experimental results [53], the activation energy of W, Re and Os are 6.49 eV, 6.18 eV and 5.58 eV, respectively, which are much larger than our calculations (4.76 eV for W, 4.48 eV for Re and 4.23 eV for Os). This can be mainly attributed to two aspects. On the one hand, the experimental data have been measured at high temperature ~2000–3000 K, and the diffusion of di-vacancies or interstitials cannot be ignored. For example, Mundy et al [54] have also measured the self-diffusion in W over the temperature range of 1700–3400 K and their data can be fitted to a sum of two exponentials with an activation energy of $5.4 \pm 0.5$ eV and $6.9 \pm 0.6$ eV. The lower activation energy corresponds to the diffusion by single vacancy, while the higher one may represent the motion either by di-vacancies or by interstitials.

On the other hand, the high temperature could induce large thermal expansion, which leads to a huge tensile strain. It has been demonstrated that the vacancy formation energy shows monotonic dependence on the volumetric strain, which increases with the increasing tensile strain but decreases with the increasing compressive strain [52]. This could be understood by the redistribution of local electron density induced by vacancy. Apparently, the formation of a vacancy will lead to the rapid decreasing of local electron density, which will drive the neighboring atoms toward vacancy to compensate this effect. The tensile stress induced by a single vacancy is ~0.68 GPa [52]. The compressive strain facilitates the balance of this tensile stress as well as the compensation of electron density decreasing, and thus reduce the vacancy formation energy. In contrast, under the tensile strain, the external stress and the local tensile stress induced by vacancy are cumulative. The distance between vacancy and its neighboring atoms will be enlarged by tensile strain, leading to more significant local electron density decreasing for vacancy formation in

comparison with the strain-free W. Therefore, the vacancy formation energy will increase under the tensile strain. According to a previous study [55], the lattice expansion ration at 2700 K is ~1.5%, leading to ~4.57% volumetric strain. Then, based on the formula proposed by Hossain *et al* [52], the activation energy will increase to 5.15 eV, which is consistent with the experimental results 5.4 ± 0.5 eV [54].

### 3.3. Interaction of single Re/Os and vacancy
Next, we investigate the interaction between Re/Os and vacancy in W. One Re/Os atom is set at the substitutional site while the vacancy occupies a different lattice point. The binding energy can be expressed as

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^{\mathrm{b}} & =n E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{V}}+m E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{X}}-E_{(\mathrm{N}-n-m) \mathrm{W}+n \mathrm{~V}+m \mathrm{X}} \\
& -(m+n-1) E_{\mathrm{NW}},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $E_{(\mathrm{N}-n-m) \mathrm{W}+n \mathrm{~V}+m \mathrm{X}}$ is the total energy of supercell containing N lattice points, $(\mathrm{N}-n-m)$ W atoms, $n$ vacancies and $m$ X atoms. $E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{V}}$ denotes the total energy of system containing a single vacancy. Figure 5(a) shows the binding energy between Re/Os and vacancy as a function of interatomic distance (the calculated numbers of binding energy are given in table S4 in the supplementary data). It can be found that there is a strong attractive interaction between Re/Os and vacancy, which suggests that Re/Os and vacancy prefer aggregation. On the other hand, it also indicates that the presence of Re/Os is beneficial to the formation of vacancy in W. This can be attributed to that the presence of Re/Os weakens the interatomic interaction of W atoms surrounding them in comparison with pure W. Re/Os will cause a redistribution of local charge density in W. In order to obtain a realistic evaluation of the interatomic bond, we calculate the differential charge density. Figure 5(b) displays the difference between the charge density of the full system of Re/Os in W, and the density of isolated W plus that of the Re/Os–vacancy pair with 1NN distance in W. It can be clearly found that the charge density between Re/Os and W is much lower than that of W–W, which means the existence of Re and Os weakens the interatomic bond of W atoms surrounding them and benefits the vacancy formation. Further, the strongest attraction occurs at the shortest distance of Re/Os–vacancy (Re/Os–V) with largest binding energy. At the most stable configuration, the binding energy is 0.24 eV and 0.54 eV for Re–V and Os–V pairs, respectively. After that, the binding energy decreases to 0.21 eV and 0.35 eV for Re–V and Os–V at the distance of 3.17 Å, respectively. Beyond this distance, the binding energy of Re–V and Os–V rapidly decreases to 0.05 eV and 0.11 eV at the distance of ~5.5 Å, respectively, ~76% and 69% lower than the binding energy of Re–V and Os–V at the distance of 3.17 Å.

We further investigate the effect of a single Re/Os atom on the stability of vacancy clusters by calculating the total binding energy of the vacancy clusters without and with Re/Os. For the Re/Os-free vacancy clusters, the total binding energy can be expressed as

$$
E_{\mathrm{V}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}}=n E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{V}}-E_{(\mathrm{N}-n) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{V}_{n}}-(n-1) E_{\mathrm{NW}}, \quad (6)
$$

where $E_{(\mathrm{N}-n) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{V}_{n}}$ denotes the total energy of system containing an $\mathrm{V}_{n}$ cluster. When there is a Re/Os atom, the total binding energy of the vacancy cluster can be obtained by

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{XV}_{n}}^{\mathrm{b}} & =E_{(\mathrm{N}-2) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{XV}}+(n-1) E_{(\mathrm{N}-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{V}} \\
& -E_{(\mathrm{N}-n-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{XV}_{n}}-(n-1) E_{\mathrm{NW}},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $E_{(\mathrm{N}-2) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{XV}}$ and $E_{(\mathrm{N}-n-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{XV}_{n}}$ represent the total energy of the supercell containing an $\mathrm{XV}$ and an $\mathrm{XV}_{n}$, respectively. As shown in figure 6, we display the calculated total binding energy of vacancy clusters without and with Re/Os as a function of the number of vacancy (the methodology for the construction of $\mathrm{Re} / \mathrm{Os}-\mathrm{V}_{n}$ are given in the supplementary data, and the calculated numbers of binding energy and the configurations of $\mathrm{Re} / \mathrm{Os}-\mathrm{V}_{n}$ are shown in table S5 and figure S2 in the supplementary data, respectively). It can be found that the presence of Re/Os has a significant effect on the stability of the vacancy clusters in W. For the pure vacancy system, figure 6 shows that the total binding energies of both $\mathrm{V}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{3}$ are near zero. After that, the total binding energy rapidly increases with the increasing of the vacancy number, which is consistent with previous studies [51]. Further, it can be found that the total binding energy of vacancy cluster with Re/Os is larger than that of the Re/Os-free case, suggesting that Re/Os could enhance the stability of vacancy clusters, especially for small vacancy cluster ($n=2$ and 3). A single Os (Re) atom can raise the total binding energies of $\mathrm{V}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{3}$ up to 0.38 eV (0.21 eV) and 0.74 eV (0.39 eV) from ~0 eV as shown in figure 6, which indicates that the presence of Re/Os facilitates the formation of small vacancy clusters. In addition, it should be noted that the influence of Os on the vacancy clusters is more significant in comparison with that of Re.

It has been demonstrated that irradiation has significant effects on the solute precipitation in W-Re/Os alloys [33, 56]. Generally, under thermal equilibrium condition, Re and Os will precipitate and form a new phase only when their concentration reach the solubility limits (~25 at.% for Re; ~5 at.% for Os) [57, 58]. However, under irradiation, Re and Os will also precipitate in W even though their concentrations are far below the solubility limits, the so-called irradiation-induced precipitation [56, 59]. This unexpected phenomenon could be related to the defects produced by irradiation, such as vacancies and SIAs. Here, we have further investigated the influence

of vacancy on Re/Os nucleation by calculating the total nucle- ation free energy change in Re/Os clusters with vacancy (the calculated numbers of formation energy are listed in table S3 in the supplementary data). A similar treatment of the total nucleation free energy change has been used as mentioned in section 3.1 [38, 39]. Here, the configurational entropy loss due to the formation of $X_{m} V$ clusters is estimated by (the calcul ation procedures of configurational entropy change are givenin the supplementary data)
$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta S_{\text {config }} / k_{\mathrm{B}} & =\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{c_{\mathrm{X}}}{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\right) \ln c_{\mathrm{X}} \\
+ & \left(m-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{c_{\mathrm{X}}}{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\right) \ln \left(c_{\mathrm{X}}-m c_{\mathrm{V}}\right)-m,
\end{aligned}\qquad(8)$$

The concentration of Re and Os has been set to 3.80 at.% and1.38 at.% respectively, corresponding to that in the first wall after five years of reactor operation [9].
Figure 9 shows the total nucleation free energy change as a function of Re/Os cluster size with one vacancy (the configura- tions and the methodology for the construction of $Re_{m} V$ and Os, V are shown in the supplementary data), which suggests that the presence of vacancy substantially affects the nuclea- tion behavior of Re/Os in W. It can be found that vacancy sig- nificantly reduces the total nucleation free energy change of Re/Os clusters. As shown in figure 9(a), the total nucleation free energy change for Os clusters with one vacancy is nega- tive, which decreases 'monotonically' with the increasing of the Os number (from 1 to 14). Further, it is always much lower than that of the vacancy-free Os clusters shown in figure 3. For the Re case, we do not calculate the total nucleation free energy change in Re cluster in bulk W due to the Re-Re repulsive inter- action. However, with the presence of vacancy, the total binding energy of the Re-vacancy cluster changes to be positive (figure8(a)), indicating an attractive interaction. Figure 9(b) shows that the total nucleation free energy change of the Re cluster is negative, when the number of Re atom in cluster is lower than7. It also decreases with the increasing of the Re number, from $Re_{1} V$ to $Re_{3} V$ . These suggest that the presence of vacancy will facilitate the nucleation of Re/Os in W, which could be attrib- uted to the attractive interaction between Re/Os and vacancy. The Re/Os-vacancy attraction will decrease the total energy of the system, and further drives Re/Os atom to aggregate.

The formation of SIA will induce a local strong compressive field in W, while the atomic radius of Re/Os is smaller than that of W. The SIA clusters will also induce a local strong compressive field in W, and thus the interaction between SIA clusters and Re/Os should be similar to that of single SIA and Re/Os. Therefore, the Re/Os atoms will have a significant effect on the evolution of SIAs in W.

### 4. Discussion
According to previous studies [15–22], the radiation defects may have an effect on the formation of Re/Os precipitation in W. In the present work, we have systemically investigated the synergistic effect of Re/Os and point defects (vacancies and SIAs). On the one hand, it is found that the presence of vacancy can significantly enhance the stability of Re/Os clusters. Re atoms are favorable to be separately in vacancy-free W due to the repulsive interaction between them. Interestingly, with the presence of vacancy, Re atoms become to energetically get together. On the other hand, it is also found there is a strong attractive interaction between Re/Os and SIA. Most importantly, the interstitial-mediated path may be the optimal diffusion path of Re/Os in W. In addition, it should be noted that Re/Os also has an effect on the stability of the vacancy clusters in W. Our research has two contributions to the actual reactor design. On the one hand, our calculations will provide a good reference to understand the typical experimental phenom- enon related to the transmutation elements, such as the Re/Os precipitation and the suppression of void formation. Further, we can roughly estimate the microstructure evolution of W under neutron irradiation based on our results in combina- tion with the experimental data, and predict the performance of W-PFMs in future fusion reactors. On the other hand, the addition of certain alloying elements has been considered as a useful approach to improve the properties of W. Re is a typical alloying element used to increase low-temperature ductility and improve high-temperature strength and plasticity [65-67]. Our findings give insight into the basic behavior of Re in W without and with defects, which can also be used as a reference for other alloying elements of W. These will be very useful for developing W-alloys for future nuclear fusion reactors.

## 5. Conclusions

Using a first-principles method, we have investigated the intrinsic properties of the transmutation elements Re and Os and their interaction with point defects in W. We show that Re is energetically favorable to disperse separately in bulk W due to the repulsive interaction between Re atoms. Although there is an attractive interaction between Os atoms, the activa- tion barrier for the formation of Os clusters is estimated to be 1.10 eV at the critical number of ten atoms. These suggest that the formation of Re and Os clusters are difficult in bulk W. Further, it is found that vacancy can significantly increase the total binding energy of Re/Os clusters and decrease their total nucleation free energy change, owing to the strong attrac- tive interaction between vacancies and Re/Os. This suggests that irradiation-induced vacancy could facilitate the forma- tion of Re/Os-rich precipitation in W. Both Re and Os in turn can notably enhance the stability of small vacancy clusters ($V_{2}$ and $V_{3}$), which suggests that the presence of Re/Os can increase the probability of the formation of small vacancy clusters. For the SIA case, there is a strong attractive interac- tion between Re/Os and SIA, leading to increasing the SIA diffusion barrier along the $\langle 111\rangle$ direction, while decreasing its rotation energy barrier. Further, the diffusion barrier of Re/ Os atom via the interstitial-mediated path is lower than that via the vacancy-mediated path, thus Re/Os may diffuse via the interstitial-mediated path to aggregate in W. Therefore, the interaction between Re/Os and point defects plays a crucial role in their behaviors in W. Our calculations will provide a good reference for understanding the fundamental mechanism of the precipitation of the transmutation elements in W. These data can be used to construct energetic models for larger-scale simulations, and then evaluate the performance of W and design W alloys for future nuclear fusion reactors.
