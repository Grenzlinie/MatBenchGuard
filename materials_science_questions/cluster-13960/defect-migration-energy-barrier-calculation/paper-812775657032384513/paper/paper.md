# Nitrogen Loss and Oxygen Evolution Reaction Activity of Perovskite Oxynitrides

Hassan Ouhbi and Ulrich Aschauer*

Department of Chemistry and Biochemistry, University of Bern, Bern, Switzerland

Supporting Information

**ABSTRACT:** Perovskite oxynitride photocatalysts were reported by experiment to evolve small amounts of $\ce{N_{2}}$, because of the self-oxidation of nitrogen ions by photogenerated holes. The $\ce{N_{2}}$ evolution rate was observed to decrease with increasing reaction time and was found to be correlated with a decrease in $\ce{O_{2}}$ evolution (OER) activity, the origin of this latter effect however being unknown. Here, we investigate, by means of density functional theory calculation, anion vacancies at the TaON-terminated (001) surface of the perovskite oxynitride $\ce{SrTaO_{2}N}$. We find an energetic preference for oxygen and nitrogen vacancies to reside at the surface, where they are spontaneously healed by *O and *OH adsorbates under OER conditions. For nitrogen vacancies, this self-healing leads to an altered stoichiometry $\ce{Ta_{4}O_{8+x}N_{4-x}}$ that is accompanied by electron doping. Substitution of N by O at the surface also leads to tensile strain, which confines the excess charge to the very surface layer, affecting the binding energy of reaction intermediates and significantly increasing the OER overpotential. This peculiar change in electronic structure thus provides an atomic-scale explanation for the experimentally observed drop in OER activity of perovskite oxynitrides.

![](./images/812775657032384513_1.jpg)

Oxynitride perovskites with the general formula $\ce{ABO_{2}N}$ have emerged as highly promising water splitting photocatalysts under visible light, because of their smaller band gaps, compared to pure oxides.$^{1-3}$ This reduction in band gap is a result of the partial substitution of oxygen by the less electronegative nitrogen. Tantalate,$^{1}$ as well as other perovskite oxynitrides$^{4}$ and oxynitrides in other crystal structures,$^{5}$ were reported to evolve $\ce{N_{2}}$ during the early stages of the photocatalytic reaction. This is due to the oxidation of the $\ce{N^{3-}}$ ions by the photo-generated holes as put in evidence by a reduced X-ray photo-electron spectroscopy (XPS) nitrogen signal after the reaction.$^{6}$ For $\ce{LaTiO_{2}N}$, it was shown that (i) the photocurrent significantly decreased within a minute and (ii) the $\ce{O_{2}}$ evolution rate gradually decreased with time.$^{6}$ While it seems likely that atomic-scale defects formed during $\ce{N_{2}}$ evolution are at the origin of the reduced OER activity, there have not been any investigations of the atomic-scale origins for this correlation.

The role of surface point-defects on the OER activity has been studied theoretically for pure oxides, where, for $\ce{SrCoO_{3}}$ surfaces, oxygen vacancies were reported to reduce the OER activity, while they can also be healed by oxygen adsorbates.$^{7,8}$ On the other hand, oxygen vacancies were reported to be stable in the subsurface of $\ce{Fe_{2}O_{3}(001)}$ and to remarkably increase the OER activity.$^{9}$ For $\ce{RuO_{2}}$, it was shown that defects can either increase or decrease the overpotential, depending on the exact geometry.$^{10}$ These results show that the defect population can indeed have a marked influence on the OER activity while the exact effect is dependent on the material and the location of the defect.

The goal of this work is to investigate the effect of oxygen and nitrogen vacancies on the OER activity of perovskite oxynitride photocatalysts, using $\ce{SrTaO_{2}N(001)}$ as a prototypical example. We first study the thermodynamic stability of oxygen and nitrogen vacancies in the three outermost atomic layers, showing a preference of both defects to reside at the surface. Subsequently, we show how surface vacancies are healed by oxygen adsorbates under photocatalytic application conditions. For nitrogen vacancies, this leads to a change in surface stoichiometry. We find a strong increase of the overpotential with decreasing nitrogen content and rationalize this in terms of an electron enrichment of the surface.

We start by investigating the formation energies of O and N vacancies in the three outermost layers of the TaON-terminated $\ce{SrTaO_{2}N(001)}$ surface (see Figure 1). The oxygen and nitrogen vacancies have charges of +2 and +3, respectively, relative to the original lattice site, which are represented as $\ce{V_{O}^{..}}$ and $\ce{V_{N}^{...}}$ in Kröger−Vink notation.$^{11}$ However, for simplicity, we will use $\ce{V_{O}}$ and $\ce{V_{N}}$ in the following. As shown in Table 1, the formation energies of anion vacancies in the topmost atomic layer $E_{\mathrm{f}}(V_{O_{1}})$ and $E_{\mathrm{f}}(V_{N_{1}})$ are smaller than those in lower layers. Therefore, anion vacancies have an energetic preference to reside at the (001) surface, compared to subsurface layers. This is likely related to larger structural relaxations, observed for the lower coordinated surface Ta sites adjacent to the defects, compared to Ta sites in the third layer. Comparing the absolute values of the $V_O$ and $V_N$ formation energies at the chosen chemical potential conditions, we observe an almost-equal formation energy in the third layer, while $V_O$ are significantly easier to form at the surface, compared to $V_N$. However, we note that, under photocatalytic conditions, there usually is a high partial pressure of oxygen at the surface, because of the OER adsorbates, while the reaction environment is significantly poorer in nitrogen. This may render the formation of $V_N$ more favorable than that of $V_O$.

To investigate the effect of these vacancies on the surface chemistry of $SrTaO_2N$, we study their interaction with OER adsorbates. Under photo-electrochemical conditions, $SrTaO_2N$ (001) surfaces will be covered with *O adsorbates,¹² but during the conventional OER mechanism that we consider here (see Figure 2), *OH and *OOH adsorbates will also be present. We observe a spontaneous and barrierless migration of the *O and *OH adsorbates into surface anion vacancies $V_{O_{1}}$ and $V_{N_{1}}$ (see Figures 3a and 3b), while the *OOH adsorbate dissociates into a O and OH fragment, the former migrating into the vacancy and the latter remaining bound to a surface Ta site. For the subsurface $V_{O_{2}}$, we observe the spontaneous and barrierless two-step process shown in Figure 3c, where a surface oxygen migrates into the subsurface vacancy (step 1), followed by migration of an *O adsorbate into the resulting surface vacancy (step 2). While we readily observed this process involving surface oxygen ions for step 1, we did not see it for surface nitrogen ions. As shown by the nudged elastic band (NEB) pathway in Figure 4a), this is related to a rather large barrier of 1.36 eV associated with the migration of a nitrogen from the first layer to the second layer. Consequently, oxygen vacancies in the second layer can only be healed by surface oxygen ions.

Since the subsurface anion vacancies $V_{O_{3}}$ and $V_{N_{3}}$ cannot be healed by surface adsorbates during water oxidation, we investigate their effect on the OER activity. We adopt the OER mechanism schematically shown in Figure 2, where first a water molecule transfers one of its protons to the electrolyte, leading to the formation of an *OH adsorbate (step A). This is followed by step B, in which the *O adsorbate is formed after deprotonation of *OH. In step C, the hydroperoxo group *OOH is formed after dissociation of a second water molecule and transfer of one of its proton to the electrolyte. Finally, the $O_{2}$ is released from the surface after deprotonation of the *OOH (step D). From the adsorbate binding energies, with respect to $H_{2}O$ and $H_{2}$ and the resulting overpotentials, shown in Table 2,

we see a significantly increase in overpotential from 1.01 V in the stoichiometric case¹² to 1.81 and 1.93 V, respectively, on a surface Ta above the defect for the $V_{N_{3}}$ and $V_{O_{3}}$. These overpotentials change slightly for Ta sites further from the defect, where we calculate 2.03 and 1.88 V for the $V_{N_{3}}$ and $V_{O_{3}}$, respectively. These increased overpotentials, compared to the stoichiometric surface, are related to a stronger binding of the *OH and *O adsorbates in the presence of the vacancies, which manifests as an increased free-energy change of step C. The presence of these kinetically stabilized anion vacancies in the third layer thus leads to a significant increase in the overpotential by up to 1 V, compared to the defect-free surface.

In contrast to anion vacancies in the third layer, these in the first and second layer ($V_{N_{1}}$ and $V_{O_{1,2}}$) can be healed by OER adsorbates, as discussed above. We compute the OER at the different $Ta_{i}$ sites shown in Figure 3, to characterize the effect of this change on the activity. From the results reported in Table 2, we see that the $Ta_{1}$ site (see Figure 3a) has an overpotential of 1.02 V, which, as expected, is almost equivalent to the stoichiometric surface,¹² since they have the same structure. The same is true when a subsurface $V_{O_{2}}$ is healed by the mechanism shown in Figure 3c). Healing of a surface $V_{N_{1}}$, on the other hand, leads to an altered local environment with three instead of two surface oxygen ligands for some of the surface Ta sites. As can be seen from the results in Table 2, the overpotentials at the inequivalent $Ta_{2}$ and $Ta_{3}$ sites increases to 1.46 and 1.25 V, respectively (detailed OER free-energy profiles are shown in the Figure S2 in the Supporting Information). Since the coordination environment of $Ta_{3}$ is not affected but its overpotential changes, the effect of substituting N with O is not local but affects neighbouring transition metal sites as well. We also notice that, for both $Ta_{2}$ and $Ta_{3}$, step D becomes the overpotential-determining step, whereas, on the stoichiometric surface, it was step C.¹² Therefore, the self-healing of $V_{N_{1}}$ by OER adsorbates significantly reduces the OER activity, which is due to a stronger bonding of the *O and *OH intermediates at both $Ta_{2}$ and $Ta_{3}$, compared to $Ta_{1}$ on the stoichiometric surface.

The above results show that nitrogen vacancies at the surface are healed by OER adsorbates, changing the stoichiometry of the surface layer to $Ta_{4}O_{8+x}N_{4-x}$, where $x$ is the number of substituted surface nitrogen atoms. Given that the OER typically occurs in nitrogen-poor environments that promote $V_{N}$ formation, and since we have shown that the substitution of a single surface nitrogen by oxygen has a marked effect on the predicted overpotential, we compute now the overpotential for different concentrations of substituted surface nitrogen ($x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$). Since this contraction is hindered by the underlying bulk, the surface layer is under tensile strain, where the increased local volume promotes accommodation of the excess charge. $^{13,14}$ The availability of excess charge leads to stronger bonds of the adsorbates, increasing the overpotential and, in addition, may also lead to increased electron−hole recombination at the surface, which also reduces the efficiency of the photocatalyst.

In summary, we have shown a preference for both oxygen and nitrogen vacancies to reside in surface layers, the migration of vacancies from the third layer upwards being associated with large energy barriers of the order of 0.9 eV. Under oxygen evolution reaction (OER) conditions, we find that anion vacancies in the first and second layer are spontaneously healed by oxygen adsorbates. Healing of nitrogen vacancies in the surface layer will lead to a change in stoichiometry and an increase of the OER overpotential with decreasing nitrogen content. We can relate this to a tensile-strain-induced localization of excess charge at the surface that leads to a stronger binding of *O and *OH reaction intermediates. Our findings thus show that nitrogen loss from oxynitride surfaces results in a reduced OER activity and provides a rationale for the experimentally observed nitrogen evolution coupled with a progressive decrease in the oxygen evolution for oxynitride photocatalysts. Our results also show that, for well-performing oxynitride photocatalysts, it is mandatory to prevent the self-oxidation of nitrogen, for example by co-catalysts, co-doping with donor elements, or protective coatings.

### COMPUTATIONAL METHODS

Our density functional theory (DFT) calculations are performed using the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange correlation functional, $^{15}$ as implemented in the Quantum ESPRESSO package. $^{16}$ For 5d transition-metal oxides, the inclusion of a $+U$ correction $^{17}$ has very little effect, as shown in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information, which is likely due to the highly covalent character of the Ta−N bond. For this reason, calculations were performed without the $+U$ correction. Ultrasoft pseudopotentials $^{18}$ with Sr(4s, 4p, 5s), Ta(5s, 5p, 5d,

6s), O(2s, 2p), and N(2s, 2p) valence states were used to describe electron-nuclear interactions and wave functions were expanded in plane waves up to a kinetic energy cutoff of 40 Ry, combined with 320 Ry for the augmented density.

We construct the TaON-terminated (001) slab (lateral dimensions $8.182\ \text{\AA} \times 8.182\ \text{\AA}$) with eight TaON/SrO layers and an in-plane *cis* order of the nitrogen ions, based on the most stable $SrTaO_2N$ bulk structure.¹² Periodic images of the slab along the surface normal direction are separated by a vacuum of $15\ \text{\AA}$, the bottom two atomic layers were fixed at bulk positions, and a dipole correction¹⁹ was introduced along the z-direction to cancel spurious electric fields across the vacuum. Reciprocal space integration is performed with a $4 \times 4 \times 1$ k-point mesh²⁰ and structures were relaxed with force and total energy thresholds of $10^{-3}\ \text{eV}\ \text{\AA}^{-1}$ and $10^{-6}\ \text{eV}$, respectively.

The thermochemical scheme developed by Nørskov and co-workers²¹,²² is used to calculate the free energies of the OER proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps under applied potential and pH conditions. The free-energy change of a reaction step $\Delta G(U_b, \text{pH})$ is calculated as

$$
\Delta G(U_b, \text{pH}) = \Delta E + (\Delta \text{ZPE} - T\Delta S) - eU_b - k_\text{B}T\ln(10)\text{pH}
\tag{2}
$$

where $\Delta E$ is the reaction energy calculated by DFT, $-eU$ is the energy shift due to the applied potential, and $-k_\text{B}T\ln(10)\text{pH}$ is the free-energy shift at a given pH. The terms $\Delta \text{ZPE}$ and $\Delta S$ represent the changes in zero point energy and entropy of the reaction intermediates, which were taken from our previous work.¹² The thermodynamic overpotential $\eta$, which we use to characterize the OER activity of a given surface, is defined as the potential for which all PCET steps have $\Delta G(U_b, \text{pH}) < 0$, relative to the equilibrium potential of 1.23 V:

$$
\eta = \frac{\max(\Delta G)}{e} - 1.23\ \text{V}
\tag{3}
$$

We note that calculations were performed without the presence of an explicit or implicit solvent, which may quantitatively affect the calculated overpotentials²³ but is unlikely to affect our results qualitatively, given the large differences in obtained overpotentials. Activation barriers for thermally activated processes are calculated using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method.²⁴
