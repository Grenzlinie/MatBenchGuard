### ABSTRACT

The trapping behaviors of hydrogen (H) in MAX phase material $Ti_3SiC_2$ have been investigated using a first-principles method with a zero point energy (ZPE) correction. Our calculated result shows that a single H atom is energetically favorable to reside near the Si-vacancy, with lower solution energy than that of the interstitial positions. The Si vacancy has the strong ability for capturing H atoms. Up to five H atoms can be trapped around by a Si vacancy without $H_2$ molecules formation, due to the repulsive interaction between H atoms. Meanwhile, the diffusion barrier for a H atom from an interstitial site to a Si vacancy is 1.17 eV, much larger than that in metals, indicating that to some extent H atoms cannot easily migrate or aggregate to form bubble in $Ti_3SiC_2$.

## 1. Introduction

The MAX phases ($M_{n+1}AX_n$, where $n=1$, 2 or 3, M is an early transition metal, A is an A-group element, and X is either carbon or nitrogen) are a group of layered hexagonal compounds. Among the compounds, $Ti_3SiC_2$ has the best comprehensive properties and thus has been investigated most extensively. It exhibits some outstanding properties combining the advantages of both metals and ceramics, such as thermal-shock resistance [1-3], high thermal conductivity [4], exceptional oxidation resistance [5], significant ductility and high strength [6,7]. Due to these excellent properties, $Ti_3SiC_2$ is showing potential as a promising candidate structural material for use in future nuclear systems such as gas-cooled fast nuclear reactors (GFR) and further fusion [8-13]. Under neutrons irradiation in reactors, large amounts of vacancy defects and H impurities would be produced continually in the structural materials. It is well known that the behaviors of H play a very important role in the mechanical properties of metals. H atoms have been shown to assist the vacancy formation and can also be trapped by the vacancy [14,15]. Due to the low energy barrier in metals, H atoms usually diffuse, and thus lead to bubble formation, surface blistering and embrittlement. The H-induced embrittlement seriously degrades the mechanical properties of materials [16]. Therefore, the problem of H-induced embrittlement is still one of the prime concerns in fusion materials research. Previous researches showed that the unique properties of $Ti_3SiC_2$ were contributed to the nanolaminate crystal structure and the bonding properties [17]. And the structure and atomic bonding properties of $Ti_3SiC_2$ are totally different from those in metals. Therefore, in order to further prove the candidacy of $Ti_3SiC_2$ for structure materials in future advanced nuclear systems, it is of crucial importance to understand the behaviors of H atom in $Ti_3SiC_2$. To date, most of studies focused on the chemical bonding of defect-free $Ti_3SiC_2$. Little work has been done on the effect of H impurity [18]. Many fundamental aspects underlying the H-vacancy interaction in $Ti_3SiC_2$ are not fully understood. Therefore, investigations on the interaction between H and vacancies in $Ti_3SiC_2$ are in great need.

In the present work, we investigate the behaviors of H in $Ti_3SiC_2$ using first-principles calculation. The structure and stability, diffusion behaviors of H, and H-vacancy interaction in $Ti_3SiC_2$ are all discussed. The influence of ZPE should not be ignored for the light elements H. Therefore, the ZPE correction is taken into account in all of our calculations. These results might be not only beneficial for designing and processing of $Ti_3SiC_2$-based materials in realistic applications, but also beneficial for understanding the physical essence.

## 3. Results and discussion

Ti₃SiC₂ has a hexagonal crystal structure with a space group of P63/mmc, where Ti occupies $2a$ and $4f$, Si $2b$, and C $4f$ Wyckoff position, as shown in Fig. 1. To distinguish the Ti atoms in two different coordination environments, we denote Ti atoms sitting at $2a$ sites as Ti (1), and Ti in $4f$ as Ti (2). After fully relaxing the structure of Ti₃SiC₂, we obtained the lattice constants of $a=3.075$ Å and $c=17.561$ Å in good agreement with the previous work [24].

We first calculate the solution energy of a single H atom in an interstitial site in order to find the most stable site of a H atom in perfect Ti₃SiC₂. The solution energy is defined as
$$
E^{s}(H)=E(ref+H)-E(ref)-E_{H}, \tag{1}
$$
where $E(ref+H)$ is the energy of Ti₃SiC₂ with a single H atom, $E(ref)$ is the energy of perfect Ti₃SiC₂ crystal and $E_{H}$ is the energy of an isolated H atom. There are three possible interstitial positions with large free volumes in Ti₃SiC₂ (Fig. 1): a tetrahedral interstitial (I-Ti) surrounded by three Ti(1) and one Ti(2) atoms, a hexahedral interstitial (I-SiTi) surrounded by three Si and two Ti(2) atoms, and a tetrahedral interstitial (I-SiC) surrounded by three Si and one C atoms. The zero-point energy (ZPE) should not be ignored when H is involved [25,26]. In the present work, the ZPE of H atom is calculated by summing up the normal model of each H atom, i.e., $ZPE=\sum_{i}h\nu_{i}/2$, where $\nu$ is a real normal mode frequency. The solution energies are calculated to be $-2.215$ eV, $-2.870$ eV and $-2.894$ eV for I-Ti, I-SiTi and I-SiC, respectively, without the ZPE correction. After considering the ZPE, the solution energies at I-Ti, I-SiTi and I-SiC are $-1.929$ eV, $-2.715$ eV and $-2.755$ eV, respectively. The energy difference between I-SiTi and I-SiC is so close, so we calculate the energies of additional H atoms segregating to the vacancy in I-SiTi and I-SiC for comparison. The values are $-2.046$ eV and $-2.839$ eV for I-SiTi and I-SiC, respectively. These results suggest that I-SiC is the most stable position for a single H atom in perfect Ti₃SiC₂. The ZPE of H atom has a significant effect on the solution energies of H in Ti₃SiC₂, but it does not changed the relative stability of H atom.

In order to investigate the interaction between two H atoms in buck Ti₃SiC₂, we first place two H atoms into I-Ti, I-SiTi and I-SiC three sites, and then calculate the binding energy between two H atoms, which is defined as
$$
E^{s}(H)=E(ref+2H)+E(ref)-2E(ref+H), \tag{2}
$$
by such a definition, negative binding energy indicates attraction, while positive one indicates repulsion. The binding energy for two H atoms in I-Ti is 0.510 eV, indicating a repulsive H-H interaction, and the corresponding distance of the two H atoms is $1.57$ Å. In I-SiTi the binding energy is 0.669 eV, exhibiting a repulsive interaction between two H atoms and the distance is $2.11$ Å. Similarly, in I-SiC the two H atoms exhibit a weak repulsive interaction with a binding energy of 0.052 eV, and the distance between the two H atoms is $1.53$ Å. The results show that two H atoms are repulsive in most cases in perfect Ti₃SiC₂, so the H atom cannot be self-trapped. And the distance of two H atoms is much larger than that in a H₂ molecule ($0.76$ Å), so the H₂ molecule cannot also be formed directly.

Using our calculation, we find that H occupation surrounding the vacancy in Ti₃SiC₂ satisfies the rule of 'optimal charge density' [30-34], as shown in Fig. 3. When a single H atom is put at a Si vacancy, it prefers to stay 1.01 Å down of the Si vacancy rather than a Si substitution site. After considering the ZPE correction, The trapping energy of a single H atom to a Si vacancy is - 1.054 eV, which is negative. And the solution energy of H in a Si vacancy is - 3.880 eV, which is much lower than that of H in perfect Ti₃SiC₂, indicating that vacancies favorably trap H atoms. It can be understood by an early model of homogeneous electron gas [35-37]. H solution energy decreases as the electron density decreases. It means that H has lower solution energy where the electron density is reduced. The isovalue of electron density surrounding the vacancy is calculated to be about 0.06 e/Å³ [Fig. 3(a)], lower than the value of H surrounding the I-SiC site in prefect Ti₃SiC₂, 0.13 e/Å³ (Fig. 4). Therefore, the presence of vacancy reduces the electron density, and thus results in lower H solution energy in the vacancy. So the vacancy favorably traps H atoms.

For two H atoms, the trapping energy is - 1.300 eV with the ZPE correction. They form a dumbbell with H atoms residing on the up and down sides of a Si vacancy [Fig. 3(c)]. The distance of the two H atoms is about 2.25 Å, much larger than that in a H₂ molecule (0.76 Å). So two H atoms cannot directly form a H₂ molecule. But the binding energy of two H atoms in a Si vacancy is negative, - 0.247 eV, exhibiting an attractive H-H interaction. When adding the third H atom in the Si vacancy, the trapping energy becomes - 0.183 eV. And the third H atom will stay 2.02 Å up side of Ti(2) atom. For the fourth and fifth H atoms, the trapping energy is - 0.182 eV and - 0.247 eV. Similarly, they both stay 2.02 Å up side of Ti(2) atom symmetrically. And the third, fourth, and fifth H atoms form a regular triangle with a H atom residing at each vertex. The side length of the triangle is 3.52 Å. It indicates that the H₂ molecule cannot be formed. But for the sixth H atom, the tapping energy becomes positive, - 0.201 eV, suggesting that the sixth H is energetically unfavorably been trapped by a Si vacancy. Therefore, the maximal number of H atoms that can be trapped by a Si vacancy is five. Among all n H-vacancy complexes explored, the 2H-vacancy configuration has the lowest H trapping energy, so the 2H-vacancy is the predominant complex in Ti₃SiC₂.

We next investigate how the structure of Ti₃SiC₂ is affected when multiple H atoms are trapped by a Si vacancy. Table 2 gives lattice parameters and volume varying in Fig. 2. The lattice parameters and volume are basically increased with increasing number of trapped H atoms. The relative volume change is - 0.2248% when a Si vacancy is introduced, exhibiting a weak shrink. After embedding more H atoms, the volume begins to expand. The maximum volume change is 0.61102% when five H atoms are trapped. And the expansion does not show an obvious anisotropy. Table 3 gives lattice parameters and volume varying in Fig. 2. The bond length generally increases with increasing number of trapped H atoms and the largest expansion is seen for the Ti(2)-Si bond, followed by the Ti(1)-C, and lastly the C-Ti(2). It can be understood as follows. The structure of Ti₃SiC₂ can be considered as Si atomic layers interleaving with the edge-shared titanium carbide layer. The Ti(2)-C-Ti(1)-C-Ti(2) are linked by one Si atom. The bonding between Ti and C is stronger than that between Ti and Si. Due to the relative weak bonding between Ti and Si, the Si-Ti(2) bond more easily be broken and slip along the basal plane, then leads to the decomposition. The common decomposition phase is TiC, which has been observed in experiments [38-41]. H solution accelerates decohesion between Si-Ti layers and brittleness fracture initiates more easily from Si-Ti layers.

We also calculated the diffusion barrier of H in Ti₃SiC₂ with and without a Si vacancy. Here we use a drag method at a fixed volume and constrain the atomic positions to relax in a hyperplane perpendi- cular to the vector from the initial to final positions [42]. As discussed above, I-SiC is the most stable site for a H atom in buck Ti₃SiC₂. Therefore, we first assume that a H atom stays at I-SiC (Site 1), as illustrated in Fig. 5(a). It can migrate to its equivalent site (Site 3) or its nearest neighboring I-SiC site (Site 2). We calculated the diffusion barrier for the optimal diffusion path from I-SiC to its equivalent site (1-3 path) and the one from I-SiC to its nearest neighboring I-SiC (1-2 path). The diffusion barrier along 1-3 path is calculated to be 0.69 eV and along 1-2 path is calculated to be 2.64 eV [Fig. 5(c)]. When introducing a Si vacancy in Ti₃SiC₂, a H atom will prefer to stay 1.01 Å down of the Si vacancy (Site 1), as illustrated in Fig. 5(b). It can migrate to a I-SiC site (Site 3 or Site 4) (Site 3 and Site 4 are equivalent). We calculate the barrier from Site 1 to Site 4. The H atom has two possible paths of diffusion: path 1 is from Site 1 to Site 4 in a straight line (1-4 path); path 2 is from Site 1, then passing by Site 2, finally to Site 4 (1-2-4 path). The diffusion barrier along 1-2-4 path is calculated to be 2.83 eV, lower than the value along 1-4 path, as shown in Fig. 5(d). The saddle point of path 2 is just at Site 2. Therefore, the most possible path is 1-2-4 path. And Site 1 is more stable than Site 4 by 1.66 eV, indicating that the vacancy behaves like a trap for H atoms. And the barrier for a H atom diffusion from a I-SiC site to a vacancy is 1.17 eV, larger than that in metals [30,43-45]. The results imply that H atom is not easy to migrate in ₃SiC₂, even when a vacancy exists. Therefore, comparing with metals, H atoms to some extent cannot easily migrate, segregate to form bubble.

## 4. Conclusion

We have investigated the behaviors of H atoms in MAX phase material using a first-principles method with the ZPE correction. The preferential site for a single H atom is not a Si vacancy center but 1.01 Å down of the Si vacancy with a solution energy of about - 3.880 eV, lower than that in the perfect Ti₃SiC₂. Two H atoms exhibit an attractive interaction at the Si vacancy, while they exhibit a repulsive interaction in the perfect Ti₃SiC₂. According to the trap energy, a Si vacancy can hold up to five H atoms, but no H₂ molecule form. Meanwhile, the energy barrier for H migration from I-SiC to its nearest neighboring site is calculated to be 2.64 eV in perfect Ti₃SiC₂ and the one from an interstitial site to a Si vacancy is 1.17 eV in Ti₃SiC₂ with a Si vacancy. The energy barriers are both larger than those in metals, which indicate that to some extent H atoms cannot easily migrate or aggregate to form bubble. These results provide a useful reference for understanding the behaviors of H in Ti₃SiC₂ and give some applications for designing and processing Ti₃SiC₂-based materials.
