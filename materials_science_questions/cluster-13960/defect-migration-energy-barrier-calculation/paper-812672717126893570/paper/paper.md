# Tunable Hydrogen Doping of Metal Oxide Semiconductors with Acid-Metal Treatment at Ambient Condition

**ABSTRACT:** Hydrogen doping of metal-oxide semiconductors is promising for manipulation of their properties towards various applications. Yet it is quite challenging because of harsh reaction conditions and expensive metal catalysts. Meanwhile, acids as a cheap source of protons have long been unappreciated. Here, we developed a sophisticated acid-metal treatment for tunable hydrogenation of metal-oxide at ambient condition. Using first-principle simulations, we first showed that with proper work function difference between metal and metal-oxide, the H diffusion into negatively charged metal-oxide can be well controlled, resulting in tunable hydrogen doping of metal-oxides with quasi-metal characteristics. This has been verified by proof-of-concept experiments that achieved the controllable hydrogenation of WO₃ using Cu and hydrochloric acid at ambient conditions. Further, hydrogen doping of other metal oxides (TiO₂/Nb₂O₅/MoO₃) has been achieved by metal-acid treatment and induced change in properties. Our work provides a promising way of fine tailoring metal-oxide via tunable hydrogen doping.

Hydrogenation is one of the most useful techniques of tuning properties of metal-oxide semiconductors.¹⁻⁵ While it often relies on extreme conditions (high pressure and/or high temperature) and precious metal catalysts to make hydrogenated metal-oxides, including black TiO₂,⁶ MoO₃,⁷ WO₃,⁸ and VO₂⁹. The source of hydrogen atoms is hard to prepare due to the high energy barrier of dissociating H₂ molecules into hydrogen atoms.¹⁰⁻¹¹ On the other side, acid solutions contain abundant protons, making them a cheap source of hydrogen atoms. Unfortunately, most metal-oxide semiconductors cannot be directly treated by acid for hydrogenation, because they are either chemically inert (such as TiO₂¹², Nb₂O₅¹³) or easily undergo corrosion (like VO₂¹⁴) in acids. External voltage is typically required to drive protons to diffuse into metal-oxides, costing extra energies¹⁵.

In this work, we conducted a systematically study of combining theoretical and experimental to demonstrate a novel electron-proton co-doping strategy, via metal-acid treatment hydrogenation metal-oxides. We investigated the hydrogenation of oxide, including TiO₂, WO₃, MoO₃, and Nb₂O₅. Most non-noble metals of Al/Zn/Cu/Ag with lower work functions donate electrons to the interfaced semiconductors. Electrons drive protons in acid to enter the oxide lattice, producing hydrogen dopants.¹⁴ Especially, as first principle calculation suggests and experiment demonstrates thereafter, the use of Cu can realize a smooth control of hydrogenation of WO₃. This is because of modest work function difference between Cu and oxide leads to modest charge transfer and subsequent H diffusion barrier. Overall, we obtained hydrogen-doped oxide materials (TiO₂/WO₃/Nb₂O₅/MoO₃) at ambient conditions by metal-acid treatment, achieving delicate control of the optical, magnetic and electric properties of the metal-oxides, which are potentially useful. This metal-acid treatment indicates that metal serves as a delicate knob to modulate the interaction between protons and metal oxides in acid, which turns into a cost-effective protocol of tunable hydrogenation of metal oxides.

The mechanism of the electron-proton co-doping strategy is illustrated in Figure 1a: (1) The difference of work-functions drives electrons flow from the metal to the interfaced metal-oxide, causing negative charge accumulation in the oxide; (2) Negative charges in the oxide attract surrounding protons to diffuse into the lattice; (3) The interaction of electrons and protons inside the oxide lattice results in neutral hydrogen dopants. Taking anatase-TiO₂ as a model metal-oxide, we identified Al (4.11 eV), Zn (4.29 eV), Cu (4.32 eV), and Ag (4.44 eV) as proper electron donors, because their work functions are lower than that of TiO₂ (6.67 eV), allowing electrons to flow from the metals to TiO₂ (Figure S1 and Figure 1a). We then built an interface model (Figure S2) of the metals and the anatase-TiO₂ (101) facet, based on which the computed differential charge distributions confirmed the transfer of electrons from the metals to TiO₂ (Figure 1b). Such interfacial charge polarization results in 0.02~0.09 electrons being transferred from each metal atom to the oxide (Table S1), leaving negative charges in TiO₂. We have also found that the thickness of metal part has little impact on the interface (Figure 1b vs Figure S3), as evidenced by the Bader charge analysis (Table S1 vs Table S2). Additionally, contact between metal and oxide is needed for effective charge transfer at the interface (Figure S4).

Afterwards, three different sites on the anatase-TiO₂ (101) surface (Figure 2a) were examined for H binding with oxygen atoms. The energy barriers along the Hydrogen migration pathways from the three binding sites were investigated (Figure S5). The reaction profiles of H migrating under different charge conditions (1 hole, neutral, and 1 electron) are collected in Figure 2b-d. The energy barriers decrease with the negative charge increase, suggesting that negative charges in anatase-TiO₂ promote H diffusion into its bulk lattice. Starting with the case of site 1 (Figure 2b), in the neutral condition (0 e), the migration of a hydrogen atom from the lattice surface to subsurface has to overcome a barrier ($\Delta E_b$) of 0.97 eV and take a heat ($\Delta E$) of 0.22 eV. In comparison, the negative charge condition (1 e) appreciably lowers $\Delta E_b$ (0.47 eV) and turns the endothermic reaction into exothermic (-0.45 eV), while the positive charge condition (1 h) raises $\Delta E_b$ (1.24 eV) and makes the reaction more endothermic ($\Delta E = 0.45$ eV). For site 2 (Figure 2c), both $\Delta E_b$ (0.65 eV) and $\Delta E$ (-0.06 eV) are lower than for site 1 in the neutral condition, and they experience a small decrease at the negative charge condition. For site 3 (Figure 2d), both $\Delta E_b$ (1.18 eV) and $\Delta E$ (0.70 eV) are highest among the three different sites. While the negative charge condition barely changes these values, the positive charged condition can substantially increase them ($\Delta E_b$=1.64 eV, $\Delta E$=1.06 eV). In short, site 1 at the negative charge condition represents the most favorable situation for hydrogen penetration.

We then carried out experiments to examine the hydrogenation effect of anatase-TiO₂ (Figure S6 shows morphology) treated in hydrochloric acid solution and Zn particles. Such metal-acid treatment turned the white pristine anatase-TiO₂ into black ones (Figure 3a). Figure 3b displays the $^1$H NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) spectra data, in which the peak at the 0.90 ppm chemical shift indicates H signal in the TiO₂ lattice.$^{[16]}$ Another $^1$H NMR peak of 3.6 ppm is probably due to hydrogen in another environment. The experimental UV-Vis-IR absorption spectra were displayed in Figure 3c. The pristine sample only absorbs UV light, while the treated one shows a considerably larger absorption in the visible and IR regions. Moreover, the simulated band structures (Figure S7) and computed absorption spectra (Figure S8) of pristine and H-doped anatase-TiO₂ confirmed the removal of the bandgap and promotion of visible light harvesting due to H-doping.

The ESR (electron spin-resonance) spectra of the H-doped anatase-TiO₂ showed a strong signal at g = 1.94 (Figure 3d), which can be attributed to the paramagnetic Ti³⁺ center. Owing to the introduced Ti³⁺ center, the H-doped TiO₂ sample exhibited higher degree of room temperature ferromagnetism than the pristine TiO₂ (Figure 3e). The calculated density of states (DOS) in Figure S9 also showed that the hydrogenation breaks the electron spin- symmetry. The DOS of the H-doped TiO₂ suggests that the hydrogenation shifts up the Fermi level and induces the metal insulator transition (MIT) transitions. This would significantly enhance the electronic charge carrier concentration.$^{[17]}$ Further experiments confirmed that electrical conductivity is increasing significantly by 5 magnitudes with hydrogenation (Figure 3f). We have then examined the H-doped TiO₂ samples by XPS (Figure S10) and confirmed that the surface is free of residual Zn. In sharp contrast, the charges transferring from noble metals Au/Pd/Pt with high workfunction to TiO₂ is much less (Figure S11a and Table S1). As expected, the Au/Pt/Pd-acid treatment in the experiment also failed to hydrogenate the TiO₂ (Figure S11b).

The metal-acid treatment for driving electron-proton co-doping has been successfully applied to other oxides (Nb₂O₅/MoO₃/WO₃). DFT computations revealed that these oxides hold higher work functions than Zn (Figure S12), allowing electron donation to oxides if interfaced with Zn as evidenced by computed differential charge distribution (Figure S13 and Table S3). The migration pathways and the energy diagrams for the H atom to diffuse from surface to subsurface of Nb₂O₅, MoO₃ and WO₃ are also determined (Figure S14-16). As expected, the barriers $\Delta E_b$ along the diffusion pathways and reaction energies $\Delta E$ decrease with increasing electron concentration (Figure 4a, Table S4 and Table S5).

The H-doping samples was validated by measured ¹H NMR signals and those panel pictures showing the conversion from white pristine oxide to darkened samples in experiments using the Zn-acid treatment (Figure 4b-d). The change of sample colors were further supported by the promoted visible-light absorptions spectra (Figure 4e and Figure S17), which are ascribed to the substantial narrowing of the energy bandgap due to hydrogenation. The DOS features of Nb₂O₅, MoO₃ and WO₃ before and after hydrogenation (Figure S18-20) confirm the shift-up of Fermi levels that induce MIT transitions. Moreover, the increasing of Fermi levels will enhance the electronic charge carrier concentration, causing significant enhancements of electrical conductivities for 4~7 magnitudes as measurements in Figure 4f. In contrast, DFT simulations revealed very high energy barriers along the H migration pathways (Figure S21-22) for SnO₂ and ZrO₂. This electron-proton co-doping strategy has poor performance in the hydrogenation of these two oxides in experiment despite their work function is higher than Zn (Figure S23). Importantly, DFT simulations suggests that hydrogenation disfavors oxygen vacancy formation and thereby promotes structural stability (Figure S24-25). Correspondingly, it was found in experiment that the hydrogenated oxide samples of TiO₂, Nb₂O₅, MoO₃ and WO₃ remained intact in acid solution for a very long time (4 weeks), confirming the substantially improved material stability (Figure S26). In contrast, no hydrogenation has been observed if the oxide particles were added after Zn were dissolved completely in the HCl solution (Figure S27).

Upon contact with WO₃, Cu supplies less electrons than Zn (Figure S12 and Table S3) because of smaller work function difference between Cu and WO₃ (Figure S1). We expect that the H diffusion barrier of Cu-acid treatment decreases less (Figure 4a), which allows us to easily tune the degree of H-doping, as evidenced by stepwise hydrogenation of WO₃ using Cu and hydrochloric acid (Figure 5a). The corresponding PDOS of HₓW₈O₂₄ (x = 1~4) are collected in Figure 5b (Their models are shown in Figure S28). The emergence of filled states below the Fermi level, acting as charge carriers, is critical for the quasi-metallic characters of these hydrogenated WO₃. Further, the linear increase (0.88, 1.78, 2.72 and 3.60) of shaded areas of PDOS, suggests that the increase of

charge carrier owing to H-doping can be quantitatively controlled by the amount of H dopants.

**Figure 5.** Tunable hydrogenation of $WO_3$. (a) $WO_3$ hydrogenated by Cu-acid treatment from 0 to 12 hour. (b) Simulated projected density of states (PDOS) of $WO_3$ with different concentrations of H dopants in the lattice.

In summary, by a combined computational and experimental study, we have established a cost-effective approach featured by acid-metal treatment to achieve tunable hydrogen doping of metal-oxide (anatase-$TiO_2/WO_3/MoO_3/Nb_2O_5$) at ambient conditions. It avoids the use of expensive catalysts and extreme reaction conditions. First principle calculations revealed that the driving force of H diffusion is the work function difference between metal and oxide. It determines the metal/metal-oxide interfacial charge transfer that decisively affect the energy barrier of H diffusion into the subsurface of metal-oxides. We then predict that Cu may be the metal for the ease of controllable hydrogen doping using this approach, because its work function is neither too small (like Zn) that may leads to rapid hydrogenation without dedicate control, nor too large (like Au/Pt/Pd) that will not be usable for this approach. This strategy cannot be applied to $SnO_2$ and $ZrO_2$ for high energy barriers. The acid-metal treatment induce MIT behavior, and substantially improves structural stability, light absorption, electric conductivity and magnetic properties of the semiconductors. Importantly, this alternative hydrogenation protocol is controllable and electronic structure of oxide ($WO_3$) can be quantitatively tunable by controlling H-doped concentration. We expect that the developed approach has a great potential for tuning electric, optical and magnetic properties of metal-oxides towards various applications including photocatalysis, optical devices, and sensors, etc.
