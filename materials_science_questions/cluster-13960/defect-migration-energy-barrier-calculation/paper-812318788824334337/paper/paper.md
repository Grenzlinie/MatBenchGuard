## ABSTRACT
The microscopic doping mechanism behind the superconductor-to-insulator transition of a thin film of $YBa_2Cu_3O_7$ was recently identified as due to the migration of O atoms from the CuO chains of the film. Here, we employ density-functional theory calculations to study the evolution of the electronic structure of a slab of $YBa_2Cu_3O_7$ in the presence of oxygen vacancies under the influence of an external electric field. We find that, under massive electric fields, isolated O atoms are pulled out of the surface consisting of CuO chains. As vacancies accumulate at the surface, a configuration with vacancies located in the chains inside the slab becomes energetically preferred, thus providing a driving force for O migration toward the surface. Regardless of the defect configuration studied, the electric field is always fully screened near the surface, thus negligibly affecting diffusion barriers across the film.

## I. INTRODUCTION
The electric double layer (EDL) technique has been employed during the past 15 years to induce extremely large electric fields at the interface with oxides¹ by making use of ionic liquids as gate dielectrics. This has allowed us to achieve extremely high carrier densities that accumulate at the interface to screen the electric field and that, in turn, modify the electronic structure of the oxide.²⁻⁶ In the case of high-temperature cuprate superconductors, the EDL technique has been used to efficiently tune the electronic properties of the oxide from the superconducting to the insulating phase.⁷⁻¹⁰ In the case of gating experiments performed on the VO₂-based EDL transistor, the suppression of the metal-to-insulator transition upon electrolyte gating was found to involve oxygen migration from the oxide to the ionic liquid.¹¹,¹² Following this discovery, EDL-gating experiments inducing a superconductor-to-insulator transition in $YBa_2Cu_3O_{7-\delta}$¹³ have recently been revisited.¹⁴ The change in x-ray absorption spectra collected *in situ* while studying transport properties during gating was interpreted using density-functional theory (DFT) calculations. The spectral changes were associated with deoxygenation of the sample, thus confirming that the doping mechanism behind the change in resistivity was primarily a chemical doping achieved by the formation of oxygen vacancies.¹⁴

In a follow-up study,¹⁵ we employed density-functional theory (DFT)-based calculations to compute Kohn–Sham-based Cu–K edge x-ray spectra and study the electronic structure resulting from different doping mechanisms in $YBa_2Cu_3O_7$ (YBCO). Although we unambiguously identified the doping mechanism as the migration of O atoms from the CuO chains (see Fig. 1),¹⁴,¹⁵ many questions still remain concerning the role of the external field on the electronic structure of YBCO and its influence in the vacancy formation process. For example, we wish to clarify the extent and localization of charge doping, its role in the vacancy formation energy, and the change in the electronic structure upon O migration. In order to address these questions, we study the evolution of the electronic structure of a thin film of YBCO as a function of externally applied electric fields, here simulated by self-consistently adding a saw-tooth potential to the bare ionic potential. Several O-vacancy concentrations and configurations within the film are studied, and the computed formation energies for increasing fields (from 2 to 30 V/nm) are discussed. The predicted negative formation energies show that massive fields can pull O atoms from the surface, in agreement with low-temperature experimental evidence.¹³,¹⁴ As vacancies accumulate on the surface, the relative stability of surface and *bulk* vacancies changes, thus driving O atoms to migrate to the surface. For none of the oxygen-deficient configurations studied here, the applied field can penetrate into the slab. This result, together

with the weak field evolution of the vacancy formation energies, suggests that the primary role of the field is to create O-vacancy defects on the surface. The large diffusion barriers computed using nudged-elastic band calculations suggest that the simple O migration models employed here cannot describe the dynamics of the process.

## II. COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS

The electronic structure and geometrical optimization were performed using the PWSCF utility of QUANTUM ESPRESSO.¹⁶ We use the PBE (Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof) functional, Rabbe-Rabe-Kaxiras-Joannopoulos ultrasoft pseudopotentials¹⁷ available within the pslibrary, and wavefunctions and charge density cutoffs of 50 and 450 Ry, respectively. Paramagnetic YBa₂Cu₃O₇ (both slab and bulk) is here modeled by performing spin unpolarized calculations without any Hubbard $U$ correction. DFT + U is only employed when explicitly mentioned. See, for example, Secs. III B and III C. Geometrical optimizations are performed until the forces are lower than 0.003 eV/Å and the stress along $z$ is less than 0.015 kbar. Calculations were performed using a slab-vacuum geometry: slabs are always CuO chain-terminated to reflect experimental evidence¹⁸,¹⁹ and consistent with a neutrally terminated surface and estimated lowest surface energy.²⁰

Because experimentally¹³,¹⁴ YBCO is grown epitaxially on top of PrBa₂Cu₃O₇/SrTiO₃ (PBCO/STO) yielding a lattice mismatch with a STO of $-1.4\%$ and $-0.7\%$ along the $a$ and $b$ axes, respectively, we adopt here a tetragonal lattice yielding a similarly strained lattice and impose a lattice constant of 3.9419 Å for the primitive cell. $2 \times 2$ supercells within the plane are then generated using this lattice constant. Geometrical optimizations are performed by fixing the in-plane lattice constant in order to be consistent with the experimental procedure. The Brillouin zone is sampled using $9 \times 9$ and $5 \times 5$ Monkhorst-Pack k-point grids for the primitive and $2 \times 2$ supercell, respectively, for band structure calculations. For the geometrical optimization of vacancies on the $2 \times 2$ supercell, a $3 \times 3$ k-point grid is used. In this case, the crystal was repeated only twice along $z$ in order to reduce the computational resources. We, nonetheless, checked that this has a negligible effect on the vacancy formation energy (vide infra). The surface-projected bulk band structures and the surface bands of the slabs are plotted by aligning the local self-consistent potential of the bulk with the same potential in the center of the slab. Calculations are performed by applying the dipole correction.²¹,²² The intrinsic dipole and the external electric field potential are treated self-consistently by adding a sawtooth potential²³ to the bare ionic potential in the direction normal to the surface such that a net electric field of the desired amount is applied. To recover periodicity, the potential must be discontinuous and we make sure that the discontinuity lies in the middle of the vacuum region. The Climbing Image-Nudged-Elastic Band (CI-NEB) calculations for the migration of vacancies through the slab are performed using a $7 \times 7$ Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid for the primitive cell and a $3 \times 3$ grid for the $2 \times 2$ supercell. Calculations were converged when forces orthogonal to the path are less than 0.1 eV/Å.

## III. RESULTS

### A. Electric field applied to stoichiometric YBa₂Cu₃O₇

We first study a slab composed of three-unit cells along the $z$ direction and primitive within the plane, as shown in Fig. 1. This choice is consistent with experiments where a film composed of three-unit cells was grown on top of STO/PBCO.¹⁴ Figure 1 shows the comparison at the zero field between the slab band structure (red lines) and the surface-projected bulk bands (shaded gray). A few surface states arising from the termination of the slab are also

shown as these will be more strongly affected by the applied field. We apply an electric field of increasing strength to the three-unit cell slab and analyze the response in terms of valence charge density, $\Delta\rho$, and potential energy, $\Delta V_{z}$. The planar average of the charge density and the electrostatic potential energy along the $z$ direction is shown in the top panels of Fig. 2 for the slab under the zero field. As expected, electrons accumulate and deplete near the surfaces. However, screening of the field at the interface is not complete and the electric field penetrates a few atomic layers. We remind that YBCO is a 2D metal with metallic states localized mainly within the $CuO_{2}$ planes and along the apical O sites. $^{15,24}$ A full screening is achieved in correspondence to the first $CuO_{2}$ metallic plane whose position is indicated (only on one side of the slab for clarity) by the vertical black dashed line in Fig. 2. The planar average of $\Delta V_{z}$ thus gives a finite value of the dielectric constant within the first three atomic layers. Because no ionic contribution is considered here, this approach yields the optical dielectric constant $\epsilon_{\infty}$. We compute $\epsilon_{\infty}$ as $D/E$, where $D$ is the applied electric field in the absence of dielectric and $E$ is the planar average of the electrostatic potential, i.e., the screened field. $^{25,26}$ Practically, we use the ratio between the coefficient of the linear regression of $D$ and $E$, i.e., between the bare potential outside the slab, in the vacuum region, and the screened potential inside the slab within a few atomic layers. We compute a value of 147. The same result was obtained, as expected, by using the effective screening medium with the metal-slab-metal by Otani and Sugino. $^{27}$ Because the saw-tooth potential scheme results in a significantly faster convergence of the self-consistent field, we adopt the former method throughout this work. Consistent with the charge doping occurring only at the surface [Fig. 2(a)], the changes in the slab band structure in the presence of electric field mostly affect the surface states shown in Fig. 1 and the most visible effect is the lifting of the degeneracy of these states [see Fig. 2(a)].

This is consistent with previous computational studies $^{24}$ and the experimental structural characterization of varying $YBa_{2}Cu_{3}O_{7-\delta}$ stoichiometries, which place the vacancy defects within the chains. $^{28-30}$ Thus, in what follows, we omit the study of defects within the planes.

First, under massive external fields, O atoms are pulled out of the surface at small concentrations [configurations $(a)$ and $(e)$]. Finally, we comment on the role of the magnetic ordering and the electronic structure on the vacancy formation energy. In order to assess the validity of our approach of using spin unpolarized calculations to study the paramagnetic phase, we compare the formation energy of O vacancies computed using PBE and a spin unpolarized calculation with those computed with an antiferromagnetic phase and a PBE + U approach. We employ a $2\times2\times1$ supercell in bulk geometry and compute one, two, and four oxygen vacancies within the CuO chain in configurations $(a)$, $(c)$, and $(g)$, respectively. The spin-polarized calculations are performed using an antiferromagnetic ordering of C-type using Hubbard $U$ values of $6\ \text{eV}$ for all Cu atoms and $4\ \text{eV}$ for the O atoms. The formation energy per O vacancy is 1.44, 1.27, and $1.33\ \text{eV}$ for one, two, and four vacancies, respectively, for the paramagnetic phase (similar to the values computed for the slab and reported in Table I). We compute 1.39, 1.29, and $1.35\ \text{eV}$, for the antiferromagnetic phase. The same trend is found for the two sets of calculations, which is expected for formation energies of vacancies located within the chains where the Cu(I) atoms exhibit a zero net magnetization even in the spin-polarized calculations.

### C. Electric field in the presence of oxygen vacancies

We assess whether the electric field can penetrate into the slab when a progressively increasing amount of O vacancies are created, first at the surface and then within the chains throughout the slab. We employed a two-unit cell slab along $z$ and either a primitive or a unit cell in-plane, depending on the case. We computed the following scenarios: (i) a slab with bulk stoichiometry $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$ and one vacancy per supercell on the surface, i.e., configuration $(a)$ in Fig. 3; (ii) a slab with $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$ bulk stoichiometry and Cu-terminated, i.e., configuration $(g)$ in Fig. 3; (iii) a slab with stoichiometry $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$, i.e., where all O atoms from every chain (there are three planes of chains in total) are removed; and (iv) a slab with $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$ bulk stoichiometry and OH terminated, i.e., a OH group is attached on top of each Cu at the surface. As in Sec. III B, scenarios (i), (ii), and (iv) were computed by performing a full geometrical optimization by fixing only the atomic positions of the surface plane far from the vacancies. We recall that $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ is a semiconductor exhibiting G-type antiferromagnetic order in the ground state. $^{15,24}$ Thus, in the absence of surface metallic states, one would expect that the external field does penetrate into the slab. Scenario (iii) was computed by adopting a $\sqrt{2}a \times \sqrt{2}a$ in-plane supercell in order to accommodate this antiferromagnetic phase. A full atomic relaxation is performed by imposing the G-type antiferromagnetic order and by fixing the lattice constant $a$ to the strained value used so far. As discussed in our previous study, $^{15}$ a Hubbard $U$ correction of 9 and 5 eV was used for Cu and inplane O atoms in order to recover the insulating phase. The slab is then built without allowing for further atomic relaxations.

Scenario (iv) also represents a neutral slab but with a larger positive nominal charge on the Cu ion. If we assume Cu(I) for the ions on the chains, in the presence of $\text{OH}^-$, these become Cu(II). The planar averages of $\Delta V_z$ and $\Delta \rho$ computed for cases (i)-(iv) are reported in Fig. 5 by aligning in all cases the first plane of Cu atoms (the vertical black line at $5$ Å in the upper left panel of Fig. 5). In none of the cases shown in Fig. 5 does the electric field penetrate into the slab. While for $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$, the electric field is fully screened in correspondence to the $\text{CuO}_2$ plane (see the black curve in the lower panel of Fig. 5), for (i)-(iv), the field is screened at the surface. Since the electrostatic potential profile along $z$ for a slab with two alternated superficial vacancies [i.e., configuration $(e)$] is similar to configuration $(a)$, it is here not shown for clarity. For cases (ii) and (iii), a strong depolarization field is found within the first three atomic planes and it vanishes at the $\text{CuO}_2$ metallic plane [vertical orange and purple lines in the upper panel of Fig. 5(a)]. The reason behind this depolarization effect may be found in the free charge carriers at the surface in both situations. In the case of the $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ slab, this can be understood by looking at the comparison between the surface-projected bulk band structure of $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ and the electronic structure of the $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ slab, shown in Fig. 5(b), lower panel. While bulk $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ is a semiconductor, the corresponding Cu-terminated $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ slab exhibits two metallic states on the surface that fully screen the electric field. In the case of $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$ with a fully emptied surface in configuration $(g)$, we see a similar situation. By comparing the band structure of configuration $(g)$ reported in Fig. 5 with the case of the stoichiometric $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$ slab in Fig. 2, we can identify the states arising from the surface defects. The state crossing the Fermi level along the $\Gamma$-X path both in the $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_6$ slab and in the superficially emptied $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_7$ slab in Fig. 5 exhibits similar character and localizes on the superficial Cu extending toward apical O.

### D. Diffusion barriers

The minimum energy profile for the O-vacancy diffusion across the slab was computed by using the CI-NEB method. $^{33}$ Since the bulk vacancies become energetically more favored than surface vacancies only at high concentrations, we compute the minimum energy path for oxygen diffusion across the slab, considering four O atoms (per unit cell) moving together from the surface toward the center of the slab [from configuration $(g)$ to $(g')$]. Practically, this is done by employing the in-plane primitive cell and two-unit cells along $z$. It corresponds to a migration of all surface O atoms

together in a concerted movement as imposed by the symmetry of the cell. The path shown in Fig. 1 was computed using five images for each of the five sub-paths, i.e., between one O position and the next across the slab, as shown in Fig. 1. All atoms in the slab are allowed to move, and only the surface plane away from the vacancies is fixed. The results shown in Fig. 6 show that the energy profile and the large diffusion barriers computed with no field are negligibly modified by the external field. A small change in the presence of the field can be observed only for the first pathway, when the vacancy moves from the surface to the apical position, i.e., from image 1 to image 2, corresponding to configurations $(g)$ and $(h)$ in Fig. 3, in agreement with our previous analysis, reporting a short field-penetration length. Since this concerted movement of all O atoms together as imposed by the model may not represent the true dynamics, the energy profile of one O-vacancy migration across the slab was computed starting from configuration $(a)$ in Fig. 3 and allowing only one vacancy per $2 \times 2$ unit cell (and two-unit cells along $z$), using seven images for each pathway. To reduce the computational resources, we allow only the O atom to move during the path optimization, while the rest of the atoms are frozen. The lower panel of Fig. 6 shows again large barriers reaching up to 3 eV. For the second and fifth path, we perform a full geometrical optimization by fixing only one surface plane. The energy profile is shown in the lower panel of Fig. 6. Although the energy barriers decrease by around a factor of 2 for these two pathways, they are still large and cannot explain the O diffusion across the slab observed during experiments at low temperature (220 K) upon application of gate voltage. $^{14}$ Further studies are needed to identify the microscopic migration mechanism of O atoms. We anticipate that an improved description of the electronic correlation and, therefore, the electronic density by using, for example, a Hubbard $U$ approach $^{34,35}$ can be expected to somehow modify the potential energy profile of the pathway of O migration, especially near the $CuO_2$ planes.

## IV. CONCLUSION

We have studied the evolution of the electronic structure and the change in vacancy formation energy of a slab of CuO chain-terminated YBCO as a function of the applied external field. Our work provides a picture consistent with experimental observations for the mechanism behind the field-induced deoxygenation of YBCO. We demonstrate that massive external fields induce the formation of isolated O vacancies at the surface. O migration can be understood in terms of competition in the stability of surface- and bulk-vacancy defects. When the slab surface is fully emptied of O atoms, the defect configuration with vacancies in the center of the slab becomes preferred and this may provide a driving force for the migration of O atoms toward the surface. These O atoms at the surface are, in turn, pulled out by the external field generated by the polarized ionic liquid in the experiment. The process starts over until there are no O atoms left in the chains, thus leading to a stoichiometry close to $YBa_3Cu_2O_6$ as found experimentally. $^{14}$ The relative stability of surface vs bulk vacancies changes when defects accumulate, even at the zero field, and the external field only slightly enhances this difference. Because the external field never penetrates into the slab, its role appears to be limited to this electric force pulling charge outward. Even when the slab is fully emptied of O atoms from every chain, thus yielding a stoichiometry of $YBa_2Cu_3O_6$, which is semiconductor in bulk, surface states with metallic character arise and fully screen the external field. Additional studies, possibly including NEB calculations with more O atoms moving independently and adopting an improved treatment of electronic correlation, should be performed to identify the diffusion mechanism.
