# Atomic-Scale Mechanisms for Low-NIEL Dopant-Type Dependent Damage in Si

M. J. Beck, L. Tsetseris, M. Caussanel, R. D. Schrimpf, *Fellow, IEEE*, D. M. Fleetwood, *Fellow, IEEE*, and S. T. Pantelides, *Member, IEEE*

**Abstract**—While calculated non-ionizing energy loss (NIEL) generally correlates well to first order with radiation-induced displacement damage rates, it does not account for some well-known differences in damage rates for $n$- and $p$-type Si. Here we show that the magnitude of these differences, $\Delta K_{n-p}$, correlates closely with the fraction of total displacement damage due to low-energy primary knock-on atom (PKA) recoils. The primary products of these displacement damage events, with PKA recoils $<\sim2$ keV, are close vacancy-interstitial pairs, or Frenkel Pairs (FPs). Based on previous studies of vacancy-dopant complex stabilities in Si, and new parameter-free quantum mechanical calculations of FP properties, details of the stable defect profiles arising from low-energy PKA recoil events are shown to give rise to non-zero values of $\Delta K_{n-p}$.

**Index Terms**—Density functional theory, displacement damage, electron traps, Frenkel pairs.

## I. INTRODUCTION

**R**ADIATION-INDUCED displacement damage rates in semiconductor materials generally correlate well with the calculated non-ionizing energy loss (NIEL) portion of the total radiative energy deposition rate [1]. Developing and refining models for the calculation of NIEL has been a continuing focus of the radiation effects research community (see, e.g., [2] and [3]). In its simplest form, a calculation of NIEL considers only the scattering cross-section and average effective recoil energy for a given target and irradiating particle, as well as the Lindhard partition function for the non-ionizing portion of the transferred energy [1]. The details of specific particle-target interactions are neglected. Thus, systems with the same overall rate of energy deposition, i.e., equivalent NIEL, may be experiencing either a small number of high-energy displacement damage events per particle per unit track length, or a large number of low-energy events per particle per unit track length.

While NIEL has enjoyed wide success as a first-order predictor of effective displacement damage rate, a number of experiments have identified discrepancies in the correlation of NIEL to observed radiation-induced damage rates for some types of devices and irradiation conditions (see [1] and citations therein). For example, it is well known that $p$- and $n$-type Si can exhibit differing damage rates, as determined from observed reductions in effective minority carrier lifetimes or diffusion lengths (see, e.g., [1], [4]–[6], and citations therein). In these cases, $p$-type Si exhibits a *lower rate of reduction* in carrier lifetimes than $n$-type Si (see Figs. 1 and 2). This difference is typically interpreted to indicate a lower concentration of radiation-induced defects—that is, less damage—in the $p$-type Si samples, due to an atomistic mechanism that must differ in effect between $p$- and $n$-type Si.

Here we show that the damage rate differences in $n$- and $p$-type Si correlate directly to the fraction of radiation-induced displacement damage due to low-energy primary knock-on atom (PKA) recoils. These low-energy damage events produce isolated vacancy-interstitial pairs (Frenkel Pairs, FPs) that quickly evolve towards thermodynamic equilibrium following the initial radiation event. The role of vacancy-impurity complexes in displacement damage has been investigated extensively over the last ~50 years. In particular, the relative stability of vacancy-impurity complexes has been shown to differ in p- and n-type Si. Vacancy-P pairs, stable to ~125 °C, form in n-Si at room temperature, but the equivalent defects in p-Si, vacancy-B pairs, are much less stable. These results have been demonstrated both in annealing studies [7] and confirmed with first-principles calculations [8] Therefore, fewer radiation induced vacancies form stable vacancy-impurity complexes in p-Si active device regions as opposed to n-Si active device regions, resulting in lower effective radiation damage rates in p-Si. The magnitude of these differences can depend on the particular devices used, the details of the radiation exposure, and the experimental technique that one uses to estimate differences in displacement damage [1]. This is evident in a comparison of data in Figs. 1 and 2.

To study the details of the atomic mechanisms that ultimately lead to differing damage rates in n- and p-type Si, we have performed new calculations of the fundamental properties of vacancy-interstitial (Frenkel) pairs (FPs) in Si using density functional theory. We show that isolated FPs either recombine, remain as interacting vacancy-interstitial pairs, or dissociate into free vacancies and interstitials, with probabilities that depend on the position of the Fermi level in the Si, i.e., the doping type. In particular, we find that, in p-Si small FPs (vacancy-interstitial separation distances ~8 Å) are more likely to be stable with respect to defect recombination than equivalent FPs in n-Si. The stability of FPs against dissociation is found to be similar to that of vacancy-B pairs, with a FP binding energy (relative to the energy of non-interacting, isolated vacancies and interstitials) of ~0.5 eV. Thus, the formation dynamics of vacancy-impurity complexes, shown here to be indirectly influenced by the fundamental properties of FPs, are likely dominant in determining observed n- and p-type Si damage rate differences for temperatures above ~0 °C, but FPs may directly influence n- and p-type differences at lower temperatures.

## II. LOW RECOIL ENERGY LIMIT

Fig. 1, reproduced from Srour *et al.* [1], plots measured damage coefficients after proton irradiation derived from the common emitter gain (a function of minority carrier lifetime) for $p-n-p$ and $n-p-n$ switching transistors normalized to 1 MeV neutron damage, while Fig. 2, reproduced from Summers, *et al.* [6], plots measured minority carrier diffusion length damage coefficients after electron irradiation in 1 Ωcm p- and n-type Si. In both data sets, the damage coefficients reported reflect the rate of *reduction* with radiation fluence of minority carrier lifetime, or equivalently, diffusion length, in the samples. These results show a divergence of damage coefficients with respect to calculated NIEL based on dopant type for two cases: electron irradiated Si and proton irradiated transistors. In these cases p-Si exhibits a lower rate of minority carrier lifetime *reduction* with irradiation than n-Si. Therefore, for $K$ the diffusion lifetime (or diffusion length) damage coefficient, we have $\Delta K_{n-p}>0$, where $\Delta K_{n-p}\equiv K_{n}-K_{p}$, and subscripts indicate dopant type. For electron-irradiated Si (Fig. 2), $\Delta K_{n-p}$ decreases with increasing NIEL, disappearing altogether at ~0.3 keV-cm²/g. In apparent contrast (Fig. 1), proton-irradiated transistors exhibit *increasing* $\Delta K_{n-p}$ with increasing NIEL from $\Delta K_{n-p}=0$ at ~2 keV-cm²/g.

While $\Delta K_{n-p}$ is a complex function of device type, damage rate measurement technique, and doping levels, as evident from a comparison of transistor data in Fig. 1 and bulk Si data in Fig. 2, Summers [6] has pointed out that the magnitude of $\Delta K_{n-p}$ for electron-irradiated Si is proportional to the fraction of NIEL resulting from PKA recoils of less than 2 keV. This "NIEL fraction" is analogous to the fraction of net PKA recoil energy due to recoils less than 2 keV—a quantity that may be directly extracted from simulations with SRIM [12]. For individual events with recoils in this range, the "initial" radiation induced damage in Si (that is, radiation damage immediately after instantaneous irradiation, and before annealing of any kind) takes the form of isolated vacancy-interstitial pairs (FPs). As all higher energy recoils contribute equivalently to the initial defect spectrum (see [13]), the correlation of $\Delta K_{n-p}$ with fraction of NIEL due to recoils of less than 2 keV is therefore a correlation with the fraction of total initial damage that takes the form of isolated FPs.

Extending this reasoning to proton irradiation, note that while NIEL is inversely proportional to the kinetic energy of incident protons (see inset scale in Fig. 1) and proportional to the kinetic energy of incident electrons, the fraction of total NIEL due to low-energy PKA recoils is inversely proportional to the kinetic energy of *both* particles. For the incident particle energy range of interest ($\sim$1 to 100 MeV for electrons, and $\sim$5 to 500 MeV for protons), electrons and protons give nearly equivalent fractions of total PKA energy from low-energy recoils. This is strong evidence that the fundamental nature of displacement damage for low PKA recoils lies at the heart of observed differences in $n$- and $p$-Si damage rates.

### III. PROPERTIES OF LOW-ENERGY PKA DAMAGE

#### A. Calculation Method

The initial damage resulting from low-energy PKA recoils primarily takes the form of isolated, close vacancy-interstitial pairs, or FPs. The stability and properties of these FPs directly influence the stable, post-irradiation defect profile. Therefore, the fundamental properties of FPs are important inputs in an understanding of damage rate differences in n- and p-type Si.
Here we report the energetics of FPs in Si, from parameter-free quantum mechanical calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) and the local density approximation (LDA), as implemented in the total energy and molecular dynamics program VASP (Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Program) [14], [15]. Core-electron interactions were modeled using Vanderbilt ultrasoft pseudopotentials [16], [17], and the Ceperley–Alder functional [18], as parameterized by Perdew and Zunger [19], was used to describe exchange and correlation. The electronic wavefunction was expanded in plane waves with a kinetic energy cutoff of 188 eV (13.6 Ryd). Reciprocal space summations were performed using a Monkhorst-Pack [20] mesh centered at the $\Gamma$-point with 9 $k$-points per unit reciprocal lattice vector. When calculating properties of systems with an excess (or deficit) of electrons, a uniform background charge of opposite sign is included to give a net neutral calculation cell. Convergence with respect to the plane wave cutoff was checked by calculating the total energy per atom and equilibrium lattice constant of bulk Si at cutoffs up to 301 eV (21.8 Ryd). These convergence parameters are consistent with the results of a recent and extensive study of calculation settings for point defects in Si [21].

Initially unrelaxed FPs were constructed in $3 \times 3 \times 3$ simple cubic cells containing 216 Si atoms with a Si lattice parameter of 5.39 Å. Nine symmetrically distinct initial configurations of a vacancy and a tetrahedral Si interstitial were studied for their total energy, relaxed stable structure and charge density. FP stability is determined simply by examining the relaxed (zero-force) ground-state structure for each initial defect configuration. Many FPs are found to recombine, consistent with previous expectations [7], however, there is a nontrivial fraction of defects that minimize their interatomic forces by adopting a relaxed structure resembling that of the initial (defected) configuration. These are considered stable defects for the purposes of our calculations, and represent a global metastable state (the defected crystal) separated from the global ground-state (the undefected crystal) by a finite energy barrier.

These results show that FPs are generally much more stable in $p$-Si than in $n$-Si. The persistence of FPs at finite temperature in $p$-type Si is controlled by both a recombination barrier of $\sim$0.7 eV, and a disassociation barrier (binding energy) of between 0.26 eV and 1.01 eV, depending on FP size and configuration. Further, for an otherwise stable FP, electron capture destabilizes the defect and can lead to recombination [see, e.g., Fig. 6(d)]. Hence the FPs we have studied here will be more important for irradiation conditions in which both the NIEL is low and the rate of ionization is low. This is more typical of low energy electron irradiation than high-energy proton irradiation, for example. Where the addition of electrons does not destabilize a particular FP configuration, it reduces the barriers to FP recombination, thereby reducing the overall stability of the defect. This ionization-dependent defect behavior is supported by previous experiments demonstrating that injection annealing and temperature annealing of $^{60}$Co gamma irradiated $p$-Si have equivalent effect on observed carrier lifetime degradation [27]. It is also important to note that the FP stabilities explored here strongly indicate that there is not a sharp, well-defined threshold displacement energy. Not only does Fermi level influence the stability of FPs, but also the configurational anisotropy in FP stability means that the threshold displacement energy depends strongly on the direction of displacement. Further, for FPs at finite temperature, the moderate barriers to defect dissociation and recombination mean that any FP is stable only in a statistical sense. Hence, the typical displacement threshold should be considered an *effective* threshold displacement energy, which represents an average deposited energy per stable FP and free V or I (that is, dissociated FPs). This requires the calculation of the yield of such defects from a range of initial FPs (and therefore PKA energies and directions) based on calculated barriers to configurational changes, including ultimate recombination or dissociation.

## IV. DOPANT-TYPE DEPENDENT DAMAGE MECHANISMS

### A. Frenkel Pair Mechanism

While the dopant-dependent stabilities of FPs described above will certainly impact the stable, post-irradiation defect profile (discussed below), it is also interesting to consider whether these behaviors of FPs themselves can affect the inferred displacement damage in electronic materials and devices in a more direct fashion. If we consider the case of a FP that is present in $p$-Si for times at least on the order of $\tau$, the minority carrier lifetime, the unoccupied FP defect states in the bulk Si bandgap near the conduction band minimum act as trapping centers to minority carrier electrons. This might at first suggest that, neglecting other defect effects, *more* radiation damage should be expected in $p$-Si than $n$-Si. However, our calculations suggest the surprising result that FPs (if present) may instead reinforce the trends that are naturally present owing to the differences in relative stability of the P-vacancy (more stable) and B-vacancy (less stable) centers in $n$-Si and $p$-Si. This is the case because, while any unrecombined FPs in $p$-Si do represent *additional* minority carrier TCs as compared to similarly irradiated $n$-Si, electron capture by these smaller FP TCs destabilizes the FPs themselves, resulting in defect recombination, and eliminating the defect levels in which the electrons were captured. Upon recombination of the FP any trapped electrons are returned to the conduction band, along with $\sim$6 eV of potential energy (that is, recombination returns to the system the cost to form the FP from an otherwise perfect bulk Si crystal, the formation energy). Hence these FPs, present at higher levels in $p$-Si than in $n$-Si, can act as a shallow, metastable electron traps.

If we neglect for the moment other defects, and consider a system with a pre-irradiation minority carrier lifetime of $\tau_i$ where the only radiation induced electrically active defects with

non-trivial lifetimes (with respect to $\tau_i$) are small FPs, we find the following results. For $N_c$ excess minority carriers excited in the system (during, say, a measurement of minority carrier lifetime), $n_c$ carriers are trapped by FP defect states. For an $n$-doped Si system, let us assume that $n_c \to 0$ for simplicity, as far fewer FPs are expected to be stable in $n$-Si than $p$-Si. For a $p$-Si system, $n_c$ can be finite and non-trivial, especially for low temperature irradiation and measurement conditions, and we may define $t_{\text{FP}}$ as the average time a particular minority carrier trapped by a FP spends localized at the defect before the destabilized FP recombines. In this case, the average lifetime of a minority carrier electron in $p$-Si is

$$
\tau_{p-\mathrm{Si}}^{*}=\frac{N_{c} \tau_{i}+n_{c} t_{\mathrm{FP}}}{N_{c}}
$$

while $\tau_{n-\mathrm{Si}}^{*} \approx \tau_{i}$. Therefore the difference in post-irradiation $n$- and $p$-Si lifetimes is proportional to $n_c t_{\text{FP}} / N_c$. For irradiation conditions that introduce other defects in addition to FPs, if the formation or electrical activity of these other defects (e.g., vacancy or interstitial clusters) has little or no dependence on dopant-type, then the difference in $n$- and $p$- damage rates would still follow

$$
\Delta K_{n-p} \sim \frac{n_{c}}{N_{c}} t_{\mathrm{FP}}.
$$

Note that $n_c$, the number of minority carriers trapped in FP defect states out of a fixed pool of $N_c$ excess carriers, is a function of the FP carrier capture cross-section, as well as the concentration of available FP trapping states relative to the concentration of other carrier trapping centers. Hence, we observe a dependence on the fraction of displacement damage resulting from low-energy PKA recoils—that is, a dependence on the initial concentration of FPs relative to other defects (again, e.g., vacancy and interstitial clusters).

Therefore, in a regime where the dominant radiation induced defects are FPs (the low recoil energy limit), and FPs are kinetically stable (e.g., for temperatures below $\sim 0$ °C, see below) the presence of stable FPs in $p$-Si may actually result in a significantly increased effective minority carrier lifetime (and subsequently diffusion length) as compared to $n$-Si, giving rise to a positive $\Delta K_{n-p}$ that is proportional to the relative fraction of damage resulting from low-energy PKA recoils. For irradiations resulting in higher fractions of damage due to high-energy PKA recoils, defect cascades are expected and the relative concentration of isolated FPs is small. Therefore, in the high-energy PKA recoil limit, the above mechanism has a small impact on overall lifetimes relative to the effects of other defects and $\Delta K_{n-p} \to 0$.

### B. V-Dopant Complex Mechanism

The above FP-mediated mechanism will only produce observable, direct effects if a non-trivial population of stable FPs created by low-energy PKA recoils persists in the active region of a device for times at least on the order of the minority carrier lifetime. The calculated diffusion barriers reported in Fig. 7 are such that FPs are expected to have stabilities against recombination (barriers of $\sim 0.7$ eV) and disassociation (binding energies of $\sim 0.5$ eV) roughly equivalent to BV pairs, which have a calculated binding energy of 0.5 eV [8]. This indicates that the majority of FPs should anneal out of the system at temperatures of order $\sim 0$ °C [7]. Huang diffuse scattering experiments support this, indicating that defect reactions in electron irradiated Si are dominated by FP recombination for temperatures below $0$ °C [29]. This implies that there may exist a temperature regime (at or below $\sim 0$ °C) where the thermal lifetime of FPs is such that direct FP influences on carrier lifetimes (as described above) would be observed as a transient effect on the time scale of the thermal FP lifetimes. For temperatures above room temperature, though, few bound FPs are expected. It remains an open question whether enough FPs will survive to add significantly to the observed displacement damage for device operation at room temperature, and it is almost certain that this mechanism disappears altogether if the device is heated significantly. However, the differences in the relative stability of FPs in $p$-Si and $n$-Si can still affect displacement damage experiments through differences in the probabilities of forming vacancy-interstitial complexes in $p$-Si and $n$-Si, owing to the higher proportion of vacancies in $p$-Si that will escape initial recombination events than in $n$-Si.

In the case of $n$-Si, many isolated vacancies created during irradiation and subsequent disassociation of FPs are captured by P or alternative $n$-dopants. The PV dopant complex has a binding energy of 1.0 eV [8], and is highly electrically active. The presence of PV pairs is expected to reduce measured carrier lifetimes significantly due to increased hole capture rates. There is no equivalent defect-B complex above room temperature due to the significantly lower binding energy stability of BV complexes (binding energy of $\sim 0.5$ eV) [8], [7]. Ignoring other vacancy-impurity complexes and metastable FPs for the moment, a significantly larger yield of isolated vacancies is expected to diffuse out of the active device region without forming stable, electrically active defects in $p$- versus $n$-Si. Hence lower rates of minority carrier lifetime reduction, and therefore less observed damage, is expected for irradiated $p$-type material from this simple mechanism.

Complicating this simple picture is the role of other impurities in $p$-Si and $n$-Si, such as vacancy-oxygen and/or hydrogen related centers, which are known to be stable above room temperature [7], [30]. Some of these defects serve as deep electron or hole traps, reducing carrier lifetime, and others serve as shallow electron traps, thereby increasing the effective lifetime inferred from many typical kinds of displacement damage experiments. The differences in relative stability of the FPs in $p$-Si and $n$-Si can break the symmetry in formation rates of these and other vacancy-impurity defects that one might otherwise expect not to depend on doping type in a significant way. Therefore, carrier lifetime effects arising from mechanisms involving vacancy-impurity or cluster defects, including V-Oxygen pairs and divacancies (as opposed to BV and PV vacancy-dopant pairs) can either have a reinforcing or offsetting effect on damage rates, and have only an indirect dependence on dopant-type mediated by the differing yields of free Vs which escape recombination. In contrast, the natural tendency towards lower defect concentrations in B-doped versus P-doped Si is a direct consequence of the intrinsic stability of vacancy-dopant

pairs, and represents a self-reinforcing effect on differences in carrier lifetimes. Finally, while previous work has surmised that an apparent quadratic dependence of low-NIEL $p$-type Si damage rates on NIEL implicates the divancancy directly in $n$- and $p$-type Si damage rate differences [6], [31], [32], a recent extensive review and analysis by Srour and Palko shows that other interpretations are possible for these data [33].

For irradiation conditions that result in higher average PKA energies, or equivalently a larger fraction of PKA events with re- coils $>2$ keV, a larger portion of the overall defect profile is ex- pected to consist of defects resulting from cascade events. These defects are predominantly V and I clusters, and are expected to form equivalently in both $n$- and $p$-Si. In this case, neither the V-dopant complex formation nor the presence of FPs will be the dominant defect introduction process, and the V-dopant stability difference between $p$- and $n$-Si should not significantly affect measured carrier lifetimes. Therefore, as in the FP mechanism described above, for higher PKA recoil energies, $\Delta K_{n-p} \to 0$.

## V. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

We have performed a theoretical study to elucidate mecha- nisms for dopant-type damage rates in Si. The magnitude of damage rate difference, $\Delta K_{n-p}$, correlates with the fraction of damage due to low-energy PKA recoils. While $\Delta K_{n-p}$ has op posite behavior with respect to NIEL for protons and electrons, when considered as a function of the low-energy recoil portion of the NIEL it exhibits the same behavior for both incident par- ticles: that is, $\Delta K_{n-p}$ increases for an increasing fraction of damage due to low-energy events.

The evolution of the primary products of these low-energy displacement damage events, isolated Frenkel pairs, has also been studied for its relevance to differing $n$- and $p$-Si damage rates in the case of low NIEL. FPs are found to be much more stable in $p$-Si than $n$-Si against both recombination and disas- sociation. For temperatures where FPs are expected to be stable against disassociation for times at least on the order of the mi- nority carrier lifetime, this gives rise to a mechanism that can result in enhanced carrier lifetimes in $p$-Si, and positive values of $\Delta K_{n-p}$. This mechanism involves the capture of free mi- nority carrier electrons by FPs in $p$-Si that are unstable in $n$-Si. This destabilizes the FPs themselves, resulting in the recombi- nation of the FP defect, followed by the release of the captured electron back into the valance band.

For higher temperatures, where FPs are expected to be less kinetically stable against disassociation, the known anisotropy in vacancy-dopant affinities between B and P dopants can lead to high concentrations of PV pairs in $n$-Si and overall lower de- fect concentrations in $p$-Si. As above, the practical result of this anisotropy is longer carrier lifetimes in $p$-Si, and therefore posi- tive values of $\Delta K_{n-p}$. The difference in relative stability of FPs in $n$- and $p$-Si can either reinforce or offset this difference by breaking symmetries that would otherwise exist in the formation rates of other vacancy-impurity centers, e.g., vacancy-oxygen and/or hydrogen centers, which can serve as deep or shallow electron traps. We believe these effects may contribute signifi- cantly to the variations observed in device displacement damage response, depending on device processing, radiation conditions, and experimental characterization techniques. This is a rich area for future study; computational techniques have now reached a level of sophistication and speed that many of these fundamental questions can begin to be addressed using first principles calcu- lations, in association with new experimental studies.
