# Molecular motions in a fluxional (η⁶-indenyl) tricarbonylchromium hemichelate: a density functional theory molecular dynamics study†

Nicolas Sieffert

A comprehensive Density Functional Theory (DFT) study is reported on molecular motions occurring in a hemichelate complex, formally composed of the (η³-2-methylallyl)Pd(ii) cation bonded to a (η⁶-indenyl) tricarbonylchromium anion, that has been recognized as being highly fluxional in a previous experimental study (C. Werlé, M. Hamdaoui, C. Bailly, X.-F. Le Goff, L. Brelot and J.-P. Djukic, *J. Am. Chem. Soc.*, 2013, **135**, 1715–1718). Its dynamics can be decomposed into three molecular motions: (i) the indenyl ligand can switch from a η¹ to a η³ binding mode to Pd, (ii) the Cr(CO)₃ moiety forms loose interactions with Pd, and can potentially rotate around the aryl-Cr axis, and (iii) the binding mode of the 2-methylallyl ligand to Pd may undergo partial decoordination or rotation around the Pd–C axis. Herein, DFT based molecular dynamics simulations have been performed to provide microscopic insights into the dynamical behaviour of the complex on a 100 ps timescale. QTAIM analyses have been performed over trajectories to investigate the rearrangement of its electronic structure over time. Next, free energy calculations, following the metadynamics approach, have been undertaken to compute thermodynamic and kinetic data relative to the molecular rearrangements occurring in the complex. The results show that the fastest motion is the ring slippage of the indenyl ligand, and that this motion is coupled with the rotation of the 2-methylallyl ligand. Conversely, the Cr(CO)₃ rotation is significantly slower, however, a fast rocking motion is observed, consisting of transient approaches of carbonyl ligands to Pd. QTAIM analyses point to loose Pd/carbonyl and Pd/Cr interactions on average, that significantly evolve over time as fluxional motions occur.

## Introduction

The term hemichelation¹ was introduced to characterize a peculiar mode of chelation where at least one coordination site at the metal interacts attractively with the ligand mostly *via* non-covalent interactions.² The ligand in question is not ordinary: in most of the cases addressed until now, the non-covalently-bound part of the ligand was an arene-bound Cr(CO)₃ moiety of a (η⁶-benzyl)tricarbonylchromium anion.³⁻⁸ Hemichelation as a mode of coordination of transition metals was often the most favoured mode¹ compared to other options entailing formal saturation of the metal centre by conventional coordination to different parts of the ligand. The electron-withdrawing character of the Cr(CO)₃ group is well known.⁹ Due to the π-accepting properties of the carbonyl ligands, this fast rotor concentrates a great portion of its charge density at the Cr and the O atoms,⁶,⁹ which makes it a rather electron-rich and a negatively charged moiety capable of establishing strong and cohesive donor–acceptor interactions with highly Lewis-acidic metals,¹⁰ such as those described by Magomedov¹¹ and Lewis.¹² In hemichelates,¹ the interaction of the Cr(CO)₃ moiety with the “chelated” metal is multifaceted, as covalent bonding (if any) is expected to be weak, whereas significant coactive non-covalent interactions (NCIs), of different nature (*i.e.* attractive coulombic, electron correlation and long range dispersion¹³ interactions),² are involved.¹,¹⁴⁻¹⁷ These NCIs were already shown to favour *syn*-facial over *antara*-facial arrangements in μ-indenyl Cr,Rh and Cr,Ir complexes¹⁷,¹⁸ and play a key cohesive role in supporting unbridged metal–metal donor–acceptor bonds in Pomeroy's complexes¹⁹ as well as in ensuring the cohesion of oligomeric forms of [(RNC)₄Rh]⁺ in solution.²⁰ This combination of weak attractive interactions enables the stabilization of electron-unsaturated metal centres.² Numerous examples of 16 e Mn(i) and Re(i),¹⁴,²¹⁻²⁴ 14 e Rh(i),¹⁷ 14 e Pd(II)¹,¹⁶,²⁵⁻²⁷ and ı²⁸) and Pt(II)¹ species stabilized by NCIs with the Cr(CO)₃ moiety have been reported in the past by Djukic *et al.*, which display common features, as follows: (1) the lack of a covalent M–Cr bond and (2) the slow

In this article, I focus on the case of hemichelate 3b (following the labelling from ref. 16 in which the synthesis and the characterization of the complex are presented) that is a binuclear metal complex composed of Pd(ii), coordinated by 2-methylallyl and tricarbonyl($\eta^6$-indenyl)chromium$^{29,30}$ anions (see Scheme 1). Interestingly, the room temperature solution $^1$H NMR signature of the complex pointed, at first glance, to a symmetric structure of the molecule wherein the Pd centre would be $\eta^3$-bonded to the indenyl moiety. This mere interpretation of the solution $^1$H NMR spectrum was contradicted by the X-ray diffraction structure of the same complex, which was displaying a rather dissymmetric structure where the Pd centre binds the indenyl fragment at one of the benzylic position and interacts with the neighbouring $Cr(CO)_3$ fragment through incipient bonding with one CO ligand. It was therefore proposed that the solution $^1$H NMR spectrum was the result of a fast dynamic behaviour consisting of a haptotropic shift of the ($\eta^3$-indenyl)Pd moiety from one benzylic position to the other one via two conformational transition states and one high energy metastable ($\eta^3$-indenyl)Pd($\eta^3$-2-methylallyl) state.$^{16}$ For simplicity, the treatment of variable temperature $^1$H NMR data was carried out assuming a mutual site exchange, neglecting the contribution of the possible synergistic response of the $Cr(CO)_3$ rotation to the movement of the ($\eta^3$-indenyl)Pd moiety and assimilating the two transition states to the metastable state. Also, the ancillary 2-methylallyl ligand was considered to play no role in the process, and its potential reorganization occurring during the haptotropic shift was neglected. This simplification of the whole mechanism of exchange produced a reasonable value for the Gibbs enthalpy of activation for the mutual exchange of around 11 kcal mol$^{-1}$, near room temperature (namely, $\Delta H^{\ddagger} = +6.0(2)$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ and $\Delta S^{\ddagger} = -15.5(9)$ cal mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$, determined from an Eyring plot, between 213(1) and 263(1) K, in $\sim$10:1 d$_8$-toluene/THF).$^{16}$ A striking observation made in this study was that the rate of exchange was so high that the use of conventional dynamic $^1$H NMR methods started to become particularly challenging. It became also obvious that a "static" Density Functional Theory (DFT) treatment could not properly localize the transition states, which should be clearly late, with geometries and electronic energies very similar to those of the metastable intermediate.

In this paper, I disclose a comprehensive study of the intramolecular dynamics of 3b (see Scheme 1), from a computational perspective. Three molecular motions are considered: (i) the haptotropic shift (ring slippage) of the indenyl ligand, (ii) the rotation of the $Cr(CO)_3$ moiety, and (iii) the rotation of the $\eta^3$-coordinated 2-methylallyl ligand (see Scheme 2). The first objective of this study is to understand what are the relative rates of these three motions, and to what extent they could occur simultaneously and be coupled to each other. This will allow one to refine the interpretation of dynamic NMR studies previously reported for 3b.$^{16}$ Also, the role of the 2-methylallyl ligand (that have been considered as being passive so far) will be clarified, as well as the influence of the haptotropic shift on the $Cr(CO)_3$ rotation and vice versa. Another objective of this study is to follow the evolution of the electronic structure of the complexes along their fluxional motions, and to better characterize the interactions between the hemichelated ligand and the metal, in the course of the dynamics. For comparison, the dynamical behaviour of 1 has also been considered, in which "fast" rotation of the $Cr(CO)_3$ moiety is expected. My investigations are essentially based on DFT molecular dynamics simulations, supported by higher-level "static" DFT calculations on selected stationary points. In the first approach, unbiased ab initio molecular dynamics simulations (referred to as DFT-MD) are undertaken, in which the studied complexes (1 and 3b) are free to undergo structural reorganizations in a timescale of 100 ps. However, because such a timescale is significantly smaller than the one for NMR, "direct observation" of any fluxional motion cannot be expected in the course of these simulations. To address this issue, a second approach is considered, in which ab initio metadynamics (DFT-MTD)$^{31,32}$ simulations were performed. # Methods
## Molecular dynamics (DFT-MD)
Ab initio MD simulations were performed using the QuickStep module $^{35}$ of CP2K, $^{36}$ which performs DFT-MD using a dual basis set method. Here, the wavefunctions are described by a Gaussian basis set while the electron density is described by an auxiliary plane wave basis set. A triple-$\zeta$ Gaussian basis set augmented with two sets of d-type and p-type polarization functions (TZV2P) was used on O, C and H, whereas "shorter range" basis sets (i.e. using less diffuse primitives) of double-$\zeta$ quality were used on Pd and Cr. $^{37}$ Plane waves were expanded up to a density cutoff of 400 Ry and used in conjunction with the GTH pseudopotentials $^{38-40}$ to describe the core electrons on all atoms. These calculations were performed using the PBE-D3 functional, consisting of the original PBE functional $^{41,42}$ augmented with the "-D3" correction from Grimme $^{43}$ with zero-damping (and without the so-called "three-body terms"). Note that the PBE-D3(bj) functional, consisting of PBE-D3 with Becke and Johnson (BJ) damping $^{44}$ was employed in a previous study. $^{16}$ However, BJ-damping is not yet available in CP2K (version 2.5.1). The original PBE-D3 functional (with zero-damping) has thus been employed and this choice has been validated at the early stage of this study (see details in the ESI$\dagger$). For every time step of 0.5 fs, the electronic structure was explicitly quenched to a tolerance of $10^{-7}$ Hartree. All simulations were run in the NVT ensemble, using a chain of Nosé-Hoover thermostats $^{45-47}$ ($T = 323$ K, frequency: $1800$ cm$^{-1}$). The simulation of $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$ was started from its experimental X-ray structure (refcode: CEWNUP), $^{16}$ whereas 1 was constructed by hand, by clipping off the Pd-(2-methylallyl) moiety from the X-ray structure of $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$. In all simulations, the DFT box was cubic with a cell-length of 18.5 Å and the DFT images were decoupled using the wavelet scheme $^{48,49}$ implemented in CP2K.

## Metadynamics simulations (DFT-MTD)
The exploration of the free energy surface of **3b** has been per- formed using the metadynamics approach. $^{31,32}$ In all simu lations, Gaussian functions were added every 80 fs. The scales of the Gaussian functions depend on the nature of the collec- tive variable. They were $0.05\ \mathring{A}$ for CV1, 0.1 for CV2, $0.05\ \mathring{A}$ for CV3, $2.5^{\circ}$ for CV4 and $3.75^{\circ}$ for CV6. Typical hill height was $0.5\ \text{kcal mol}^{-1}$ (see ESI$\dagger$ for more details on the sampling pro- cedure, see also Fig. S1$\dagger$ for time evolution of the collective variables). The exact same set of parameters as in DFT-MD was employed in DFT-MTD simulations (NVT ensemble, $T = 323$ K,400 Ry cutoff, TZV2P basis sets, GTH pseudopotentials, cubic box of cell length $18.5\ \mathring{A}$ and decoupling of periodic DFT images). All DFT-MTD simulations were started from $\eta^{1}$-**3b**, after 30 ps of DFT-MD pre-equilibration. The uncertainty in free energy values (related to metadynamics) can be roughly estimated as being in the range of the height of a Gaussian function, i.e. $0.5\ \text{kcal mol}^{-1}$, at least. Reconstruction of the free energy surfaces from MTD simulations and calculation of minimum free-energy paths were performed using the "fes" program from the CP2K package.

## Frequency maps
Frequency maps are two-dimensional histograms obtained by counting structures over the last 60 ps of DFT-MD (120 000 snapshots) in bins of sizes 0.005 by 0.005, in the (CV2b, CV2a),(CV2b, CV2c) and (CV2a, CV2c) spaces. The maps are normal- ized on a scale ranging from 0 (lowest occurrence) to 100(highest occurrence).

## Static calculations
All static calculations were performed at the same PBE-D3 level as MD simulations. "TM" denotes geometry optimizations per- formed with the TURBOMOLE package, $^{50}$ using the def2- TZVPP basis set on all elements plus a small core Effective Core Potential (ECP) on Pd. $^{51}$ Large numerical quadrature grids of size m4 (in TURBOMOLE notation) were employed. "CP2K-opt" stands for geometry optimizations performed with the CP2K package, using the same set of parameters as in DFT-MD simulations (400 Ry cutoff, TZV2P basis sets, GTH pseudopotentials, cubic box of cell length $18.5\ \mathring{A}$ and decou- pling of periodic DFT images). The reaction profile for the rotation of the $Cr(CO)_{3}$ moiety in **1** was obtained from a series of constrained geometry optimizations, using the C11-C10- Cr-C22 dihedral angle as a constraint. The latter ranged from $16.06^{\circ}$ (corresponding to $CV4 = 0^{\circ}$) to $136.06^{\circ}$ by a step of $2^{\circ}$. CV4 has been recomputed on every structure and the corres- ponding profile has been plotted as a function of CV4 instead of the C11-C10-Cr-C22 dihedral angle (see Fig. S2$\dagger$ for details). 3D representations of the optimized structures were drawn with CYLview software. $^{52}$

## Quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM), non-covalent interactions (NCIs) and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses
QTAIM analyses $^{34}$ were performed with the AIMAll package(version 15.09.27). $^{53}$ Wavefunctions were obtained by perform ing single point calculations at the PBE0 $^{54}$ level of theory, in conjunction with the def2-TZVPP basis set on all elements except for Pd. The SDD effective core potential (ECP) and its associated basis set were employed on Pd. These calculations were performed with the Gaussian09 (revision D01) $^{55}$ package and allowed one to generate the ".wfx" files requested by AIMAll, including additional atomic core electron densities to represent the ECP-modelled electrons. $^{56}$ These calculations were achieved either on TM and CP2K optimized structures or on snapshots taken during the DFT-MD simulations. In the latter case, time evolution of the electron density at bond criti- cal points ( $\rho$, given in atomic units, i.e. e bohr $^{-3}$ ) and delocali zation indices $(\delta)$ were obtained by performing 302 single points, over 12.5250 ps, on structures taken from the DFT-MD trajectory starting from $\eta^{3}$-**3b** (simulation **MD3**). Snapshots were taken every 0.05 ps between 24.1415 and 29.1415 ps (i.e. before the $\eta^{3}\to\eta^{1}$ exchange) and between 31.6665 and36.6665 ps (i.e. after the $\eta^{3}\to\eta^{1}$ exchange). In the transition region (29.1415 ps $< t < 31.6665$ ps), snapshots were taken every 0.025 fs. A combined graphical representation of QTAIM and NCI analyses (shown in Fig. 3 and S5$\dagger$) was done using the AIMStudio module of AIMAll, by mapping the magnitude of the reduced density gradient (so called $|$RDG$|$) isosurface with the function constructed as the electron density multi- plied by the sign of the second Hessian eigenvalue (referred to as $sign(\lambda_{2})\rho$), i.e. following the definition from Johnson and co- workers. $^{57}$ The RDG isosurface is computed up to a cutoff of $s < 0.4$ a.u., and the mapped (signed) electron density is shown within the range $-0.04$ a.u. $< \rho < +0.04$ a.u. for all complexes in this study. For validation purpose, RDG vs. $sign(\lambda_{2})\rho$ is plotted in Fig. S3$\dagger$ in the case of complex **1**, along with a comparison between the results obtained by the original NCIplot program $^{58}$ and AIMStudio. NBO analyses were performed on the same 302 snapshots taken from **MD3**, at the same PBE0/def2-TZVPP level of theory, using the NBO module(version 3.1) $^{59}$ of Gaussian09.

Because the solvent (benzene, toluene or toluene/THF mix- tures) employed in the experiments $^{16}$ is expected to have only a minor role in the dynamical behaviour of the complexes, all calculations were performed under vacuum (i.e. the solvent

was not taken into account). Note also that all complexes were described at the restricted DFT level, in their (first) singlet states, throughout.

## Results and discussion

The paper is organized as follows: I first validate the DFT method by performing static calculations at different computational levels on six stereoisomers of 3b: $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$, $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_2}$, $\boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_1}$, $\boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_2}$, $\boldsymbol{anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b}$, and $\boldsymbol{TS_{rot}}$. The subscripts "1" and "2" distinguish the different orientations of the 2-methylallyl ligand (see Fig. 2). $\text{TS}_{\text{rot}}$ is the transition state for the $\text{Cr(CO)}_3$ rotation (see Scheme 2). Optimized structures and the relative energies of the complexes obtained at different computational levels are compared, and topological analyses of their electronic structures are presented. Next, I focus on the dynamical behaviours of complexes $\boldsymbol{1}$, $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b}$, and $\boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b}$ by performing (unbiased) DFT-MD simulations. Analyses of the time evolution of their electronic structures are presented and the existence of correlations between internal motions is investigated. Finally, I present the results of free energy calculations, as obtained from DFT-MTD (metadynamics) simulations. The free energy surfaces underlying the three molecular motions described in Scheme 2 are reported, in the case of $\boldsymbol{3b}$.

### Static calculations

Structures and relative energies of selected stereoisomers. In order to validate the DFT setup employed in the DFT-MD simulations (based on CP2K), six stereoisomers of complex $\boldsymbol{3b}$ have been considered, namely: $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$, $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_2}$, $\boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_1}$, $\boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_2}$, $\boldsymbol{anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b}$ and $\boldsymbol{TS_{rot}}$ (see Fig. 2). The structures and relative energies obtained at the CP2K-opt level are compared to those obtained from the TM calculations (see Table S1†). A comparison of TM with the CP2K-opt results of the six stereoisomers of $\boldsymbol{3b}$ shows that the two approaches are of similar accuracies, since the structures of the complexes optimized at the two levels are very close (CP2K-opt distances are within *ca.* 0.015 Å from the TM optimized structures; see Table S1†). The comparison of the relative energies of the complexes at the two levels (see Fig. 2) also shows that TM and CP2K-opt yield very similar results: at the TM level, the relative energies of the complexes are: $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$ (0.0 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_2}$ (0.5 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_1}$ (8.2 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_2}$ (9.1 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_1}$ (9.9 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{TS_{rot}}$ (14.0 kcal mol⁻¹). At the CP2K-opt level, the relative order is identical and the relative energies are within 0.9 kcal mol⁻¹ from the TM values, namely: $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$ (0.0 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_2}$ (0.9 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_1}$ (8.6 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_2}$ (9.5 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b_1}$ (10.8 kcal mol⁻¹) $< \boldsymbol{TS_{rot}}$ (14.9 kcal mol⁻¹). Note that these relative energies also correlate well with previous static calculations,¹⁶ performed at the PBE-D3(bj)/ZORA/TZP level. All these results taken together show that the CP2K parameters employed herein provide a satisfactory description of the studied complexes.

QTAIM and NCIplot analyses on optimized structures. For further comparisons with the results obtained from DFT-MD (*vide infra*), the electronic structures of the six stereoisomers of $\boldsymbol{3b}$ shown in Fig. 2 have been analysed by QTAIM. I mainly focused on the bonding environment of the Pd centre, and I report the density at bond critical points and delocalization indices, namely $\rho(\text{Pd,X})$ and $\delta(\text{Pd,X})$ respectively (X being either Cr or C atom from the indenyl, carbonyl and 2-methylallyl ligands; see Tables S2 and S3† for values, and Fig. S4 and S5† for the location of bond critical points). The structure of the contact pair "[(benzene)Cr(CO)₃]···[Pd(2-methylallyl)₂]", namely $\boldsymbol{2}$ in the following, has also been considered for comparison.

Interestingly, no bond critical point is found between the two metals in all the considered complexes. However, some

interactions should still be present, especially in the cases of the $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b}$ stereoisomers, where $\delta(\text{Pd,Cr})$ is *ca.* 0.30. The latter considerably decreases in the two $\boldsymbol{syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b}$ stereoisomers (*ca.* 0.10) and vanishes in $\boldsymbol{anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b}$ (0.03) and 2 (0.00). This feature correlates with the increase of the Pd-Cr distances reported in Table S1.† Considering the Pd/indenyl interaction, only a single bond critical point is found, either between Pd and C11 in the two $\eta^1\text{-}3b$ stereoisomers, or between Pd and C13 in the two $\eta^3\text{-}3b$ stereoisomers. Interestingly, the electronic density at the bond critical point does not vary when moving from $\eta^1\text{-}3b$ to $syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b$ and $anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b$, as the latter is *ca.* 0.080 a.u. throughout. Delocalization indices point to significant covalent interactions between Pd and the indenyl ligand: in the two $\eta^1\text{-}3b$ stereoisomers, the delocalized electrons are concentrated between Pd and C11 (*ca.* 0.55). In contrast, a significant amount of electrons are found between Pd and the three $\text{C}_{indenyl}$ atoms in all $\eta^3\text{-}3b$ stereoisomers. In the latter, delocalization indexes on Pd-C11 and Pd-C14 are identical (*ca.* 0.42) while $\delta(\text{Pd, C13})$ is smaller (*ca.* 0.38). Interestingly, the QTAIM results suggest that indenyl is more strongly bonded to the metal in the $anti\ \eta^3\text{-}3b$ form compared to $syn\ \eta^3\text{-}3b$ stereoisomers. However, the latter is more stable than the former (see Fig. 2) because of some other contributions, such as Pd interactions with Cr and CO, that also take part in the overall stabilities of the complexes.

Interestingly, Pd interacts only slightly with carbonyl ligands. A single bond critical point is found for the Pd-C22 bond, and only in the case of $\eta^1$ stereoisomers. The associated values of $\rho$ are particularly small: 0.059 a.u. in $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_1}$ and 0.049 a.u. in $\boldsymbol{\eta^1\text{-}3b_2}$. Delocalization indices are almost zero for Pd-C20, as expected because it corresponds to the carbonyl ligand that is the remotest from the metal. Conversely, the interactions with the two other carbonyls are non-negligible, particularly in the case of $\eta^1$-stereoisomers. In $\eta^1\text{-}3b_1$, $\delta(\text{Pd-C22})$ is 0.36 and $\delta(\text{Pd,C21})$ is 0.14, in keeping with a smaller Pd-C21 distance compared to Pd-C22 (CV5 = −0.464 Å). In $\eta^1\text{-}3b_2$, the two Pd-carbonyl distances are more similar (CV5 = −0.158 Å), leading to closer $\delta(\text{Pd-C22})$ and $\delta(\text{Pd-C21})$ values (0.28 and 0.22, respectively). Interestingly, summing $\delta(\text{Pd-C21})$ and $\delta(\text{Pd-C22})$ on the two complexes shows that the same amount of electrons are involved in the Pd-carbonyl bonding in $\eta^1\text{-}3b_1$ and $\eta^1\text{-}3b_2$ (*ca.* 0.50), but the latter are partitioned differently according to the nature of the stereoisomer.

Finally, three bond critical points are found for the interaction between Pd and the 2-methylallyl ligand. ### Exploration of the free energy surface of 3b (metadynamics simulations)

In this section, metadynamics simulations are undertaken in order to "observe" molecular motions that are too "slow" to spontaneously occur in the course of unconstrained DFT-MD (*vide supra*). Also, these simulations will provide an estimation of the underlying free energy surfaces. Because of the high computational cost of metadynamics simulations, only two-dimensional free energy surfaces are considered (see Fig. 9). Gaussian functions are thus added in the space of two CVs only (referred to as "active CVs" in the following), and the time evolution of the remaining CVs is monitored only (see Fig. S1†). Three metadynamics simulations (labelled **MTD1-MTD3**) have been performed, involving three different pairs of active CVs chosen among CV1-CV6 (as defined in Fig. 1).

The corresponding activation barrier is 8.1 kcal mol⁻¹ (corresponding to $TS_{ind}$), in fairly good agreement with the experimental value (11.0 kcal mol⁻¹ at $T=323$ K).¹⁶

These metadynamics results are thus also consistent with a very small extra-activation barrier (*ca.* 1.1 kcal mol⁻¹) for the $\eta^1 \to \eta^3$ conversion process, as stated in ref. 16. Additionally, the free energy difference between $\eta^1$-3b₁ and $\eta^1$-3b₂ is only 0.6 kcal mol⁻¹, and is certainly within the order of magnitude of the accuracy of metadynamics simulations (because the typical height of an added Gaussian function is 0.5 kcal mol⁻¹).

Indenyl ring slippage and rotation of Cr(CO)₃ (MTD2). To investigate the relative rates of ring slippage *vs.* chromium-tricarbonyl rotation processes, both motions have been considered together in a single DFT-MTD simulation, by choosing CV1 (describing the ring slippage) and CV4 (describing the orientation of Cr(CO)₃ relatively to the indenyl ligand) as active CVs. No constraints were applied on CV6, so that the 2-methylallyl ligand can freely rotate during the DFT-MTD (see Fig. S1†). Thus, this situation is not ideal because two isomers (*e.g.* $\eta^1$-3b₁ and $\eta^1$-3b₂) correspond to the same point in the CV space, and because they cannot spontaneously exchange from one to another (as found from unbiased DFT-MD simulations on these complexes, *vide supra*). However, this simulation has been attempted bearing in mind that the two isomers have very similar free energies (within 0.6 kcal mol⁻¹, *vide supra*). In principle, nine basins are thus expected on the free energy surface, six of them (where CV1 ~ ±1.25 Å) corresponding to a mixture of $\eta^1$-3b₁ and $\eta^1$-3b₂, and three of them (where CV1 ~ 0 Å) corresponding to a mixture of $\eta^3$-3b₁ and $\eta^3$-3b₂ isomers. To save computational time, the simulation has been stopped after the addition of 12 500 hills (which already corresponds to a significantly long trajectory of 500 ps). This timescale was sufficient to observe sample $\eta^1 \leftrightarrow \eta^3$ interchange processes and rotation of the Cr(CO)₃ rotor, thus allowing for consistent comparison of their relative free energy barriers. The resulting free energy surface (see Fig. 9) clearly shows that indenyl slippage is faster than Cr(CO)₃ rotation, as the activation barrier is *ca.* 10 kcal mol⁻¹ for the former and *ca.* 16 kcal mol⁻¹ for the latter. Because this simulation involves mixtures of isomers at a given point in the CV space, no free energy profiles have been extracted from **MTD2**, and the free energy barriers reported above are only rough estimates from the visual inspection of Fig. 9.

Rotation of the 2-methylallyl ligand and ring slippage (MTD3). Finally, the rotational motion of the 2-methylallyl ligand occurring during the indenyl ring slippage process has been explicitly considered in MTD3, using CV1 and CV6 as active collective variables. Again, the simulation has been stopped after 500 ps (corresponding to 12 500 added hills). The resulting free energy surface (see Fig. 9) exhibits six main domains: two corresponding to $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$, two corresponding to $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$, one corresponding to $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ and one corresponding to $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$. Visual inspection of the free energy surface reveals that the rotation of the allyl ligand is more kinetically favourable when coupled to indenyl slippage (thus leading to the conversion of $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ to its enantiomer via $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$), so that higher activation barriers are found when allyl rotation occurs independently. As a result, two minimum free energy paths can be defined, corresponding to $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ $\rightarrow$ $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ and $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$ $\rightarrow$ $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ $\rightarrow$ $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$. The former is computed in two parts (see Fig. 10) that should, in principle, meet at $(0, \pm180^\circ)$. In practice, a small gap (of less than 0.5 kcal mol$^{-1}$) is found, due to the roughness of the free energy surface (related to the height of the added Gaussian functions). As shown in Fig. 10, the formation of $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$ from $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ is thermodynamically uphill by 8.0 kcal mol$^{-1}$ and the corresponding activation barrier is *ca.* 9.1 kcal mol$^{-1}$. Also, the two $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ complexes should, in principle, be isoenergetic, but a small free energy difference of 0.8 kcal mol$^{-1}$ is found between the two minima. This feature helps to provide an estimation of the accuracy of metadynamics simulations (within less than 1 kcal mol$^{-1}$). Even longer trajectories and smaller Gaussian functions should have been necessary to obtain more accurate results. However, keeping these limitations in mind, it can be noted that the results of MTD3 are in good agreement with those of MTD1, as both simulations exhibit similar activation barriers and exchange free energies between $\eta^1$ and $\eta^3$ stereoisomers. Also, such $\sim$1 kcal mol$^{-1}$ accuracy clearly allows one to distinguish between activation barriers for indenyl slippage (8-9 kcal mol$^{-1}$) and for 2-methylallyl and Cr(CO)$_3$ rotations, as the latter are significantly higher in free energy (>14-16 kcal mol$^{-1}$). Finally, it is worth noting that the 2-methylallyl ligand remains bound in a $\eta^3$ manner to Pd and thus never switches to a $\eta^1$ coordination mode in the rotational process.

## Conclusions

I presented a comprehensive DFT study of the dynamical behaviour of a fluxional hemichelate complex ($\mathbf{3b}$) based on a ($\eta^6$-indenyl)tricarbonylchromium moiety. I investigated two molecular motions that have been previously identified by thorough experimental NMR studies, consisting of ring slippage of the indenyl ligand and rotation of the Cr(CO)$_3$ fragment. Additionally, the possible rotation of the 2-methylallyl ligand has also been examined. After careful validation of the DFT setup against the experiment and static calculations, (unbiased) DFT-MD simulations have been undertaken on $\mathbf{1}$, $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ and $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$. A comparison of $\mathbf{1}$ with $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ revealed that Cr(CO)$_3$ rotation spontaneously occurs within 100 ps in the case of $\mathbf{1}$, but that such rotation is precluded in $\mathbf{3b}$ within the same timescale, due to hemichelation. Instead, a fast "rocking" motion of the Cr(CO)$_3$ rotor is spontaneously observed in $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ and $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_2}$, within 100 ps, where the carbon atoms of the two neighbouring CO ligands alternatively approach the Pd centre. DFT-MD simulations also showed that $\eta^3$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ is metastable, and that it spontaneously rearranges into its $\eta^1$ analogue. The reverse process does not spontaneously occur within 100 ps of DFT-MD. A QTAIM analysis of the evolution of the electronic structure of the complex during the $\eta^3$ $\rightarrow$ $\eta^1$ rearrangement revealed that a decrease of the Pd-CO interaction is observed in the process, concomitant to an increase of the Pd-Cr interaction, while Pd-C(allyl) interactions remain similar. Also, the molecular graphs of the different isomers of $\mathbf{3b}$ are all found to evolve significantly over time, leading to the appearance and disappearance of bond critical points on the trajectories.

Finally, three metadynamics simulations were performed to investigate interchange processes of one stereoisomer to another, and to characterize the underlying free energy surfaces. The latter were computed over particularly long trajectories (500 ps) using the well-tempered MTD algorithm to ensure an efficient sampling and a good convergence of the free energy surfaces. The MTD results confirm that $\eta^1$-$\mathbf{3b_1}$ is indeed the most stable isomer, and that it is highly fluxional at near room temperature. The $\eta^1$ $\rightarrow$ $\eta^3$ conversion is thermodynamically uphill, by *ca.* 7-8 kcal mol$^{-1}$, and the associated free energy of activation is *ca.* 8-9 kcal mol$^{-1}$, in fairly good agreement with the experimental value (11.0 kcal mol$^{-1}$ at $T$ = 323 K).$^{16}$ Rotational motions of both Cr(CO)$_3$ and 2-methylallyl are found to be slower, as higher activation barriers are found for these processes (over 14 kcal mol$^{-1}$) when they occur solely (*i.e.* without coupling with any other motion). Finally, it is found that the "passive" role of the 2-methylallyl ligand stated in ref. 16 has to be re-evaluated, since the latter actually plays an active role during the $\eta^1$ $\leftrightarrow$ $\eta^3$ interchange process, since a rotation of the allyl ligand can be coupled to the indenyl ring slippage.

To summarize, an overall picture of the dynamical behaviour of $\mathbf{3b}$ can be drawn from all simulation results: the fastest motion is $\eta^1$ $\leftrightarrow$ $\eta^3$ interchange in the indenyl coordination mode to Pd. The latter is accompanied by rotation of the 2-methylallyl ligand, which occurs in a concerted manner with indenyl slippage. The rotation of the Cr(CO)$_3$ moiety is significantly slowed down upon hemichelation (compare $\mathbf{3b}$ and $\mathbf{1}$), and is also significantly slower compared to indenyl slippage. Beyond the particular case of the hemichelate $\mathbf{3b}$ investigated herein, this study emphasizes the significance of DFT methods$^{70}$ and *ab initio* molecular dynamics simulations$^{33,65,71}$ to rationalize the behaviour of fluxional complexes. The microscopic insights revealed herein may stimulate new theoretical and experimental investigations to further characterize dynamical effects in hemichelates$^1$ in particular, and in a wide range of fluxional inorganic and organometallic compounds in general.
