# Embedded-atom method applied to bimetallic clusters: The Cu-Ni and Cu-Pd systems

J. M. Montejano-Carrizales,* M. P. Iñiguez, and J. A. Alonso
Departamento de Física Teórica, Universidad de Valladolid, E47011 Valladolid, Spain
(Received 29 November 1993)

A study of ordering and segregation in bimetallic clusters has been carried out using the embedded-atom method. Icosahedral and cuboctahedral clusters with up to 147 atoms have been considered. The model has been applied to the Cu-Ni and Cu-Pd systems and we have found that the icosahedral structure is favored over the cuboctahedral one. Cu segregates to the surface in the Cu-Ni clusters, and a subtle interplay between ordering and segregation is observed in the Cu-Pd clusters.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The knowledge of the composition and degree of order in an alloy near its spatial boundaries (surfaces or interfaces) is important in many physical, chemical, and metallurgical phenomena: magnetic properties, $^{1-3}$ catalytic activity, $^{4-6}$ corrosive behavior, $^{7}$ etc. Those two effects are particularly interesting in clusters because of the very high ratio (called dispersion) between the number of surface atoms and the total number of atoms. The excellent catalytic properties of small clusters stem from the fact that a large number of atoms, the surface atoms, can be exposed to chemical reactions. Bimetallic catalysts are widely used in the petroleum industry, $^{8,9}$ despite the fact that many of their properties are not yet fully understood.

The detailed knowledge of the atomic structure of clusters, and the dependence of structure on size, is an intriguing issue. It is known that metal clusters usually adopt compact shapes. $^{10-12}$ Although studies of bimetallic clusters are scarce, a number of experimental and theoretical investigations of the atomic structure of pure transition-and noble-metal clusters have been performed. Experimentally, it has been observed $^{10-12}$ that the equilibrium shape of the clusters depends on their size. The icosahedron and the cuboctahedron are between the most common structures found, at least in the case of transition-and noble-metal clusters. Chemical probes of cluster structure have shown evidence of icosahedral packing in $Ni$ and $Cu$ clusters. $^{13}$ Photoionization studies (combined with time-of-flight mass spectrometry) by Pellarin et al. $^{14}$ also indicate the icosahedral structure of $Ni$ and $Co$ clusters produced by laser vaporization. Using electron diffraction to analyze the structure of particles produced by aggregation in an inert-gas atmosphere, it has been observed $^{15-17}$ that the relative proportion of multiply twinned and fcc $Cu$ and $Ag$ particles in the size range between 20 and $40 \AA$ in diameter depends on the conditions controlling particle production (pressure of the carrier gas, temperature, etc.). One of the conclusions of the experimental studies is that the icosahedral structure, predominant for small clusters, transforms to the cuboctahedral structure for large enough sizes, although the critical size for the transformation is still a subject of debate, since it depends on the element and on the production conditions.

Theoretical studies also support this view. Several methods, with different degrees of complexity, have been applied to study the atomic structure of clusters of transition- and noble-metal elements. A simple bond-energy model for clusters made up of a single component has been used by one of the authors to compare the relative stabilities of icosahedral and cuboctahedral clusters as a function of size. $^{18}$ The model shows that the stability of the icosahedral phase becomes strongly reduced (compared to the cuboctahedron) as the cluster size increases. Mansikka-aho, Manninen, and Hammanén and Valkealahti and Manninen have arrived at the same conclusions by applying the Hückel method and the effective-medium theory to copper clusters. $^{19,20}$ The energetics and structure of large nickel clusters have been explored over a broad size range by Cleveland and Landman $^{21}$ within the context of the embedded-atom method(EAM), $^{22-24}$ with the result that the icosahedral clusters are favored for small cluster sizes. The predominance of the icosahedral structure at small sizes, and the transition to the fcc structure for large sizes, has also been found inour recent EAM study of $Ni, Cu, Ag$ , and Pd clusters. $^{25}$

Our aim in this paper is to study ordering and segregation effects in clusters composed of two types of transition- or noble-metal atoms using the versatile embedded-atom method. For this purpose we concentrate on the systems $Cu-Ni$ and $Cu-Pd$ , and we study clusters with 55 and 147 atoms. Moreover, we investigate the relative stability of the icosahedral and cuboctahedral structures for those mixed clusters.

The EAM was proposed by Daw and Baskes $^{23,24}$ on the basis of an earlier theory, the effective-medium theory. $^{26-28}$ The EAM assumes that the energy of the system is the sum of the energy necessary to embed each atom in the background electron density set up by the neighboring atoms (the "embedding energy") and a contribution from the two-body core-core interactions. This method has been successfully applied to such problems asdefects in metals, $^{22,24}$ surfaces, $^{24,29}$ alloys, $^{22}$ fracture, $^{24}$  and the structure of liquid metals. $^{30}$ The EAM has also been applied to transition-metal clusters. $^{21,25,31}$ There are several semiempirical versions of the EAM, which differ in the form of the functions involved and in the method used for their parametrization. Here we use the prescrip-

tion of Foiles, Baskes, and Daw,²² who proposed a set of embedding functions and pair interactions allowing for the description of the pure fcc metals Cu, Ag, Au, Ni, Pd, Pt, and their alloys. Previously we have applied this method to investigate the competition between icosahedral and cuboctahedral structures in pure metal clusters.²⁵

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II we describe the ingredients of the model potential used in our calculations. In Sec. III we give details of the geometrical characteristics of the clusters. In Sec. IV we present and discuss the results of the application of the model to Cu-Ni and Cu-Pd clusters. The main conclusions are summarized in Sec. IV.

## II. MODEL POTENTIAL

To obtain the expression for the total energy of a bimetallic system within the EAM, we first describe the model for a system of $N$ equal atoms. The total energy is written, in this case,²²⁻²⁴ as
$$
E=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[F_{i}\left(\bar{\rho}_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j(\neq i)} \phi_{i j}\left(R_{i j}\right)\right], \tag{1}
$$
where $F_{i}(\bar{\rho}_{i})$ is the energy required to embed atom $i$ into the background electron density $\bar{\rho}_{i}$ at site $i$, and $\phi_{i j}(R_{i j})$ is the core-core pair interaction between atoms $i$ and $j$ separated by the distance $R_{i j}=|\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}|$. The host electron density $\bar{\rho}_{i}$ is approximated by a linear superposition of the spherically averaged electron densities of all atoms surrounding atom $i$,
$$
\bar{\rho}_{i}=\sum_{j(\neq i)} \rho_{j}\left(R_{i j}\right), \tag{2}
$$
where $\rho_{j}(R_{i j})$ is the electron density of atom $j$ at the position of the nucleus of atom $i$. If the atomic density function $\rho(R)$ and the pair interaction $\phi(R)$ are both known, the embedding energy function $F(\bar{\rho})$ can be uniquely defined by requiring the total energy given by Eq. (1) to match the "universal" equation of state proposed by Rose *et al.*,³² which gives the cohesive energy of the metal as a function of the lattice constant.

In the version of the EAM due to Foiles, Baskes, and Daw,²² the atomic densities are obtained from the atomic Hartree-Fock calculations of Clementi and Roetti³³ and MacLean and Maclean³⁴ by writing
$$
\rho(R)=n_{s} \rho_{s}(R)+\left(n-n_{s}\right) \rho_{d}(R), \tag{3}
$$
where $n$ is the total number of outer ($s$ plus $d$) electrons, $n_{s}$ is a measure of the number of outer $s$ electrons, and $\rho_{s}$ and $\rho_{d}$ are the partial densities associated with the $s$ and $d$ wave functions, respectively. The atomic density of each metal is thus a function of the single parameter $n_{s}$, which allows for variations of the relative proportions of $s$ and $d$ electrons with respect to the electronic configuration of the free atom. These changes of electronic configuration with respect to the atomic one are well documented in solid-state physics.³⁵ Finally, the pair interaction is written as a short-range, purely repulsive potential of the form
$$
\phi(R)=\frac{Z_{0}^{2}}{R}\left(1+\beta R^{v}\right)^{2} e^{-2 \alpha R}, \tag{4}
$$
where $Z_{0}$ equals $n$, the number of outer electrons of the atom, and the parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $v$ are taken as adjustable.

In the case of a bimetallic system with $N_{A}$ atoms of type $A$ and $N_{B}$ atoms of type $B$, $N=N_{A}+N_{B}$, we also calculate the total energy from an equation formally identical to Eq. (1), but now the embedding term separates into sums over $A$- and $B$-type atoms:
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N} F_{i}\left(\bar{\rho}_{i}\right) \to \sum_{i=1}^{N_{A}} F_{i}^{A}\left(\bar{\rho}_{i}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{B}} F_{j}^{B}\left(\bar{\rho}_{j}\right). \tag{5}
$$
Here $F_{i}^{A}(\bar{\rho}_{i})$ is the energy required to embed an atom of type $A$ into the background electron density $\bar{\rho}_{i}$ at the site $\mathbf{R}_{i}$ occupied by this atom, and a similar meaning holds for $F_{j}^{B}(\bar{\rho}_{j})$. A peculiarity of the case of alloy clusters is that the background densities are now obtained as a sum of electron densities from two different types of atoms:
$$
\bar{\rho}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{B}} \rho_{j}^{B}\left(R_{i j}\right)+\sum_{i^{\prime} \neq i}^{N_{A}} \rho_{i^{\prime}}^{A}\left(R_{i i^{\prime}}\right), \tag{6}
$$
with a similar expression for $\bar{\rho}_{j}$. The atomic densities $\rho^{A}(R)$ or $\rho^{B}(R)$ are of the form of Eq. (3), where now $n_{s}$ can be different for $A$ and $B$ atoms. The $s$- and $d$-partial densities $\rho_{s}^{A}(R)$, $\rho_{d}^{A}(R)$, $\rho_{s}^{B}(R)$, and $\rho_{d}^{B}(R)$ are taken, as in the homonuclear case, from atomic Hartree-Fock calculations.³³,³⁴ The practical characterization of the embedding functions $F(\bar{\rho})$ will be given below.

The pair-interaction terms are purely repulsive. Following common practice in pair-potential calculations, it is assumed that the pair interaction between two different species can be approximated by the geometric mean of the pair interactions for the individual species. This leads to
$$
\phi_{A B}(R)=Z_{A}(R) Z_{B}(R) / R, \tag{7}
$$
where $Z_{A}(R)$ and $Z_{B}(R)$ are both of the form
$$
Z(R)=Z_{0}\left(1+\beta R^{v}\right) e^{-\alpha R}, \tag{8}
$$
but with parameters $Z_{0}$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $v$ characteristic of $A$ and $B$, respectively.

Foiles, Baskes, and Daw²² determined $\alpha$, $\beta$, $v$, and $n_{s}$ for the set of fcc metals Cu, Ag, Au, Ni, Pd, and Pt by optimizing the agreement with experimental data for the elastic constants and vacancy formation energies of the pure metals, and for the heats of solution of the associated binary alloys. Furthermore, we stress again that the embedding functions were obtained by requiring Eq. (1) to match the universal equation of Rose *et al.*.³² This procedure ensures perfect agreement with equilibrium lattice constants, cohesive energies, and bulk moduli of the pure metals. The values of the parameters and the embedding functions obtained by Foiles, Baskes, and Daw²² for Cu, Ni, and Pd will be used in the bimetallic cluster calculations of this paper.

From the above presentation one can observe that the embedding functions $F^{A}(\bar{\rho})$ and $F^{B}(\bar{\rho})$ are characteristic

of the elements $A$ and $B$ but that these are universal, in the sense that the same function is used when the atom $A$ is to be embedded in metal $A$ (or cluster $A_N$) and in the alloy (or bimetallic cluster). This, first of all, is consistent with the original idea of the effective-medium theory, $^{26-28}$ which states that, in lowest order, the energy required to embed an atom in an inhomogeneous medium depends only on the local density at the embedding site. Of course this is only a local approximation, and by taking higher-order corrections the atom could sample more sensitively the background density in a sizable region around the embedding site. In this sense, corrections to the embedding function should be needed in moving from pure metals (or clusters) to alloys (or bimetallic clusters). Foiles, Baskes, and Daw $^{22}$ take account of those corrections in a smart way. Apart from pure metal data, they had taken the heats of solution of the dilute alloys within the data base used for the optimization of the parameters and embedding functions. Actually, the parameter most sensitive to the heats of solution is $n_s$, and of course the embedding functions are influenced by the values of $n_s$. For this reason we are confident that the embedding functions calculated by Foiles, Baskes, and Daw $^{22}$ give a unified account of the embedding energy of atoms both in metals and alloys, and for our present purposes, both in pure metal and alloy clusters.

## III. CLUSTER GEOMETRY
Geometrical information pertinent to the icosahedron and cuboctahedron is given in Refs. 36 and 37. We can consider the cluster as an onion (nonspherical in our case), formed by several concentric crusts. The number $v_c$ of crusts defines the order of the cluster, and the number $N$ of atoms in the cluster depends on $v_c$. The number of atoms in each crust, and then $N$, is the same for perfect cuboctahedral and icosahedral clusters. $^{36}$ However, not all atoms in a given crust are at the same distance from the cluster center. Notice, for instance, that the 12 vertex atoms on the surface crust of an icosahedron are at a longer distance from the cluster center than the edge atoms or the face atoms. On the other hand, those 12 vertex atoms are equivalent. Thus we can view a crust as being formed by several shells. The atoms in each shell occupy equivalent sites: they are at the same distance from the cluster center and have the same distribution of nearest-neighbor "sites" (of course the actual distribution of nearest-neighbor "atoms" can be different in bimetallic clusters due to the presence of $A$ and $B$ atoms).

As a useful way to characterize the atomic distribution in a bimetallic cluster of a given size $N$, and structure (icosahedral or cuboctahedral) and partial compositions $N_A$ and $N_B$ (with $N_A + N_B = N$), we list the number of $A$ atoms in each of the shells of the cluster. For instance, for a 55-atom icosahedral cluster with compositions $N_A=27, N_B=28$, the notation $(1,10,4,12)$ means that one $A$ atom occupies the central site, ten $A$ atoms are on the first shell (the first shell has 12 sites and it forms the first crust), four $A$ atoms are on the second shell, and 12 $A$ atoms are on the third shell (the second shell has 30 sites and the third shell 12 sites; the second and third shells form the second crust, which is the surface crust of the 55-atom icosahedron). The symbol $(1,10,4,12)$ given above will here be called an "atomic configuration." It specifies the global occupation of the different shells by the $A$ and $B$ atoms, although consistent with a given atomic configuration there are many ways (states) of distributing the $A$ and $B$ atoms within the shells which lead, within the EAM, to different cluster energies at $T=0$ K. The strategy to find out the atomic configuration and the states with the lowest energy will be discussed in Sec. IV.

A perfect cuboctahedron is formed by eight triangular and six square faces joined by 24 edges and 12 vertices; consequently the sites on its surface can be of square $(S)$, triangular $(T)$, edge $(E)$, and vertex $(V)$ types. A cuboctahedron with 147 atoms is formed by a central site and three crusts: the first (or innermost) crust is formed by a shell of $V$-type atoms, the second crust by $S$-, $E$-, and $V$-type shells (in order of increasing shell radii), and the third crust is formed by $S$-, $T$-, $E$-, and $V$-type shells. On the other hand, a perfect icosahedron is formed by 20 triangular faces joined by 30 edges and 12 vertices, and it can be obtained by an appropriate distortion of the cuboctahedron; $^{37}$ the sites on the surface of the icosahedron can be of triangular $(T)$, edge $(E)$, and vertex $(V)$ types. An icosahedron with 147 atoms is formed by a central site and three crusts: the first crust is formed by a $V$-type shell, the second by $E$- and $V$-type shells, and the third by $T$-, $E$-, and $V$-type shells.

The cuboctahedron can be cut out of a perfect face-centered-cubic (fcc) lattice. On the other hand, the icosahedron is not formed by points that belong to a crystalline lattice, and it does not have a well-defined (unique) nearest-neighbor distance. In the icosahedron, the distance between nearest neighbors on the same crust is 1.05 times the distance $d$ between nearest neighbors in adjacent crusts. $^{37}$ We then consider as the nearest neighbors of an atom all those atoms that are inside a distance of $1.05d$; this number is 12 except, of course, for atoms on the surface of the icosahedron. ## IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We have used the EAM for bimetallic (Cu-Ni and Cu- Pd) clusters with different sizes ($N=55$ and 147), struc- ture, and concentration. The two structures considered are cuboctahedral and icosahedral. Our interest in this paper is to study low-temperature surface segregation (more generally, phase separation) and ordering. To this end we have to calculate, for a given cluster size, struc- ture, and Cu concentration, the configuration (or atomic distribution) of the minimum total energy. To simplify the calculations, the structures of the bimetallic clusters are considered as perfect. By this we mean that in the cuboctahedral clusters all the nearest-neighbor distances are equal independently if we have $A$-$A$, $B$-$B$, or $A$-$B$ pairs. This interatomic distance is taken as the concen- tration average

$$
\bar{d}=\frac{N_{A}}{N} d_{A}+\frac{N_{B}}{N} d_{B}, \quad(9)
$$

of the interatomic distances $d_{A}, d_{B}$ for pure cuboc tahedral clusters$^{25}$ of the same size $(N)$. A similar ap proximation is used for the distance between nearest neighbors in adjacent crusts of the icosahedron.

Our search for the lowest-energy configuration for a given cluster size, structure, and Cu concentration was performed by randomly generating a large number of atomic configurations and states compatible with each of those configurations and comparing their energies. More details will be given below. Segregation and phase sepa- ration have been studied by calculating the average "radi- al" distances $\bar{r}_{A}, \bar{r}_{B}$, of the $A$ and $B$ atoms with respect to the cluster center. Significant surface segregation leads to one of these two distances being much larger than the other. The degree of ordering was analyzed by compar- ing the number of bonds between equal and unequal atoms. A predominance of $A$-$B$ bonds is the signature of the ordering. Although in this analysis we concentrate on bonds between nearest neighbors, we emphasize that the calculations of total energies were performed by tak- ing interactions between all atoms in the cluster.

### A. Cu/Ni clusters
Cu-Ni alloys have been the subject of intensive investi- gation. In particular, data have been obtained about the bulk phase diagram, $^{2}$ surface segregation, $^{38-42}$ catalytic properties, $^{4,43}$ etc. This system forms solid solutions with face-centered-cubic (fcc) structures and presents a misci- bility gap below $\simeq 630 \mathrm{~K}$. These features can be under stood from the fact that the lattice constants of the two components are very similar $[a_{0}(\mathrm{Cu})=3.615 \AA$ and $a_{0}(\mathrm{Ni})=3.52 \AA],{ }^{44}$ and the heats of solution of $\mathrm{Cu}$ in $\mathrm{Ni}$ and $\mathrm{Ni}$ in $\mathrm{Cu}$ are both positive but small. $^{45}$ The solid solutions are then stabilized by entropic effects.

The Cu-Ni alloys represent a prototype system with respect to surface segregation. Equilibrium segregation as well as Gibbsian segregation $^{40,41}$ experiments $^{38,39}$ show the segregation of copper to the surface of the alloys. Calculations using the EAM approach $^{46}$ and first principles methods $^{47}$ also predict segregation of copper to the surface of bulk alloys. Cu segregation to the surface of Cu-Ni clusters has been predicted, $^{18,48}$ although exper iments to confirm this prediction have not yet been per- formed to our knowledge.

### B. The Cu/Pd system
Cu-Pd alloys $^{45}$ form continuous solid solutions with fcc structure at high temperatures over the whole range of composition, but complex ordering phenomena occur below $\sim 900 ~K$ . Three ordered phases exist centered at the nominal concentrations $Cu_{3} Pd, Cu_{4} Pd$ , and CuPd, with fcc, tetragonal, and body-centered-cubic structures, respectively. Surface segregation has been studied in this system under various experimental conditions. Equilibri- um segregation studies reveal that Pd segregates to the surface of the alloys. $^{49}$ The heats of formation of the solid solutions and ordered phases are negative and rath- er large. $^{45}$ This suggests that ordering phenomena may also occur in small Cu-Pd clusters. Independently of this expectation, we can still ask the question of what element will preferentially occupy surface sites in the cluster.

We begin by discussing the clusters with $N=55$ . We have studied $Cu_{13} Pd_{42}, Cu_{27} Pd_{28}$ , and $Cu_{42} Pd_{13}$ in both icosahedral and cuboctahedral structures. Due to the ex- pected ordering tendency, plots like those of Fig. 1 are not very informative in this case and are not reproduced here, although they at least show that, for a given value of $\bar{r}_{Cu}$ , the icosahedral structure is more stable than the cuboctahedral one, which is also the trend observed for Cu-Ni. We have found that the lowest-energy atomic configuration of the $Cu_{13} Pd_{42}$ icosahedron is of the type(0,12,0,1). This means that 12 Cu atoms fill the inner crust, and the remaining Cu atom is on the surface. By adopting this particular atomic configuration, each Cu atom maximizes the number of Pd nearest neighbors. Notice from Table II that most Cu atoms placed on the inner crust have seven Pd nearest neighbors (one of them is the central atom, and the other six sit on the cluster surface). At the same time this configuration indicates that the surface is composed of Pd atoms, which is con- sistent with the observed segregation of Pd to the surface of Cu-Pd alloys. $^{49}$ We conclude that the surface segrega tion effect and the ordering effect perfectly coexist with each other in this cluster.

The lowest-energy configuration obtained for the $Cu_{27} Pd_{28}$ icosahedron is (1,12,2,12); that is, one Cu atom at the cluster center, 12 on the first crust, and the rest on the surface, 12 of them occupying the 12 vertex sites. The 28 Pd atoms occupy edge sites on the surface. The surface of this cluster is plotted in Fig. 3. This atomic configuration arises as a compromise between the order- ing and segregation effects. Since the number of Cu atoms is 27, some of them must necessarily occupy sur- face sites. However, the number of Pd atoms at surface sites (28) is much larger than the number of Cu surface atoms (14), which is again an indication of the surface segregation tendency of Pd. The tendency to ordering is also manifested in this atomic configuration, which ap- pears to maximize the number of Cu-Pd surface bonds. We think that an important stabilizing contribution comes from the arrangement of the 12 Cu atoms at vertex positions on the surface. Each of those vertex atoms has five nearest neighbors on edge positions. By placing all the Pd atoms at edge sites on the surface, and by pushing

The surface is formed by Pd atoms, which is consistent with the segregation tendency of this element. Furthermore, the atomic arrangement maximizes the number of Pd-Cu nearest neighbors. This is immediately seen for the icosahedral case in Table II: each of the Cu atoms in the atomic configuration (1,0,0,12,0,0,0) is fully surrounded by 12 Pd atoms. Again, as we have found for the 55-atom clusters, segregation and ordering coexist without interfering with each other.

Next we turn to the cluster composition $Cu_{27}Pd_{120}$ in the icosahedral structure. The atomic configuration of lowest energy is (0,12,3,12,0,0,0). This indicates a pure Pd surface. The majority of Cu atoms is placed on the innermost crust (12 atoms) and on vertex sites of the middle crust (12 atoms). The Pd atoms which are not on the cluster surface occupy edge sites on the middle crust. From Table II we see that the Cu atoms on vertex sites of the middle crust are mainly surrounded by Pd atoms, and this is also essentially true for the 12 Cu atoms on the innermost crust. Additionally we have observed that exchanging a few Cu atoms between the innermost crust and edge sites on the middle crust requires a very small amount of energy. For instance, the total binding energy of the configuration (0,9,6,12,0,0,0) differs by only 0.044 eV from that of the ground-state configuration given above. In summary, the picture is pretty much the same one already described for $Cu_{13}Pd_{134}$.

A drastic change is observed when we further increase the Cu concentration in the 147-atom icosahedron. The lowest-energy configuration for $Cu_{73}Pd_{74}$ is (0,12,30,12,10,1,8). The picture becomes clearer if we give the number of Pd atoms in the different shells. These numbers are 1, 0, 0, 0, 10, 59, and 4. This means that 73 surface sites are occupied by Pd atoms and only 19 surface sites by Cu atoms. The Cu atoms fill the innermost and middle crusts. We again see a clear Pd enrichment of the surface. In this case the number of Cu atoms is so large that it is difficult to preserve the coexistence of the ordering and Pd-segregation effects. The surface segregation effect appears to dominate: a large portion of the Cu atoms is pushed toward the inside of the cluster, even at the expense of increasing the number of Cu-Cu bonds. Some "surface ordering" seems to be present because eight Cu atoms are at vertex sites and ten at triangular sites on the surface. The surface neighbors of those vertex and face atoms are edge atoms. Consequently this "surface ordering" maximizes the number of Cu-Pd surface bonds.

Finally we have studied $Cu_{93}Pd_{54}$ in the icosahedral structure. The calculated lowest-energy atomic configuration is such that all the 54 Pd atoms occupy edge positions on the surface crust. This gives again evidence of Pd surface segregation and also of the "surface ordering" discussed above. All the vertex and triangular sites of the surface crust are occupied by Cu atoms, which are surrounded by the Pd atoms on edge sites (notice that the number of edge sites is 60, and 54 of these are occupied by Pd atoms).

Although the segregation of Pd atoms to the cluster surface agrees with the experimental observation for bulk Cu-Pd alloys, $^{49}$ it is not easy at first sight to understand this behavior. The cohesive and surface energies of Cu are lower than the corresponding magnitudes for Pd, $^{35}$ so one could expect Cu segregation to the surface of bulk alloys or alloy clusters to be favored. We think that the surprising behavior of Pd is related to atomic size effects.

The lattice constants of the pure metals are substantially different, namely $a_0(\text{Cu})=3.615$ Å and $a_0(\text{Pd})=3.89$ Å (this is in contrast to the Cu-Ni case already discussed). In the alloy (bulk or cluster) the interatomic distances are intermediate between those of the pure metals (ideally, they are the concentration average of those of the metals; this is usually called Vegard's law $^{35}$). One can then consider that the Cu atoms feel an "expanded" lattice and the Pd atoms a "compressed" lattice in the Cu-Pd alloy. The "elastic" energies that have to be paid in the effective expansion of Cu and compression of Pd are very sensitive to the bulk moduli. The bulk modulus of Pd (1.95 $\text{erg/cm}^3$) is much larger than that of Cu (1.35 $\text{erg/cm}^3$), $^{50}$ so a fractional change $|\Delta V|/V$ in the volume per atom costs more energy for Pd than for Cu, and the Pd atoms contribute more to the elastic energy stored in the alloy. However, the surface atoms have a reduced coordination compared to bulk atoms (see Tables I and II for the cluster case), and part of the elastic stress is relieved at the surfaces. Since Pd is the more stressed species, it is not surprising that the surface becomes enriched in Pd.

To give some support to this argument we plot in Fig. 5 the average energies per atom for $\text{Cu}_{55}$, $\text{Pd}_{55}$, $\text{Cu}_{147}$, and $\text{Pd}_{147}$ in the icosahedral structure, as a function of the distance between nearest neighbors on adjacent crusts. We observe that the curvature of the Pd curves is higher, a feature that we interpret as indicative that the Pd clusters are less compressible than the Cu clusters. This is just a consequence of the analogous property for the two bulk metals. The compressibility of the bulk metals enters the EAM through the fitting of the energy of Eq. (1) to the equation of state. $^{31,32}$ An estimation of the elastic energy associated with Cu and Pd atoms in the mixed cluster can be obtained by calculating the energies of the "pure" Cu and Pd clusters with a nearest-neighbor distance equal to that of the mixed cluster [see Eq. (9)], and subtracting from those the energies of the pure clusters with the equilibrium nearest-neighbor distances. For $\text{Cu}_{27}\text{Pd}_{28}$ we have obtained $\Delta E_{\text{elastic}}(\text{Cu})=0.023$ eV/atom and $\Delta E_{\text{elastic}}(\text{Pd})=0.081$ eV/atom, and the results for $\text{Cu}_{73}\text{Pd}_{74}$ are $\Delta E_{\text{elastic}}(\text{Cu})=0.054$ eV/atom and $\Delta E_{\text{elastic}}=0.091$ eV/atom. This corroborates that at near-equiatomic composition, when both species necessarily have atoms on the cluster surface, the Pd atoms are more stressed and part of the elastic energy stored in the cluster can be relieved by Pd segregation to the surface.

FIG. 5. Average energy per atom of $\text{Cu}_{55}$ ($\bigstar$), $\text{Pd}_{55}$ ($\bullet$), $\text{Cu}_{147}$ ($\bigtriangleup$), and $\text{Pd}_{147}$ ($\lozenge$) as a function of the distance between nearest neighbors on two adjacent crusts.

This "qualitative" discussion has concentrated on local elastic energies as a way to explain Pd segregation. However, the elastic energy is only a part of the energy of the alloy or mixed cluster. The Cu-Pd alloy has also important energetic contributions that favor the formation of the alloy, arising from (1) the fact that Cu-Pd bonds are stronger than the average of Cu-Cu and Pd-Pd bonds, and (2) Fermi-surface (or structural) contributions favoring some particular crystal structures, concentrations, and chemical ordering of the atoms. Those two contributions lead to the stability of intermetallic compounds with fixed composition ($\text{Cu}_{4}\text{Pd}$, $\text{Cu}_{3}\text{Pd}$, and CuPd) at low temperature. The bonding contributions have to be taken together with the elastic contribution discussed above to understand the structure and cohesion of the alloys as well as their surface structure. Returning now to the clusters, all those contributions are included in the EAM through the fitting of the parameters of the model to metal properties and to the heats of solution of the alloys, $^{22}$ and the interplay of the elastic and bonding contributions is responsible for the atomic configurations calculated for the mixed clusters. The EAM, however, is not explicitly formulated in terms of the concepts that we have used in our qualitative discussion (elastic energies, etc.) and a quantitative analysis of the results of the EAM in terms of those concepts may not be easy.

## V. SUMMARY

Based on the version of the embedded-atom method in Foiles, Baskes, and Daw, $^{22}$ we have studied segregation and ordering effects in bimetallic Cu-Ni and Cu-Pd clusters as a function of composition and cluster structure and size. First of all we have found, that, for given cluster size and composition, icosahedral clusters are more stable than cuboctahedral ones. In the Cu-Ni clusters the surface is rich in Cu atoms, and the Ni atoms have a tendency to occupy the interior of the cluster. The segregation of Cu atoms to the surface is consistent with the miscibility gap $^{2}$ that occurs in the solid Cu-Ni alloys below $\simeq 630$ K.

The behavior of Cu-Pd clusters is more complex. In this case the lowest-energy atomic distribution obtained can be understood as arising from the interplay of two effects: one is the tendency for Pd segregation to the surface, and the other is the tendency to form ordered compounds in bulk alloys at low temperature. $^{45}$ When the number of Cu atoms in the cluster is small, both effects coexist: the Cu atoms are surrounded by Pd atoms and the Pd atoms occupy the cluster surface. When the number of Cu atoms is large, the two effects enter in mutual competition. The surface segregation effect is the dominant one, and the surface becomes enriched in Pd. Consequently a large number of Cu atoms is pushed toward the interior of the cluster. However, since the cluster is

small some Cu atoms necessarily have to occupy surface sites. The compromise achieved in this case between the two effects is a kind of "surface ordering," in which the Cu atoms become surrounded by Pd atoms on the sur- face.

The behavior of Cu-Pd clusters appears to be con- sistent with the low-temperature behavior of bulk Cu-Pd alloys. Evidently a very large cluster size is needed for the ordering that occurs in bulk alloys to develop: first of all, the precise atomic concentration and the adequate crystal structure would both be needed; and, further- more, the cluster should be large enough for the surface effects to be negligible. These surface effects, as we have shown in this paper, are dominant when the cluster size is not too large.

The investigation performed in this paper can be ex- tended and improved in several ways. The condition of ideal structures with fixed interatomic distances [given by Eq. (9)] should be relaxed. This can be effected by apply- ing steepest-descent relaxation methods. The effect of the temperature should also be explored by calculating free energies. Molecular dynamics simulations based on the EAM formulation of the energy would give interesting insight into the thermodynamic properties of those mixed clusters, and can be feasible at least for small cluster sizes.
