# Molecular Dynamics Simulations of Metal Clusters and Metal Deposition on Metal Surfaces
Masahiko KATAGIRI*1, Momoji KUBO, Ryo YAMAUCHI, Akira MIYAMOTO, Yasuo NOZUE¹, Osamu TERASAKI¹, Terry R. COLEY², Yongsheng Sean LI²,*2 and John M. NEWSAM²

Department of Molecular Chemistry and Engineering, Faculty of Engineering, Tohoku University, Sendai, Miyagi 980-77, Japan
¹Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, Tohoku University, Sendai, Miyagi 980-77, Japan
²BIOSYM/MSI San Diego, California 92121-3752, USA

(Received July 14, 1995; accepted for publication September 13, 1995)

The geometries and energies of $Pd_{n}$ clusters $(n=2-15)$ and the deposition of Pd atoms on the Cu(110) surface at several temperatures have been studied by molecular dynamics using embedded-atom type potentials. Compact structures were obtained for the small $Pd_{n}$ clusters; these geometries are compared with results for Many-Body Alloy (MBA) and Lennard-Jones potentials. For Pd atoms on the Cu(110) surface, the simulations indicate no substantial Pd penetration into the bulk, but unconstrained thin slab models do exhibit surface-driven melting and Pd penetration. The motion of Pd adatoms on the surface is influenced by the surface dynamics, which manifests anharmonicity and roughening.

KEYWORDS: EAM, molecular dynamics, adatom, deposition, epitaxial growth, anharmonicity, roughening, metal surface, metal cluster, diffusion

## 1. Introduction
Much attention has been devoted to the geometrical structures of small clusters. $^{1-5)}$ Quantum methods, such as density functional theory, have been applied successfully to such systems and the relative stabilities of various atomic clusters have been reported. For example, the stabilities of alkali metal clusters are determined by electron shell considerations, with planar configurations being stable for less than 7 atoms. Semiconductor cluster geometries are governed by covalent bonding rules. The structures of rare gas clusters are explicable based on the filling of geometrical shells and subshells of icosahedra. Recent developments in semi-empirical potential functions for transition metals such as the embedded-atom method (EAM), $^{6-9)}$ effective medium theory (EMT) $^{10,11)}$ and many-body alloy (MBA) $^{12,13)}$ potentials provide a basis for simulating transition metal systems. In addition to fundamental insight into cluster physics and chemistry, such cluster studies are of interest given the importance of metal clusters in, for example, heterogeneous catalysis and the synthesis of advanced materials. Theoretical approaches $^{14,15)}$ to investigate the fundamental structures and dynamics of metal clusters on metal surfaces are desirable complements to the recent advances in experimental preparation and characterization techniques. In this study, we have applied molecular dynamics (MD) using EAM potentials to both the geometrical structures of discrete metal clusters and the deposition and diffusion of metal atoms on metal surfaces, namely Pd adatoms and clusters on a Cu(110) surface. Previously, we reported the deposition of Cu on $Pt(110),^{16)}$ in which a $Cu_{13}$ cluster was formed in vapor phase before deposition on the surface; calculations of the cohesive energies of $Cu_{n}$ clusters demonstrated that the geometrical structure of cluster formed in the vapor phase was governed by its stability. The present work addresses the deposition of Pd, which has a stronger self binding energy than Cu, and the diffusion of the deposited adatoms and clusters.

## 2. Embedded-Atom Method (EAM) Potentials Implemented via an Interprocess Communication (IPC) Protocol
In metals, functionals of the local electron density are commonly used to describe the N-body interaction potentials. $^{17,18)}$ The embedded-atom method was originally developed by Daw and Baskes on the basis of density functional theory for simulating the structures and energetics of face-centered cubic (FCC) metal systems. $^{6-9)}$ In EAM, the cohesive energy combines an embedding energy with a repulsive pair electrostatic interaction as in eq. (1). The embedding energy is formulated as the energy change of an atom immersed into the local electron density generated by the remaining atoms.
$$
E_{\mathrm{coh}}=\sum_{i} F_{i}\left[\rho_{h, i}\right]+\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j(j \neq i)} \phi_{i j}\left(R_{i j}\right), \quad(1)
$$
where, $F_{i}$ is the embedding energy, $\rho_{h, i}$ is the electron density and $\phi_{i j}$ is the electrostatic, pairwise interaction due to the ionic core electrons. $\rho_{h, i}$ is calculated simply by superposition of the electron density tails from all of the other atoms at the given nuclear position according to eq. (2), computed based on an analytical description of the radial electron density distribution for an isolated atom. This assumption dramatically decreases the computational expense.
$$
\rho_{h, i}=\sum_{j(j \neq i)} \rho_{j}^{a}\left(R_{i j}\right). \quad(2)
$$
The EAM parameters in the functions $F_{i}[\rho_{h, i}]$ and $\phi_{i j}(R_{i j})$ were fitted by Foiles et al. $^{9)}$ and were used here

The EAM formalism is an analytical formulation of the interatomic interactions in a specialized set of chemical systems. As such, it provides an exemplary requirement for a materials simulations system and, therefore, a neat example of the effectiveness of the new open simulation environment. The evaluation of EAM-based contributions to the system energy and gradients with respect to atomic coordinates and unit cell metrics communicates with Discover95³⁵⁾ through a bi-directional, pipebased interprocess communication protocol (IPC). This arrangement thus employs an external energy and force evaluation within a generalized open simulation system, connected via these efficient interprocess communication protocols. This generalized procedure can accommodate basic formalisms for both classical and quantum mechanical interactions; indeed, any technique which estimates energies and derivatives at each atom center can be used. The present study illustrates the efficacy of the open simulation mechanics in which EAM potentials are used as the means of quantifying the interatomic interactions.

## 3. Results and Discussion
### 3.1 Metal clusters
Before simulating deposition and surface diffusion phenomena, the EAM potentials were further validated in application to elementary metal cluster physics. The most stable structures for Pdₙ (n=2-13) clusters obtained from a simulated annealing (SA) procedure using EAM potential are shown in Fig. 1. The stable structures which were obtained using Lennard-Jones (12-6) (LJ) potentials and conjugate gradient (Polak-Ribiere) and quasi-Newton-Raphson (BFGS) optimization methods are shown in Fig. 2. The final structures from EAM-SA were used as the initial structures for the LJ optimizations. Figure 3 shows the Pd₁₄ and Pd₁₅ structures predicted by EAM and LJ using similar procedures. Compact polyhedral structures were obtained from both methods. The structures obtained for Pdₙ (n=2-13) using EAM are similar to those obtained by simulated annealing techniques using MBA¹²⁾ and LJ potentials. This indicates that the Pd clusters simulated by EAM show a similar structural geometry sequence to the small rare gas clusters, which are well described by LJ. The most stable structures of Pd₁₃ and Pd₁₉ are icosahedral and double icosahedral, respectively. Pd₁₃ and Pd₁₉ clusters are found to be relatively stable by the analysis of the second derivatives of the cohesive energy with respect to size (magic number=13.19) This result is the same as for Cu₁₃ and Cu₁₉ clusters using EAM without corrections for electronic shell structure, as previously reported in ref. 16. The geometry obtained for Pd₁₄ by EAM has C₂ᵥ symmetry, while that by MBA or LJ has C₃ᵥ (capped icosahedron) symmetry. The C₃ᵥ structure has slightly higher energy than the C₂ᵥ structure; the binding energy of the C₂ᵥ structure is −39.40 eV and that of C₃ᵥ structure is −39.30 eV in the EAM approximation. However, the energy difference among these structures is low and the potential barrier is also low. The binding energies for the optimized structures by DMol are −30.315 eV (C₂ᵥ) and −30.821 eV (C₃ᵥ), and the C₃ᵥ structure is slightly stabler than the C₂ᵥ structure. The structure of Pd₁₅ by EAM has the same symmetry (D₆d) as that by LJ. In general, the EAM structures tend to be the same as those predicted by LJ potentials and compact polyhedral structures are stable at small cluster sizes. Small Pdₙ clusters tend to have the same symmetrical arrangements as the raregas clusters. The compact structure has the smallest surface area and the smallest surface energy. This governs energy minimum geometries of small Pdₙ clusters, while in small Cuₙ clusters bond directionality constraints due to the one 4 s electron lead to planar structures being most stable up to 6 atoms.¹⁶⁾ In small clusters, obvious differences between Pd clusters and rare gas clusters do not to appear. However, differences are expected to appear at relatively large cluster sizes, because the structural geometry sequence of Pd is expected to lead to clusters with the smallest surface area. This difference is expected to be pronounced for Pt, Au and Pd, which have strong cohesive forces. As for these systems, the second derivatives of the embedding function are more positive and three-body terms are more substantial compared to Ag, Ni and Cu. For Pt, Au and Pd, the effective pair interactions at the surface differ significantly from those in the bulk and the surface energy depends strongly on the surface index. Differences between the transition metal cluster geometries, especially for Pt, Au and Pd, and those of the rare gas clusters are therefore expected to become apparent at relatively large cluster sizes having large surface areas. This anticipated trend is caused by the valence electron delocalization. In transition metal clusters, the delocalized nature of the valence electrons creates the many-body longer-range force. However, in rare gas clusters, the localized valence electrons create the shorter-ranged dispersion force and each cluster of n rare gas atoms is formed by capping the cluster of n-1atoms around a five fold symmetry axis.¹²⁾

These features caused by the many-body forces appear in the surface systems. For example, Pt(110) and Au(110) surfaces reconstruct to the (1×2) missing row structures. By this reconstruction, the number of second neighbor atoms decreases. From the viewpoint of pairwise interaction, this reconstruction is not preferable energetically. However in the EAM representation, the reconstruction is preferable because the repulsive three body interactions decrease.⁶⁾ This reconstruction has been reported for Pt(110) and Au(110) surfaces by LEED (960 K for Pt(110) and 650 K for Au(110)⁶⁾) which have strong repulsive three-body interactions, while it has not been reported for Ni(110) and Cu(110). The structures of the transition metal clusters which have strong cohesive forces can not be explained straightforward from only the viewpoint of close-packed tendency by many-body effects.

As mentioned above, the geometries of Pdₙ clusters are significantly different from the structures of Cuₙ clusters which have only one 4s valence electron per atom. For Pdₙ clusters, three dimensional structures appear from $n=4$. However for Cuₙ clusters, the three dimensional structures appear from $n=7$, and this behavior is similar to alkali metal clusters.¹⁶⁾ These planar structures are caused by the nature of the bond direction. The difference between the metallic bond and the covalent bond is not clear because it is difficult to define the band gap exactly in small clusters. However very small Cuₙ clusters exhibit bond direction and this governs the geometrical stabilities of the clusters. The corrections of EAM for this bond direction should be made for small Cuₙ clusters. The effective-medium approach for Cuₙ clusters with corrections for electronic shell structure has been reported.¹⁹⁾ In their study, Cu₈, Cu₁₈ and Cu₂₀ are relatively stable (magic number=8, 18, 20) and only Cu₈, Cu₂₀ and the close-packed Cu₁₃ are almost spherical. Their corrections are validated by experiments. The magic numbers of Cuₙ and Agₙ clusters have been reported by experimental technique and magic numbers are 8 and 20.²⁰⁾ Note that Ag has similar electronic structure to Cu. Contrary to this successful application of corrected EMT, non-corrected EAM does not give the correct answer. Without correction terms, the magic numbers are 13 and 19 for Cuₙ clusters.¹⁶⁾ The most stable structures of Cu₁₃ and Cu₁₉ are the icosahedron and double icosahedron respectively and are same as Pdₙ clusters by non-corrected EAM. However, the non-corrected EAM is expected to be applicable for the large Cuₙ clusters and actually, EMT without corrections for shell structure were successfully applied to large Cuₙ clusters ($n<2000$).²¹⁾ This successful application for large Cuₙ clusters encourages us to apply non-corrected EAM to Cu surfaces.

### 3.2 Deposition and diffusion of metals on metal surfaces

We applied EAM to the deposition of Pd atoms onto Cu(110) surface. The deposition and diffusion using $LJ^{32)}$ or $EAM^{33)}$ have been reported and these studies focused on the deposition with energetic atoms and revealed the dynamics of layer growth. We focus on the influence of deposition on the surface. We started from the randomly disordered vapor phase as the initial structure. The cell parameters are $a$=21.6880 Å, $b$=20.4480 Å and $c$=50.0 Å, where $c$-axis is perpendicular to the surface. Three dimensional periodic boundary conditions were used in all simulations. Since three dimensional periodic conditions were used to model the surface, the $c$-axis was elongated to model a two dimensional surface. However, the atoms in the vapor phase see both the surfaces during the simulation. Deposition on either surface is effectively equivalent. We did not impose the energetic effects on atoms. The numbers of Pd and Cu atoms are 100 and 432, respectively, and the number of Cu layers is 9. We found that the energetic influence of surface arrives at about 10 layers in EAM. We did not fix the bottom layer atoms to avoid the influences of fixing to the systems. The dynamics behavior which appears in the correlation functions such as velocity autocorrelation functions (VAFs) is strongly influenced by fixing the bottom atoms. All simulations were performed using the Verlet method and 3 fs time step. The initial disordered structures were prepared by very high temperature simulations on the vapor while all surface atoms were fixed. Figures 5-8 show the snapshots of the deposition during 15 ps at 300 K, 600 K, 900 K and 1200 K, respectively. At higher temperatures, especially at 900 K and 1200 K, the Pd atoms diffused into the bulk. This behavior is related to the surface dynamics described above. In the anharmonic and roughening regions, the diffusion into the bulk is extensive. Moreover, we found that the diffusion of deposited atoms on the surface was strongly influenced by the surface dynamics. Below the anharmonic region (600 K-1000 K) at 300 K, the Pd atoms diffuse or oscillate on the surface and the diffusion of Pd into the bulk was not observed. In addition, Pd atoms tend to form clusters in the vapor phase because of the strong cohesive force and the deposition process of the clusters was observed. These deposited clusters maintained their structures on the surface after the long run. In contrast to this behavior, for the states at 600 K and 900 K, the Pd atoms diffused into the bulk, and the diffusion into the bulk at 900 K was more obvious than that at 600 K. At 600 K, the diffusion was observed around a few surface layers within this simulation time. In the roughening region (1000 K～) at 1200 K, diffusion of Pd atoms into the bulk was extensively observed and premelting was observed for the surface system. $^{29)}$ This indicates

## 4. Conclusions
The EAM potentials provide an efficient method to describe cluster and surface physics for face-centered cubic metals. We have found that small $Pd_{n}$ clusters have compact structures which differ from alkali metal and $Cu_{n}$ clusters in which bond directionality is clearly important. This suggests that the geometrical structure sequence of $Pd_{n}$ clusters can be explained by a tendency to decrease the surface area due to electron delocalization. For small clusters, the structures of $Pd_{n}$ and rare gas clusters are found to be basically similar. However, due to the many-body effects in the embedding energy, differences between $Pd_{n}$ and rare gas clusters are expected to appear at relatively large cluster sizes. Having further validated the applicability of EAM to $Pd_{n}$ clusters, we studied the deposition of $Pd$ atoms on a $Cu(110)$ surface at $300 ~K, 600 ~K, 900 ~K$ and $1200 ~K$. The diffusion of $Pd$ atoms into the bulk of the $Cu$ system was observed at $600 ~K, 900 ~K$ and extensively at $1200 ~K$ only when the thin slab geometry was allowed to relax without constraint. The molten surface state was found to be needed for the $Pd$ atoms to diffuse into the bulk. In addition, the surface dynamics which were remarkable in the anharmonic and roughening region were found to increase the diffusion of deposited atoms.
