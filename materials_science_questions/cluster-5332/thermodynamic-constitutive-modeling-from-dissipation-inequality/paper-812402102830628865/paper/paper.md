# FUNCTIONS OF STATE OF ELONGATED POLYMERS*

V. P. PETROSYAN

Erevan State University

(Received 2 June 1988)

Thermodynamic functions of state have been derived for uniaxially elongated polymers.
Changes of these functions can be deduced from the stress dependence of sample length.

THE functions of state of deformed polymers are normally deduced by combining the entropic ($f_S$) and energetic ($f_U$) contributions to the retractive force [1-3]. The derivation leads to an expression for the coefficient which describes the temperature dependence of force $f$, required to elongate the specimen to a given length $l$. The quantities $f_S$ and $f_U$ are defined by

$$
f_{S}=T\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}\right)_{l}, \quad f_{U}=f-f_{S}
$$

This approach does not provide direct information on the separate changes of energy and entropy, however. As shown in the present paper, these can be deduced by adapting a method developed for estimating the effect of electric field [4].

The change of energy accompanying a change $dl$ of sample length under the action of force $f$ is

$$
d U=T d S+f d l \tag{1}
$$

If temperature and external force are selected as independent variables, the changes of entropy $S$ and length $l$ are given by

$$
d S=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{f} d T+\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial f}\right)_{T} d f
$$

$$
d l=\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f} d T+\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T} d f
$$

Combining these two equations we have

$$
d U=\left[T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{f}+f\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}\right] d T+\left[T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial f}\right)_{T}+f\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T}\right] d f
$$

The energy $U$ as a state variable of the system can be written as

$$
d U=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_{f} d T+\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial f}\right)_{T} d f
$$

A comparison of the last two equations then yields

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_{f}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{f}+f\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial f}\right)_{T}+f\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T} \tag{2}
$$

In view of

$$
\frac{\partial^{2} U}{\partial T \partial f}=\frac{\partial^{2} U}{\partial f \partial T},
$$

$$
\frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial T \partial f}=\frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial f \partial T} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial^{2} l}{\partial T \partial f}=\frac{\partial^{2} l}{\partial f \partial T},
$$

we find that

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f} \tag{3}
$$

By inserting the last equation into (3) we immediately see that

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=T\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}+f\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T} \tag{4}
$$

As the free energy is connected with the internal energy and entropy via the relationship $F=U-TS$, we have

$$
\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=f\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T} \tag{5}
$$

Formulae (3), (4), and (5) form the basis of experimental evaluation of the change in functions of state.

In contrast to existing methods we concentrate here on the coefficients which de- scribe the temperature dependence of length at constant elongating force and, on the other hand, the dependence of length on the magnitude of the external force at constant temperature.

This approach also enables one to deduce separate expressions for the respective energetic and entropic components of the retractive force. To this end we first write the force $f$ from equation (1) as

$$
f=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial l}\right)_{T}-T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial l}\right)_{T}=f_{U}+f_{S},
$$

where

$$
f_{U}=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial l}\right)_{T} \quad \text{and} \quad f_{S}=-T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial l}\right)_{T}.
$$

The last equation can be written in the form

$$
f_{S}=-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial f}\right)_{T}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}\right)_{T} \tag{6}
$$

In combination with equation (3) we then have

$$
f_{S}=-T\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}\right)_{T},
$$

and the term $f_{U}$ is

$$
f_{U}=f+T\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}\right)_{T}
$$

It is thus apparent that for establishing the changes in the thermodynamic state variables one must know the temperature dependence of sample length at constant force.

The resulting deformation involves contributions from all modes of motion; two such modes of particle motion are relevant for polymeric materials: one is associated with vibrations around the equilibrium positions, the other with orientation of mono- meric units with respect to the direction of elongating force; the latter forms the basis of polymer high elasticity.

When the two terms contributing to sample elongation are designated as $l_{v}$ and $l_{e}$, we may write

$$
l=l_{0}+l_{v}+l_{e},
$$

where $l_{0}$ is the initial length of non-deformed sample.

As the quantity $l_{v}$ can be neglected against $l_{e}$, for polymers in the highly elastic state we are justified in writing

$$
l \cong l_{0}+l_{e} \tag{7}
$$

The elastic deformation can be evaluated by considering the action of the external force $f$ on a chain segment (of length $l_{0}^{*}$) participating in deformation. The energy in volved is $U_{\theta}=-l_{0}^{*} f \cos \theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the direction of the external force and the segment orientation.

The total elastic deformation is

$$
l_{e}=N l_{0}^{*}\langle\cos \theta\rangle=N l_{0}^{*} L a,
$$

where $N$ is the number of chain segments which participate in sample deformation and $L a$, the Langevin function, is defined as

$$
L a=\frac{\exp (a)+\exp (-a)}{\exp (a)-\exp (-a)}-\frac{1}{a} \tag{8}
$$

where the parameter $a$ is given by

$$
a=\frac{l_{0}^{*}}{k T} f=\kappa f, \quad \text { where } \kappa=\frac{l_{0}^{*}}{k T}.
$$

If the external force is very large, $a \gg 1$ applies and, in the absence of plastic flow, the sample length approaches a maximum value $l_{m}$, because under these conditions the Langevin function becomes unity. The maximum elastic deformation is $l_{e, m}=l_{m}-l_{0}$

$=Nl_{0}^{*}$ and equation (7) can be written in the form

$$
l=l_{0}+\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right) L a \tag{9}
$$

In the region of small external forces we have

$$
L a \cong \frac{1}{3} a=\frac{1}{3} \kappa f
$$

and the sample length is

$$
l=l_{0}+(1 / 3)\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right) \kappa f
$$

i.e., $l$ is a linear function of $f$. The length of the chain segment $l_{0}^{*}$ can be estimated from this expression, since $\tan \alpha=(1 / 3)\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right)\left(l_{0}^{*} / k T\right)$ and $l_{0}^{*}=(3 k T \tan \alpha) /\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right)$.

The magnitude of $a$ can be determined at the same time as $a=(3 f \tan \alpha) /\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right)$, and we have

$$
\kappa=\frac{3 \tan \alpha}{l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}}
$$

For polymers in the highly elastic (rubbery) state one can consider the segments of macromolecular chains between the crosslinks to behave as freely jointed chains. In practice this means that the quantity $\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right)$ which characterizes the maximum sample deformation is independent of temperature.

The change in the functions of state can be then derived from equation (9); the coefficients are

$$
\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}=\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right) \frac{\partial L a}{\partial a} \frac{\partial a}{\partial T}=-\frac{l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}}{T} a \frac{\partial L a}{\partial a}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right) \frac{\partial L a}{\partial f}=\frac{l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}}{f} a \frac{\partial L a}{\partial a}
$$

By inserting into equation (4), we obtain the condition

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=0, \tag{10}
$$

i.e., the change of energy accompanying highly elastic deformation equals zero. This

result is obvious since it conforms to the basic assumption inherent in the model. All the same, equation (10) serves as a check on the correctness of the derivation.

The entropy of the system can be deduced from equation (3). Taking the entropy of non-deformed sample to be zero, the quantity $S_f$ is

$$
S_{f}=-\frac{l-l_{0}}{\kappa T}\left\{a L a-\ln \left[\frac{1}{2 a}\left(e^{a}-e^{-a}\right)\right]\right\}
$$

The calculation thus confirms that elongation of macromolecular chains leads to a decrease of entropy.

It is now possible to derive the entropic and energetic terms in the equation for the elastic force; formula (6) shows that $f_{S}=f, f_{U}=0$.

We note that the highly elastic deformation proceeds at constant volume. De- noting by $l_{1}, l_{2}$, and $l_{3}$ the sample dimensions along the three perpendicular directions and assuming the force to be oriented in parallel with the direction of $l_{1}$, the values of the remaining modes can be found from the condition

$$
l_{0}^{3}=l_{1} l_{2} l_{3}=l_{2}^{2}\left[l_{0}+\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right) L a\right],
$$

and, consequently,

$$
l_{2}=\left[\frac{l_{0}^{3}}{l_{0}+\left(l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}\right) L a}\right]^{1 / 2}
$$

The last equation, when combined with the formulae derived previously, allows one to calculate the change of entropy for the remaining two deformation modes. The overall change of entropy is then the sum total of all three values.

Highly elastic deformation vanishes below the glass-transition temperature, where the polymer assumes a rigid conformational structure and only a Hookean, displace- ment-based deformation is possible; the latter is known from experiment to be a linear function of temperature. For this type of deformation one may therefore write $l_{v}=\alpha_{v} T f$, where $\alpha_{v}$ is a parameter to be evaluated by experiment.

The sample length then is

$$
l=l_{0}+\alpha_{v} T f
$$

The coefficients that express the variation of length with temperature and force are

$$
\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}=\alpha_{v} f \quad \text { and } \quad\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=\alpha_{v} T
$$

By inserting these results into equation (4), we obtain

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=2 \alpha_{v} T f \quad \text { and } \quad U_{f}=U_{0}+\alpha_{v} T f^{2}
$$

Under the assumption that $U_{f}=0$ at $f=0$, we immediately see that $U_{f}=\alpha_{v} T f^{2}$.

The change of entropy follows from equation (3),

$$S_{f}=\frac{1}{2} \alpha_{v} f^{2}$$

The last formula shows that in this case the entropy rises with increasing sample deformation, in conformity with the data on elongation of normal, crystalline solids.

Vitrification, provided it proceeds relatively slowly, leads to a decrease of elastic deformation. Strengthening of intermolecular interactions leading to the formation of rigid conformational structure results in a lowering of the quantity $\Delta l=l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}$.

A typical variation of sample length under the action of tensile force at various temperatures is shown in Fig. 2. Below the glass-transition temperature $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ the elongation $\Delta l$ becomes zero, whilst above the melting temperature $T_{\mathrm{m}}$ it increases boundlessly. The temperature dependence of $\Delta l$ (Fig. 3) can be described by the formula

$$\Delta l=\Delta_{0} l \exp \left[\alpha\left(T-T_{0}\right)\right], \tag{11}$$

$T_{0}=(1 / 2)\left(T_{\mathrm{m}}+T_{\mathrm{g}}\right)$ is the mean temperature of the main transition.

The parameters $\alpha$ and $\Delta_{0} l$ must be determined experimentally. The value of $\alpha$ corresponding to the above curve is defined by

$$\alpha=4 /\left(T_{\mathrm{m}}-T_{\mathrm{g}}\right)$$

It then follows that, when the sample length is given by equations (9) and (11), the coefficients characterizing its dependence on temperature and force can be calculated from

$$
\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial T}\right)_{f}=-\frac{\Delta l}{T} a \frac{\partial L a}{\partial a}+L a \frac{\partial(\Delta l)}{\partial T}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial l}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=\frac{l_{\mathrm{m}}-l_{0}}{f} a \frac{\partial L a}{\partial a}
$$

Equation (4) then yields

$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial f}\right)_{T}=T L a \frac{\partial(\Delta l)}{\partial T}
$$

$$
U_{f}=T \frac{\partial(\Delta l)}{\partial T} \int L a d f+U_{0},
$$

where $U_{0}$ is an integration constant. By inserting equation (8) into the last formula, we find that

$$
U_{f}=\frac{\partial(\Delta l)}{\partial T} \frac{k T^{2}}{l_{0}^{*}} \ln \left[\frac{1}{2 a}\left(e^{a}-e^{-a}\right)\right]
$$

where the integration constant was selected so as to ensure the energy to be zero at $f=0$.

By inserting for $\Delta l$ from equation (11), we obtain

$$
U_{f}=\alpha \Delta l \frac{k T^{2}}{l_{0}^{*}} \ln \left[\frac{1}{2 a}\left(e^{a}-e^{-a}\right)\right]
$$

Similarly, the entropy of the system can be evaluated as

$$
S_{f}=-\frac{l-l_{0}}{\kappa T}\left\{a L a-\ln \left[\frac{1}{2 a}\left(e^{a}-e^{-a}\right)\right]\right\}+\alpha \Delta l \frac{k T}{l_{0}^{*}} \ln \left[\frac{1}{2 a}\left(e^{a}-e^{-a}\right)\right]
$$

The energetic and entropic contributions to the elastic force are then calculated from the formulae

$$
f_{S}=\left(1-\frac{\alpha T L a}{a \partial / \partial a}\right) f \quad \text { and } \quad f_{U}=\frac{\alpha T L a}{a \partial L a / \partial a} f
$$

Thus, the derived thermodynamic equations and the formulae for the sample length, valid for different modes of deformation, enable one to calculate changes of heat func- tions of a polymer as well as the energetic and entropic components of the elastic force.
