# Metal behavior at extreme loading rates: Thermodynamics
Aleksander Zubelewicz
Los Alamos National Laboratory, Los Alamos, New Mexico 87545, USA

(Received 26 November 2007; published 20 June 2008)

The objective of the study is to develop thermodynamics based approach, which explains several nonequi- librium phenomena in metals at extreme loading rates. It is a microscale analysis in which mobile defects such as dislocations enable the redistribution of energy within the stressed metal. The redistribution promotes the formation of submicrometer defect structures (slip bands and/or dislocation cells). The noncoincidental ar- rangement of the defects allows minimizing of free energy and offers the material the best opportunity to survive under the loading conditions. The slip bands are homogenized within a characteristic temporal and spatial scale, and appear in the form of perturbations in the field of particle velocity.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Metals and other materials subjected to nonequilibrium thermodynamic stimuli may incur irreversible and well- structured damages. In this development, slip bands and dis- location cells are the defects of our interest. The query here is not so much whether the microstructural arrangement oc- curs. We know that it does. Our interest is in answering a more fundamental question, namely what are the mecha- nisms which necessitate the reconfiguration. Prigogine $^{1}$ stated that the secret to the system's survivability with its microstructural integrity intact is in an exchange of energy. A stable complex system receives low-entropy energy from the environment while giving away energy that is entropy rich. A metal subjected to extreme strain rates $(10^{3} / sec$ and above) becomes such a thermodynamically open system character- ized by the flux of energy due to the high mobility of dislocations. $^{2}$ In stressed crystals, there is a synergetic cou pling between the redistribution of energy and the formation of slip bands and other defects. Consequently, the defects are reshuffled into well-spaced domains and cause a portion of internal energy to be stored while a locally negative rate of entropy becomes a probable outcome. One could forecast many such processes of the energy redistribution but only some of them pave the way toward the state of steadiness and equilibrium. It is shown that the optimal defect configu- ration provides the stressed material with an opportunity to settle down as close to the thermodynamic equilibrium as possible.

The theory describes the dynamic behavior, which has been experimentally observed and/or theoretically predicted in metals at extreme loading conditions. Unfortunately, there is not a single metal subjected to extreme loading conditions whose dynamic properties were fully characterized at the mi- crometer scale. Typically, postshocked samples are analyzed for the presence of the stress defects. But the materials have already experienced the entire loading history and, therefore, the instantaneous states cannot be properly recovered. In Sec. II, we present several experimental observations for various metals, which together challenge the current understanding of the dynamic behavior. This section is meant to set the foundation for the theoretical considerations to follow. The proposed approach is based on the conjecture that there is a well-defined path of the energy redistribution in the stressed material, and that the path can be described in terms of a spatial and temporal variable. Fundamental statements and assumptions in this regard are presented in Sec. III. Next, we introduce the concept of the stress-induced heterogeneity. The heterogeneity is evaluated with the use of kinematical compatibility equations. In continuum dynamics, often boundary-value problems are formulated in terms of veloci- ties or displacements and, therefore, the compatibility is sat- isfied without any further consideration. However, at small length scales (micrometers in range), dislocations cause dis- continuities in the field of particle velocity. Consequently, the line defects violate the material's compatibility. Discussion on this topic is presented in Sec. IV and Appendix A. Sub- sequently, we propose the nonequilibrium thermodynamics concept and offer physics based justification for the forma- tion of well-organized defect structures (Sec. V). In Sec. VI, capabilities of the theory are demonstrated with the use of an exact solution for a copperlike metal subjected to shock at uniaxial strain condition. As shown, the theory is capable of explaining several of the seemingly disconnected phenomena discussed in Sec. II.

## II. METAL BEHAVIOR IN LOADING EXTREMES

The shock physics community is accustomed to the use of scaleless equations of state (EOS) and strength models. We question the appropriateness of the models in application to metals at extreme loading rates. There is a well-documented evidence $^{3,4}$ that shock and/or isentropic loading promote the formation of strong but not quite stable dislocation struc- tures, wherein there is a continuous competition between the dislocation nucleation, storage, and thermal annihilation. The dislocation structures coexist with or are replaced by defor- mation twins, as experimentally observed. $^{5,6}$ The dynamic reconfiguration is responsible for an abrupt increase in the dynamic hardening $^{7-9}$ at strain rates greater than $10^{3} / sec$ . Often, shocked metals preserve some of the stress-induced defects but do not retain full memory of the instantaneous shock strengthening. For instance, shocked copper and tan- talum exhibit an abrupt strengthening during the active loading process; however the preshocked material tested again at quasistatic conditions displays a substantially milder

Similar behavior was observed in other metals as well. Usually, grain size is considered the primary cause for these perturbations. $^{14,15}$ The effects are also linked to other defects such as inclusions or porosity. $^{16}$ The role of slip bands and deformation twins should not be underestimated either. Three-dimensional (3D) dislocation dynamics simulations were employed to study the dynamic behavior of a single-crystal copper under a broad range of high strain rates $^{17}(10^{4} / sec-10^{6} / sec)$ . ### III. NONSTEADY PATH OF ENERGY REDISTRIBUTION IN STRESSED METAL
At small length scales (in the range of nanometers and micrometers), the stress-induced damage processes such as the formation of slip and/or microshear bands are directly linked to the fluctuations of the particle velocity and the redistribution of energy within the material. The perturbations occur both in time and space. The temporal and spatial coupling is an essential element of the description because it captures the kinetics of the nanoscale and mesoscale rearrangements. We may envision a simple scenario where the processes are steady and are described in terms of a path $s_{k}^{steady}=x_{k}-v_{k}^{s} t$. The state variables such as internal energy, entropy, and temperature remain constant at fixed positions along $s_{k}^{steady}$. In this notation, the path is defined in terms of the current spatial position $\{x_{k}\}$ , which is moving with the velocity $v_{k}^{s}$ in the stressed material. All the variables are presented in Cartesian coordinate system where $k=1,2,3$ . It is worth noting that an observer placed at the point $\{x_{k}\}$ and moving with the constant velocity $v_{k}^{s}$ does not detect any changes in the surroundings. We expand the concept of steadiness to nonsteady processes and write the path in a more general form,
$$s_{k}=x_{k}^{s}\left(x_{l}, t\right)-u_{k}^{s}.\qquad(1)$$

As before, all thermodynamic variables are described in terms of the nonsteady path. At first, the local coordinate system $\{x_{k}^{s}\}$ coincides with the initial position of the material particle $\{x_{k}\}$ and the travel distance $u_{k}^{s}$ is equal to zero. During an active deformation process, the local coordinate system $\{x_{k}^{s}\}$ follows the process of the energy redistribution. The progression is monitored in time $t$ and also is affected by the current location of the particles $\{x_{k}\}$ . The travel distance $u_{k}^{s}$ is measured from the departure position. In this construction, the nonsteady path $s_{k}$ evolves during the thermodynamic process (Fig. 1). The complex behavior can be brought into the state of steadiness (the evolution of the path is complete) and equilibrium where there is no further exchange of energy. Any fixed position along the path $(s_{k}=$ const $)$ is constrained in time and space [Eq. (1)], namely

$$v_{k}^{s}=\omega_{k l} v_{l}^{l}+v_{k}^{0}.\qquad(2)$$

In this expression, energy is redistributed with the velocity $v_{k}^{s}=\partial u_{k}^{s} / \partial t$ . The velocity in the nondeformed material is $v_{k}^{0}$  $=\partial x_{k}^{s} / \partial t$ and the current particle velocity is $v_{l}^{l}=\partial x_{l} / \partial t$ . Lastly, the mechanism of the energy transfer is controlled by the tensor $\omega_{k l}=\partial x_{k}^{s} / \partial x_{l}$ . In general, the variables $v_{k}^{s}$ and $\omega_{k l}$ vary during the active loading process. In steady state, the velocity $v_{k}^{s}$ and the mechanism $\omega_{k l}$ remain unchanged, and in equilibrium, $v_{k}^{0}$ and $v_{l}^{l}$ are equal to zero.

In shock physics, there is an empirical Hugoniot relationship that couples the shock velocity $v_{k}^{s}$ with the velocity of particles $v_{l}^{l}$ . The relation is defined in the so-called end state, which is arbitrarily positioned behind the shock front. The cut-off velocity $v_{k}^{0}$ , found at $v_{k}^{s}=0$ , is approximately the sound velocity. In many materials (such as magnesium, molybdenum, or gallium), the end states gathered for a broad range of shock pressures can be summarized in the form of a linear relation between the wave velocity and the particle velocity. $^{18}$ Some nonlinearity in this relation is observed in copper, nickel, lead, tantalum, and other materials. This nonlinearity is traced to the alterations in the deformation mechanism $\omega_{k l}$ at various pressures and temperatures.

The concept of the energy redistribution is further explored for metals at extremes loading rates in small scales. For this purpose, we select a small representative volume element (RVE) in the micrometer range defined through the length $l_{c}$ . The volume contains a small portion of the material, which is embedded into a uniform field of stresses and strains. Inside RVE and in its immediate surroundings, there is a short-range exchange of energy due to the mobility of dislocations. $^{2}$ These processes occur with a high frequency. The energy redistribution is aligned with the directions of the dominant plastic slippage $\omega_{i j}^{p}=(n_{i} s_{j}+s_{i} n_{j})$ along the active metallurgical planes $(s_{k}, n_{k})$ and $(n_{k}, s_{k})$ . The rate of specific plastic strain is
$$\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{p}=\frac{1}{2} \omega_{i j}^{p} \dot{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{p},\qquad(3)$$
where $\dot{\varepsilon}_{eq}^{p}$ is the magnitude of the slippage scaled through mass density. The word "specific" refers to the strain rate

scaled through mass density. The choice of the specific strain rate is convenient because it matches the description of clas- sic thermodynamics. For instance, it is a common practice to describe internal energy in terms of hydrostatic pressure, specific volume (inverse of the current mass density), and specific entropy. In our case, the rate of specific shear strain is scaled through the current atom-to-atom distances (mass density). Consequently, the path [Eq. (1)] becomes $\varsigma_{k}$ $=\omega_{k l}^{p} x_{l}-u_{k}^{\varsigma}$ and it is described in terms of two independent components

$$
\varsigma_{s}=s_{k} \varsigma_{k}=n_{l} x_{l}-u_{s}^{\varsigma},
$$

$$
\varsigma_{n}=n_{k} \varsigma_{k}=s_{l} x_{l}-u_{n}^{\varsigma}. \tag{4}
$$

The reference framework $\{x_{k}^{\varsigma}\}$ coincides with the current po sitions $\{x_{k}\}$ in RVE. In this case, an observer located within the representative volume does not detect any global changes in the material. However, there are noticeable fluctuations caused by dislocations traveling along slip planes,

$$
u_{q}^{\varsigma}=\int_{\text {s-process }}\left(N v_{d}\right)_{q} d t, \tag{5}
$$

where $q=s, n$. There are $N$ in such dislocations that enter and leave RVE, and they do so in a nondiscriminatory manner. This means that there is no net flux of dislocations through the volume. The path of the energy redistribution may bifur- cate and/or form discontinuities inside RVE. When the per- turbations are not resolvable at the current temporal and spa- tial scale, then a higher order microstructural arrangement emerges. The formation of slip bands in a deformed metal is such an event, where a highly localized slippage is associ- ated with rapid adiabatic heating and causes an abrupt redis- tribution of internal energy. At a higher length scale, shear bands spaced apart tens or hundreds of micrometers $^{19}$ play an equally important role in the stressed material.

## IV. FORMATION OF DYNAMIC DEFECT STRUCTURES

In loading extremes, materials exhibit a nonequilibrium hierarchical behavior. At nanoscale, a portion of the stressed crystal is cleaned from nucleating dislocations while the de- fects migrate with an average velocity $v_{d}$ and are collected elsewhere in regularly spaced domains. As discussed earlier, the mobile dislocations form submicrometer structures that consist of coarse slip bands or dislocation cells. Strongest of the events become the nucleation sites for higher order ar- rangements (Fig. 2). At mesoscale (tens and hundreds of mi- crometers), the most pronounced slip bands and/or other de- fects promote the formation of unstable thermoplastic shear bands. $^{19}$ Only some of the shear bands and/or spots of a highly localized inelastic deformation cause macrodamages. Spatial and temporal distribution of the mesostructures and macrostructures is controlled by the rate of the energy redis- tribution in the heterogeneous material. For instance, shocked metal may not have enough time for reaching an equilibrium configuration and remains in a metastable state for a considerable time. The heterogeneities are analyzed with the use of kine- matical compatibility relations presented in Appendix A. The relations grant a unique coupling between six components of the symmetric part of the deformation gradient and three components of the particle velocity. Any violation of kine- matical compatibility means that there are some inconsisten- cies in the velocity field. We link the rate of the incompat- ibility with the motion of dislocations. The measurable heterogeneity (incompatibility) becomes the additional vari- able added to the list of stresses, strain rates, velocities, and mass density. As previously shown $^{20,21}$ and discussed in Ap pendix A, the rate of the incompatibility $\dot{\psi}_{i j}^{inc }$ is expressed in terms of the rate of specific fluctuating strain, where $(i, j)$  $=1,2,3$ , and the rate of the incompatibility is $\dot{\psi}_{i j}^{inc }=\partial \psi_{i j}^{inc } / \partial t$ . From Eq. (3) and based on the derivations in Appendix A, the kinematical incompatibility is reduced to a single scalar relation

$$
\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\frac{\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right)}{2} \frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i} \partial x_{j}}\left(\omega_{i j}^{p} \dot{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{p}\right), \tag{6}
$$

and is a function of $\varsigma_{s}$ and $\varsigma_{n}$ along

$$
\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right) \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{p}}{\partial \varsigma_{s} \partial \varsigma_{n}},\qquad(7)
$$

where $\partial / \partial \varsigma_{s}=s_{k} \partial / \partial x_{k}$ and $\partial / \partial \varsigma_{n}=n_{k} \partial / \partial x_{k}$. In these relations, the length $l_{n}$ describes an average size of the dislocation loop and $l_{m}$ is an average travel distance, thus, the density of mobile dislocations becomes $\rho_{d}^{m}=1 /\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right)$. This form of the incompatibility is valid so long as the mechanism $\omega_{i j}^{p}$ itself is not a differentiable quantity within the representative volume element.

Evolving systems exhibit the tendency toward increasing their homogeneity. $^{22}$ According to this statement the process of energy redistribution is not random. Instead, it takes place along an optimal path in order to compensate for the stress-induced damages accumulated along the path $\dot{\psi}^{\text {inc }}$ $=\dot{\psi}^{\text {inc }}\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)$. Our objective is to minimize the incompatibility by homogenizing it within a sufficiently large segment of the path such that the incompatibility vanishes. From all the kinematically admissible segments, we select the smallest one and call it the characteristic scale $\varsigma_{c}$. The characteristic scale contains a representative number of slip bands and/or other stress-induced defects. Consequently, the scale $\varsigma_{c}=l_{c}$ $-u_{c}\left(\Delta t_{c}\right)$ is defined in terms of the characteristic distance between the defects $l_{c}$ while the frequency of the energy reverberations is given by $\Delta t_{c}^{-1}$. As a result, the macrocompatibility criterion

$$
\dot{\psi}_{\text {inc }}^{\text {macro }}=\left(1 / \Delta \Gamma_{c}\right) \int_{\Delta \Gamma_{c}\left(\varsigma_{c}\right)} \dot{\psi}^{\text {inc }}\left(\varsigma_{k}+z_{k}\right) d \Gamma\left(z_{k}\right)=0\qquad(8)
$$

is added to the list of governing equations. The incompatibility is homogenized within the surroundings $\left\{z_{k}\right\}$ of the position $\left\{\varsigma_{k}\right\}$, where $\Delta \Gamma_{c}$ is the characteristic domain of the path. In Appendix A, the integral form of the criterion [Eq. (8)] is further revised and presented in the form of an elliptic differential equation also known as the Helmholtz equation,

$$
\left[1+\frac{\varsigma_{c}^{2}}{24}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \varsigma_{s}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \varsigma_{n}^{2}}\right)\right] \dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)=0.\qquad(9)
$$

As discussed earlier, the components $\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)$ are aligned with the dominant plane $\left(s_{k}, n_{k}\right)$ of metallurgical slip, namely $\varsigma_{s}$ $=\varsigma_{k} s_{k}$ and $\varsigma_{n}=\varsigma_{k} n_{k}$. The criterion [Eq. (9)] suggests that the incompatibility $\dot{\psi}^{\text {inc }}\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)$ is a harmonic function and the slip bands form repetitive structures.

A different homogenization scenario applies at tensile stresses. $^{23}$ It has already been shown that the processes of void nucleation, growth, and coalescence restore compatibility. As theoretically predicted, the mechanism of void nucleation is distinctly different from the processes of void growth and coalescence. Voids nucleate under the Gurson's mechanism $^{24}$ described in terms of the stress triaxiality ratio (the ratio of hydrostatic pressure and shear stress). The nucleation mechanism initiates the process of growth, which is now controlled by the maximum tensile stress. At the most advanced stage of the ductile damage, localized shear bands connect the adjacent voids. Experimental observations $^{25}$ suggest that more than one damage mechanism is available in a stressed material.

## V. THERMODYNAMICS CONSIDERATIONS

Mobile dislocations perturb the field of particle velocity. Since dislocations do not carry mass or alter material's volume, the velocity fluctuations $\delta v_{i}^{f}$ become volumeless distortions $\delta v_{i, i}^{f}=0$. The expression for internal energy rate $\dot{U}$ per unit mass is

$$
\rho \dot{U}=\sigma_{i j} v_{i, j}+\sigma_{i j} \delta v_{j, i}^{f}+\dot{q}_{i, i},\qquad(10)
$$

where $\sigma_{i j}$ are components of the Cauchy stress, the homogeneous velocity of particles is $v_{i}$, and $\dot{q}_{i}$ represents the rate of heat flow per unit area. The exchange of energy due to the mobility of dislocations is $\sigma_{i j} \delta v_{j, i}^{f}$. In the analysis to follow, the homogeneous process is assumed reversible. Total particle velocity is composed of the homogeneous and fluctuating contributions $v_{i}^{f}=v_{i}+\delta v_{i}^{f}$. The fluctuations $\delta v_{i}^{f}$ are linked to the average dislocation velocity $v_{d}$ and are coupled with the incompatibility $\dot{\psi}_{\text {inc }}$.

In continuum dynamics, we select a conjugate pair of stresses $T_{i j}$ and strains $\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{t}$ that satisfy the Hill's condition $^{26}$ $\left[\sigma_{i j} v_{i, j}+\sigma_{i j} \delta v_{i, j}^{f}\right] / \rho=T_{i j} \dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{t}$. In this analysis, $T_{i j}$ is taken to be the Cauchy stress $\left(T_{i j}=\sigma_{i j}\right)$ and, consequently, the conjugate specific rate of the specific distortion

$$
\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{t}=D_{i j} / \rho\qquad(11)
$$

is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient. $D_{i j}=\left(v_{i, j}^{t}\right.$ $\left.+v_{j, i}^{t}\right) / 2$ is scaled through mass density. Frequently, the pair of conjugate stresses and strains is reduced to hydrostatic pressure $p=-\sigma_{k k} / 3$, and specific volume $V^{h}=1 / \rho$. In here, the rate of specific volume is $\dot{V}^{h}=\dot{\varepsilon}_{k k}^{t}$. The rate of total specific strain is decomposed into the rates of the specific fluctuations $\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{f}$, homogeneous shear $\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{h}$, and specific volume $\dot{\varepsilon}_{k k}^{t}$ such that $\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{t}=\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{f}+\dot{\varepsilon}_{i j}^{h}+\delta_{i j} \dot{\varepsilon}_{k k}^{h} / 3$. As stated earlier $\dot{\varepsilon}_{k k}^{f}=\delta v_{k, k}^{f}$ $=0$. In an isotropic elastic and von Mises plastic material, internal energy is written as $U=U\left(V^{h}, e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}, S\right)$. In this expression $S$ is entropy while the invariant shear strain is $e_{\text {eq }}^{h}$ $=\sqrt{e_{i j}^{h} e_{i j}^{h} / 2}$. Entropy is defined in two domains; the homogeneous (background) portion of specific entropy is $S^{h}$ while $S^{\text {inc }}$ is specific entropy of the distorted crystal. Then, total entropy is

$$
S=(1-\xi) S^{h}+\xi S^{\text {inc }},\qquad(12)
$$

where the volume fraction of the distorted crystal is $\xi$. Temperature $T=(\partial U / \partial S)_{\mid\left(V^{h}, e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}\right)}$ is accumulated from the two domains. First,

$$
T^{h}=\left.(\partial U / \partial S^{h})\right|_{\left(V^{h}, e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}, \xi\right)}=(1-\xi) T\qquad(13)
$$

is the background temperature of a "clean" crystal and

$$
T^{\mathrm{inc}}=\left.(\partial U / \partial S^{\mathrm{inc}})\right|_{\left(V^{h}, e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}, \xi\right)}=\xi T\qquad(14)
$$

is the local temperature increase due to the incompressible plastic distortion. From Eqs. (13) and (14) it is evident that $T \equiv T^{h}+T^{\text {inc }}$. The differential temperature is observed only in the nonequilibrium state of the material. The thermodynamic force that enables the microstructural rearrangements is

$$
\left.(\partial U / \partial \xi)\right|_{\left(V^{h}, e_{i j}^{h}, S^{h}\right)}=T\left(S^{\mathrm{inc}}-S^{h}\right). \qquad (15)
$$

Eventually, the force weakens and the defects settle into a semiequilibrium state. Along with the redistribution of energy, also the entropy excess $(S^{\mathrm{inc}}-S^{h})$ is arranged within an optimal configuration. As suggested in Eq. (9), the defects form repetitive structures along the path $(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n})$ aligned with the slip and normal directions. We minimize severity of the damage by restoring compatibility within the characteristic scale $\varsigma_{c}$. In this manner, the optimal defect structure provides the material with the best opportunity to survive under the dynamic loads. At the level of perturbed continuum, the defect evolution is treated as an Euler's homogeneous process $\left.(\xi \partial U / \partial \xi)\right|_{\left(V^{h}, e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}, S^{h}\right)}=k_{s} U$, where $k_{s}$ is defined in the range 0 $\leq k_{s}<1$. From Eqs. (12) and (15) the change of entropy $d S$ $=d S^{h}+d\left[\xi\left(S^{\mathrm{inc}}-S^{h}\right)\right]$ is written as
$$
d S=d S^{h}+d\left(k_{s} U / T\right). \qquad (16)
$$

The term $k_{s} U / T$ represents the entropy increase above $S^{h}$. It has already been assured that entropy production is equal to zero at each fixed position along $(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n})$. However, the defects are reshuffled in time into well-spaced domains and so is entropy. Therefore, a locally negative rate of entropy $(k_{s} U / T)^{\bullet}$ in the stressed metal is an acceptable outcome. We emphasize that the nonequilibrium phenomena are not observed at the scale of homogeneity. Using Legendre transformation, the homogeneous (reversible) portion of internal energy per mass density is converted into instantaneous free energy $F^{\text {inst }}=-\max _{\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)}\left\{T S^{h}-U\right\}$. Next, with the use of Eq. (16), the expression becomes
$$
F^{\mathrm{inst}}=-\max _{\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)}\left\{T S-\left(1+k_{s}\right) U\right\}. \qquad (17)
$$

The transformation is biased by the choice of the path $(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n})$. In Eqs. (16) and (17), the parameter $k_{s}$ represents the fraction of internal energy instantaneously stored in the deformed material. The energy storage results from the energy fluctuations in Eq. (10) and is integrated over the deformation process along the path
$$
k_{s}=\frac{T}{U} \int_{\varsigma \text {-process }} \frac{1}{T} \sigma_{i j} \frac{\partial \varepsilon_{i j}^{f}}{\partial \varsigma_{q}} d \varsigma_{q}, \qquad (18)
$$
where $q=s, n$. In our case, the storage will be evaluated in a material point. Then, the path evolves in time alone. The dynamic storage was measured in copper and zirconium $^{27}$ at strain rates 3700/sec. The experimental results suggest that the energy fluctuations and the instantaneous storage may, in some instances, amount to about 50% of the total plastic dissipation. Instantaneous free energy [Eq. (18)] is affected by two competing mechanisms. First, $F^{\text {inst }}$ increases due to the accumulation of stress defects (Fig. 3). However, the distorted crystal evolves toward the state of lowest energy by redistributing the defects. The material regains homogeneity upon a complete annihilation of the defects $F^{\text {inst }}=F^{\text {equil }}$. As stated earlier, temperature is obtained from the known relation $T=\partial U /\partial S|_{(V^{h},e_{eq}^{h})}$, while entropy is calculated directly from instantaneous free energy at constant microscopic configuration $S=-\partial F^{\text {inst }}/\partial T|_{(V^{h},e_{eq}^{h},k_{s})}$. The increment of instantaneous free energy is is

d F^{\mathrm{inst}}=-p^{\mathrm{inst}} d V^{h}+\tau_{i j}^{\mathrm{inst}} d e_{i j}^{h}-S d T, \qquad (19)
$$

$$
p^{\mathrm{inst}}=\left(1+k_{s}\right) p^{\mathrm{equil}}+\left.U \partial k_{s} /\partial V^{h}\right|_{\left(e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}, S^{h}\right)},\qquad (20)

$$

In the isotropic elastic and von Mises incompressible plastic material, the instantaneous shear stress $\tau_{i j}^{\text {inst }}$ is also a stress deviator and the instantaneous stress is $\sigma_{i j}^{\text {inst }}=\tau_{i j}^{\text {inst }}-p^{\text {inst }} \delta_{i j}$. The equilibrium stresses (pressure and stress deviator) are $p^{\text {equil }}=-\partial U/\partial V_{(e_{\text {eq }}^{h}, S)}$ and $\tau_{i j}^{\text {equil }}=\left.(\partial U/\partial e_{i j}^{h})\right|_{(V^{h}, S)}$, respectively. Typically, slip bands have little or no effect on mass density. Under such circumstances, instantaneous pressure becomes $p^{\text {inst }}=(1+k_{s}) p^{\text {equil }}$. The above argument also applies to the processes of martensitic transformations where volume changes are negligible. Quite a different result is possible during crystallographic transformations where the transformation is associated with a measurable change in mass density. In these instances, the parameter $k_{s}$ is also a function of the specific volume $V^{h}$.

During nonequilibrium thermodynamic processes, energy and stress-induced defects are rearranged along the nonsteady path $(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n})$. There is no entropy production at each fixed position of the path and, therefore,
$$
\partial\left[S^{h}+\left(k_{s} U / T\right)\right]/\partial t|_{(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n})}=0. \qquad (22)
$$

The proposed construction guarantees that instantaneous free energy $F^{\text {inst }}$ has a clearly defined pathway toward the states of steadiness $(k_{s}=$ const) and equilibrium $(k_{s}=0)$. As a result, there is a need for $k_{s}$ to be at minimum at any instance and in any place of the material. This requirement defines the optimal path of the energy redistribution. For completeness of the analysis, we introduce material functions that describe reversible processes, namely

$$
\rho B=\left.\frac{\partial^{2} U}{\partial\left(V^{h}\right)^{2}}\right|_{\left(e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}, S\right)}, \quad 2 \rho G=\left.\frac{\partial^{2} U}{\partial\left(e_{\mathrm{eq}}^{h}\right)^{2}}\right|_{\left(V^{h}, S\right)}, \quad(23)
$$

in which $B$ and $G$ are bulk and shear moduli.

## VI. EXAMPLE: METAL SHOCKED AT UNIAXIAL STRAIN

We examine the capabilities of the theory by investigating the behavior of metals subjected to shock propagating in the direction $(x)$. The metal behavior is affected by the shortrange exchange of energy within RVE and also with its surroundings. The path of the energy redistribution [Eq. (6)] becomes $\varsigma=x-N v_{d} t$. In an optimal configuration, two dislocations cross the area $\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right)$ such that one of them enters and the second leaves the macroparticle. Since there are two such areas at two opposite sides of RVE, the total number of dislocation is equal to four $(N=4)$. The material responses are monitored in a very short-time interval (in the range of $10^{-7} / \mathrm{sec}$ ). For this reason, a linear form of the strength model $\sigma_{\mathrm{eq}}=\sigma_{0}+\rho \theta_{\psi} \psi^{\text {inc }}$ seems reasonable. In this relation $\sigma_{0}$ is the yield stress and $\theta_{\psi}$ is the dynamic hardening due to the motion of dislocations. The unknown variables are: $\sigma_{x}(\varsigma)$, $\sigma_{\text {eq }}(\varsigma), \delta v_{x}^{f}(\varsigma), \dot{e}_{x}^{f}(\varsigma), \dot{e}_{y}^{f}=\dot{e}_{y}^{f}(\varsigma)$, and $\psi_{\text {inc }}(\varsigma)$. The expression that describes the dynamic hardening $\theta_{\psi}$ is not assumed and is determined from the solution. The governing equations of the mesocontinuum are:

### (a) Constitutive equations

$$
\dot{e}_{x}^{f}=\frac{1-\nu}{1-2 \nu} \dot{\varepsilon}_{x}^{t}+\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{x}}{2 \rho G}, \quad \dot{e}_{y}^{f}=\dot{e}_{z}^{f}=-\frac{1}{2} \dot{e}_{x}^{f},
$$

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{eq}}=\sigma_{0}+\rho \theta_{\psi} \psi_{\mathrm{inc}}, \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{1-2 \nu}{1-\nu}\left|\sigma_{x}\right| . \quad(24)
$$

In these relations, $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio, the total strain rate is $\dot{\varepsilon}_{x}^{t}=v_{x, x}^{t} / \rho=\delta v_{x, x}^{f} / \rho$, stress in $x$ direction is denoted as $\sigma_{x}$, and Huber-von Mises stress is $\sigma_{\text {eq }}=\sqrt{3 J_{2}}$, where $J_{2}=1 / 2 S_{i k} S_{k i}$ and stress deviator is $S_{i j}=\sigma_{i j}-\sigma_{k k} / 3 \delta_{i j}$.

### (b) For uniaxial strain the equation of motion is

$$
\frac{\partial \sigma_{x}}{\partial x}=\rho \frac{\partial \delta v_{x}^{f}}{\partial t}, \quad(25)
$$

in which $\rho$ is mass density.

### (c) The rate of the kinematical incompatibility [Eq. (A2)] and the requirement of the macrocompatibility are

$$
\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right) \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{y}^{f}}{\partial x^{2}}, \quad\left(1+\frac{\varsigma_{c}^{2}}{24} \frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \varsigma^{2}}\right) \dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=0 . \quad(26)
$$

As suggested in Eq. (A3), dislocations enter and leave RVE in a nondiscriminatory manner making the process a harmonic one $\psi^{\mathrm{inc}}=\psi_{\mathrm{inc}}^{s}+\psi_{\mathrm{inc}}^{f}(s)$. In this expression, the variable $s=\sqrt{24} \varsigma / \varsigma_{c}$ is expressed in terms of the optimal path $\varsigma$. Furthermore, $\psi_{\text {inc }}^{s}$ describes the storage of the dynamic structures and the fluctuating part of the incompatibility is $\psi_{\text {inc }}^{f}$ $=\psi_{1} \sin s+\psi_{2} \cos s$. The scale $\varsigma_{c}=l_{c}-N v_{d} \Delta t_{c}$ includes the characteristic length $l_{c}$ and microscale excitations occurring with the frequency $\Delta t_{c}^{-1}$. In metals, the frequency is linked to the dislocation frequency $^{28}$ and is known to be in the order of $10^{10} / \mathrm{sec}$. The scaleless parameters $\psi_{1}$ and $\psi_{2}$ define the magnitude of the fluctuations. Despite simplicity of the analysis, we make quite insightful observations regarding the material responses at high strain rates. First, the average dislocation velocity $v_{d}$ is found to have an upper limit

$$
v_{c r}=\frac{\sqrt{2} c_{s}}{N} \sqrt{\frac{1-\nu}{1-2 \nu}},
$$

where shear velocity is $c_{s}=\sqrt{G / \rho}$. Existence of such a limiting velocity is a known property of metals. $^{2,29}$ Solution of the problem is presented in terms of the dynamic hardening

$$
\theta_{\psi}=\frac{G \rho_{d}^{m} \varsigma_{c}^{2}}{3 N^{2}} \frac{\left(c_{s} / v_{c r}\right)^{2}\left(v_{d} / v_{c r}\right)^{2}}{1-\left(v_{d} / v_{c r}\right)^{2}} .
$$

The formula resembles the expression for the drag controlled motion of dislocations. $^{28}$ The gradient of the fluctuating strain along the path $\varsigma$ is

$$
\partial \varepsilon_{x}^{f} / \partial \varsigma=-\frac{\rho_{d}^{m} \varsigma_{c}^{2}}{12} \frac{\partial \psi^{\mathrm{inc}}}{\partial \varsigma},
$$

where $\rho_{d}^{m}=1 /\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right)$. In loading extremes, the dislocations are moving with a nearly critical velocity $\left(v_{d} / v_{c r} \rightarrow 1\right)$ and then the material experiences an abrupt dynamic strengthening. This condition promotes the formation of strong defect structures. The instantaneous storage $k_{s}$ is also derived in terms of the ratio $v_{d} / v_{c r}$, where variations in temperature are neglected. The storage is

$$
k_{s}=U_{\varsigma}^{f} / U_{0}^{f},
$$

where $U_{\varsigma}^{f}$ is plastic dissipation calculated at an arbitrary position along the path $\varsigma$

$$
U_{\varsigma}^{f}=U_{0}^{f}-\frac{\varsigma_{c}^{2} \rho_{d}^{m}}{12}\left(\sigma_{0} \psi_{\mathrm{inc}}+\frac{1}{2} \theta_{\psi} \psi_{\mathrm{inc}}^{2}\right),
$$

and the far-field dissipation is $U_{0}^{f}=l_{c}^{2} \rho_{d}^{m} \sigma_{0} \psi_{\text {inc }}^{s} / 12$. The analysis is constructed for a copperlike material where the material constants are given in the Tables I and II. The predictions are summarized in Fig. 4. In the thermally activated regime $\left(v_{d} / v_{c r}<0.3\right)$, the storage $k_{s}$ is in the expected range of $10 \%$. The instantaneous storage is more pronounced in the dislocation drag mechanism $\left(v_{d} / v_{c r}>0.3\right)$, where $50 \%$ of the instantaneous entrapment of energy is a measurable result. $^{27}$ The frequency of the events has no specific meaning because the fluctuations are taken at a randomly selected position

## VII. FINAL REMARKS

The objective of the analysis has been to present thermodynamics based theory that is capable of predicting the dynamic behavior of metals at complex nonsteady conditions. At micrometer scales, the analysis suggests that dislocations form repetitive slip bands. Many such microstructural configurations are possible but only a small number of them bring instantaneous energy $F^{\text{inst}}$ toward Helmholtz energy $F^{\text{eq}}$. We explain that the thermodynamically favorable processes occur along the optimal path of the energy redistribution. The path is linked to the motion of dislocations. In a limiting case, when the dislocation velocity approaches the critical (forbidden) velocity, the energy path breaks down and the material experiences a higher order defect arrangement.

In course of the analysis, we explained several phenomena observed in metals at extreme dynamic conditions. The theory captures the abrupt dynamic strengthening in all metals tested at strain rates $^{7}$ greater than $10^{3}/\text{sec}$. It is worth noting that the derived formula for the dynamic hardening $\theta_{\psi}$ resembles closely the relationship of the dislocation viscous drag. $^{28}$ Shock experiments conducted at uniaxial strain conditions show that in copper, titanium, tantalum, and aluminum, the magnitude of the dynamic hardening increases with applied shock pressure, $^{30}$ and then, it reaches a plateau or even decreases. The dynamic hardening is linked here to the dislocation mobility and storage. In the medium range of pressures (typically below 50 GPa), the higher the pressure is the faster the dislocations travel and the stronger the dynamic hardening becomes. For instance, in copper subjected to pressure of 30 GPa, the hardening was found in the order of 1 GPa. However, the postshock samples retested again at quasistatic conditions displayed a significantly milder hardening measured in hundreds of MPa. $^{10}$ The stress-induced heterogeneity is both fluctuating and stored in the stressed metal. We calculated the energy storage for a copperlike material showing that the fraction of the dynamic storage significantly exceeds the commonly used value of 10%. The recent experimental measurements $^{27}$ support the prediction. In other set of experiments, line-VISAR measurements $^{12}$ suggest that shocked metals experience strong fluctuations in particle velocity. Unfortunately, the experiments were conducted on polycrystalline metals only. Single crystal systems were analyzed with the use of large-scale nonequilibrium molecular-dynamics simulations. $^{31}$ It was shown that small dislocation loops are created from thermal fluctuations just behind the shock front. The subsequent growth and interactions of the dislocations perturb the particle velocity even further.

The requirement of kinematical compatibility is
$$
e_{p k l} e_{s r j} \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{i j}^{t}}{\partial x_{k} \partial x_{r}}=0, \tag{A1}
$$
where $e_{i j k}$ denotes permutation tensor and $(i,j,k)=1,2,3$. In this form, the relation in Eq. (A1) represents six differential equations that must be satisfied in order to preserve a unique correlation between the symmetric part of the deformation gradient and the particle velocity. In this analysis, the fluctuating strain rate does not satisfy compatibility
$$
\dot{\psi}_{s p}^{\text{inc}}=\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right) e_{p k l} e_{s r j} \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{i j}^{f}}{\partial x_{k} \partial x_{r}} \neq 0. \tag{A2}
$$

The fluctuating strain rate is defined as
$$
\dot{e}_{i j}^{f}=\frac{1}{2} \omega_{i j}^{p} \dot{e}_{\text{eq.}}^{f}. \tag{A3}
$$

The symmetric tensor $\omega_{i j}^{p}=(n_{i} s_{j}+s_{i} n_{j})$ is defined on the basis of the unit normal and slip vectors $(n_{k},s_{k})$, which taken together with the lateral vector $m_{k}$ form an orthogonal triad. The magnitude of the specific plastic slippage is $\dot{e}_{\text{eq.}}^{f}$. The relation in Eq. (A3) is substituted into the compatibility relation in Eq. (A2) and rederived into the form
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\psi}_{s p}^{\text{inc}}= & m_{p} m_{s} \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{k r}^{f}}{\partial x_{k} \partial x_{r}}-m_{p}\left(m_{r} \partial / \partial x_{r}\right) \frac{\partial \dot{e}_{k s}^{f}}{\partial x_{k}}-m_{s}\left(m_{k} \partial / \partial x_{k}\right) \frac{\partial \dot{e}_{p r}^{f}}{\partial x_{r}} \\
& +\left(m_{k} m_{r} \partial^{2} / \partial x_{k} \partial x_{r}\right) \dot{e}_{s p}^{f} \neq 0
\end{aligned} \tag{A4}
$$

Since the dominant slippage occurs along the direction $s_{k}$ and $n_{k}$, we neglect gradients of the strain rate in the lateral direction $(m_{k} \partial / \partial x_{k})$ and then the tensor $\dot{\psi}_{s p}^{\text{inc}}$ has only one nontrivial component

$$
\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right) \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{i j}^{f}}{\partial x_{i} \partial x_{j}}. \tag{A5}
$$

The incompatibility is a function of the path $\varsigma_{k}=\omega_{k l}^{p} x_{l}-u_{k}^{\varsigma}$. Therefore, we simplify the incompatibility even further and express it as
$$
\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\frac{\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right)}{2} \omega_{i j}^{p} \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{f}}{\partial x_{i} \partial x_{j}}=\left(l_{n} l_{m}\right) \frac{\partial^{2} \dot{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{f}\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)}{\partial \varsigma_{s} \partial \varsigma_{n}} \tag{A6}
$$

As explained in Sec. III, the components $\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)$ are aligned with the directions $\left(s_{k}, n_{k}\right)$ of the plastic slippage $\varsigma_{s}=\varsigma_{k} s_{k}$ and $\varsigma_{n}=\varsigma_{k} n_{k}$. Our objective is to restore compatibility at macroscale [Eq. (8)] by homogenizing the material within the characteristic scale $\varsigma_{c}$ consisting of the length $l_{c}$ and the defect frequency $\Delta t_{c}^{-1}$. The incompatibility $\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)$ is expanded about the position $\left\{\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right\}$ with the use of Taylor series; the expression is truncated at the third term and integrated within the characteristic scale $\varsigma_{c}=\left(l_{c}-N v_{d} \Delta t_{c}\right)$. The macrocompatibility criterion becomes
$$
\dot{\psi}_{\text {inc }}^{\text {macro }} \cong\left[1+\frac{\xi_{c}^{2}}{24}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \varsigma_{s}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \varsigma_{n}^{2}}\right)\right] \dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{inc}}\left(\varsigma_{s}, \varsigma_{n}\right)=0. \quad(\mathrm{A} 7)
$$
