RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Stability of the $\mathrm{H}_{2}-(\mathrm{He}, \mathrm{Ne}, \mathrm{Ar})$ fluid mixtures

S.M. Osman*, R.N. Singh and I. Ali

Department of Physics, College of Science, Sultan Qaboos University, Oman

(Received 2 February 2008; final version received 3 March 2008)

A theory of mixtures based on a statistical mechanical perturbation scheme is used to compute the excess free energy of mixing, the excess entropy of mixing and the concentration fluctuations in the long wavelength limit as functions of composition $(c)$ over a wide range of temperature $(T=150$ to $350 \mathrm{~K})$ and pressure ( $p=10 \mathrm{MPa}$ to $10 \mathrm{GPa}$ ). This has been utilized to investigate the effects of $c, T$ and $p$ on the solubility of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ (the first element of the periodic table) to $\mathrm{He}, \mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{Ar}$ (the first three elements of the last group) and the thermodynamic stability of the mixture. The long-range correlations among the constituent species are included through the double Yukawa potential which acts as a perturbation to the hard sphere reference mixture. The non-additivity of the potentials of the constituent species is linked to the second virial coefficients which can be determined from the experimental data. Necessary corrections to the equation of state for dimerisation of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ molecule and quantum effects are included. At a given $T=150 \mathrm{~K}$ and $p=100 \mathrm{MPa}, \mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ mixture exhibits greater thermodynamic stability than $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$.

Keywords: fluids; mixtures; stability; free energy; solubility

## 1. Introduction

Fluid mixtures exhibit in many cases interesting characteristic phenomena. Of considerable importance is the mutual solubility of the mixture components, which depends on the composition $(c)$ of the constituent fluids at different pressure $(p)$ and temperature $(T)$. A knowledge of the physical conditions enumerating the specific $c, p$ and $T$ at which the mutual solubility is either 'very low' or 'high' is extremely useful to design separation equipment or to synthesize a stable phase, respectively. From a theoretical point of view, such a study is equally important not only to determine the intermolecular forces between unlike molecules from a knowledge of the forces between like molecules, but also to serve as a guide to understanding the properties of these mixtures at extreme conditions of $T$ and $p$, which are experimentally inaccessible. In this work, we investigate the solubility of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ (the first element of the first group of the periodic table) to the first three elements (He, Ne and Ar) of the last group using the theory of mixtures based on the statistical mechanical perturbation scheme. The physical properties, like the melting point, boiling point and density of these pure substances differ considerably from one another. Also, the intermolecular forces in $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ are quite different from those of $\mathrm{He}, \mathrm{Ne}$ and Ar. For the $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$ system, in particular, the masses of the constituent species are light resulting in significant quantum contributions at low temperatures. Such corrections are expected to carry over when forming mixtures at low temperatures giving significant effects [1].

Inter-atomic interactions play a crucial role in determining the phase stability of a mixture. These interactions are determined either empirically or numerically resulting in rather complicated expressions. In this work, however, we model the short-range repulsive interactions using a reference system made of hard spheres. The long-range attractive interaction among the constituent species, on the other hand, has been included via the double Yukawa (DY) potential which acts as a perturbation on the reference system. We shall see that the long-range correlations play a dominant role on the solubility. The advantage of using the DY potential is that it allows the variational integral equations of the energies to be determined analytically in terms of the Laplace transform of the radial distribution function. Also, one can readily calculate the DY potential parameters by fitting the DY potential to the empirical potentials, available in the literature for the elements $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ [2], He [2], Ne [3] and Ar [4,5].

In order to establish the potential of the mixture, we have used a simple scheme where the non-additivity (i.e. deviation from the Lorentz-Berthelot mixing rule) of the potentials has been linked to the second virial coefficient. The values of the second virial coefficients for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ are available experimentally [6] at different $T$, making it possible to express the non-additive energy parameter ($\alpha$) for these mixtures as a function of $T$. The dimerisation of the $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ molecule is treated as a hard convex body fluid for which the equation of state can be derived based on the scaled particle theory [7,8]. An improved [9–11] version of the equation of state for a hard sphere mixture is used which satisfies the thermodynamics consistency conditions vis-á-vis standardizing the fourth and fifth virial coefficients.

The stability of the binary mixture has been investigated as a function of composition over a wide range of $T$ (150 to 350 K) and $p$ (10 MPa to 10 GPa) through the concentration fluctuations, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)$. The latter has been widely used (for a review see [12]) to analyse the mixing behaviour and the phase separation in fluid mixtures, which can readily be derived in terms of the Gibbs free energy, $G$,

$$
S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)=N k_{\mathrm{B}} T\left(\frac{\partial^{2} G}{\partial c^{2}}\right)_{T, p, N}^{-1}. \tag{1}
$$

The significance of $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)$ emerges by the virtue of the curvature $\left(\partial^{2} G / \partial c^{2}\right)_{T, p, N}$ which exhibits characteristic features depending upon the physico-chemical conditions of the mixture. It may be noted that $\left(\partial^{2} G / \partial c^{2}\right)_{T, p, N}$ is also known as the stability function for the mixture originally introduced by Darken [13]. Also, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)$ is directly related to the chemical short-range order parameter [14].

The potentials describing the long-range correlations in the binary fluid mixtures ($\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$), including the $T$-dependence of the non-additivity energy parameter ($\alpha$) in terms of the second virial coefficients, are discussed in Section 2. Section 3 covers the formalism of the Helmholtz free energy and the equation of state. The results of the stability are presented in Section 4. We summarize and conclude in Section 5.

## 2. Potential energy functions for mixtures
### 2.1. Double Yukawa potential
The basis of the statistical mechanical perturbation theory [15] is to divide the pair potential, $v_{i j}(r)$, into a repulsive potential, $v_{i j}^{0}(r)$, and an attractive tail potential, $v_{i j}^{\mathrm{t}}(r)$,

$$
v_{i j}(r)=v_{i j}^{0}(r)+v_{i j}^{\mathrm{t}}(r) \tag{2}
$$

with

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{i j}^{0}(r) & =\infty, & & r<\sigma_{i j}^{0}, \\
& =0, & & r>\sigma_{i j}^{0}, \\
v_{i j}^{\mathrm{t}}(r) & =0, & & r<\sigma_{i j}^{0}, \\
& =v_{i j}^{\mathrm{DY}}(r), & & r>\sigma_{i j}^{0}.
\end{aligned} \tag{3}
$$

The repulsive part corresponds to the reference fluid which we take as a hard sphere mixture with the components $i j$ having hard sphere diameters of $\sigma_{i j}^{0}$. The softness [16] of the hard sphere reference due to the repulsive potential is considered by choosing the hard sphere diameter $\sigma_{i j}$ to have temperature dependence,

$$
\sigma_{i j}=\int_{0}^{\sigma_{i j}^{0}}\left\{1-\exp \left[-\frac{v_{i j}^{0}(r)}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}\right]\right\} \mathrm{d} r. \tag{4}
$$

$v_{i j}^{\mathrm{DY}}(r)$ in Equation (3) stands for the double Yukawa potential,

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{i j}^{\mathrm{DY}}(r)= & \epsilon_{i j} A_{i j} \frac{\sigma_{i j}^{0}}{r}\left\{\exp \left[-\lambda_{i j}\left(\frac{r}{\sigma_{i j}^{0}}-1\right)\right]\right. \\
& \left.-\exp \left[-v_{i j}\left(\frac{r}{\sigma_{i j}^{0}}-1\right)\right]\right\}.
\end{aligned} \tag{5}
$$

$\varepsilon_{i j}$ represents the potential's minimum and $\sigma_{i j}^{0}$ is the value of $r$ at which the potential is effectively zero. $A_{i j}$, $\lambda_{i j}$ and $v_{i j}$ are controlling parameters of the potential. By a good choice of these parameters, $v^{\mathrm{DY}}$ can be easily fitted to different empirical potentials of pure elements. We will see in the next section the the DY potential allows the variational integrals of the energies to be determined analytically in terms of the Laplace transform of the radial distribution function.

For a mixture, the cross-link parameters ($A_{12}, \sigma_{12}^{0}$, $\lambda_{12}$ and $v_{12}$) are assumed to be additive, i.e.

$$
\begin{gathered}
A_{12}=\frac{A_{11}+A_{22}}{2} ; \quad \sigma_{12}^{0}=\frac{\sigma_{11}^{0}+\sigma_{22}^{0}}{2}, \\
\lambda_{12}=\frac{\lambda_{11}+\lambda_{22}}{2} ; \quad v_{12}=\frac{v_{11}+v_{22}}{2}.
\end{gathered} \tag{6}
$$

The pairwise potential $\epsilon_{12}$ of the mixture is non-additive, i.e. it violates the Lorentz-Berthelot rule, requiring the parameter $\alpha$,

$$
\epsilon_{12}=\alpha\left(\epsilon_{11} \epsilon_{22}\right)^{1 / 2}, \tag{7}
$$

where $\alpha$ is often called the non-additive parameter of the potential. It represents a relative strength of the unlike pair well depths. The role of $\alpha$ in probing the phase separation and compound formation in binary mixtures, in general, for Lennard-Jones potential, has been discussed at length by Osman and Singh [17]. It has been clearly demonstrated there that for a mixture having no size effect $(\sigma_{11}=\sigma_{22})$ and no energy mismatch $(\epsilon_{11}=\epsilon_{22})$, $\alpha=1$ corresponds to the ideal mixing condition, $\alpha<1$ exhibits homocoordination or segregation and $\alpha>1$ results in heterocoordination or compound formation. Below we provide a simple scheme to evaluate the numerical values of the DY potential parameters for pure elements and their mixtures.

### 2.3. Determination of the non-additive energy parameter ($\alpha$) for a mixture through the virial coefficients

As regards to the DY potential for the binary mixture, the cross-link parameters ($A_{12}$, $\sigma_{12}^0$, $\lambda_{12}$ and $v_{12}$) have been determined from the values of pure elements via the additive rules (see Equation (6)). The foremost task is to determine the non-additive energy parameter $\alpha$ in Equation (7). In the literature it is usually treated as an adjustable parameter. Here, we suggest a scheme to determine $\alpha$ from the virial coefficients. To facilitate the evaluation we introduce the following reduced quantities:
$$
r^{*}=\frac{r}{\sigma_{11}} ; \quad T^{*}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{\epsilon_{11}} ; \quad \sigma_{i j}^{*}=\frac{\sigma_{i j}}{\sigma_{11}}. \qquad(8)
$$

We also introduce the dimensionless quantities $\gamma$ (the size ratio), $\beta$ (the energy ratio) and $\alpha$ (the non-additive energy parameter), as
$$
\gamma=\frac{\sigma_{22}}{\sigma_{11}} ; \quad \beta=\frac{\epsilon_{22}}{\epsilon_{11}} ; \quad \alpha=\frac{\epsilon_{12}}{\left(\epsilon_{11} \epsilon_{22}\right)^{1 / 2}}. \qquad(9)
$$

Therefore, Equation (5) can now be expressed as
$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{11}\left(r^{*}\right)= & \frac{v_{11}^{\mathrm{DY}}\left(r^{*}\right)}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T} \\
= & \frac{A_{11}}{T^{*} r^{*}}\left[\exp \left(-\lambda_{11}\left(r^{*}-1\right)\right)-\exp \left(-v_{11}\left(r^{*}-1\right)\right)\right], \\
& (10)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{22}\left(r^{*}\right)= & \frac{v_{22}^{\mathrm{DY}}\left(r^{*}\right)}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T} \\
= & \frac{A_{22} \beta \gamma}{T^{*} r^{*}}\left[\exp \left(-\lambda_{22}\left(r^{*} / \gamma-1\right)\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\exp \left(-v_{22}\left(r^{*} / \gamma-1\right)\right)\right], \quad(11)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{12}\left(r^{*}\right)= & \frac{v_{12}^{\mathrm{DY}}\left(r^{*}\right)}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T} \\
= & \frac{A_{12} \alpha(\beta \gamma)^{1 / 2}}{T^{*} r^{*}}\left[\exp \left(-\lambda_{12}\left(r^{*} / \gamma-1\right)\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\exp \left(-v_{12}\left(r^{*} / \gamma-1\right)\right)\right]. \quad(12)
\end{aligned}
$$

We further recall that the pairwise potential $\phi$ is related [6] to the second virial coefficient $B$ as
$$
B=\int_{0}^{\infty}[1-\exp (-\phi(r))] \mathrm{d} b_{0}(r),\qquad(13)
$$
where $r$ is the distance between molecular centres and $b_0$ stands for
$$
b_{0}=\frac{2}{3} \pi N_{\mathrm{A}} r^{3}. \qquad(14)
$$

$N_{\mathrm{A}}$ is Avogadro's number. We therefore write for the partial virial coefficients $B_{i j}$ as
$$
\begin{aligned}
B_{11} & =2 \pi N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3} \int_{0}^{\infty}\left[1-\exp \left(-\phi_{11}\left(r^{*}\right)\right)\right] r^{* 2} \mathrm{~d} r^{*} \\
& =N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3} B_{11}^{*}, \quad(15)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
B_{22} & =2 \pi N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3} \int_{0}^{\infty}\left[1-\exp \left(-\phi_{22}\left(r^{*}\right)\right)\right] r^{* 2} \mathrm{~d} r^{*} \\
& =N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3} B_{22}^{*}, \quad(16)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
B_{12} & =2 \pi N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3} \int_{0}^{\infty}\left[1-\exp \left(-\phi_{12}\left(r^{*}\right)\right)\right] r^{* 2} \mathrm{~d} r^{*} \\
& =N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3} B_{12}^{*}, \quad(17)
\end{aligned}
$$

with $B_{21}=B_{12}$. The reduced virial coefficients, $B_{i j}^{*}$, in the above equations stand for
$$
B_{i j}^{*}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[1-\exp \left(-\phi_{i j}\left(r^{*}\right)\right)\right] r^{* 2} \mathrm{~d} r^{*}.\qquad(18)
$$

The virial coefficient, $B_{i j}$, can now be used to define the excess value for the mixture as
$$
E_{\mathrm{xs}}=B_{12}-\frac{B_{11}+B_{22}}{2}, \qquad(19)
$$
or, equivalently,
$$
E_{\mathrm{xs}}=N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3}\left(B_{12}^{*}-\frac{B_{11}^{*}+B_{22}^{*}}{2}\right),\qquad(20)
$$
which, in reduced units, is given by
$$
E_{\mathrm{ex}}^{*}=\frac{E}{N_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{11}^{3}}. \qquad(21)
$$

## 3. Computational results for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-(\mathrm{He}, \mathrm{Ne}, \mathrm{Ar})$ fluid mixtures

### 3.1. Excess free energy of mixing as a function of composition, pressure and temperature

The Gibbs free energy $(G)$ plays a vital role in determining the thermodynamic stability (see Equation (1)) of the binary fluid mixtures. An explicit expression for $G$ can be written in terms of the Helmholtz free energy, $F$, and the pressure $p$,

$$
\frac{G}{N_{\mathrm{A}} k_{\mathrm{B}} T}=\frac{F}{N_{\mathrm{A}} k_{\mathrm{B}} T}+\frac{p}{\rho k_{\mathrm{B}} T}, \tag{24}
$$

where $\rho$ is the density. Making use of the equation of state, Ali et al. [18] have derived an expression of $F$ for binary mixtures,

$$
F=F^{\mathrm{id}}+F^{\mathrm{HCB}}+F^{\mathrm{t}}+F^{\mathrm{Q}}. \tag{25}
$$

$F^{\text {id }}$ stands for the Helmholtz free energy of an ideal mixture. $F^{\mathrm{HCB}}$ is the Helmoltz free energy for a hard convex body (HCB) fluid for which the relevant expression has been derived [18] from the hard sphere system based on the scaled particle theory [7,8]. The HCB fluid takes into account the dimerisation of the $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ molecule which is one of the constituent species of the mixtures we investigate in this work ($\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$). The size and shape of the larger molecules evolving from the assembly of elemental particles are included through an averaged molecular volume of the mixture molecule and the non-sphericity parameter (geometrical shape factor) of the mixture molecule.

$F^{\mathrm{t}}$ is the first order perturbation contribution which arises due to the long-range attractive potential. The latter is treated within the framework of the DY potential (see Equation (5)). The quantum effect, which is important for light elements and at low temperatures, has been included via $F^{\mathrm{Q}}$. The terms $F^{\mathrm{t}}$ and $F^{\mathrm{Q}}$ have been of central significance to the development of the theory of mixtures in recent years. They are explicitly expressed as

$$
\frac{F^{\mathrm{t}}}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}=\frac{\rho}{2 k_{\mathrm{B}} T} \sum_{i j} c_{i} c_{j} \int_{\sigma_{i j}^{0}}^{\infty} v_{i j}^{\mathrm{DY}}(r) g_{i j}^{\mathrm{HS}}(r) \bar{V}_{i j} 4 \pi r^{2} \mathrm{~d} r, \quad (26)
$$

$$
\frac{F^{\mathrm{Q}}}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}=\frac{h^{2} N \rho^{*}}{96 \pi^{2} k_{\mathrm{B}}^{2} T^{2}} \sum_{i j} \frac{c_{i} c_{j}}{m_{i j}} \int_{\sigma_{i j}}^{\infty} \nabla^{2} v_{i j}(r) g_{i j}^{\mathrm{HS}}(r) \bar{V}_{i j} 4 \pi r^{2} \mathrm{~d} r, \tag{27}
$$

where $\overline{V}_{ij}$ is the volume factor for the average molecular volume [1], which takes into account the molecular shape (this factor is unity for spherically symmetric molecules).

Analytical solution of the integrals of Equations (26) and (27) were obtained [1] for $v_{ij}^{\mathrm{DY}}(r)$ given in Equation (5). Since $v_{ij}^{\mathrm{DY}}(r)$ is in a double-Yukawa form, this enables the integrals in Equations (26) and (27) to be expressed as the Laplace transforms of $rg^{\mathrm{HS}}(r)$, which is analytically available [19]. In view of the energy Equation (25), the pressure $p$ becomes

$$
p=p^{\mathrm{id}}+p^{\mathrm{HCB}}+p^{\mathrm{t}}+p^{\mathrm{Q}}. \tag{28}
$$

The respective contributions, $p^{i}$, to pressure are obtained from the energy contributions $F^{i}$,

$$
\frac{\beta p^{i}}{\rho}=p \frac{\partial}{\partial \rho}\left(\frac{F^{i}}{N k_{\mathrm{B}} T}\right), \tag{29}
$$

where $i$ stands for id, HCB, t and Q terms.

Having determined Gibbs free energy $G$ from Equation (24), it is convenient to obtain excess free energy of mixing $G_{\mathrm{xs}}$ as

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_{\mathrm{xs}}(c, T, p) & =G(c, T, P)-\sum_{i} c_{i} G_{i}^{0}(c, T, p) \\
& -N k_{\mathrm{B}} T \sum_{i} c_{i} \ln c_{i}, \tag{30}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $G_{i}^{0}[=G(c_{j} \to 0)]$ are the Gibbs energy of the constituent species $i$. An expression for $G_{i}^{0}$ for various components is obtained from the expressions above by setting $c_{i}$ or $c_{j}$ tending to zero. The inputs of Tables 1 and 2 are used to compute $G_{\mathrm{xs}}$ for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$, $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ mixtures as a function of composition for a given temperature and pressure. The computed results for $T=150 \mathrm{~K}$ and $p=1.0 \mathrm{GPa}$ are plotted in Figure 3. It can be seen that the $G_{\mathrm{xs}}$ of the three mixtures differs substantially from one another for a given $T$ and $p$. $|G_{\mathrm{xs}}|$ for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$ mixture is much greater than that of $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$. Unlike $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$, $G_{\mathrm{xs}}$ for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ are quite asymmetric about $c_{\mathrm{H}_{2}}=0.5$. Towards the rich end of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$, $G_{\mathrm{xs}}$ for both $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ are positive whereas for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$ it stays negative at all compositions. It should be noted that neither experimental nor simulations results are available for these mixtures, hence, our results could not be compared. Nevertheless, the current results are relevant to delineate the effects of $c$, $p$ and $T$ on the mixtures.

### 3.2. Effect of $T$ and $p$ on excess entropy of mixing
The excess entropy of mixing, $S_{\mathrm{xs}}$, is obtained from

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\mathrm{xs}}(c, T, p)=S(c, T, p)-\sum c_{i} S_{i}^{0}(T, p)+N k_{\mathrm{B}} \sum c_{i} \ln c_{i},
\tag{31}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $S(c, T, p)$ is the entropy of the mixture at a given $c, T$ and $p$; $S_{i}^{0}(T, p)$ are the entropy of constituent species at the same $T$ and $p$. The values of the various contributions for entropy are obtained by taking the temperature derivative of

the Gibbs free energy at a given composition and pressure,
$$
\begin{aligned}
S & =-\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_{p} \\
& =-\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left(F^{\mathrm{id}}+F^{\mathrm{HCB}}+F^{\mathrm{t}}+F^{\mathrm{Q}}\right)_{p}.
\end{aligned} \quad (32)
$$

The evaluation of the different terms in Equation (32) involve the hard sphere diameters $\sigma_{ij}$ and the non-additive energy parameter $\alpha$. Both $\sigma_{ij}$ and $\alpha$ are temperature dependent and hence contribute to the entropy.

The computed values of $S_{\mathrm{xs}}$ for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}, \mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ mixtures are plotted in Figure 5(a) as a function of composition for a given $p=1.0$ GPa and $T=150$ K. Under this condition of $p$ and $T$, $S_{\mathrm{xs}}$ for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}$ is large negative in comparison to values for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$. To be specific, $S_{\mathrm{xs}} \approx-0.8 R,-0.2 R$ and $-0.09 R$, respectively, for $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{He}, \mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}-\mathrm{Ar}$

### 3.3. Thermodynamic stability for different range of $T$ and $p$

The values of Gibbs free energy computed in Section 3.1 as a function of composition, temperature and pressure are of considerable significance to

examine the thermodynamic stability of the phase in a binary fluid mixture. Bhatia and Thornton [20] explicitly showed that $(\partial^2 G_M/\partial c^2)$ is directly related to the long wavelength limit $(q \to 0)$ of the partial concentration–concentration structure factor,

$$
S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)=N\left\langle(\Delta c)^{2}\right\rangle=N k_{\mathrm{B}} T\left(\frac{\partial^{2} G_{M}}{\partial c^{2}}\right)_{T, p, N}^{-1}, \quad (33)
$$

where $\langle(\Delta c)^2\rangle$ represents the mean square fluctuations in concentration. $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)$, widely known as concentration fluctuations, diverges strongly as one approaches the criticality: $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0) \to \infty$ as $c \to c_{\mathrm{c}}$ and $T \to T_{\mathrm{c}}$. The points of inflection of $G_M - c$ variation are often used to evaluate the critical composition $(c_{\mathrm{c}})$ and the critical temperature $(T_{\mathrm{c}})$, i.e.

$$
\left(\frac{\partial^{2} G_{M}}{\partial c^{2}}\right)_{c=c_{\mathrm{c}}}=\left(\frac{\partial^{3} G_{M}}{\partial c^{3}}\right)_{c=c_{\mathrm{c}}}=0, \quad T=T_{\mathrm{c}}. \quad (34)
$$

For a given $c$, $T$ and $p$ the possibilities are: (i) $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)=c_1 c_2=S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{\mathrm{id}}(0)$, meaning an ideal solution behaviour for which heat of mixing and the excess entropy of mixing are identically zero; (ii) $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0) \gg S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{\mathrm{id}}(0)$, signifying a tendency of segregation (like atoms tend to pair as nearest neighbours); and (iii) $S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0) \ll S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{\mathrm{id}}(0)$, indicating hetero-coordination (unlike atoms tend to pair as nearest neighbours).

The computed values of $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)[=S_{\mathrm{cc}}(0)/S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{\mathrm{id}}(0)]$ at $p=1.0\,\mathrm{GPa}$ and $T=150\,\mathrm{K}$ as a function of composition for $\mathrm{H_2-He}$, $\mathrm{H_2-Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ mixtures are depicted in Figure 6. $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ are found to be quite different for the three mixtures. For equiatomic composition $(c_{H_2}=0.5)$, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ for the $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ mixture is the lowest in comparison to the $\mathrm{H_2-He}$ and $\mathrm{H_2-Ne}$ mixtures. The $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ mixture exhibits larger thermodynamic stability which extends over a wide range of composition. On the other hand, for smaller concentration of $c_{H_2}$, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ for $\mathrm{H_2-Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ even tend to values larger than 1, which indicates a segregation tendency starts setting in. The larger than 1 value of $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ is more pronounced in the $\mathrm{H_2-Ne}$ mixture in the vicinity of $c_{H_2}=0.05$.

To investigate exclusively the impact of $p$ and $T$ on $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$, we have further considered the equiatomic composition of $\mathrm{H_2-He}$, $\mathrm{H_2-Ne}$ and $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ mixtures. The dependence of $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ on pressure is shown in Figures 7(a)–(c). $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ is found to decrease with increasing pressure. At very high pressure, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ tend to flatten. The gradient $(\partial S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)/\partial p)_T$ is quite noticeable in all three mixtures at relatively low pressure. However in the same range of $T$ and $p$, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ of $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ is quite in contrast to other two mixtures.

For example, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ increases steeply and becomes greater than 1.0 at smaller pressures.

Temperature dependence of $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$, Figures 8(a)–(c), is also very interesting. The impact is different in the hetero-coordinated region compared to that in the segregation region. In the hetero-coordinated region $(S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)<1.0)$, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ decreases with decreasing temperature. But in the segregating region, the effect of $T$ and $p$ on $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ is different. For example, $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ increases with decreasing temperature at a given pressure. The effect is distinct in the case of the $\mathrm{H_2-Ar}$ mixture. The increase of pressure, also, enhances the segregation for the segregating system (see the cross-over of $S_{\mathrm{cc}}^{*}(0)$ in the segregating region). Such an anomalous effect was also observed by Osman and Singh [17] where they had applied an entirely different approach.
