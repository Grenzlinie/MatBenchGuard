# Study of electrooxidation of binary electrolytic deposit palladium-bismuth deposited on graphite electrode surface

Zhamilya K. Sabitova $^{1}$ · Yulia A. Oskina $^{1}$ · Nina A. Kolpakova $^{1}$

Received: 21 April 2018 / Revised: 16 August 2018 / Accepted: 20 August 2018
(C) Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2018

## Abstract
The phase structure of palladium-bismuth binary electrolytic deposit was studied. The electrodeposition of deposit components on the surface of a graphite electrode (GE) was carried out in situ. On current-voltage curves, the peak of bismuth electrooxidation, the peak of palladium electrooxidation and an additional peak at 0.15 V vs. Ag/AgCl are observed. The peak current at 0.15 V depends both on the concentration of palladium(II) ions and on that of bismuth(III) ions. The thermodynamic theory of alloys in the approximation of the theory of regular solutions was used for calculations. The mixing heat of the binary alloy components and the equilibrium potential of bismuth in the bismuth-palladium alloy are calculated. The calculated equilibrium potential of bismuth in an alloy with palladium is 0.12 V vs. Ag/AgCl which corresponds to the $Bi_{2}Pd$ intermetallic compound (IMC). Investigation of the surface of a GE with a palladium-bismuth deposit with use of scanning electron microscope showed that the electrolytic deposit contains bismuth, palladium and the $Bi_{2}Pd$ IMC. Peak at the potential of plus 0.15 V vs. Ag/AgCl is due to selective bismuth electrooxidation from the $Bi_{2}Pd$ IMC.

Keywords Modified electrode · Bismuth-palladium deposit · Regular solution · Mixing heat of components · Stripping voltammetry

## Introduction
Carbon-containing electrodes in the form of carbon composites and various carbon materials, which are used in stripping voltammetry, are insensitive because of their small surface. In order to increase the surface of electrodes, they are modified with metals [1, 2].

The most convenient method of modifying electrodes with metals is in situ, when the component and the modifier are simultaneously deposited on the surface of a carbon-containing electrode from the analysed solution. Due to this treatment, the electrode acquires additional functionality which can increase its reactivity and selectivity.

When two or more metals are deposited on the graphite electrode (GE) surface, the current-voltage curve of the anodic oxidation of the deposit can significantly differ from the current-voltage electrooxidation curves of individual components. Interaction of components in the electrolytic deposit can lead to a shift in the peak potentials, their disappearance and the appearance of additional anodic peaks [3, 4]. It is connected with change in the phase composition of the electrolytic deposit which could be rather diverse. Mechanical mixtures, solid solutions of variable composition or intermetallic compounds (IMCs) can be formed on the electrode surface. Under conditions of electrodeposition, intermetallic compounds as well as solid solutions are formed both in equilibrium and in a metastable state [3]. The electrooxidation of components from the electrolytic deposit can occur both uniformly and selectively [5]. During the electrolysis, one of the IMCs can be formed on the electrode surface. In the process of selective electrooxidation of one of the alloy components, an IMC of another composition can be formed on the electrode [6], which will result in an additional anodic peak on the current-voltage curve.

The study of the phase composition of electrolytic deposits with a nanoscale structure (4 to 10 atomic layers) is quite a complex task. A promising way to address the challenge adequately is to use electrochemical methods of analysis in combination with the study of the deposit on the electrode surface by means of electron spectroscopy. There are works which describe palladium-bismuth inter- metallic compounds, including $PdBi_{2}$. The compounds are useful as catalysts for converting aldehydes and alcohols into carboxylic esters [7]. Also, there are known works on the use of intermetallic compounds as catalysts for chemical reactions and catalytic systems [8]. However, determination of the com- position of IMC of bismuth and palladium using thermody- namic calculations is not presented in the literature.

The literature describes the processes of electrooxidation of binary electrolytic deposits of palladium with copper and mer- cury [9, 10]. However, the nature of additional anodic peaks was not considered in these works.

The purpose of the research was to investigate the process of electrooxidation of the binary electrolytic palladium-bis- muth deposit deposited on the surface of a GE at the prelim- inary electrolysis stage and to establish the phase nature of additional anodic peak observed on the current-voltage curve of electrooxidation of a palladium-bismuth deposit as well as the possibility of using it for determining the palladium(II) ions by stripping voltammetry.

In general, to solve such a problem is quite difficult. Usually, thermodynamic description of the electrooxidation process of a component from an alloy is carried out in the approximation of regular solution theory [11, 12]. The term "regular solution" was introduced by Hildenbrand to charac- terise systems in which a randomly distributed distribution of components with entropy of mixing equal to ideal entropy is observed [13]. The interatomic interaction of components in these solutions, the consequence of which is $\Delta \overline{H}_{m} \neq 0$, does not change the atomic arrangement in the alloy; therefore, $\Delta \overline{S}_{m}=0$. For regular solutions, $\Delta \overline{G}_{m} \cong \Delta H_{m}$. This theory is applicable to IMCs, since the location of atoms in the crystal lattice is constant. A large number of metal alloys for which the theory of regular solutions is applicable are known [13-15].

### Experimental

In the course of the work, a TA-4 voltammetric analyser with a three-electrode cell (LLC Tom'Analit, Tomsk, Russia) was used. A working electrode was a GE impregnated with paraf- fin and low-pressure polyethylene prepared in accordance with [16]; an auxiliary electrode and a reference electrode were a saturated silver/silver chloride electrodes filled with1 M KCI solution. Deaeration of solutions was not carried out. Stirring of solution during electrolysis was carried out by the analyser and was done automatically by the vibration of a working electrode throughout the experiment.

Stock solutions used in research were prepared in 1 M HCI by dilution using state standard samples (Krasnoyarsk). The concentration of Pd(II) ions was from 0.4 to $15 mg L^{-1}$ . The concentration of Bi(III) ions was from 2 to $15 mg L^{-1}$ .

The electrodeposition of palladium-bismuth binary deposit was carried out on the surface of a GE in situ at the potential of minus 0.8 V minus 1.0 V for 60/120 s from 1 M HCI solutions containing bismuth(III) and palladium(II) ions. The electrooxidation of deposits was carried out at linear changes in the potential $(v=60 mV s^{-1})$ window from minus 0.4 V to plus 1.0 V.

Binary electrolytic bismuth-palladium deposits on a GE surface have been studied by means of an S-3400 N Hitachi scanning microscope at the resolution of 3 nm coupled with a Bruker XFlash 4010 Silicon Drift Detector.

The dependence of the bismuth electrooxidation currents ($E_{\mathrm{pa}}=-0.3$ V) and electrooxidation currents of deposit observed at $E_{\mathrm{pa}}=0.15$ V on the concentration of palladium(II) ions in the solution are presented in Fig. 3. It is seen from the figure that, as the palladium concentration increases in the deposit and the bismuth(III) ion concentration is constant in the solution, the peak current at a potential of 0.15 V increases to a certain limit and the peak current of bismuth electrooxidation decreases.

The experimental data suggest that the peak at the potential of 0.15 V is associated with the process of selective bismuth electrooxidation from the intermetallic compound with palladium. According to the phase diagram, palladium and bismuth can form the following IMCs: $\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Pd}$, $\mathrm{BiPd}$, $\mathrm{BiPd}_{3}$ and solid solutions based on palladium [19].

We have suggested that the peak at the potential of 0.15 V is associated with the selective electrooxidation of bismuth from an IMC with palladium. The fact that the peak at the potential of 0.15 V is associated with selective electrooxidation of bismuth from an IMC with palladium indicates that the total area under the peaks of bismuth electrooxidation from a GE surface and bismuth deposits from the surface of the electrodeposited palladium is equal to the area under the peak of bismuth electrooxidation from a GE surface without palladium.

To establish the phase composition of the deposit formed on the surface of palladium centres, the equilibrium potential of bismuth electrooxidation from the bismuth–palladium electrolytic alloy was calculated. Since the formation of an alloy on the surface of palladium centres such as a solid solution or an IMC is associated with a change in the free energy of the components due to the appearance of the heat of mixing, their equilibrium potentials should shift to the positive region by $\Delta G/zF$ V. The equation relating the change in the equilibrium potential of the alloy of the electronegative component from the IMC ($\Delta E$) to the composition of the IMC ($X_{\mathrm{i}}$) was obtained based on the following considerations. It is known from the literature data [3] that the change in the partial Gibbs energy in the formation of a solid solution or IMC in the approximation of the theory of regular solutions [13] is equal to:

$$
\Delta \overline{G}_{\mathrm{im}}=R T \ln X_{\mathrm{i}}-(1-X_{\mathrm{i}})^{2} \Delta H_{\mathrm{m}} \tag{1}
$$

![](./images/812999867042365440_7.jpg)

where $\Delta H_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the integral mixing heat of the compounds forming the solid solution or IMC and $X_{\mathrm{i}}$ is the mole fraction of the component in the alloy. Then the displacement of the equilibrium potential of bismuth during its electrooxidation from alloy when a solid solution or IMC forms on electrode can be calculated by the ratio:

$$
\Delta E=E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}}^{\ominus}-E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Pd}}^{\ominus}=\frac{R T}{z F} \ln X_{\mathrm{Bi}}-\frac{\left(1-X_{\mathrm{Bi}}\right)^{2}}{z F} \Delta H_{\mathrm{m}}(2)
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}}^{\ominus}$ is the equilibrium potential of the electronegative component in the pure phase, $E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Pd}}^{\ominus}$ is the equilibrium potential of the electronegative component in the metal alloy, and $X_{\mathrm{Bi}}$ is the mole fraction of the electronegative component in the palladium-bismuth alloy.

The equilibrium potential of bismuth/bismuth-palladium electrode shall be calculated by the formula:

$$
E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Pd}}^{\ominus}=E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}}^{\ominus}-\Delta E \tag{3}
$$

Since the potential of the electrooxidation of the additional peak $(E_{\mathrm{pa}}=0.15 \mathrm{~V})$ does not change when the contents of the components in the alloy change, it can be assumed that the selective electrooxidation of bismuth occurs from the IMC with palladium. The mole fractions of bismuth in IMCs of different composition are equal to 0.67 in $\mathrm{Bi}_{2} \mathrm{Pd}, 0.5$ in $\mathrm{BiPd}$ and 0.25 in $\mathrm{BiPd}_{3}$.

The mixing heats of the compounds during the formation of bismuth-palladium IMC with a different mole fraction of bismuth is estimated from the "pair interaction" model [13]:

$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{m}}=z_{\mathrm{Bi}} \cdot n_{\mathrm{Bi}} \cdot \frac{r_{\mathrm{Bi}}}{r_{\mathrm{Pd}}}\left[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Pd}}-\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pd}-\mathrm{Pd}}}{2}\right]-z_{\mathrm{Bi}} \cdot n_{\mathrm{Bi}} \cdot \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Bi}}}{2} \tag{4}
$$

where $r_{\mathrm{Bi}}=1.82 \AA$ and $r_{\mathrm{Pd}}=1.37 \AA$ are the metallic radii [22], $z_{\mathrm{Bi}}=5$ is the coordination number of single bonds of bismuth atoms on the surface of the alloy with a face-centred cubic crystalline structure [12], $n$ is the number of bismuth atoms in the alloy, and $\varepsilon$ is the energy of rupture of chemical bonds [23]: $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pd}-\mathrm{Pd}}=66416 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1} ; \varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Bi}}=200406 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$.

The mixing heats are directly related to the energy of intermolecular interactions. Each pair of chemical compounds has a certain energy that will be released/absorbed when mixing pure substances. The binding energy of bismuth-palladium in the literature is absent; therefore, it was calculated from the Pauling equation [24] which takes into account the ionicity of the bond:

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Pd}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bi}-\mathrm{Bi}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Pd}-\mathrm{Pd}}\right)+100\left(\chi_{\mathrm{Bi}}-\chi_{\mathrm{Pd}}\right)^{2}-6.5\left(\chi_{\mathrm{Bi}}-\chi_{\mathrm{Pd}}\right)^{4} \tag{5}
$$

The palladium-bismuth binding energy calculated from Eq. (5) is $133,420 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$. Data on the mixing heats of compounds for various IMCs of bismuth with palladium calculated from Eq. (3) is presented in Table 1.

According to the literature, bismuth in chloride electrolytes is in the form of BiOCl complexes. The standard equilibrium potential of the bismuth electrode corresponding to the process $\mathrm{BiOCl}+2 \mathrm{H}^{+}+3 \mathrm{e} \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{Bi}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}+\mathrm{Cl}^{-}$is $0.06 \mathrm{~V}$ in relation to a silver chloride electrode [25]. The equilibrium potential of the bismuth electrode is calculated using the Nernst formula:

$$
E_{\mathrm{Bi}^{3+} / \mathrm{Bi}}^{\ominus}=0.06+\frac{0.059}{3} \lg \frac{a_{\mathrm{BiOCl}} \times c_{\mathrm{H}^{+}}^{2} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{H}^{+}}^{2}}{c_{\mathrm{Cl}^{-}} \times \gamma_{\mathrm{Cl}^{-}}} \tag{6}
$$

Taking into account that the potential of selective electrooxidation of bismuth from IMC with palladium should have some overvoltage for dissolution, the closest to the experimentally observed peak at 0.15 V is the equilibrium potential of the Bi₂Pd alloy, which is equal to 0.12 V. Considering the data obtained, it can be assumed that the peak observed at the potential of 0.15 V is the peak of selective bismuth electrooxidation from Bi₂Pd IMC.

Bismuth–palladium deposit on a GE surface has been studied by means of an S-3400 N Hitachi scanning microscope jointly with the Bruker XFlash 4010 power drift detector (Fig. 5a, b).

Figure 5a shows the morphology of bismuth–palladium deposits from the surface of a GE of 4.8 nm and up to 20.7 nm in size. Figure 5b shows that ratio of the percentage contents of bismuth to palladium is 1.83, which may indicate the presence of the composition Bi₂Pd on the electrode surface.

Scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy showed that palladium–bismuth deposits have a spherical shape and some images have glowing edges and a centre that differs in its glow from the main mass. The deposit represents the palladium core and shell of bismuth. Electrolytic deposits of a similar type are described in [26–31].

Since the peak at the potential of 0.15 V depends on the concentration of palladium(II) ions at a constant bismuth concentration, it can be used as an analytical signal for determining the palladium content with stripping voltammetry along with the peak of electrooxidation of palladium from GE surface that occurs at the potential of plus 0.4 V.

To assess the effectiveness of using a GE and a bismuth-modified GE, the slope of the calibration line (sensitivity of the method) was used to determine palladium using stripping voltammetry.

The calibration dependences of currents’ changes of the anodic peaks for the palladium electrooxidation from the surface of a GE (curve 1), and currents of selective electrooxidation of bismuth from the Bi₂Pd IMC from the bismuth-modified GE surface (curve 2) on the concentration of palladium(II) ions in solution are presented in Fig. 6.

&= 1 \times 10^{-9} \mathrm{mol} \mathrm{L}^{-1}
\end{aligned} \tag{7}
$$

where $S_{bl}$ is the standard deviation of the blank test; $\text{tg}\theta$ is the sensitivity coefficient of the calibration line (Fig. 6, curve 2). The detection limit Pd, calculated from the peak of the palladium electrooxidation from the surface of a GE (Fig. 6, curve 1) is $0.5 \times 10^{-9} \text{molL}^{-1}$.

The data obtained indicate that the ratio of the slope of the calibration line (1) and (2) is close to two; i.e., the sensitivity of the determining palladium from the peak of selective electrooxidation of bismuth from the $\text{Bi}_2\text{Pd}$ IMC is twice as great as at the peak of the electrooxidation of palladium from GE surface. This is an additional confirmation that the peak at the potential of plus 0.15 V is the peak of selective electrooxidation of bismuth from the $\text{Bi}_2\text{Pd}$ IMC.

## Conclusions
1.  The process of electrooxidation of electrolytic palladium–bismuth deposits was studied. It is shown that during the electrooxidation of an electrolytic palladium–bismuth deposit, an additional peak at 0.15 V appears on the current–voltage curve; the current at this peak depends both on the concentration of palladium(II) ions and on the concentration of bismuth(III) ions.

2.  The equilibrium potential of $\text{Bi}^{3+}/\text{Bi–Pd}$ is calculated in the approximation of regular solution theory and shows the peak at the potential of 0.15 V can be due to the selective electrooxidation of bismuth from the $\text{Bi}_2\text{Pd}$ IMC.

3.  The phase nature of the peak at the potential of plus 0.15 is supported by data obtained using scanning electron microscopy and the calibration dependences for palladium determination at the palladium peak and the peak of selective electrooxidation of bismuth from the $\text{Bi}_2\text{Pd}$ IMC.

4.  The possibility of determining palladium using stripping voltammetry from the peak of palladium electrooxidation is shown both from the surface of unmodified GE ($E_{\text{pa}} =$ 0.4 V) and from the selective electrooxidation peak of bismuth from the $\text{Bi}_2\text{Pd}$ IMC ($E_{\text{pa}} =$0.15 V). The sensitivity of the determination of palladium from the peak of selective electrooxidation of bismuth from the $\text{Bi}_2\text{Pd}$ IMC is twice as great as that at the peak of electrooxidation of palladium from the surface of the GE due to the composition of IMC formed on the surface of the GE at the preliminary stage of electrolysis.
