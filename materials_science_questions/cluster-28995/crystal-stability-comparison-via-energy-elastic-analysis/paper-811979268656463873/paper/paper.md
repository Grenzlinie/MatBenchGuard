# Electron Structure of Atoms of the 3d Transition Metals

By
F. M. GALPERIN

Electron structures (configurations) of the atoms of all the 3d transition metals are calculated. It is shown that the regularities to which the electron configurations of the atom of the crystalline elements (3d metals) follow are much similar to those ones for free atoms of the Periodic Table of Elements.

В данной работе вычисляются электронные структуры (конфигурации) атомов всех переходных 3d-металлов. Показано, что закономерности, кото-рым следуют электронные конфигурации атомов кристаллических элементов (3d-металлов) во многом схожи с закономерностями для свободных атомов Периодической таблицы элементов.

## 1. Some Regularities of Mendeleev's Periodic Table (MPT)

If we consider the first long transition period of MPT, as shown in Table 1, we see that the free atoms in this period have an external filled (with a pair of electrons with antiparallel spins) 4s shell. We shall call these atoms normal free atoms (the property "free" is provided by the filled 4s shell just as an inert element atom is characterized by the filled electron shell).

However, anomalous free chromium and copper atoms, having only one un-paired (with non-compensated spin) 4s electron, are characteristic for the period. It is known that an atom of a chemical compound, for example, gets in touch with its neighbours in the crystalline lattice only due to fact that it has unpaired electrons. Thus, a free atom with a hidden (potentially possible) bond would rather be associated with the anomalous chromium atom than a free atom. Indeed, as our calculation shows, a metallic chromium atom has the same anomalous electron configuration EC3d⁵4s¹ (see Table 2). In the anomalous free chromium atom the total number of the 3d electrons is naturally higher (five) than in the normal free atom (EC 3d⁴4s²). We assume by analogy that in the atom of any 3d transition metal the total number of the 3d electrons is higher than in the normal free atom (see Table 2).

Anomalous copper atoms (3d¹⁰4s¹) and those of chromium (3d⁵4s¹) are similar in many ways. With EC 3d⁹4s² the copper itself would have been a transition element. Thus, chromium and copper are characterized by anomalous electron configurations. Scandium is an exception and it is anomalous in another respect: the 3d electron in a free atom is more separated from the nucleus than in the other atoms of the period (the effective radius of the 3d electrons $r$ (3d) has a maximum value, see Table 1). Due to this fact the filled 3s shell is located within the unfilled 3d shell of the atom but not out of it. As calculations of Watson and Freeman [1] show this leads to the fact that the contribution of the 3s shell to the hyperfine field on the nucleus of the free atom of scandium is negative (it is positive in the case of other elements of the period).

Marshall [2] analysing the exceptional pecularity of the free scandium atom noticed that it would be observed, as a rule, in atoms of all 3d transition metals. We assumed before, for another reasons, that the 3d electron of the atom of any 3d transition metal is considerably more separated from the nucleus than in a free atom (extension of the charge density of the 3d electrons of a metal atom). This especially ensures the arrangement of the filled 3s shell of the atom of any 3d transition metal within its 3d region and, consequently, its negative contri- bution to the hyperfine field on the nucleus. On the basis of this assumption in good agreement with the experiment we have calculated a number of the most important properties of atoms of the 3d transition elements in the crystalline state. These properties like corresponding properties of free atoms of MPT have a periodic character. This is not surprising since, as the examples show, the main features of the atomic properties have been determined with MPT regu- larities taken into consideration. By analogy the Periodic Table of Elements in the crystalline state may be indicated as PTEC.

It is seen from the Table 1 that the periodic regularity in MPT manifests itself specifically in the fact that the numbers of the 3d electrons in any two free atoms which are neighbours in the period differ by 1. Thus proceeding from the regularity and knowing the number of the 3d electrons in a free atom one may determine this number for the rest atoms of the period. In the case of elemental crystals, as our calculations show, the total numbers of the 3d elec- trons in any two atoms which are neighbours in the period of PTEC differ by 0.9 and the numbers of the magnetic 3d electrons (magnetic moments) of atoms differ by $1.1^{1)}$.

We believe that this pecularity of the periodic regularity in the crystalline properties discussed, is connected with the partial filling of the 3d shells of free and bound (in the lattice) atoms (transition elements). To this partial filling and to the atomic properties caused by it, according to Hartree [3] the coefficient $1/l\ (2l + 1) = 0.1\ (l = 2$ for the 3d electrons) corresponds. The latter, as the other hidden possibilities in MPT, manifests itself only due to the extension of the 3d charge density in the atoms of the crystalline elements. Therefore, the above-discussed difference between the total numbers of the 3d electrons and also between the numerical values of the atomic magnetic moments of the two neighbouring atoms in PTEC shows the periodic regularities $1 - 1/l\ (2l + 1) == 0.9$ and $1 + 1/l\ (2l + 1) = 1.1$, respectively. $^{1)}$

## 2. Extension of the 3d Charge Density of the 3d Metal Atom

It was assumed above that in the atom of any 3d transition metal the 3d electron (or better, the maximum density of its charge) is more distant from the nucleus than in a free atom (extension of the 3d charge density [4 to 6]). This fundamental phenomenon was confirmed theoretically by other authors [7, 8] for metallic iron and experimentally for the most 3d metal [9].

We shall denote the effective radius (the radius of the sphere on whose surface the distribution probability density is maximum) of the 3d electrons of a metal atom and that of a free atom as $R$ and $r(3d)$, respectively. $r(3d)$ and the effective radius of the 4s electrons of a free atom $r(4s)$ are calculated by Slater [10] and

### 3. Electron Configuration of the Atom (Ion) Stabilized by the Crystal Field

Now we shall consider the electron configurations $3 \mathrm{~d}^{8}$ and $3 \mathrm{~d}^{3}$ stabilized by the crystal field of cubic symmetry (stabilization energy $E_{\text {stab }}=-1.2 \Delta$, where $\Delta$ is the crystal field splitting parameter).

It is well known that EC $3 \mathrm{~d}^{8}$ and $3 \mathrm{~d}^{10-8}=3 \mathrm{~d}^{2}$ correspond to the mutually changed splitting schemes of the terms. EC $3 \mathrm{~d}^{3}$ and $3 \mathrm{~d}^{5-3}=3 \mathrm{~d}^{2}$ have also mutually changed splitting schemes of the terms. In this sense the configurations $3 \mathrm{~d}^{8}$ and $3 \mathrm{~d}^{3}$ are analogous. The first one is taken into consideration in the difference $C-8$ occurring in the relationship (1). Thus the relationship (1) makes it possible to calculate the effective radius for the 3d shell stabilized by the crystal field. The latter contains 4s electrons which number is $(C-8)$ for $C \geqq 7$ and $(C-3)$ for $C \leqq 6$.

### 5. Ferromagnetic Criterion
The existence of an electron configuration stabilized by the crystal field $(3 \mathrm{~d}^{8}$ in the case of $C \geqq 7$ and $3 \mathrm{~d}^{3}$ in the case of $C \leqq 6)$ and the spatial criterion $(R < r_{\mathrm{s}})$ are considered to be the general ferromagnetism criterion. In the principal case $(C \geqq 7)$ the above-mentioned spatial criterion is realized easier since the effective radius $R$ is rather small whereas in the second case $(C \leqq 6)$ it is large (see Table 3). The metallic scandium and titanium crystallize in the h.c.p. lattice and therefore they are non-ferromagnetic (paramagnetic). In a simple cube (s.c.) with a large lattice constant, entering the f.c.c. lattice as one of the sub-lattices, these metals, as we think, should display ferromagnetism. Such a possibility has been discussed formerly in the case of scandium [11, 12].

### 6. The Effective Nuclear Charge Acting on the 3d Electrons
According to Hartree [3] the effective nuclear charge acting on the 3d electron is equal to $9 / r_{\max }$, where $r_{\max }$ is the distance of the $3 \mathrm{~d}$ charge density from the nucleus (9 is the square of the main quantum number of the $3 \mathrm{~d}$ electrons).

It was mentioned above that Slater has investigated the case of $r_{\max }=r(3 \mathrm{~d})$ and we have investigated the case of $r_{\max }=R$. So the discussed nuclear charge for a free atom and for a neutral atom (ion) of a metal are expressed by the relationships, respectively,
$$
z(3 \mathrm{~d})=\frac{9}{r(3 \mathrm{~d})}, \quad Z(3 \mathrm{~d})=\frac{9}{R}. \tag{2}
$$

It follows from (2) that in a metal $Z(3 \mathrm{~d}) < z(3 \mathrm{~d})$. From this reason one can expect that some part of the $3 \mathrm{~d}$ electrons of the neutral atom (ion) associated with the value $Z(3 \mathrm{~d})$ is collectivized with some of them being transferred to the conduction band and some electrons of the latter being transferred to the mag- netic $3 \mathrm{~d}$ shell of the atom. As a result, the hybridized $3 \mathrm{~d}-4 \mathrm{~s}-4 \mathrm{p}$ conduction electrons and hybridized $3 \mathrm{~d}-4 \mathrm{~s}-4 \mathrm{p}$ magnetic electrons are formed.

### 7. Inter-electronic Interactions and the Conception of the Neutral Atom (Ion) in a Metal
Atom (I) has been discussed above. Now we shall consider atom (II). Atom (II) in the cell interacts with its nearest neighbours in the lattice. Denote the number and the distance between them as $n_{1}$ and $2 r_{1}$, respectively. We shall consider f.c.c. Ni, for example. In this case $r_{1} > R$ (see Table 3). If we take along these distances the effective radii $R$ for the $3 \mathrm{~d}$ electrons of the neighbouring atoms, we see that the distance between these electrons is $2(r_{1}-R)$. For building up the Wigner-Seitz cell, as it is known, half of the mentioned inter- atomic distance is taken. That is the reason why half of the above-mentioned inter-electronic distance is taken further on, i.e. $r_{1}-R$, where $r_{1}$ is the distance from the nucleus to the surface of the cell mentioned. Thus taking into con- sideration the conception of the neutral atom in a metal we find the correct

numerical values of the inter-electronic distances $r_1 - R$ that makes it possible to calculate precisely an exchange and Coulomb inter-electronic interactions in metals [13].

Further, knowing the inter-electronic distance $(r_{ns} - R)$ [between polarizable $ns$ electrons of the argon shell $(n = 1, 2, 3)$ and the magnetic 3d electrons by which they are polarized] we can calculate the exchange spin polarization; the hyperfine fields on the nuclei of the atoms depend on the latter [14].

To describe the inter-electronic interaction under consideration we introduce the values

$$
b_1 = 0.6780n_1 \left(r_1 - R\right), \tag{3}
$$

$$
b_2 = 0.6780n_2 \left(r_2 - R\right), \tag{4}
$$

where $n_2$ and $2r_2$ are the numbers of the next nearest neighbours of the atom and the distance between them, respectively.

### 8. 3d Transition Metals Sc, Ti, V

The value $b_1$ considered in the previous section gives a positive number of the ferromagnetic collectivized 3d electrons per atom in the case of $(r_1 - R) > 0$ (nickel) and a negative number of the overlapping 3d electrons of the nearest neighbouring atoms in the lattice in the case of $(r_1 - R) < 0$ (iron and cobalt) (see Table 3). For these and other mètals $(C \geqq 7)$ characterized by a comparatively small numerical value of $R$ we confine ourselves to the nearest neighbours of the atom.

However, in the case of scandium, titanium and vanadium $(C \leqq 6)$ the effective radius $R$ is much more than $r_1$. Hence, $b_1$ (with a negative sign) has a great absolute value (see Table 3). ## 9. Electron Structure of the Atom of the 3d Transition Metal

### 9.1 Total number of the 3d electrons in the atom of a ferromagnetic f.c.c. metal

A neutral atom (I) in the lattice of a ferromagnetic f.c.c. metal (nickel and cobalt) has not eight but a lesser number of 3d electrons since some part due to the extension of the 3d charge density, being characterized by the value $Z(3d)$, is collectivized (especially, transfer to the conduction band). The number of collectivized 3d electrons, associated with the value, depends on the number $n$ of atoms (I) forming the sub-lattice in a cubic cell (see Section 4). For the octa- hedric sub-lattice in f.c.c. $n=3$, and for a s.c. sub-lattice in f.c.c. or b.c.c. $n=1$ and so on. These numbers $n$ enter the formulas (7 a, b, c) as coefficients before $Z(3d)$.

The number of the 3d electrons of an atom (II) also differs from eight owing to the interaction (specifically, overlapping) of its 3d electrons with those of the neighbouring atoms which is characterized by the value $b_1$ or $b_2$ and also conditioning the effect of collectivization of these electrons.

We shall assume that the resulting fractional number of collectivized 3d electrons is being formed owing to both mentioned sources of collectivization. The number is characterized simultaneously by the values $Z(3d)$ and $b_1$ or $b_2$ and determines the fractional part $\Delta m$ of the magnetic moment and the fractional part of the electron configuration of a ferromagnetic metal atom being numeri- cally equal.

For an atom of a ferromagnetic f.c.c. metal (nickel and cobalt)

$$
\Delta m=\left[3 Z(3 \mathrm{~d})-K b_{1}-C_{\max }\right] / 2, \tag{7a}
$$

where $C_{\max}=C$ for a sub-group atom having the largest number of electrons; $C_{\max}=10$ for a nickel atom in the nickel sub-group; $C_{\max}=6$ for a Cr atom in the V sub-group and so on.

For a metal atom of a V sub-group of s.c. structure in f.c.c.

$$
\Delta m=\left[Z(3 \mathrm{~d})-C_{\max }\right] / 2+b_{2}. \tag{7b}
$$

For an atom of ferromagnetic b.c.c. Fe

$$
\Delta m=\left[Z(3 \mathrm{~d})-d_{\max }\right] / 2-b_{2}, \tag{7c}
$$

where $d_{\max}$ is the maximum number of 3d electrons in the mentioned stabilized 3d atom shell; $d_{\max}=2$ (the reversed scheme of term splitting is taken into consideration).

### 9.2 Total number of the 3d electrons in V, Ti, Sc atoms

Substituting the numerical values of $N_{\text{tot}}^{\text{free}}(3\text{d})$ and $\Delta m$ for the 3d transition
