# Structural stability of multiply twinned FePt nanoparticles

Michael Müller *, Karsten Albe

Institut für Materialwissenschaft, Technische Universität Darmstadt, Petersenstrasse 23, D-64287 Darmstadt, Germany

Received 12 February 2007; received in revised form 26 April 2007; accepted 13 August 2007

## Abstract
The structural stability of FePt nanoparticles in multiply twinned and single crystalline morphologies is investigated by means of molecular statics calculations based on a recently developed analytic bond-order potential. The results obtained from the atomistic calculations are used for validating a continuum model which allows the contributions of elastic strain, surface and twin boundary energies to be assessed separately. The static model calculations predict a strong energetic preference for single crystalline morphologies in the ordered $L1_0$ and disordered $A1$ phases. If estimates of vibrational entropy contributions are taken into account, however, icosahedral particles can become thermodynamically stable at elevated temperatures.

## 1. Introduction
The prospect of realizing magnetic nanoparticles that can be applied in high-density recording or medical applications has driven a large number of studies in recent years. Among the candidate materials, FePt in the face-centered tetragonal $L1_0$ phase has attracted much attention [1,2]. This structure is characterized by alternating Fe and Pt layers in the $c$-direction and possesses a high uniaxial magneto-crystalline anisotropy energy (MAE) [3]. The superparamagnetic limit in $L1_0$ ordered FePt nanoparticles is reached for a particle diameter of about 4 nm, if a stable magnetization is maintained on a time scale of years [3].

To date, a number of different experimental techniques for preparing isolated $L1_0$ ordered FePt particles have been proposed, including chemical synthesis from solution-phase [1,2] and gas-phase preparations [4]. The predominant structural motif for as-prepared particles from solution-phase synthesis are single crystalline shapes. Only a small number of decahedral and icosahedral multiply twinned particles (MTPs) have been identified [5]. The single crystalline particles, however, do not exhibit the desired $L1_0$ structure. Instead, the high-temperature random face-centered cubic (fcc) phase ($A1$) prevails and a post-annealing process at temperatures around 800 K is required for transforming the particles into the $L1_0$ phase [6]. This subsequent temperature treatment, however, leads to the undesirable effect of particle coalescence.

The preparation of FePt nanoparticles from the gas phase, in contrast, provides the possibility of thermally annealing the particles prior to deposition [4]. With this technique, however, the formation of single crystalline $L1_0$ ordered particles has so far only be realized at high pressures, leading to the coalescence of the condensed particles [4]. For lower gas pressures, where isolated, monodisperse particles can be obtained, the multiply twinned icosahedral shape is predominant [4]. These experiments also indicate that a size-dependent transition from an icosahedral to a single crystalline structure may occur in FePt nanoparticles: the mean diameter of the icosahedral particles was 5.9 nm, while a larger size of 7.6 nm has been reported for the single crystalline particles [4]. Along with single crystalline particles, MTPs in icosahedral and decahedral shapes have also been observed in other studies on gas-phase synthesized FePt nanoparticles [7,8].

In consequence, attention has been directed at the sup- pression of MTPs in the gas-phase synthesis route. Attempts include the suppression of twinning by increasing the oxygen partial pressure [4] and ion beam-induced trans- formation from multiply twinned to single crystalline par- ticles [9]. While both methods succeed in producing single crystalline particles, drawbacks are particle agglomeration with a high oxygen supply and the absence of $L1_{0}$ order in particles transformed by ion beam irradiation.

In order to understand the occurrence of different struc- tural motifs of FePt nanoparticles, a detailed knowledge of the energetics of particles in the various conformations is necessary. In order to establish whether FePt nanoparticles exhibit a stability range for MTPs, the most stable particle morphology as a function of size has to be identified. Also, energy differences between the various shapes determine the driving force for transitions between the structural motifs.

Energy considerations show that MTPs optimize the surface energy at the expense of elastic strain in the volume and twin boundary energies [10,11]. In consequence, MTPs can be energetically favored over single crystalline shapes at small or intermediate particle sizes where the surface to volume ratio is sufficiently high. For elemental systems, this size-dependent transition from multiply twinned to sin- gle crystalline particles has been shown for copper [11] and gold particles [12].

In this work, the structural stability of FePt nanoparti- cles is investigated by atomic scale and continuum model descriptions. The size-dependent energies of the different structural motifs are calculated by atomistic simulations employing a recently developed interatomic potential for FePt [13]. The results from atomistic calculations are then used to test a continuum model which allows the contribu- tions of elastic strain, surface and twin boundary energies to be assessed separately. Based on these calculations, the energetic stability of multiply twinned FePt particles is pre- dicted. Moreover, the extent to which entropy contribu- tions to the free energy affect the structural stability is discussed.

## 2. Particle shapes

### 2.1. Single crystalline particles

In the case of single crystalline metal particles, the ther- modynamically stable shape is determined by a Wulff con- struction. If $d_{hkl}$ denotes the distance from the particle center of a $\{hkl\}$-facet with surface energy per unit area $\gamma_{hkl}$, then the Wulff construction states that the energy- minimizing shape is characterized by a constant ratio $\gamma_{hkl}/d_{hkl}$ [14]. For many fcc metals, low index surface ener- gies obey the ordering sequence $\gamma_{111}<\gamma_{100}<\gamma_{110}$.

When limited by strong facetting $(\gamma_{110}/\gamma_{111}\geqslant\sqrt{3/2})$, the Wulff shape is a truncated octahedron surrounded only by $\{111\}$ and $\{100\}$ facets [10]. This shape is schematically depicted in Fig. 1.

![](./images/811961709156630528_1.jpg)

Fig. 1. Construction of a Wulff polyhedron from a regular octahedron in the strong facetting model. $\Gamma$ denotes the ratio of the $\{100\}$ to $\{111\}$ surface energies.

Within a strong facetting limit and for a given ratio $\Gamma=\gamma_{100}/\gamma_{111}$, the Wulff polyhedron for single crystalline particles can be constructed starting from a regular octahe- dron of side length $a$ with only $\{111\}$ facets, which are at a distance $d_{111}=a/\sqrt{6}$ from the center. By truncating the vertices of the octahedron at a distance $d_{100}=\Gamma a/\sqrt{6}$ from the center, six square $\{100\}$ facets of side length $l_{100}=a(1-\Gamma/\sqrt{3})$ are laid open. The hexagonal $\{111\}$ facets are limited by three edges of length $l_{100}$ and three edges of length $l_{111}=a(2\Gamma/\sqrt{3}-1)$. For $\Gamma=\sqrt{3}/2$, $l_{111}$ becomes zero and the Wulff shape is a cuboctahedron. For $\Gamma=2/\sqrt{3}$, the edge lengths are equal $(l_{100}=l_{111}=a/3)$ and a regular truncated octahedron is obtained. For $\Gamma=\sqrt{3}$, $l_{100}$ vanishes and the octahedron remains uncut. For the following considerations, $\Gamma$ therefore has to obey the constraint $\sqrt{3}/2\leqslant\Gamma\leqslant\sqrt{3}$.

The volume $V$ and the total area of $\{111\}$ and $\{100\}$ facets of the Wulff polyhedron can be expressed by

$$
V=d_{111}^{3}4(\Gamma^{3}-3\sqrt{3}\Gamma^{2}+9\Gamma-2\sqrt{3}),\tag{1}
$$

$$
A_{111}=d_{111}^{2}\sqrt{3}(3\sqrt{3}\Gamma-\frac{3}{2}\Gamma^{2}-3),\tag{2}
$$

$$
A_{100}=d_{111}^{2}2(\Gamma^{2}-2\sqrt{3}\Gamma+3).\tag{3}
$$

For a Wulff polyhedron consisting of $N$ atoms with atomic volume $\Omega$, the volume is given by $V=N\Omega$, which leads to the condition

$$
d_{111}(N)=(N\Omega/4g)^{1/3}\tag{4}
$$

with the geometry factor

$$
g=\Gamma^{3}-3\sqrt{3}\Gamma^{2}+9\Gamma-2\sqrt{3}.\tag{5}
$$

For distinguishing the various structures, two indices $(n_{\text{l}}$, $n_{\text{cut}})$ are sufficient according to Baletto and Ferrando [15], where $n_{\text{l}}$ is the edge length (in number of atoms) of the uncut octahedron and $n_{\text{cut}}$ is the number of layers cut from the vertices.

### 2.2. Icosahedral MTPs

The icosahedron is a possible shape for an MTP which has a low surface to volume ratio and is solely terminated by {111} facets [16]. It is depicted schematically in Fig. 2. An icosahedral particle can be built from 20 regular tetrahedra sharing a common vertex. For each of the tetrahedra, one of the {111} facets is on the surface, while the remaining facets form twin boundaries to the neighboring tetrahedra. In an icosahedral MTP, this leads to 30 internal twin boundaries. Since this construction does not fill the volume and leads to a geometric gap, the tetrahedra have to be elastically strained. In general, icosahedral MTPs can therefore only be energetically favored over single crystalline shapes at very small particle sizes. If the surface to volume ratio is sufficiently large, the energy gain achieved by the formation of only low energy {111} facets compensates for the strain and twinning energies [10,17,18,15].

The size of an MTP can be related to the number of atoms $N$ if the particle volume is taken from the unstrained state. The volume, surface area and twin boundary area are then given by the following relations
$$
V=\frac{5 \sqrt{2}}{3} a^{3}, \tag{6}
$$

$$
A_{111}=5 \sqrt{3} a^{2}, \tag{7}
$$

$$
A_{\text {twin }}=\frac{15 \sqrt{3}}{2} a^{2}, \tag{8}
$$
where $a$ is the edge length of an undistorted tetrahedral building block of the icosahedron.

With the replacement $V=N \Omega$, this leads to the condition
$$
a(N)=\left(3 N \Omega / 5 \sqrt{2}\right)^{1 / 3}. \tag{9}
$$

The strain energy density $W_{\text {Ic }}$ in icosahedral particles has been estimated by Ino [10], assuming homogeneous strains and linear displacements of the atoms. A refined estimate was proposed by Howie and Marks [17]. They considered angular and radial inhomogeneities of internal strain and found a smaller strain energy density $W_{\text {Ic }}$ compared with the result of Ino. For an elastically isotropic material with shear modulus $\mu$ and Poisson's ratio $v$, the strain energy density as derived by Howie and Marks is
$$
W_{\mathrm{Ic}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}^{2} \frac{2 \mu(1+v)}{(1-v) \Omega}, \tag{10}
$$
with $\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}=0.0205$ [17].

For modeling an elastically anisotropic cubic material, we will use this relation and replace $\mu$ by the effective Voigt average of the two principal shear moduli $(c_{11}-c_{12}+3 c_{44}) / 5$ [19].

In addition to $W_{\text {Ic }}$, Howie and Marks also introduced a surface stress term to correct the surface energy of the strained particle [17,18]. However, we believe an analytic expression for this term is still missing.

According to Ref. [15], an icosahedron can be formally identified by a single index $k$, denoting the number of atomic shells building the particle.

### 2.3. Decahedral MTPs

Another family of MTPs is derived from a decahedron in the form of a pentagonal bipyramid. A decahedron is constructed by five regular tetrahedra sharing a common edge in $\{110\}$ direction and each contributing two $\{111\}$ facets to the surface of the decahedron. Again, the tetrahedra have to be distorted to join the remaining two $\{111\}$ facets with the neighboring tetrahedra. The strain energy density is considerably smaller than in the case of an icosahedron [17]. Because of their lower strain energy density, decahedral particles are often found to be the energetically most stable shape at intermediate particle sizes, between the stability ranges of icosahedral and single crystalline particles [10,17,18,15].

A regular decahedron is surrounded by $\{111\}$ facets only, but its shape is far from being spherical and has a rather large surface to volume ratio. As depicted in Fig. 3, energetically more favorable shapes can therefore be obtained by truncating the decahedron along the edges, which exposes $\{100\}$ facets, and from the vertices, exposing re-entrant $\{111\}$ facets. The corresponding star-like shapes are designated as Marks decahedra [15].

Following Ref. [15], Marks decahedra can be characterized by a triplet of integers $(m, n, p)$, where $m$ and $n$ are the number of atoms along the edges of the $\{100\}$ facets perpendicular and parallel to the fivefold $\{110\}$ symmetry axis and $p$ is the depth of the re-entrant $\{111\}$ facets.

Building analytic expressions for the volume, surface and twin boundary area in Marks decahedra that cover the whole range of possible index combinations in the triplet $(m, n, p)$ renders the treatment unnecessarily complex. The following considerations are therefore restricted to Marks decahedra with fixed re-entrant size and square $\{100\}$ facets. The corresponding particles can be constructed from a decahedron, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Starting from a regular decahedron composed of tetrahedra of edge length $2 a$, 10 tetrahedra of edge length $a$ are cut from the corners, giving rise to a star-like shape with pointed tips having only $\{111\}$ facets. Furthermore, by truncating the tips of the star at a distance $c$ along the edge, square

![](./images/811961709156630528_3.jpg)

Fig. 3. Construction of a Marks multiply twinned decahedron with re-entrant {111} facets and square {100} facets starting from a regular decahedron. Lengths $a$ and $c$ are measured in the unstrained state.

{100} facets are exposed and the surface to volume ratio is significantly reduced. For a given ratio $\lambda = c/a$, the volume, surface and twin boundary area of the Marks decahedron can now be given by

$$
V=\frac{5 \sqrt{2}}{2} a^{3}\left(1-\lambda^{3} / 3\right), \quad(11)
$$

$$
A_{111}=5 \sqrt{3} a^{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\lambda^{2}\right), \quad(12)
$$

$$
A_{100}=5 a^{2} \lambda^{2}, \quad(13)
$$

$$
A_{\text {twin }}=\frac{15 \sqrt{3}}{4} a^{2}. \quad(14)
$$

Matching the volume of $N$ atoms with the volume of the Marks decahedron leads to this expression for the edge length:

$$
a(N)=\left(\frac{2 N \Omega}{5 \sqrt{2}\left(1-\lambda^{3} / 3\right)}\right)^{1 / 3}. \quad(15)
$$

In the remainder of the article $\lambda$ is fixed at $1 / 2$, describing Marks decahedra of equal indices $(m=n=p)$, which we find best minimizes the energy in the atomistic calculations.

According to Howie and Marks, the strain energy density in decahedral particles is given by [17]

$$
W_{\mathrm{Dh}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}^{2} \frac{\mu}{4(1-v) \Omega} \quad(16)
$$

with $\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}=0.0205$.

Atomic interactions are described by a newly developed FePt analytic bond order potential (ABOP). Details of this model potential can be found elsewhere [13]. A summary of the basic formalism and parameters is given in Appendix A. Notably, the twin boundary energy is comparably high and of the same order as in pure Pt [20]. The surface energies of the random $A 1$ phase fulfill the condition of strong facetting $(\gamma_{110} /$ $\gamma_{111}=1.35$). For each structural motif, closed shell particles in the size range from 500 to 10,000 atoms, corresponding to a diameter of from 2.4 to 6.4 nm, are generated by systemati- cally scanning through all feasible combinations of integer indices while the stoichiometry of Fe and Pt is fixed at a 1:1 composition. In the case of chemically disordered particles in the $A1$ structure, energy fluctuations arising from a sta- tistical distribution of atoms within the particle are mini- mized by averaging the energies of at least 20 different configurations. The standard error is then found to be less than $1\ \text{meV atom}^{-1}$.

In chemically ordered particles, the layered structure of the $L1_0$ phase leads to a deviation from the equiatomic composition of Fe and Pt [24]. For comparing energies of different structural motifs, particles of equal composition have to be considered. Therefore, an equiatomic composi- tion is enforced by randomly distributing a corresponding number of anti-site defects within the ordered particles. Again, energy fluctuations have been minimized by averag- ing over multiple configurations.

Furthermore, the icosahedron, which is composed of tetrahedral units with different orientations, does not allow the adjustment of $L1_0$ order without introducing additional anti-phase boundaries. For identifying lowest energy con- figurations, ordered icosahedral particles were generated by simulated annealing, employing a Metropolis Monte Carlo algorithm where atom displacements as well as atom exchanges are attempted and the temperature is lowered from 1200 to 0 K over a large number of $2\times10^{4}$ Monte Carlo steps. The structure proposed by the Monte Carlo simulation has then been further relaxed by the molecular statics method.

The particle energy as a function of cluster size is shown in Fig. 4 for single crystalline, decahedral and icosahedral particles in the random $A1$ phase. Since the particle ener- gies vary with the shape of each structural motif, for each sequence only particles with lowest energy are shown. For single crystalline particles in the random phase, truncated octahedra with relatively small {100} facets are observed, described by the indices $n_{\text{cut}}\approx0.26n_{1}$. The ratio $d_{100}/d_{111}$ is then close to 1.284, which corresponds to the Wulff poly- hedron defined by the surface energies for the $A1$ phase listed in Table 1. In experiments, truncated octahedra with small {100} facets have indeed been found in as-prepared particles from gas-phase synthesis [8]. For decahedral par- ticles, the atomistic calculations predict that shapes with approximately equal indices $(m,n,p)$ are most favorable. These resemble the Marks decahedron depicted in Fig. 3 and have also been observed in gas-phase synthesis studies [8]. In general, even for the smallest particle sizes studied, the single crystalline shapes are energetically favored over MTPs by at least $10\ \text{meV atom}^{-1}$. Icosahedral particles exhibit a much higher energy than decahedral ones because of their higher internal strain and larger twin boundary areas. The same holds true if particles in the $L1_0$ structure are compared. As shown in Fig. 5, we observe again that single crystalline particles are favored over both multiply twinned shapes. As a result of the anti-phase boundaries in icosahedral particles, their energy difference to the other morphologies is even increased compared with the disor- dered state.

A comparison between ordered and disordered particles is shown in Fig. 6. Here, the particle energies are plotted relative to a reference energy obtained from the continuum model for single crystalline particles, which is described in the next section (Eq. (17)). As expected, the ordered struc- tures are energetically favored over the disordered ones in the $T=0$ K regime.

In conclusion, our results based on the atomistic calcu- lations by means of an ABOP do not provide evidence for an energetic stability of multiply twinned FePt nanoparti- cles. ## 4. Continuum model calculations

In the following, we apply a continuum model that provides the possibility of assessing the strain, twin boundary and surface energy contributions to the structural energy of the particles separately. The model allows the investigation of the influence of surface and twin boundary energies on the stability of MTPs beyond the values defined by the ABOP. As described in the last section, this is of particular interesting considering that the comparison with electronic structure calculations suggests that the ABOP overestimates the twin boundary energy approximately by a factor 2, while it may somewhat underestimate the surface energy (see Table 1).

### 4.1. Model description

The energy of a nanoparticle can be approximated by the sum of the volume, surface and twin boundary energy terms, if excess energies of edges and vertices as well as surface stress contributions are neglected:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(N)= & N(E_{\mathrm{c}}+W)+A_{\mathrm{twin}}(N)\gamma_{\mathrm{twin}}+\sum_{hkl}A_{hkl}(N)\gamma_{hkl}. \\
& (17)
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, $E_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the cohesive energy per atom in the bulk phase and $W$ is the average strain energy per atom, which is zero for single crystalline particles. $A_{\mathrm{twin}}$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{twin}}$ denote the twin boundary area and its energy, while $A_{hkl}$ and $\gamma_{hkl}$ denote the total area and energies of the $hkl$ facets terminating the particle surface. For MTPs, Eq. (17) assumes that the only necessary correction due to the distortion of the tetrahedral building blocks is the additional volume strain energy density. The values of surface and twin boundary energies are considered to be unchanged as compared with the unstrained crystal.

Cleveland and Landman [11] validated this model for metallic nanoparticles without considering the energies of the twinning planes. They found a good agreement for single crystalline and decahedral nanoparticles, but failed to describe icosahedral particles with Ino's model [10] for the strain energy.

For describing FePt nanoalloys, we extended the continuum model by excess energy contributions of the twin boundaries and used the improved estimate for the strain energy in MTPs of Howie and Marks [17]. For the different morphologies, the size of the surface and twin boundary areas as well as expressions for the strain energy density are given in Section 2.

### 4.2. Comparison with atomistic calculations

For validating the continuum model by comparison with the atomistic calculations, values for cohesive energies, elastic constants, surface and twin boundary energies as predicted by the FePt ABOP are substituted into Eq. (17). For the random $A1$ phase, the values from Table 1 can be employed directly. However, for taking the anti-site defects in ordered particles into account, a volume term in addition to the $L1_0$ cohesive energy has to be considered. Also, the anti-phase boundaries in ordered icosahedral particles have to be modeled by an energy penalty that scales with $N^{2/3}$.

In Figs. 4-6, the results from Eq. (17) are compared with the atomistic calculations. For disordered particles, where the continuum model can be applied without any adjustable parameters, an excellent agreement is found for all morphologies at particle sizes larger than 4 nm in diameter. In particular, it is evident that the refined estimate of strain energies by Howie and Marks (Ref. [17]) also allows to apply the model to icosahedral particles. Moreover, the agreement suggests that correction terms due to the strain in the surface of icosahedral particles are negligible. For ordered particles, accounting for anti-site defects by increasing the cohesive energy of the $L1_0$ phase by $6\ \text{meV atom}^{-1}$ also leads to a good agreement between atomistic and continuum calculations. Furthermore, the anti-phase boundaries in icosahedral particles have been modeled by the term $2\times10^{-5}N^{2/3}$ added to Eq. (17).

For $d<4$ nm, small deviations between the continuum model and the atomistic calculations occur. These can be attributed to the neglect of edge and vertex atom contributions in the continuum model, which become more important as the size reduces. The size limit for a quantitative applicability of Eq. (17) coincides with the superparamagnetic limit in FePt nanoparticles. Therefore, the continuum model proves useful for FePt particle sizes that are relevant

for the envisaged technological applications, which rely on the hard magnetic properties of FePt.

### 4.3. Assessment of surface and twin boundary energy contributions

Given the quantitative character of the continuum model, it can now be applied for investigating the stability of MTPs for surface and twin boundary energies beyond the values defined by the ABOP. For the $A1$ disordered phase, the energy of twinned and single crystalline particles have been calculated from Eq. (17) for twin boundary energies ranging from 0 to 13 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$. The surface energy has been varied by adjusting $\gamma_{111}$ between 72 and 125 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$ and keeping the ratio $\Gamma = \gamma_{100}/\gamma_{111}$ constant at the value predicted by the ABOP ($\Gamma=1.284$). The data are used for evaluating the phase boundaries between stability domains of decahedral, icosahedral and single crystalline particles. In Fig. 7, the most stable morphology for disordered particles is indicated as a function of twin boundary energy, surface energy and particle size.

Random FePt alloys described by the FePt ABOP are located in the region of parameter space where single crystalline particles are most stable at any size. However, Fig. 7 shows that a rather small reduction of $\gamma_{\text{twin}}$ and/or an increase of $\gamma_{111}$ leads to the stability of icosahedral particles at small sizes. In contrast, decahedral particles become stable at intermediate size ranges only for low twin boundary energies less than 3–4 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$. Because of their larger twin boundary area, a reduction of $\gamma_{\text{twin}}$ has a higher impact on the stability of icosahedral than decahedral particles. Also, because of the lowest surface to volume ratio, an increase of surface energy favors the icosahedral shape over the competing morphologies.

Following the same procedure, a phase diagram has also been calculated for particles in the $L1_0$ ordered phase, as shown in Fig. 8. The features of this phase diagram are qualitatively the same as in Fig. 7. However, the different ratio of low-index surface energies and the additional incorporation of anti-phase boundaries in icosahedral particles restrict the stability of MTPs to lower sizes as compared with the disordered phase.

For estimating which set of $\gamma_{\text{twin}}$ and $\gamma_{111}$ energies best describes reality, reference values from first principles calculations have been considered for $L1_0$ FePt (see Table 1) [22,23]. With 109 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$, the (111) surface energy from DFT calculations is 22 % higher than the value given by the ABOP (89 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$). In the case of $\gamma_{\text{twin}}$, the reference value only amounts to around half the value predicted by the ABOP (6.5 as compared with 11.7 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$). As is shown in Fig. 8, for this combination of $\gamma_{\text{twin}}$ and $\gamma_{111}$ energies, the continuum model gives rise to the stability of icosahedral morphologies in the $L1_0$ ordered phase at very small particle sizes of $d<2.7$ nm.

Furthermore, presuming that the relative deviations of the ABOP compared with these methods remain constant, a simple scaling may also provide reasonable estimates for reference values in the disordered phase. This procedure gives $\gamma_{111} \approx 100$ meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$ (an $\approx 22$ % increase of the ABOP value of 81 meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$) and $\gamma_{\text{twin}} \approx 6.5$ meV $\mathring{\text{A}}^{-2}$ (the ABOP produces identical results for the twin boundary energy in the $A1$ and $L1_0$ phases). For this set of energies, the maximum diameter for stable icosahedrons in the disordered phase is 2.6 nm, as indicated in Fig. 7.

### 5. Role of temperature

In the present study, structural stabilities of single crystalline and multiply twinned particles were discriminated solely by energy considerations and are therefore strictly valid only at zero Kelvin. It is well known from literature, however, that vibrational entropy contributions can lead to a cross-over between structural motifs at finite temperatures [25], because twinned particles exhibit softer vibration modes than single crystalline ones. Therefore, the effect of vibrational contributions to free energies can change the stability regions for FePt nanoparticles. In the following,

we restrict ourselves to a simple estimate of the vibrational entropy contributions in FePt nanoparticles of 4 nm diameter, which are the most relevant for prospective applications. If we take surface and twinning energies from first principles calculations, the continuum model predicts a static lattice energy difference between the icosahedral structure and the truncated octahedron in the disordered phase of $\Delta E_{\text{fcc-ico}}^{A1} = 5.9$ meV atom$^{-1}$ and in the ordered phase of $\Delta E_{\text{fcc-ico}}^{L1_0} = 11.9$ meV atom$^{-1}$. The transition temperature can then be estimated by the relation [25]

$$
T \approx \frac{\Delta E_{\text{fcc-ico}}}{3k_{\text{B}} \ln \frac{\bar{v}_{\text{ico}}}{\bar{v}_{\text{fcc}}}},
\tag{18}
$$

where $\bar{v}_{\text{ico}}$ and $\bar{v}_{\text{fcc}}$ are the average vibrational frequencies in the icosahedral and single crystalline particle. The vibrational spectrum of nanoparticles can, in principle, be calculated by atomistic simulation methods (see e.g. [25,26]). As a first estimate for FePt nanoparticles, however, we assume that the frequency is independent of size and adopt the value of $\bar{v}_{\text{ico}}/\bar{v}_{\text{fcc}} = 0.9798$, as reported by Doye and Calvo for Lennard-Jones particles [25]. Using this value, we arrive at a transition temperature of 1100 K in the disordered phase and 2250 K in the ordered phase, respectively. For comparison, the melting point is 1873 K and the order–disorder transition temperature $T_{\text{c}}$ is 1573 K [21] (according to several theoretical studies, including our own, $T_{\text{c}}$ is not lowered significantly in small particles [27,24,28,29]).

From these simple estimates, single crystalline particles with $d = 4$ nm should always be thermodynamically stable as long as the particle is in the ordered state, i.e. up to $T_{\text{c}}$. At $T_{\text{c}}$, the particle transforms into the disordered state. Since the transition temperature for a disordered particle from fcc to icosahedral is smaller than $T_{\text{c}}$, the order–disorder transition is accompanied by a simultaneous change of particle morphology, and icosahedral shapes become stable up to the melting point.

For disordered particles with a diameter of 6 nm ($\Delta E_{\text{fcc-ico}}^{A1} = 9.6$ meV atom$^{-1}$), the estimated transition between single crystalline and icosahedral particles is about 1820 K, which is close to the melting point. This size should therefore approximately define the limit where MTPs are unstable in both the $L1_0$ and the $A1$ phase at all temperatures.

In the case of decahedral particles, smaller energy differences to single crystalline shapes are obtained from the continuum model. However, as the ratio of vibrational frequencies is close to one ($\bar{v}_{\text{deca}}/\bar{v}_{\text{fcc}} = 0.99826$), Eq. (18) predicts transition temperatures between single crystalline and decahedral particles well above the melting point. In this context, the decahedral morphology can therefore be disregarded.

## 6. Discussion

The considerations on particle energies and temperature effects provide a reasonable explanation for the predominance of icosahedral FePt nanoparticles prepared by inert gas condensation. Since icosahedral particles are energetically most stable for small sizes below $d = 2.6$ nm, it is likely that icosahedral seeds evolve during the nucleation stage of inert gas condensation. When growing further, kinetic trapping of the particles in the metastable morphology can then lead to the presence of icosahedral particles even at larger sizes. According to Baletto and Ferrando, kinetic trapping is mainly responsible for the formation of large icosahedral MTPs during solid-state growth of metal systems [15].

Moreover, as the free energy difference between single crystalline and multiply twinned particles diminishes with increasing temperature, annealing processes at high temperatures will not have the desired effect of inducing the transformation towards single crystalline particles. Further, the particles will remain in the disordered state, since icosahedral morphologies can be the thermodynamically stable structure at elevated temperatures.

For particles larger than 6 nm, our calculations suggest that single crystalline particles remain stable up to the melting point in both the ordered and disordered states. This estimate is consistent with the crossing from icosahedral to single crystalline particles at $d > 6$ nm in the gas-phase preparation process reported in Ref. [4].

The treatment of particle energies presented in this paper presumes that the particles are non-interacting and free in vacuum. For particles that are supported on a substrate or dispersed in solution, modifications of the energetics are expected. For example, the predominance of single crystalline morphologies from the solution-phase synthesis route [1,30] can be explained by the reduced surface energy of particles in solution. As is evident from Figs. 7 and 8, smaller surface energies extend the stability range of single crystalline particles.

## 7. Summary

The structural stability of FePt nanoparticles has been investigated by atomistic and continuum model calculations. Using an analytic bond-order potential for describing the Fe–Pt interactions [13], the molecular statics method has been employed for calculating the energies of FePt nanoparticles in single crystalline and multiply twinned icosahedral and decahedral morphologies. In the potential description, single crystalline shapes are found to be most stable for all particle sizes, in both the disordered and ordered phases.

The results of the atomistic calculations have further been used for verifying the accuracy of a continuum model, describing particle energies solely by volume and surface energy contributions. In addition to previous applications [11], and owing to its importance in Pt alloys, the excess twin boundary energy has been included in the continuum model. Furthermore, an improved description of volume strain contributions in MTPs has been employed [17]. With these expansions, the applicability of the continuum model now extends to the icosahedral morphology. For particle

sizes larger than $d=4$ nm, an accurate agreement with particle energies from atomistic calculations could be demonstrated.

With the continuum model, the influence of surface and twin boundary energies on the relative stabilities of multiply twinned and single crystalline particles has been assessed. By employing $\gamma_{\text{twin}}$ and $\gamma_{111}$ energies based on first principles calculations [22,23], the calculations suggest that for particle sizes larger than 2.6 nm single crystalline structures are energetically preferred. Simple estimates of temperature dependent entropy contributions [25], however, provide evidence that the icosahedral motif is becoming thermodynamically stable in the disordered state at elevated temperatures.

Based on these considerations, the observation of icosahedral particles in gas-phase condensation can be explained by the nucleation of stable twinned structures that are kinetically trapped up to larger sizes. Because of the larger vibrational entropy in icosahedrons, annealing processes at high temperatures fail to induce a transformation to single crystalline structures. Furthermore, the crossing of the predominant structural motif observed in experiments at $d>6$ nm (Ref. [4]) coincides with the estimated size limit from which on single crystalline particles are stable up to the melting point. Instead, the predominance of crystalline particles from solution-phase synthesis is a result of the reduced surface energy contributions.

### Appendix A. Bond-order potential for FePt
The functional form of the bond-order potential for FePt equals that proposed in Ref. [31] for modeling Pt–C interactions. A detailed description of the parametrization for FePt can be found in Ref. [13]. Here, we only summarize the basic formula and provide the full parameter set (see Table A.1).

The potential energy is written as a sum over individual bond energies
$$
E=\sum_{i<j} f_{i j}^{\mathrm{c}}\left(r_{i j}\right)\left[V_{i j}^{\mathrm{R}}\left(r_{i j}\right)-\frac{b_{i j}+b_{j i}}{2} V_{i j}^{\mathrm{A}}\left(r_{i j}\right)\right]. \tag{A.1}
$$

The pair-like repulsive and attractive terms are taken as Morse-like pair potentials
$$
\begin{aligned}
& V^{\mathrm{R}}(r)=\frac{D_{0}}{S-1} \exp \left(-\beta \sqrt{2 S}\left(r-r_{0}\right)\right), \\
& V^{\mathrm{A}}(r)=\frac{S D_{0}}{S-1} \exp \left(-\beta \sqrt{2 / S}\left(r-r_{0}\right)\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{A.2}
$$
