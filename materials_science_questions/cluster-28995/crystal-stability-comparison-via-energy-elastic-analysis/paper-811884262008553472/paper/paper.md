# Strain-induced interaction of hydrogen atoms in hcp rare earth metals

M.S. Blanter$^{\text{a},*}$, E.B. Granovskiy$^{\text{b}}$

$^{\text{a}}$Moscow State Academy of Instrumental Engineering and Information Science, Stromynka 20, 107846, Moscow, Russia
$^{\text{b}}$Chemical Abstracts Service, Columbus, OH, USA

Received 31 May 2001; accepted 21 June 2001

## Abstract
The energies of strain-induced (elastic) H–H and D–D interaction are calculated for the hcp rare earth metals Sc, Y, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, and Lu taking into account the discrete atomic structure of the host lattice. The elastic constants, lattice spacing of the host lattice, and concentration expansion coefficients of the solid solution lattice due to dissolved atoms are the input numerical parameters used. The interaction is oscillating and is of the long-range nature. In all solid solutions, a significant elastic attraction exists in the first two coordination shells. A comparative analysis of H–H and D–D interaction energies in hcp metals with those in bcc and fcc solid solutions revealed the determining effect of the crystal lattice type on both interaction energy value and its dependence on interatomic distances. The difference between the interaction energies for different rare earth metals is also significant. In general, the strain-induced H–H and D–D interactions in hcp rare earth metals are weaker than in bcc solid solutions and are of the same order as in fcc solid solutions.

## 1. Introduction
The interaction of solute atoms in metals has been the subject of numerous experimental studies since its understanding is indispensable for explaining many basic physical processes such as short-range order, segregation, ordering, diffusion, etc. The interaction energies are necessary for calculating the phase equilibria and phase diagrams, as well as the mechanical and physical properties of solid solutions.

The theory of solute interactions is currently developed insufficiently for obtaining the interaction energies for many alloys according to the first principles. For many interstitial and substitutional solid solutions in metals, the elastic interaction is essential [1,2]. The theory of strain-induced (elastic) interaction in the framework of a discrete crystal lattice model has been developed [1–3]. The pair interaction energies of interstitials and substitutionals have been evaluated for many bcc [4–10,38] and fcc metals [7,9–13].

Despite the limitations of pair interatomic interaction the model is useful for solving many problems of solid state physics. For example, the energies of interstitial-substitutional pair strain-induced interaction in the case of bcc metals proved to be essential for analysis of internal friction spectra [14–16].

In the case of hcp metals, the calculations of energies for strain-induced (elastic) interaction of interstitials were used in analysis of possible ordered structures [3,17,18], but the energies of solute atom interactions with one another were published for H in $\alpha$-Y, $\alpha$-Ti [18] and Tc [19] and for O in $\alpha$-Ti [19] only. These data are plotted graphically, and are difficult to use in further calculations.

Solid solutions of H and D in hcp rare earth metals feature unique properties [20], and their structure and properties have recently been the subject of many articles (for example Refs. [21–26]). Thus, a study of strain-induced (elastic) long-range H–H and D–D interaction in these metals would be interesting. For these calculations made in the discrete crystal lattice model approach, the elastic constants of metal–solvent were used to describe the elastic behavior of crystal lattice and concentration coefficients of crystal lattice expansion due to hydrogen or deuterium atoms to describe distortions created by dissolved atoms in metals. ## 2. Method for energy calculation

The calculation technique employed in this paper is based on the theory for lattice statics formulated by Khachaturyan in Refs. [1,2]. This technique was developed by Bugaev and Tatarenko in Refs. [3,18,19] for the hcp crystal lattice. The calculation method described below follows Refs. [3,18,19] with some alterations.

Interstitial H or D atoms are located in tetrahedral interstices of a hcp crystal lattice in rare earth metals [20]. Each unit cell of the hcp lattice has two metal atoms in the metal sublattices $\lambda=1$ or 2 and four tetrahedral interstices in the interstice sublattices $p=1,2,3$ or 4 (Fig. 1). In this notation, $\lambda=1$ denotes the sublattice containing the metal atom located at the unit cell origin $(\vec{\xi}_{1}=0)$, and $\lambda=2$ denotes the sublattice containing the metal atom located in the origin unit cell at position $\vec{\xi}_{2}=(2/3)\vec{a}_{1}+(1/3)\vec{a}_{2}+(1/2)\vec{a}_{3}$, with $\vec{a}_{1}$, $\vec{a}_{2}$ and $\vec{a}_{3}$ being the primitive hexagonal lattice vectors. In Cartesian coordinates, $\vec{\xi}_{2}=(a/2,a/2\sqrt{3},c/2)$, where $a$ and $c$ are the parameters of the hcp crystal lattice.

The Cartesian coordinates of four tetrahedral interstices embedded in four interstice sublattices with the numbers $p=1,2,3,4$ relative to the unit cell origin are determined by the vectors $\vec{h}_{1}=(a/2,a/2\sqrt{3},z)$, $\vec{h}_{2}=(a/2,a/2\sqrt{3},c-z)$, $\vec{h}_{3}=(0,0,c/2-z)$, and $\vec{h}_{4}=(0,0,c/2+z)$. The parameter $z=c[1/4-1/3\gamma^{2}]$ ($\gamma=c/a$) is determined by the equality condition of the distance between the center of the tetrahedral interstice and the four closest lattice sites. In a crystal having an 'ideal' relation $\gamma=1.633$, the value $z=c/8$.

![](./images/811884262008553472_3.jpg)

Fig. 1. Disposition of tetrahedral interstices in the hcp crystal lattice: $a_{1}$, $a_{2}$ and $a_{3}$ are the primitive hexagonal-lattice vectors; filled circles denote metal atoms, stars denote tetrahedral interstices.

The real strain-induced interaction energies $W(\vec{r})$ between a pair of interstitial atoms located in the interstice sublattices $p$ and $q$ and separated by the vector $\vec{r}$ are calculated by means of the inverse Fourier transformation

$$
W(\vec{r})=(1/N)\sum_{\vec{k}}V_{pq}(\vec{k})\exp(i\vec{k}(\vec{R}_{p}-\vec{R}_{q})), \tag{1}
$$

where the summation is carried out over all $N$ points of quasi-continuum inside the first Brillouin zone of the hcp lattice, allowed by the cyclic boundary condition; $\vec{k}$ is the wave vector; $V_{pq}(\vec{k})$ is the Fourier transform of the interaction energies; $\vec{R}_{p}$ and $\vec{R}_{q}$ are vectors indicating the unit cell sites where the interacting interstitial atoms occupy the tetrahedral interstices of the sublattices $p$ and $q$, respectively. The description of the first Brillouin zone for the hcp lattice can be found in Ref. [27].

According to Refs. [1–3], the function $V_{pq}(\vec{k})$ can be expressed through material parameters such as the concentration expansion coefficients of the host lattice $L_{xx}=L_{yy}$ and $L_{zz}$ and the crystal lattice vibration frequencies or elastic constants. This function looks like the scalar product,

$$
V_{pq}(\vec{k})=-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{2}\vec{F}_{p}^{*\lambda}(\vec{k})\vec{\vartheta}_{q}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})+Q_{0}\delta_{pq}, \tag{2}
$$

where * indicates a complex conjugate and

$$
F_{pi}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})=\sum_{\vec{k}}f_{pi}^{\lambda}(\vec{R})\exp(-i\vec{k}\vec{R}) \tag{3}
$$

is a Fourier transform of the coupling force $\vec{f}_{p}^{\lambda}(\vec{R})$ acting on an undisplaced host atom in the metal sublattice $\lambda$ in the unit cell at the site $\vec{R}$ produced by an interstitial atom located in the tetrahedral interstice of the sublattice $p$ in the unit cell at $\vec{R}=0$ (a Kanzaki force); $i=x,y,z$ indicates a Cartesian component of the vectors. The vectors $\vec{F}_{p}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})$ are the material constants. Eq. (2) is not valid for $\vec{k}=0$.

$$
\vartheta_{qi}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})=\sum_{\vec{R}}U_{qi}^{\lambda}(\vec{R})\exp(-i\vec{k}\vec{R}) \tag{4}
$$

is a Fourier transform of the $i$-component displacement $\vec{U}_{q}^{\lambda}(\vec{R})$ of a host atom in the metal sublattice $\lambda$ in the unit cell at $\vec{R}$ produced by an interstitial atom in the tetrahedral interstice of the sublattice $q$ in the unit cell at $\vec{R}=0$.

The $Q_{0}$ value corresponds to the part of deformation energy that is not related to the mutual arrangement of interstitial atoms and is determined in accordance with the requirement of the so-called 'strain-induced self-action' effect elimination. For determination of $W(\vec{r})$ values, the

term $Q_{0} \delta_{p q}$ is omitted ($\delta_{p q}$ is the Kronecker delta). The expression for $Q_{0}$ can be found in Ref. [19].

The value $\vartheta_{p i}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})$ can be found from the equation of lattice static

$$
\sum_{\mu=1}^{2} \sum_{j=1}^{3} A_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k}) \vartheta_{j}^{\mu}(\vec{k})=F_{p i}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})
\tag{5}
$$

where $i, j=x, y, z$ are the Cartesian indices and $A_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k})$ is a $(6 \times 6)$ Hermitian block $(2 \times 2)$ matrix of the $k$-Fourier-component of the matrices for the coefficients $A_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{R}-\vec{R}')$ of quasielastic forces of interionic metal-metal interaction. $A_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k})=m D_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k})$, where $D_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k})$ is a dynamic matrix of the hcp crystal of the host metal, and $m$ is the mass of the metal atom.

In calculations of the interaction energies of dissolved atoms in bcc and fcc metals [1,2,4,9], the dynamic matrix was found using Born-von Karman constants of the host lattice determined from the inelastic neutron scattering data. Because of lower symmetry of the hcp crystal lattice, some difficulties arose in the description of the dynamic matrix $D_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k})$ for any wave vectors $\vec{k}$ in the first Brillouin zone using the Born-von Karman constants determined according to dispersion curves along only the high symmetry directions. That is why the representation of the matrix $A_{i j}^{\lambda \mu}(\vec{k})$ through the elastic constants $C_{i j}$ of the hcp host crystal lattice described in Ref. [28] was used for the present study. The matrix elements [28] were calculated in the phenomenological model approach, where non-pair (three-particle) covalent forces were added to the central forces of interionic interaction (limited to the three nearest coordination shells).

Consider the case when the coupling forces $\vec{f}_{p}^{\lambda}(\vec{R})$ do not vanish only for the two nearest coordination shells around an interstitial atom embedded in a tetrahedral interstice of the hcp crystal lattice and located along the straight line from the interstitial atom toward the host atom. In this case, the Fourier transform $\vec{F}_{p}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})$ has the form presented in Appendix A [18] (with some misprints corrected). It can be seen that the elastic constants of the host crystal lattice and the coefficients of the concentration expansion are necessary in order to calculate the Kanzaki force.

The concentration expansion coefficients of the hcp host lattice due to interstitial atoms $L_{x x}=L_{y y}$ and $L_{z z}$ can be obtained according to the crystal lattice parameter dependence on the concentration $\beta$

$$
L_{x x}=\left(\frac{1}{2 a}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} a}{\mathrm{~d} \beta} \quad \text { and } \quad L_{z z}=\left(\frac{1}{2 c}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} c}{\mathrm{~d} \beta},
\tag{6}
$$

where $\beta$ is the atomic fraction of interstitials. The numerical values of $L_{x x}$ and $L_{z z}$ at $300 \mathrm{~K}$ calculated according to the experimental data of lattice parameter concentration dependence from Vajda's review [20] are listed in Appendix B along with the other data used for calculations. In the case of $\mathrm{Tb}-\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{Tb}-\mathrm{D}, \mathrm{Dy}-\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{Dy}-\mathrm{D}, \mathrm{Ho}-\mathrm{H}$, and $\mathrm{Ho}-\mathrm{D}$ solid solutions, the lattice parameters $a$ and $c$ of solid solutions at $300 \mathrm{~K}$ were calculated using the linear expansion thermal coefficients presented in Ref. [20].

### 3. Results and discussion

Since $\vec{F}_{p}^{\lambda}(\vec{k})$ are the linear functions of concentration distortion parameter $\theta=L_{x x} / L_{z z}$, it follows from Eqs. (2) and (5) that the energies $W(\vec{r})$ are the quadratic functions of $\theta$

$$
W(\vec{r})=L_{z z}^{2}\left[\alpha(\vec{r})+\varepsilon(\vec{r}) \theta+\mu(\vec{r}) \theta^{2}\right]
\tag{7}
$$

where the universal coefficients $\alpha(\vec{r}), \varepsilon(\vec{r})$, and $\mu(\vec{r})$ are the same for all solid solutions based on the relevant host lattice in which the interstitials are located in the tetrahedral interstices. Table 1 gives the numerical values of the coefficients for various distances $\vec{r}$ between the interstitial atoms for Sc, Y, Dy, Ho, Gd, Tb, Er, and Lu found via numerical computer calculations. Table 2 and Fig. 2 both explain the mutual arrangements of the atoms incorporated in the tetrahedral interstices in different coordination shells. However, the coefficients allow the strain-induced pair wise interaction energies to be evaluated directly (without involving numerical methods), provided that the concentration expansion coefficients for the host lattice with respect to the relevant interstitial atoms are known. This approach is convenient because the concentration coefficients of the expansion of the host crystal lattice by the embedded atoms are determined with some approximation and could be refined when the new data are published. Table 1 can be directly used for numerical calculation of interaction energies.

One can easily see from Eqs. (2) and (5) that in the case of interaction between different kinds of interstitial atoms (H and D), their interaction energies $W^{\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{D}}(\vec{r})$ are

$$
\begin{aligned}
W^{\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{D}}(\vec{r})= & L_{z z}^{\mathrm{H}} L_{z z}^{\mathrm{D}}\left[\alpha(\vec{r})+\varepsilon(\vec{r}) \frac{\left(\theta^{\mathrm{H}}+\theta^{\mathrm{D}}\right)}{2}\right. \\
& \left.+\mu(\vec{r}) \theta^{\mathrm{H}} \theta^{\mathrm{D}}\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

where $L_{z z}^{\mathrm{H}}$ and $\theta^{\mathrm{H}}$ are parameters related to $\mathrm{H}$ and $L_{z z}^{\mathrm{D}}$ and $\theta^{\mathrm{D}}$ are related to $\mathrm{D}$, respectively. $\alpha(\vec{r}), \varepsilon(\vec{r})$, and $\mu(\vec{r})$ are the same coefficients that were used in Eq. (7) and shown in Table 1.

It follows from Eq. (8) that $W^{\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{H}}(\vec{r})<W^{\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{D}}(\vec{r})<$ $W^{\mathrm{D}-\mathrm{D}}(\vec{r})$ if $W^{\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{H}}(\vec{r})<W^{\mathrm{D}-\mathrm{D}}(\vec{r})$. This means that for an interstitial solid solution containing two solute elements in the host lattice, the strongest interaction is observed between the pairs of similar atoms.

The interaction is determined by the elastic constants, as well as by the crystal lattice distortion from embedded atoms, that is by $L_{zz}$ and $\theta$. For other metals, to track the relations between the H–H and D–D interactions, elastic properties of the metal, and distortions produced by embedded H and D atoms is practically impossible.

The energies of H–H interaction in yttrium calculated in this study differ slightly from the data in Ref. [18]. However, considering that in Ref. [18] the summation was conducted over the so-called special points of the first Brillouin zone only and that the data were displayed graphically, the difference can be thought of as negligible.

When the strain-induced (elastic) H–H and D–D interactions are compared for the metals with different type of crystal lattice – bcc, fcc, and hcp (Fig. 4), it may be concluded that the distance dependence of interaction energy is determined mostly by the crystal lattice type and not by individual properties of the solvent metal. In general, the analysis of interaction energies reveals that the H–H interaction in the hcp rare earth metals is weaker than in those bcc metals for which this information is available. This interaction is of approximately the same order as in the fcc metals.

It is well known that the long-range strain-induced interaction is not the only kind of interaction between hydrogen and deuterium atoms. The analysis of this interaction in bcc metals revealed that for short distances it should be supplemented by repulsion that exceeds the strain-induced attraction [1]. The screened Coulomb interaction of charged interstitial ions could be the possible reason for repulsion. In bcc metals, the repulsion extends approximately up to the first three coordination shells [9] (the repulsion distance equals $\approx$0.2 nm for Nb and Ta). The radius of the first coordination shell for tetrahedral interstices in hcp rare earth metals equals 0.12–0.13 nm, so it should be expected that in those metals the strain-induced attraction in the first shell will also be overcome by the Coulomb repulsion. The case of the second shell with radius of $\approx$0.25 nm is more complicated, so the blocking for the second shell cannot be evaluated without more detailed analysis. However, since the H–H and D–D pairs do not exist in the 1st and 2nd coordination shells of the hcp rare earth metal-based ordered solid solutions [20,22,23], it appears that the repulsion is also in effect in the 2nd coordination shell. To solve this problem, some characteristics of solid solutions should be calculated that depend on the interaction energies of dissolved atoms (for example, the concentration dependence of thermodynamic activity of H or D atoms as it was done for carbon in austenite [13]) and compared with the experimental data.

## 4. Conclusions
Numerical calculations of strain-induced (elastic) interaction energies of H–H and D–D atoms in hcp rare earth metals were performed. The microscopic theory used in this paper allows for consideration of the atomic structure of solid solutions. The elastic constants and lattice parameters of the host lattice were the input parameters in the computations of numerical coefficients $\alpha(\vec{r})$, $\varepsilon(\vec{r})$, and $\mu(\vec{r})$ which were used as material constants for the metals studied. In their turn, these coefficients enabled us to directly calculate the strain-induced pair wise interaction energies between any interstitial atoms embedded in tetrahedral interstices in many coordination shells. This was possible provided that the coefficients of the concentration expansion of the host lattice for the relevant interstitial atoms were known.

Generally, the H–H and D–D interactions in hcp rare earth metals are weaker than in bcc metals and it is about of the same order as in the fcc metals, however they must be taken into account when analysing the structure and properties of solid solutions.
