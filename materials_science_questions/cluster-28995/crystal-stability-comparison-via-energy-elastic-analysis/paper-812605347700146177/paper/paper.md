# The crystal structures of the elements: pseudopotential theory revisited

J Hafner† and Volker Heine‡

† Institut für Theoretische Physik, Technische Universität Wien, Karlsplatz 13, A-1040 Wien, Austria
‡ Cavendish Laboratory, University of Cambridge, Madingley Road, Cambridge CB3 0HE, UK

Received 3 March 1983, in final form 29 June 1983

Abstract. In this paper we reconsider the trends among the crystal structures of the elements in terms of the real-space pair interaction. A lot of progress has been achieved since the pseudopotential theory of cohesion and structure has been reviewed in 1970 by Heine and Weaire. Experience with the on-Fermi-sphere approximation suggests that a simple empty-core pseudopotential is sufficient to explain the trends and indeed we find it so. Calculations of the interatomic potentials have been made for different core radius $R_{\mathrm{c}}$, atomic radius $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ and valence $Z$. These are presented in a form to demonstrate the trends within groups I to V and within periods of the periodic table. We show that the structural trends arise from a characteristic variation of the real-space interatomic potential with electron density and pseudopotential. This more critical and detailed discussion now justifies and supplements the qualitative ideas advanced in the 1960s. An important effect not previously discussed stems from the disappearance of the Friedel oscillations as $2k_{\mathrm{F}}R_{\mathrm{c}}$ approaches $\frac{1}{2}\pi$.

## 1. Introduction

A decade ago Heine and Weaire (1970, to be referred to as HW) reviewed the pseudopotential theory of the crystal structures of the sp-bonded elements, and discussed in a tentative way trends through the periodic table. Since then there has been a lot of progress in such pseudopotential calculations by Cohen, Martin and others with the total energy method (Yin and Cohen 1978, 1981, Ihm and Cohen 1981, Wendel and Martin 1979, Kunc and Martin 1981, Averill 1971, McMahan and Moriarty 1982 and references given therein) and by Moriarty, Hafner and others using second-order perturbation theory (Hafner 1974, 1975, 1976, Hafner and Eschrig 1975, Hafner and Hittmair 1975, Moriarty 1973, 1977, Morita and Soma 1972, Schiferl 1979, Abe *et al* 1977, Maknovetskii and Krasko 1977, Kelly 1979, Rahman and Rahman 1981 and references cited therein). It is therefore possible to return now to the trends in the periodic table in greater detail and with greater confidence.

The specific trends we shall discuss are as follows. (i) The change from metallic structures in groups I to III to covalent structures in groups IV to VI with decreasing coordination number. (ii) In group II the increasing distortion as the atomic number $A$ increases (Zn, Cd), culminating in a unique structure in Hg. (iii) In group III a similar phenomenon of a unique structure for Ga and a distorted one for In, but then the return to metallic structure in Tl as $A$ increases further which we term 'coming out the other side'.

(iv) The tendency in groups IV to VI to more metallic behaviour in the high-$A$ elements, most pronounced in group IV with Sn and Pb. (v) The occurrence of very strong covalency in the first row of the periodic table, e.g. boron, which we shall only comment on briefly. (vi) The phase changes under pressure result in increasing coordination number which suggests that the interatomic distance to nearest neighbours wants to keep nearly constant. Moreover, especially in groups III and IV they tend to echo trends produced by chemical substitution, leading to a 'universal phase diagram' describing both.

What a rich variety of structures one in fact has, resulting from the interplay of three length scales that enter the preferred near-neighbour spacing and geometry: the radius $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ of the ion core of the atom, a screening length $\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}$ characterising the screening of the valence electron gas, and the Friedel wavelength

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=2 \pi / 2 k_{\mathrm{F}}
$$

defining the ripples caused by any local perturbation of the electron gas. The latter two are both functions of the electron density but depend on it in different ways and hence must be included separately to describe the physical effects. In $\S 2$ we shall interpret the trends in the computed interatomic interaction potential $\Phi(R)$ (see $\mathrm{HW}$, p 276) in terms of these three lengths. In a separate publication (Hafner and Heine 1984a) we shall show how some of the effects we see in $\Phi(R)$ can be derived analytically following the ideas of Pettifor (1982), but that is immaterial to the argument. We choose to discuss the crystal structures in terms of $\Phi(R)$ in real space because it gives a better physical picture than reciprocal $q$ space and the reasoning can be extended better to alloys (Hafner and Heine 1984b).

The $\Phi(R)$ is spherically symmetric and hence is concerned purely with distance, but it does include most of the effect of sp hybridisation as is evident from the $q$-space formulation from which it is derived (HW, pp 284-94). Even in a typically open covalent material like Si the main cause of the diamond structure is the general desire of the spherically symmetric rearrangement potential $\Phi(R)$ at constant volume to have a small number of close neighbours for a given atomic volume (HW, pp 291, 424). The three-dimensional effects from a full shell of directed $\mathrm{sp}^{3}$ hybrids then determine specially the diamond structure, but the dominant contribution to the structural energy is still that of the spherically symmetric $\Phi(R)$ (Weaire 1970, Hafner 1974). By structural energy we mean the structure-dependent part of the total energy.

The variety of crystal structures and the interplay of three length scales causes a certain problem: every element is almost unique, at least among the dozen or so in the centre of our field of study where most of the action is found. Even with a one-parameter pseudopotential, an element must be characterised by its valence $Z$ (number of electrons outside the core of closed shells) and its atomic radius $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ (defined in terms of the sphere with the observed volume per atom). The elements are therefore dotted rather sparsely in this three-parameter manifold. Nor must one approximate too much: structural energies are very small of the order of $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-3}$ au per atom. (We use atomic units throughout where the unit of energy is $27.2 \mathrm{eV}=2$ Ryd.) Of course strictly speaking the pseudopotential determines the atomic volume $\Omega_{\mathrm{a}}$, and in proper calculations good agreement is indeed obtained for $\Omega_{\mathrm{a}}$ and for the volume changes at phase transitions (see for example Hafner 1974, 1975, 1976, Hafner and Hittmair 1975, Moriarty 1973, 1977). However the pseudopotential is a complicated non-local operator and a slightly different combination of its parts is involved in determining the bottom of the band and equilibrium atomic volume from that which determines the structural energy. Thus taking $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ as given is equivalent to choosing a second parameter to characterise the non-locality of the pseudopotential.

We make two moves to help us see trends among so few elements dotted around our parameter space. Firstly we note that in the computer the atomic radius $R_{\mathrm{a}}$, pseudopotential parameter $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ and even the valence $Z$ can all be treated as continuous variables so that we can generate a three-fold continuum of 'elements'. Since the computer is effectively programmed to solve the same Schrödinger equation as we believe applies to the real world, it is perfectly valid to include within our compass this expanded computer-generated data base. For example we can define an 'eka-mercury' with a more extreme pseudopotential than Hg and show that with $Z=2$ one has the same return to close-packed structures as we noted (trend (iii)) for Tl in the $Z=3$ series.

Secondly we make some helpful transformations among our independent variables to
$$
R_{\mathrm{s}}=R_{\mathrm{a}} Z^{-1 / 3} \quad \zeta=R_{\mathrm{a}} Z^{1 / 3}. \tag{1.2}
$$

Here $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the usual electron density parameter which determines the screening and Friedel lengths $\lambda_{\mathrm{sc}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}$, and $\zeta$ is its 'orthogonal' partner. We find in $\S 2$ that the shape of $\Phi(R)$ is very insensitive to $\zeta$ which can therefore be dispensed with, thus reducing us to a two-parameter manifold of $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{s}}$. This is made even more true by using a reduced $\Phi_{\text {red }}$ defined by factoring out the major amplitude factor $Z^{2} / R_{\mathrm{a}}$:
$$
\Phi(R)=\left(Z^{2} / R_{\mathrm{a}}\right) \Phi_{\mathrm{red}}(R). \tag{1.3}
$$

Finally we express distance in terms of the scaled variable
$$
x=R / \lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=2 k_{\mathrm{F}} R / 2 \pi \tag{1.4}
$$
which places the ideal Friedel oscillations in $\Phi$ at integer values of $x$ so that phase shifts due to the pseudopotential stand out clearly ( $\S 2$ ).

We emphasise that the general discussion by Hw of crystal structures remains valid and is embedded in our story. For example the occurrence of the unique Ga and Hg structures is best seen qualitatively in $q$ space in terms of $q_{0}$ where the pseudopotential $v(q)$ passes through zero (HW, pp 263, 275). (We will pursue elsewhere the analytic connection between the $q$-space picture and the interaction $\Phi(R)$ in real space (Hafner and Heine 1984a).) Indeed the history of structural energy calculations has confirmed the validity of the on-Fermi-sphere approximation (Cohen and Heine 1970, p 48) as a zero-order model for the structural energy (Weaire 1970, Morita and Soma 1972, Schiferl 1976, Abe et al 1977). We therefore adopt the Ashcroft empty-core model
$$
v_{\mathrm{ion}}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}
0 & r \leqslant R_{\mathrm{c}} \\
-Z / r & r>R_{\mathrm{c}}
\end{array}\right. \tag{1.5a}
$$

$$
v_{\mathrm{ion}}(q)=-\frac{4 \pi Z}{\Omega_{\mathrm{a}} q^{2}} \cos \left(q R_{\mathrm{c}}\right) \tag{1.5b}
$$
(HW, p 260, Ashcroft 1966) where $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ is related to the best fitted values of $q_{0}$ by
$$
q_{0} R_{\mathrm{c}}=\pi / 2. \tag{1.6}
$$

We note from table 18 of Cohen and Heine (1970) that $q_{0} / 2 k_{\mathrm{F}}$ has the smoothest trend in each group of the periodic table which confirms us in our choice of $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ related to it. We give in table 1 the values we have adopted together with other relevant data. The on-Fermi-sphere approximation exaggerates differences in structural energy (HW, pp 324, 409) without disturbing them qualitatively. This can be understood in terms of the energy-wavenumber characteristic (HW, p 273) which has a maximum value of zero at $q_{0}$ in the on-Fermi-sphere approximation which is somewhat washed out by non-locality (HW,

We shall therefore find it no surprise that the trends in structure are given very well by our one-parameter pseudopotential in $\S 3$, but that the value of $R_c$ for the individual elements has to be stretched here and there to obtain a correct correspondence to the observed structure. However, as stated above, this moderate stretching is within the computer-generated continuum which has all the correct trends, and the correct structures for the elements are obtained by careful pseudopotential calculations including non-locality, etc.

Incidentally we have nothing to add to the discussion of Cohen and Heine (1970, p 228) and HW (p 259) (see also Austin and Heine 1966) about how $q_0$ is determined by the properties of the free atom. To a first approximation $R_c$ is the actual radius of the ionic core, which is then shifted slightly by non-locality; e.g. in the heavy elements relativistic effects result in an extra binding of s states relative to p states, which can easily be shown to increase $q_0$. Our present work therefore completes the link between the basic atomic properties of the elements and the crystal structures in the solid state.

We have been very careful about the effect of exchange and correlation in the screening, using the prescription of Ichimaru and Utsumi (1981). A detailed motivation for this particular choice is given in $\S 2$. The $\Phi(R)$ contains a very strong Coulomb repulsion superposed on much weaker oscillations (HW, p 276). The stronger the exchange and correlation, the shorter the screening length so that more oscillations become exposed in the crucial region of nearest-neighbour spacing, or conversely covered up (Shaw and Heine 1972). Nor is that all: the $q$ dependence of the screening is essential because a simple Thomas-Fermi screening with the pseudopotential (1.5) gives no oscillations in $\Phi(R)$. Of

course one knows that for close-packed metals all this must cancel out in structural energy differences because one knows exchange and correlation have a relatively small effect in the $q$-space formulation, but this is not true for more open structures nor alloys. We therefore treat exchange and correlation carefully, especially as it is so simple within our set of parameters.

Let us return to the relationship of this study to earlier work, and to what we believe we have achieved. The discussion of figure 20 of HW (we reproduce it here as our figure 1) for the occurrence of unique structures in Ga and Hg is archetypal of structure arguments (see § 3): the nearest neighbours in a hypothetical close-packed structure would sit near a maximum of $\Phi(R)$ and hence be unstable. We confirm this picture for Ga and Hg, but there was always something wrong with it for indium: it would inevitably lead to rhombohedral distortion instead of tetragonal (see § 3), as indeed was found in a number of calculations (Hafner 1973, Williams and Appapillai 1973). This problem is now resolved, and we find it is related to the 'coming out the other side' (trend (iii)) and to the occurrence of metallic structure for Pb (trend (iv)). In brief it relates to the way the size and phase of oscillations are influenced by the pseudopotential. This is an example of how we have in § 3 a much clearer picture than previously for the reasons for the crystal structures and trends. In § 4 we discuss pressure effects by studying what aspects of $\Phi(R)$ are related to $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ and remain fixed, and which to $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ which decreases under pressure. In a two-parameter manifold of course a 'universal' phase diagram (trend (vi)) is not possible, but we find some dominant features of $\Phi(R)$ are determined by the ratio $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ and clearly this can be altered equivalently either by pressure decreasing $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ or by chemical substitution changing $R_{\mathrm{c}}$.

Finally in § 5 we take a further step of approximation by noting that the atomic volumes of most of the elements cluster not too far from 150 au. If we apply this as a universal figure for all elements and adopt a value of $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ characteristic for each row of the periodic table, we get a truly universal phase diagram for all sp-bonded elements. This of course needs a bit more stretch to meet all the observations, but all the right trends are there and it concludes our story nicely.

## 2. Trends in the interatomic potentials

The pair potential consists of the direct Coulomb repulsion between the ions, plus the attraction of the first ion to the screening charge distribution induced by the pseudopotential of the second ion:

$$
\Phi(R)=Z^{2} / R+\iint v_{\text {ion }}(\boldsymbol{r}) \chi\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) v_{\text {ion }}^{\mathrm{sc}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{R}\right) \mathrm{d}^{3} r \mathrm{~d}^{3} r^{\prime}.
\tag{2.1}
$$

$\chi(r)$ is the dielectric susceptibility of the interacting electron gas. Much effort has been spent to find the best way of incorporating exchange and correlation effects in the susceptibility for a recent review see Ichimaru 1982 and the apparently very drastic effects of different possible approximations to $\chi(r)$ on $\Phi(R)$ have been widely discussed e.g. Jacucci and Taylor 1981. What has often been overlooked in the discussion is that the susceptibility function of the strongly coupled degenerate electron gas has to satisfy a number of consistency conditions relating $\chi(r)$ to the correlation energy, the compressibility of the electron gas, to the electron-electron pair-correlation function, etc. After eliminating all approximations which violate one or more of these requirements, we find that the difference in the $\Phi(R)$ produced by different accepted $\chi(r)$ is quite small and irrelevant in the present context. The dielectric function of Ichimaru and Utsumi 1981 used in this work represents in our view an optimum compromise between accuracy and computational simplicity. Some would assert that the local density approximation would be better although only satisfying one of the three conditions above. However our earlier calculations e.g. Hafner 1975 suggest that it is inadequate in the important range $q=k_{\mathrm{F}}$ to $2 k_{\mathrm{F}}$ which significantly affects $\Phi(R)$ in the region of interest here.

The general form of the interatomic potential is well known: it consists of a strongly repulsive core plus an oscillatory potential at intermediate and large distances. The repulsive part of the potential is well known from Thomas-Fermi theory, where it takes the form Faber 1972, p 67,

$$
\Phi(R) \simeq \frac{Z^{2}}{R} \cosh ^{2}\left(R_{\mathrm{c}} / \lambda_{\mathrm{TF}}\right) \exp \left(-R / \lambda_{\mathrm{TF}}\right)
\tag{2.2}
$$

where the Thomas-Fermi screening length is given in terms of the Fermi momentum by $\lambda_{\mathrm{TF}}=\left(\pi / 4 k_{\mathrm{F}}\right)^{1 / 2}$. The asymptotic large-$R$ behaviour of $\Phi(R)$ is given by the Friedel form HW, p 276:

$$
\Phi(R) \propto \cos \left(2 k_{\mathrm{F}} R_{\mathrm{c}}\right)^{2} \frac{\cos \left(2 k_{\mathrm{F}} R\right)}{\left(2 k_{\mathrm{F}} R\right)^{3}}.
\tag{2.3}
$$

The oscillations appear not only at large distances: they also extend under the repulsive core and may be exposed by a variation of its radius. However, around the nearest-neighbour distance the $\Phi(R)$ does not follow equation (2.3); the pseudopotential not only sets the amplitude of the oscillations, but also determines a large $R$-dependent phase shift as has also been noted by Pettifor 1982. An analytical explanation for the appearance of an $R$-dependent phase shift in $\Phi(R)$ has been given by Duesbery and Taylor 1977 and will be discussed further in a forthcoming paper Hafner and Heine 1984a. This shift in the position of the main attractive minimum in $\Phi(R)$ around the nearest-neighbour distance is absolutely crucial to our structural argument. (iii) For the repulsive diameter $D_{\mathrm{r}}$ the simple relation (2.4) is confirmed.

(iv) From (ii) and (iii) we see that at variable $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ and constant $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ the position of the minimum and the repulsive diameter move by the same amount-which would suggest that the shape of the curve is unaltered. However, the amplitude of the oscillation changes (trend (i)) so that, e.g., for $R_{\mathrm{s}}=2.09$ the minimum is well developed for $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}=0.58$, degenerates into a horizontal turning point for $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}=0.50$ and turns into a strongly repulsive potential for lower $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$. A similar sequence is seen for $R_{\mathrm{s}}=2.36$.

Hence the covering/uncovering of the minimum is related to two essential mechanisms.

(i) At constant $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ and variable $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ the repulsive core expands with $R_{\mathrm{s}}^{1 / 2}$ (equation (2.4)) while $R_{\min }$ remains constant-the core moves over the attractive wiggle with decreasing electron density (increasing screening length). This is just the 'scotch-egg' behaviour.

(ii) More important, however, is the amplitude effect: as $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ increases (by decreasing $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ ) the pseudopotential $v\left(2 k_{\mathrm{F}}\right) \propto \cos \left(2 k_{\mathrm{F}} R_{\mathrm{c}}\right)$ decreases, the oscillations become weaker and eventually (at sufficiently small $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ ) the first minimum is lost and swamped by the repulsive core. We emphasise that this trend brings a fuller explanation than previously of the form of the interatomic potential $\Phi(R)$ around the nearest-neighbour distance in its dependence on both the electron density and pseudopotential. It is precisely the latter trend which will turn out to be crucial for our understanding of the structural trends within a given group of the periodic table and of the structural phase transitions under pressure.

## 3. Trends in the crystal structures

The result of a second-order perturbation calculation for the total energy of a metal may be written in a real-space formulation (Finnis 1974) as

$$
U=E_{0}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(2 N)^{-1} \sum_{i} \sum_{j}^{\prime} \Phi\left(R_{i j}\right)\tag{3.1}
$$

where $E_{0}(\Omega_{\mathrm{a}})$ is a volume energy and the second term represents just the sum over the effective pair interactions between the ions. $E_{0}(\Omega_{\mathrm{a}})$ accounts for almost all of the binding energy because $\Phi(R)$ is a screened potential which has dropped to a few $10^{-3}$ au at the nearest-neighbour distance. The zero-pressure condition $p=-(\partial U / \partial \Omega)=0$ at the equilibrium density is met by a compensation of non-vanishing pressures arising from $E_{0}$ and the pair-interaction terms. However, the structural energy depends only on $\Phi(R)$. It is well known that because of the non-locality of the pseudopotential the $E_{0}(\Omega_{\mathrm{a}})$ and $\Phi(R)$ simultaneously may not be fitted by a one-parameter potential. Therefore we follow the approach that has already been described briefly in the introduction: we consider the equilibrium atomic radius $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ as given-it is in fact determined by a second parameter modelling the non-locality of the pseudopotential-and discuss structural energies at constant volume in terms of a one-parameter pseudopotential.

However, even taking $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ as given, we should remember the most important trends in the size of the atoms (see Austin and Heine 1966). (i) The atoms increase in size as the principal quantum number $n$ increases. This is because the cancellation theorem applies at each shell in the core-each new shell is pushed outwards. This trend dominates the variation of $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ in groups Ia and IIa. (ii) The existence of a $\mathrm{d}^{10}$ core makes the pseudopotential strongly attractive and hence $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ shrinks. This is responsible for the non-monotonic variation of $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ within the IIb group. (iii) As the valence $Z$ increases, the potentials become more hydrogenic with all the $n l$ orbitals having nearly the same radius. This trend causes the general decrease in $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ with increasing $Z$ and the smoothing of the discontinuity for higher group numbers. With this in mind we are now well prepared to discuss the trends in the crystal structures.

### 3.1. From sodium to phosphorus

Each row in the periodic table is characterised by a trend from close-packed structures with high coordination numbers on the left-hand side to open structures with low coordination numbers on the right-hand side. Figure 7 shows the interatomic potentials for the second-row elements from $\mathrm{Na}$ to $\mathrm{P}$ calculated with the $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ values taken from table 1 (the first-row elements are a special case to be discussed below). We see immediately that for $\mathrm{Na}, \mathrm{Mg}$ and $\mathrm{Al}$ the interatomic distance $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ for a close-packed (cubic or hexagonal) structure coincides with a minimum in $\Phi(R)$; hence the structure is stable. For $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{P} D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ corresponds to a region where both the tangential force constant $\mathscr{J}(R)=R^{-1} \Phi^{\prime}(R)$ and the radial force constant $\mathscr{R}(R)=\Phi^{\prime \prime}(R)$ are negative and a close-packed structure is clearly unstable.

What is this trend due to? From table 1 we see that $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ is approximately constant and the broken curves in figure 6 demonstrate that indeed a calculation with $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}=0.48$ for all the elements reproduces the trend very well. $(R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}})=$ constant means that at low $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ the repulsive core begins to cover the attractive wiggle, but the main effect is the different $(R_{\mathrm{a}} / R_{\mathrm{s}})$ due to the different valencies. Since by definition $D_{\mathrm{cp}}=1.809 R_{\mathrm{a}}=1.809 R_{\mathrm{s}} Z^{1 / 3}$, it follows from equation (2.5) that

$$
\frac{R_{\min }}{D_{\mathrm{cp}}}=1.106\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{c}}}{R_{\mathrm{s}}}\right) \frac{1}{Z^{1 / 3}}+\frac{1.824}{R_{\mathrm{s}} Z^{1 / 3}}. \tag{3.2}
$$

The values of $(R_{\min } / D_{\mathrm{cp}})$ calculated from equation (3.2) are 1.00 (Na), 0.98 (Mg), 0.99 (Al), 0.91 (Si) and 0.87 (P) and demonstrate that the variation of $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ with $Z^{1 / 3}$ is responsible for the instability of the close-packed structures in $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{P}$.

For many years calculations of structural energy differences have been performed in $R$ space as well as $q$ space (see e.g. Hafner 1973), and it is interesting that our interatomic potentials are sufficiently accurate to see more subtle points such as the structural energy differences between FCC and HCP in Al, Mg and Na in terms of interactions with more distant neighbours (Basinski et al 1976). In Al, the neighbours in the second to fifth shells of the FCC lattice manage to avoid both the attractive and the repulsive wiggles in the potential; their contribution to the pair-interaction energy is essentially zero. In the HCP lattice some neighbours fall right next to the maximum of the second repulsive wiggle (figure 7). Hence FCC Al will be more stable than HCP Al. For Mg, the HCP structure has more neighbours in the second attractive well and less in the second repulsive one than FCC; hence HCP is stable. Essentially the same result has been found recently by Pettifor (1982, and private communication). For Na the situation is essentially the same as for Mg.

For Si and P, the simple $\Phi(R)$ predicts the correct sign for the structural energy differences in FCC versus diamond (Si) and FCC versus simple cubic (P), but evidently to expect an explanation of every detail of the covalent structures would be to ask for too

much. However, we see that the trend to smaller coordination numbers is dominated by the decrease of $R_{\text{min}}$ and $D_{\text{r}}$ with respect to $D_{\text{cp}}$. Also $D_{\text{nn}} = 0.5\ (R_{\text{min}} + D_{\text{r}})$ is a rough estimate for the shortest possible nearest-neighbour distance in a distorted structure. Following the simplified treatment given in HW (p 424), which relates the nearest-neighbour distance and the coordination number at a fixed atomic volume, we estimate the coordination number $C$ from the relation

$$
\frac{D_{\text{nn}}}{D_{\text{cp}}}=\left(\frac{C+1}{13}\right)^{1 / 3}. \tag{3.3}
$$

Using the relations (2.4) and (2.5) for $D_{\text{r}}$ and $R_{\text{min}}$ respectively, this yields $C=5.2$ for Si (where we have $C=4$ in the diamond structure and $C=6$ in the high-pressure modification) and $C=3.1$ for P (where $C=3$ is the observed coordination). Our theory therefore accounts well for the coordination number even in the covalent structures which is as much as one can expect in a theory with spherically symmetric forces.

The alkali and the alkaline-earth metals are characterised by a strong increase of the

Both Li and Be have strongly attractive, non-local pseudopotentials which are approximated better with a somewhat larger $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$. ### 3.3. Group IIb
The elements of this group are characterised by a transition from hexagonal close-packed Be and Mg to distorted hexagonal structures in Zn and Cd and further to a unique structure in Hg. The density variable $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ varies non-monotonically, while the ratio $\left(R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}\right)$ decreases monotonically from Be to Hg (cf table 1). To a first approximation, we may even set $R_{\mathrm{s}}=2.56=$ constant throughout this series and vary only $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$. Figure 9 demonstrates that this explains the structural trend very well indeed: with decreasing $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ both $D_{\mathrm{r}}$ and $R_{\min }$ decrease relative to $D_{\mathrm{cp}}, D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ moves from a minimum in $\Phi(R)$ to a maximum. The structures of Zn and Cd are related to the ideal HCP structure by a shear distortion; hence the instability of the close-packed arrangement should show up as a softening in its elastic shear moduli. In terms of nearest-neighbour interactions, the shear moduli of the HCP lattice are given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
& C_{44}=\left(4 \Omega_{\mathrm{a}}\right)^{-1 / 3}(10 \mathscr{J} / 3+2 \mathscr{R} / 3) \\
& C^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(C_{11}-C_{12}\right)=\left(4 \Omega_{\mathrm{a}}\right)^{-1 / 3}(19 \mathscr{J} / 6+5 \mathscr{R} / 6) \\
& C=\frac{1}{6}\left(C_{11}+C_{12}+2 C_{33}-4 C_{13}\right)=\left(4 \Omega_{\mathrm{a}}\right)^{-1 / 3}(3 \mathscr{J}+\mathscr{R}).
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.4}
$$

As $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ decreases, $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ moves along the rising slope of $\Phi(R)$ : the tangential force constant $\mathscr{J}$ decreases slowly (but remains positive), the radial force constant $\mathscr{R}$ decreases rapidly, changes sign at the turning point of $\Phi(R)$ and turns negative. It is clear that at $\mathscr{J} \sim 0$ an increasingly negative $\mathscr{R}$ will lead to a softening of all three shear moduli, but from equations (3.4) it is clear that the first elastic modulus to vanish will be $C$-which is precisely the one related to a shear along the hexagonal axis. Note that even before $C$ is actually zero, a distorted structure with $(c / a)>(c / a)_{\text {ideal }}$ will be energetically more favourable: at $\left(R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}\right)=0.44$ the axial ratio calculated by minimising the configurational energy is $(c / a)=1.76$. At a still lower $\left(R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}\right)$, the other shear constants are softened as well, and an entirely new structure is formed. This is the case of Hg.

The two polymorphic forms of Hg may be considered as tetragonal $(\beta \mathrm{Hg})$ or rhombohedral $(\alpha \mathrm{Hg})$ distortions of an FCC structure. It has been shown (HW, p 404) that after some adjustment a pseudopotential calculation can predict an FCC lattice that is unstable against rhombohedral and metastable with regard to tetragonal distortions, and

This is a first manifestation of the 'coming out on the other side effect' which will be discussed in more detail for the group III and IV metals.

The effect which is mainly responsible for the structural trend in the group II metals is the shrinking of $D_{r}$ and $R_{min}$ relative to $D_{cp}$, but the damping of the amplitudes of the oscillations is very important as well. Note, however, that in order to reproduce the observed structural trend, we have to stretch the variation of $(R_{c}/R_{s})$ from Be to Hg slightly compared to the values deduced from the fitted pseudopotential form factors (table 1). This is not at all unexpected since it is well known that a fully non-local

calculation yields a larger effective $q_{0}$ (corresponding to a smaller $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ by virtue of equation (1.6)) for the energy-wavenumber characteristic than the on-Fermi-sphere approximation (Shaw, as cited by HW, p 324).

Beryllium fits in quite well with the trends discussed, but it has special features such as a negative Cauchy pressure presumably due to the special effects (see $\S 3.7$ below) in first-row elements.

### 3.4. Group III
Essentially the same physical effects dominate the structural trends in the group III elements, so we may again use the same approximations: we set $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ equal to a constant corresponding to the mean electron density of the $Z=3$ metals $(R_{\mathrm{s}}=2.20)$ and vary only $(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}})$ (see figure 9). Corresponding to the lower $R_{\mathrm{s}}$, the potential is already less attractive for $\mathrm{Al}$ than for $\mathrm{Mg}$, but still FCC is stable. A slight reduction of $(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}})$ is sufficient to make both elastic shear constants $C$ and $C'$ given in the nearest-neighbour approximation by
$$
\begin{aligned}
& C=C_{44}=\left(4 \Omega_{\mathrm{a}}\right)^{-1 / 3}(3 \mathscr{J}+\mathscr{R}) \\
& C^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(C_{11}-C_{12}\right)=\left(4 \Omega_{\mathrm{a}}\right)^{-1 / 3}(7 \mathscr{J} / 2+\mathscr{R} / 2)
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.5}
$$
negative ( $\mathscr{J}$ becomes very small and $\mathscr{R}$ negative). Note that the shear modulus $C$ corresponding to a rhombohedral distortion of the FCC lattice will vanish first. This corresponds to the formation of a new structure for Ga. Actually, the amount of distortion with respect to a close-packed arrangement is limited by the sharp upturn of the repulsive part of the potential which prohibits the formation of an open covalent structure with nearest-neighbour distances even smaller than those found in the Ga structure. Following the trend to smaller values of $(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}})$, we go through a region where both $\mathscr{J}$ and $\mathscr{R}$ are negative, but then $\mathscr{R}$ is again positive because the position of the wiggle in $\Phi(R)$ moves to smaller distances and it is flattened out because of the amplitude effect (cf $\S 2$ ). The increase of $\mathscr{R}$ stabilises a close-packed structure against shear distortions: from equations (3.5) we see that the instability with regard to rhombohedral shear will be removed first, but that a much larger $\mathscr{R}$ will be needed to stabilise the FCC lattice with regard to tetragonal distortions. This is precisely the case for In: at $R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}}=0.36$ we have $C>0$ and $C'<0$. The actual amount of the equilibrium tetragonal distortion depends very sensitively on the curvature of potential around the close-packing distance; again it is the sharp onset of the repulsive slope which limits the maximum allowable distortion.

At even smaller $(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}})$ the close-packed structures are again stable; but by comparing equations (3.4) and (3.5) it is immediately evident that a larger $\mathscr{R}$ is necessary to stabilise the FCC than the HCP structure: at $R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}}=0.33$ all three shear constants of the hexagonal structure are positive, but the cubic structure is still unstable against tetragonal shear. This is just $\mathrm{Tl}$ which is stable in a HCP phase at zero pressure and becomes FCC under pressure.

This means that the structural trends for $Z=3$ may be understood in the same way as for $Z=2$: the destabilisation of the close-packed structures and the 'coming out on the other side' are both related to the change of $R_{\min }$ with respect to $D_{\text {cp }}$ and-most important-to the new amplitude effect on $\Phi(R)$. Indeed the reappearance of close-packed structures in the heavy multivalent metals is connected mainly to the nearly complete damping of the oscillatory part of the potential. For In, $\mathrm{Tl}$ and $\mathrm{Pb}$, the interatomic potential is reduced to a screened repulsive potential and of course a close-packing arrangement will be energetically most favourable.

Again we had to stretch the trend in the $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ ratio slightly to fit the observed trend and we refer to the above comments on non-locality.

### 3.5. Group IV
The trends observed in group IV (figure 9) substantiate the analysis presented in the pre- ceding subsections. The larger difference between $D_{\mathrm{r}}$ and $R_{\min }$ on the one hand and $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ on the other hand allow for a larger distortion and hence covalent structures in the light elements; for the heavier elements large positive values of the radial force constant $\mathscr{R}$ help to stabilise the FCC structure in Pb.

### 3.6. Group V
The structural trends in the $Z=5$ group again fit very nicely into the framework of our arguments. The reason for the low coordination numbers was already discussed in $\S 3.1$ and from figure 9 we see that the trend from P to Bi goes in the direction of decreasing distortion and hence increasing coordination number as observed, with Bi (nearly simple cubic) not yet being close packed.

### 3.7. A special case—the first-row elements
It is well known that the first-row elements Li to N have strongly non-local pseudopotentials. Still Li and Be, whose conduction bands are predominantly s like, fit very well into the structural trends discussed above. This is not so for B, C and N which form open structures of low symmetry. For these elements the p-like electrons see the full crystal potential and the non-locality of the potential is extremely strong. Looking at the non-local model potentials and the fitted pseudopotential form factors for B and C (HW, p 206ff), we see that their values around $q=2 k_{\mathrm{F}}$ are at least five times bigger than those of the other elements and of course also five times bigger than predicted by the simple Ashcroft potential. The simplest way to correct our model for the effect of non-locality is to multiply the pseudopotential form factor of the empty-core model with a function $Q(q)$

## 4. Phase transitions under pressure
It has been pointed out that the phase changes of the heavy $Z=3$ elements (Ga, In, Tl) and of the $Z=4$ and $Z=5$ elements parallel the change from open to close-packed structures with increasing principal quantum number (see e.g. Klement and Jayaraman 1967, Evdokimova 1966). For each group a generalised phase diagram may be constructed that contains all the information on the pressure-induced phase transformations of the elements of that group. An example of such a generalised phase diagram for the group IV elements is shown in figure 11.

In terms of our model (equations (2.4), (2.5)) a compression corresponds to a linear variation of $D_{\text{cp}}$ with $R_{\text{s}}$, $D_{\text{r}}$ varying only as $R_{\text{s}}^{1/2}$ and $R_{\text{min}}$ staying constant. In terms of our reduced variable $x=2k_{\text{F}}R/2\pi$ we expect to see the repulsive core move over the attractive wiggle and into the region of nearest-neighbour distance for close packing. Figure 12 clearly displays this trend. For Ga and Si the radial force constant $\mathscr{R}$ becomes strongly positive when the attractive minimum has been covered by the repulsive core of the pair potential and stabilises a more close-packed structure. In agreement with experiment we see that a small compression is sufficient to do so for Ga, while a rather large compression is necessary in the case of Si. The example of Mg demonstrates that the stability of close-packed structures is not affected by compression.

Note that while compression and chemical substitution by an element with a higher atomic number result in the same structural trend, the underlying physics is quite different

We see Ga under compression first transforms to a tetragonal structure and eventually becomes close-packed under still higher pressure. Indium remains tetragonal under large compressions. Thallium is HCP at zero pressure. In all these regards the diagram is in agreement with experiment. Under pressure Tl becomes FCC whereas in the diagram it stays HCP: however its representative point is near the stability region of FCC and we regard this as a small error introduced by our on-Fermi-sphere approximation. The latter also has the effect of exaggerating somewhat the tendency towards distorted structures as remarked in the introduction, especially at low $R_{\mathrm{s}}$. In fully non-local calculations the stability range of the open structures like Ga is expected to be narrowed, thus explaining why In and Tl have been adjusted to lower $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ in figure 13 than in table 1. It also demonstrates clearly the 'coming out on the other side' effect: the field of open structures in the $(R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}})$ versus $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ plane is surrounded fairly symmetrically on the upper and lower sides by more close-packed structures. Starting from a distorted structure, we can induce a transition to a more symmetrical structure by increasing $R_{\mathrm{c}} / R_{\mathrm{s}}$ by pressure or appropriate chemical substitution. The element can undergo the same sequence of transformations in the two cases but the driving physical mechanisms are different.

## 5. Universal phase diagrams and conclusions

To get a really universal phase diagram which summarises the structural trends discussed in this article, we must make a further approximation and we do this by noting that the atomic volumes of all the elements scatter around a value of $\Omega_{\mathrm{a}} \approx 150$ au. Taking this as a universal value we are able to construct a universal phase diagram in a $(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}})$ versus $Z$ plane. Phase boundaries are constructed in the manner indicated above (figure 14).

The $A$ group elements and $\mathrm{Al}$ are scattered around the line defined by $D_{\mathrm{cp}}=R_{\mathrm{min}}$ defining the region of maximum stability of a close-packed structure. The lines $\mathscr{R}(D_{\mathrm{cp}})=0$ mean that the effective pair potential $\Phi(R)$ has an inflection point at the nearest-neighbour distance for close packing and this marks the onset of a phase change. The line $C(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}}, Z)=0$ defines the stability limit of the FCC and HCP structures, with an instability beyond this line against shear along the cubic (111) axis or the hexagonal axis, respectively. The line $C'(R_{\mathrm{c}}/R_{\mathrm{s}}, Z)=0$ indicates the vanishing of a second shear constant and the necessity to form an entirely new structure in the region beyond. The fact that these two phase boundaries are so close together explains why it is so difficult to get the structures of $\mathrm{Zn}$ and $\mathrm{Cd}$ right. The elements $\mathrm{Hg}$, $\mathrm{Ga}$, $\mathrm{Si}$, $\mathrm{Ge}$, $\mathrm{P}$ and $\mathrm{As}$ fall into a broad region where distorted, open structures are predicted. This diagram summarises very nicely our description of the trends in the crystal structure of the elements and their interpretation in terms of the interatomic potentials. For the first time we have a clear picture of the variations of $\Phi(R)$ depending on electron density and pseudopotentials: the position of the main attractive minimum is determined by the core radius $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ and not by the Friedel oscillations, the strength of the minimum is related to the expansion of the repulsive core (and hence to the screening length) and to the strength of the pseudopotential at $q=2k_{\mathrm{F}}$ setting the amplitude of the oscillations. At low electron densities and large core radii we find interatomic potentials with a minimum at the nearest-neighbour distance $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ for close packing, resulting in stable close-packed structures for the $A$ group elements and Be, Mg and Al. As the electron density increases and/or the core radius decreases, the minimum is shifted relative to $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ and flattened—this yields distorted structures for Zn, Cd, Hg, Ga, In, Si, Ge, Sn and the group V elements at first, but then to a return to close-packed structures for Tl and Pb as the last trace of an oscillation of $\Phi(R)$ at $D_{\mathrm{cp}}$ has disappeared.
