# Directional Compton profiles, the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface, and scattering factors for benzenet

by W. JOHN JANIS, PER KAIJSER†, VEDENE H. SMITH, JR.,
and MYUNG-HWAN WHANGBO§

Department of Chemistry, Queen's University,
Kingston, Ontario K7L 3N6, Canada

(Received 16 August 1977; revision received 26 October 1977)

Directional Compton profiles and molecular scattering factors for the benzene molecule were calculated by using an *ab initio* SCF-LCAO-MO wavefunction. The relation between these properties and the electron distribution in both momentum and coordinate spaces are discussed. The $J(\mathbf{0})$-surface, i.e. the set of directional Compton profiles $J(\mathbf{q})$ for $|\mathbf{q}|=0$, is introduced and shown to be a convenient tool for analysing the electron momentum distribution.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

In recent years there have been a number of studies on molecular properties in momentum space ($\mathbf{p}$-space), and the related Compton profiles [1-3]. Most of these are concerned with isotropic Compton profiles (ICP), for which both theoretical and experimental determinations have been performed, since the explanation of the broadening of the Compton line by Dumond in 1929 [4]. Directional Compton profiles (DCP), with less than a decade of history [5], provide a more detailed insight into matter and its momentum distributions. Both molecules and solids are the targets for theoretical investigations and DCP experiments have been performed for several solids in the form of single crystals [3].

While Compton profiles $J(\mathbf{q})$ are defined in terms of the electron distribution in $\mathbf{p}$-space, the scattering factors, $F(\mathbf{s})$, are defined in terms of the corresponding quantity in $\mathbf{r}$-space [6]. Scattering factors and Compton profiles are thus complementary, in that two reciprocal spaces are probed and together may provide new insight for studying molecular properties.

## 2. THEORY
The Compton profiles were evaluated within the context of the impulse approximation [7]. In this case the isotropic Compton profile, $\overline{J(q)}$, is defined by
$$
\overline{J(q)}=2 \pi \int_{|q|}^{\infty} p \overline{\rho(p)} d p, \quad(1)
$$
where $\overline{\rho(p)}$ is the spherical average of the electron momentum density $\rho(p)$ and $q$ is the projection of the electron momentum $p$ on the scattering vector. For a complete description of the basic theory of the physical experiment and the hypotheses of the impulse approximation, the reader is referred to any of the recent reviews on the subject [1-3, 8].

The directional Compton profiles are similarly defined by
$$
J(\mathbf{q})=\int \rho(\mathbf{p}) \delta(\mathbf{p} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{q}}-q) d \mathbf{p}, \quad(2)
$$
where $\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ is the unit vector along the scattering vector, and $q=|\mathbf{q}|$. For $|\mathbf{q}|=0$, the integration in equation (2) is over a plane passing through the origin in p-space, which is perpendicular to q. Since momentum densities are largely concentrated in the vicinity of the origin [8,9], it is not surprising, as we shall show in the next section, that one can obtain useful information about $\rho(p)$ from the $J(0)$ surface, i.e. $J(q)$ for $|q|=0$.

Scattering factors $F(s)$ represent the magnitude of the fourier-transformed position space electron charge density $\gamma(r)$ [10] of a molecule. They are defined by
$$
F(\mathbf{s})=\left|\int \exp (i \mathbf{s} \cdot \mathbf{r}) \gamma(\mathbf{r}) d \mathbf{r}\right|. \quad(3)
$$

The magnitude of the scattering vector $s$ is normally given as $\lambda^{-1} \sin \theta(\AA^{-1})$, which is related to $s=|s|$ in atomic units (a.u.) by
$$
s=(4 \pi)(0.529167) \lambda^{-1} \sin \theta.
$$

It should be noted that $F(s)$ is different from the electron momentum density, $\rho(p)$, which is constructed from the fourier-transformed wavefunctions of the r-space representation [6,8,11]. The integral in equation (3) may be viewed as a one-dimensional fourier transform of $\gamma(r)$ projected on the scattering vector $s$.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The *ab initio* SCF-LCAO-MO wavefunction for the present study of benzene was obtained by using the GAUSSIAN 70 programme with an STO-3G minimal basis set [12-14]. In our calculation the CC and CH bond lengths were 1.397 and $1.084\ \text{\AA}$, respectively, and the coordinates were chosen such that the molecule lies in the $xy$ plane with two CC bonds parallel to the $y$ axis (see figure $3(a)$). The resulting total energy for the system was $-227.891$ hartree$\dagger$.

### 3.1. Scattering factors

The scattering factors $F(\mathbf{s})$ were evaluated for three mutually orthogonal directions of the scattering vector $\mathbf{s}$. One direction was chosen to be orthogonal to the molecular plane $(\mathbf{s}=s\mathbf{e}_{z})$, and the other two to lie in that plane, parallel $(\mathbf{s}=s\mathbf{e}_{y})$ and orthogonal $(\mathbf{s}=s\mathbf{e}_{x})$ to a CC bond. The results are listed in table 1 and also shown in figure 1. $F(s\mathbf{e}_{z})$ decreases more slowly with increasing $s$ than do the other two. This is easily understood by projecting the electron charge density $\gamma(\mathbf{r})$ on the scattering vector $\mathbf{s}$. The projection on the $z$ axis will show a marked peak, since the charge density is concentrated near the molecular plane. Since the scattering factor can be considered as a one-dimensional fourier transform of this projected density, the behaviour of $F(s\mathbf{e}_{z})$ follows. Similarly, the projected charge densities along the $x$ and $y$ axes oscillate and so do the corresponding scattering factors. $F(s\mathbf{e}_{y})$, with two of the projected peaks farther apart, decays more rapidly to zero, than does $F(s\mathbf{e}_{x})$. However, $F(s\mathbf{e}_{y})$ and $F(s\mathbf{e}_{x})$ are very similar to each other, in agreement with our intuitive picture of the charge density of benzene.

### 3.2. Compton profiles

The isotropic Compton profile, together with the directional Compton profiles for the same three directions as examined for the scattering factors, are listed in table 2. The ICP from the present calculation is in essential agreement with those reported in the literature [15-18] from other wavefunctions. One of these [16], calculated from an STO minimal basis set, is given in table 2 for comparison. Also included in this table is the experimental ICP obtained by Eisenberger and Marra [19], to which the contribution from the carbon 1s core electrons has been added [20]. The experimental profile shows a noticeably lower peak than do the theoretical ones. It may be speculated that elimination of multiple-scattering effects in the measurement might lead to a raising of the experimental peak [3].

The shapes of the directional Compton profiles are, on the whole, rather similar over a large range of $q$ values. In order to examine their differences in some detail the isotropic Compton profile was subtracted from them, and the results are shown in figure 2 for $0 \leqslant |q| \leqslant 2$. The two profiles $J(q\mathbf{e}_x)$ and $J(q\mathbf{e}_y)$ are observed to be related to each other, in that they oscillate with a similar frequency, although out of phase. They are both closer to the ICP than is $J(q\mathbf{e}_z)$ and their average, shown by the dashed line in figure 2, is in fact quite similar to the ICP. We have included in table 2 the percentage differences $\Delta J(\mathbf{q})/\overline{J(q)}$ for the $x$, $y$, and $z$ directions. $\Delta J(\mathbf{q})$ is defined as $J(\mathbf{q})-\overline{J(q)}$.

The shapes of the curves $J(\mathbf{q})-\overline{J(q)}$ of figure 2 can be explained in terms of the behaviour of $\sigma$ and $\pi$-momentum densities reported in earlier studies [9, 16, 21-23]. It should be pointed out that the $\pi$ orbitals in the cited works on diatomic molecules have cylindrical symmetry about their bonds and are entirely different in form to the $\pi$ orbital in benzene, which is basically similar to an atomic $p_{z}$ orbital. This can be seen in figure $4(a)$ where the $\pi$-momentum density of benzene is determined from a similar quality wavefunction [16]. The corresponding $\sigma$ density is shown in figure $4(b)$. Both are for the $p_{y} p_{z}$ plane, i.e. along the $p_{y}$ axis (or the equivalent $p_{\mathrm{A}}$ or $p_{\mathrm{B}}$ axes) in figure $3(b)$.

Consider now $J(q \mathbf{e}_{z})-\bar{J}(q)$. We observe that it has a narrow maximum at $q=0$ because the integration planes slice through the $\sigma$ density in the plane of the molecule. It should be noted that the $\pi$ density gives zero contribution to $J(q \mathbf{e}_{z})$ at the origin. $J(q \mathbf{e}_{z})$ passes through a minimum until the integration planes begin to penetrate significantly through the larger parts of the $\pi$ density. It should be remembered that the total contribution of the $\pi$ density is six out of a total of 42 electrons.

The other two profiles, $J(q \mathbf{e}_{x})$ and $J(q \mathbf{e}_{y})$ are larger than $\overline{J(q)}$ for most smaller $q$ values, due to the contributions from the $\pi$ density. The oscillations of $J(q \mathbf{e}_{x})$ and $J(q \mathbf{e}_{y})$ about $\bar{J}(q)$ are caused by the $\sigma$ density which is elongated along the directions of each of the C-C bonds of the framework. This may be seen by inspection of figure $4(b)$ or inferred from the known behaviour [9,21,

22] of $\sigma$ densities. The momentum density of the $\sigma$ framework may be considered as the superposition of six C-C and six C-H bonds [24]. The momentum density contour maps due to C-C and C-H bonds exhibit elongations along the bond-momentum direction [16, 24]. Thus their superposition would lead to the total $\sigma$ momentum density for benzene shown in figure 4 (b) along the $p_{y}$ (or $p_{A}$ or $p_{B}$) direction in $\mathbf{p}$-space. For this reason, $J(q \mathbf{e}_{x})$ is larger than $J(\mathbf{e}_{y})$ near the origin, i.e. in the vicinity of the peak, since the integration planes for the former are parallel to C-C bonds as opposed to those of the latter.

### 3.3. $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface

An alternative approach for the analysis of directional Compton profiles and their relation to the momentum distribution is to construct a graphical picture of the DCP's $J(\mathbf{q})$ at $|\mathbf{q}|=0$ for various scattering directions. This $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface is shown in figure 5. A point on this surface is defined by a vector centred at the origin, whose direction and length are given by $\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ and $J(\mathbf{q})$ at $|\mathbf{q}|=0$, respectively. It is convenient to subtract a suitable constant from these values of $J(\mathbf{0})$ in order to enlarge the fluctuations in the figure, as is done in figure 5 (17 electron/a.u.).

![](./images/812087816958771200_6.jpg)

Figure 5. The $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface for the benzene molecule in the half-plane $z>0$ (the molecule lies in the plane $z=0$).

In fact, such a surface can be constructed for any non-zero value of $|\mathbf{q}|$. However, the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface has the following attractive features.

(i) The electron momentum density is concentrated and centred around the origin although the maximum value of $\rho(\mathbf{p})$ does not necessarily occur at $|\mathbf{p}|=0$. [8, 9]. Experimentally, Compton scattering is most accurate for small momentum transfer where the profile is due mainly to the valence electrons [1]. They are the electrons of primary chemical interest and are responsible for the

structure in the momentum density. The planes of integration for the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface slice through precisely this dominating and important region of the momentum distribution. This does not necessarily mean that $J(\mathbf{q})$ has its maximum value for a given direction $q$ at $|\mathbf{q}|=0$ [22] although $\overline{J(q)}$ does.

(ii) The momentum distribution is symmetric with respect to inversion [25], and thus only $\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ vectors in any half-sphere need to be considered. Higher symmetries of $\rho(\mathbf{p})$ are reflected in $J(\mathbf{0})$, so that even smaller subsets of $\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ vectors determine the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface. For example, in the case of linear molecules (along the $z$ axis) with cylindrically symmetric momentum distributions, the function $J_{z}(q), 0 \leqslant \vartheta \leqslant \pi / 2$, where $\vartheta$ is defined by $\hat{\mathbf{q}} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{z}=\cos \vartheta$, determines all of the DCP's [22].

(iii) The DCP's are particularly easy to evaluate for the specific value of $|\mathbf{q}|=0$ [11].

The first point stresses that the more important information from the momentum density, particularly that which is of chemical interest, is contained in the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface. The last two points refer to the economy of thinking and preparation, i.e. its condensed form and relatively inexpensive evaluation.

The relation between the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface and the momentum density can be examined by reference to equation (2). It shows that the vector defining the surface is a measure of momentum density in the plane through the origin orthogonal to the direction in question. To interpret the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface of benzene in figure 5 it is convenient to divide it, for our discussion, into two regions in terms of the polar angle $\theta$, the angle between $\hat{\mathbf{q}}$ and the $p_{z}$ axis.

The region for small $\theta\left(<30^{\circ}\right)$ is smooth, with a rather steep decrease in elevation for $\theta$ around $30^{\circ}$. This reflects that the planes of integration pene- trate a density of almost constant amount for smaller $\theta$, but the density decreases sharply when the integration plane deviates more from the molecular plane. Thus there is a large flat momentum distribution in the molecular plane. No drastic structure is found in the region $0<\theta<30^{\circ}$ compared with the other region $\left(30^{\circ}<\theta<90^{\circ}\right)$. In the latter, the $C_{6}$ symmetry of $J(\mathbf{0})$ about the $z$ axis is visible. This star-shaped structure is due to structure-dependent factors (called 'diffraction factors' by Coulson [26]) in $\rho(\mathbf{p})$ which are explicitly dependent $[9,11,22]$ upon the internuclear vectors i.e. $\left(\mathbf{p} \cdot\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right)\right)$. In benzene these cause the $\sigma$ density to align along the direction of the bonds, i.e. $p_{y}, p_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{B}}$. The $\pi$ density is affected to a lesser degree by these structure- dependent factors, and thus all the planes of integration for $\theta=90^{\circ}$ contain a similar amount of $\pi$ density. Therefore the oscillations observed in the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface are directly related to similar oscillations $\left(p_{y}, p_{\mathrm{A}}\right.$ and $p_{\mathrm{B}}$ in figure $\left.3(b)\right)$ in the $(\sigma)$ momentum distribution but out of phase by $90^{\circ}$.

We see that such qualitative information about the momentum density is fairly easy to obtain from our graphical representation of the DCP's, the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface.

## 4. SUMMARY
Scattering factors of molecular benzene were calculated for three orthogonal directions and marked differences were found. The two with the scattering vectors in the plane exhibit oscillations and decay much more rapidly with increasing $s$ than the one with the scattering vector $\mathbf{s}$ orthogonal to the molecular plane. The directional Compton profiles of benzene for the same directions

differ only slightly from their spherical average, the isotropic profile. The DCP with the scattering vector orthogonal to the molecular plane deviates more from the ICP than do those with $\mathbf{q}$ in the plane. The present ICP is in reasonable agreement with other calculations using wavefunctions of similar quality [16, 18]. All of these show a higher peak than experiment indicates [19]. Closer agree- ment between theory and experiment should be obtained by employing better quality wavefunctions in the calculations and correcting the experimental data for multiple scattering [3].

For a qualitative understanding of the relation between the DCP's and the electron moment distribution, the $J(\mathbf{0})$ surface was introduced and shown to be useful. It is our hope that experimental $J(\mathbf{0})$ surfaces will be constructed for those systems where experimental measurement of DCP's is feasible.
