# Electron momentum density and phase transition in SrO

R. Kumar$^{a}$, N. Munjal$^{b}$, G. Sharma$^{b*}$, V. Vyas$^{b}$,
M.S. Dhaka$^{c}$ and B.K. Sharma$^{a}$

$^{a}$Department of Physics, University of Rajasthan, Jaipur 302004, Rajasthan, India;
$^{b}$Department of Physics, Banasthali University, Banasthali 304022, Rajasthan, India;
$^{c}$Department of Physics, Engineering College Bikaner, Bikaner 334004, Rajasthan, India

(Received 18 January 2012; final version received 15 February 2012)

In this article, we present electron momentum density distribution and phase transition in SrO. The experimental values of momentum density have been measured using $5\text{Ci}\ ^{241}\text{Am}$ Compton spectrometer and analyzed using theoretical data obtained from the *ab-initio* linear combination of atomic orbitals method. The first-principles calculations of the total energy of SrO as a function of cell volume have also been carried out for the cubic rocksalt (B1) and cesium chloride (B2) phases. Several structural parameters, i.e. equilibrium lattice constant, transition pressure, bulk modulus, etc. of B1 and B2 phases have been calculated and compared with the previous investigations. We conclude that the stable phase of SrO is B1 and the phase transition from B1 to B2 occurs at 35.8 GPa.

**Keywords**: SrO; electron momentum density; phase transition; LCAO method

**PACS**: 71.15.Dx; 71.20.Nr; 78.70.-g

## 1. Introduction

The strontium oxide, i.e. SrO, is technologically important II–VI compound formed by an ionic bond between Sr and O atom. The geometric and electronic structure of SrO are very similar to MgO. It is a fundamental material for industrial sciences due to its wide applications in various technological fields such as electrochemistry, catalysis, and microelectronics. At normal conditions, it crystallizes in the B1 structure and shows structural phase transition to the B2 structure under pressure. Most of the earlier work on SrO is devoted to the phase transition, high-pressure structural, electronic and elastic properties, etc. (see, for example, [1–23]). However, study of the electron momentum density (EMD) distribution has not been attempted to our knowledge for SrO. It is well known that Compton scattering technique provides a useful test of the empirical, semi-empirical, or *ab-initio* methods employed to determine the EMD distribution in solids [24,25]. The Compton profile, $J(p_{z})$, is the EMD along the scattering vector direction and

it can be correlated with the experimentally measured double differential scattering cross section. The EMD, i.e. $\rho(\boldsymbol{p})$, is given as

$$
\rho(\boldsymbol{p}) \alpha \sum_{i}\left|\int \psi_{i}(\boldsymbol{r}) \exp (-i \boldsymbol{p} . \boldsymbol{r}) \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}\right|^{2} \tag{1}
$$

where $\psi_{i}(\boldsymbol{r})$ are the electron wave functions and summation extends over all occupied states [24,25].

In this article, we present the first-ever experimental Compton profile of SrO using 5 Ci $^{241}$Am gamma-ray Compton spectrometer. For the theoretical Compton profile, first-principles DFT–LCAO (DFT: density functional theory, LCAO: linear combination of atomic orbitals) method is employed. We also compare the nature of bonding in isostructural and isovalent II–VI compounds SrO and CaO by determining equal valence electron density (EVED) profiles. Further, the ionic model has been applied to estimate the charge transfer in SrO on compound formation. Besides these, we also perform first-principles total energy calculations of the two competing B1 and B2 structures of SrO to compute the equilibrium lattice constant, bulk modulus, its pressure derivative, and examine the structural phase transition.

This article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we give a brief description of the experiment. The details of theoretical calculations are presented in Section 3. In Section 4, we present and discuss results and conclusions are given in Section 5. Unless specified, all quantities are in atomic units (a.u.) where $e=\hbar=m=1$ and $c=137.036$, giving unit momentum $=1.9929 \times 10^{-24} \mathrm{~kg} \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$, unit energy $=27.212 \mathrm{eV}$, and unit length $=5.2918 \times 10^{-11} \mathrm{~m}$.

## 2. Experimental details
The measurement on SrO was performed using 5 Ci $^{241}$Am gamma-ray Compton spectrometer. The details of the experimental setup are available in Ref. [26]. The incident gamma-rays of $59.54 \mathrm{keV}$ were scattered at an angle $166^{\circ} \pm 3.0^{\circ}$ by the polycrystalline sample of SrO (pellet of $18 \mathrm{~mm}$ diameter, $3.2 \mathrm{~mm}$ thickness, and $1.5834 \mathrm{~g} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ effective density). The scattered radiation was analyzed using a high purity germanium detector (Canberra model, GL0110S) which was cooled with liquid nitrogen providing overall momentum resolution 0.6 a.u. The spectra were recorded with a multichannel analyzer with 4096 channels. The calibration of the spectrometer was checked regularly using weak $^{241}$Am gamma-ray source. The Compton spectra were measured around $36 \mathrm{~h}$ to collect 52,000 counts at the Compton peak. To deduce the true Compton profile, the raw data were processed for several systematic corrections like background, instrumental resolution, sample absorption, scattering cross section, and multiple scattering using the computer code of the Warwick group [27,28]. Finally, the corrected profile was normalized to 19.502 electrons in the range from 0 to $+7$ a.u., being the area of free-atom Compton profile [29] in the given range. The 1s electrons of Sr were neglected since they do not contribute in the experimental profile.

## 3. Computational details
The *ab-initio* LCAO method embodied in the CRYSTAL code [30] is used to compute the theoretical Compton profile and total energy for SrO. In this method, each crystalline

orbital, $\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{k})$, is a linear combination of Bloch functions, $\varphi_{\mu}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{k})$, defined in terms of local functions, $\varphi_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})$, normally referred as atomic orbitals. The local functions are expressed as linear combination of certain number of individually normalized Gaussian type functions. For Sr and O, the local functions were constructed from the Gaussian type functions (http://www.tcm.phy.cam.ac.uk). In the present calculations, the exchange scheme of Becke [31] and the correlation scheme of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) [32] are considered. The Hartree-Fock (HF) and hybrid B3PW [30,33] schemes are also used for the compound. The self-consistent calculations were performed considering $72k$ points in the irreducible Brillouin zone with tight tolerances. To achieve self-consistency, $45\%$ mixing of successive cycles was considered and was achieved within 23 cycles.

In the present first-principles calculations, we have considered the zero temperature. At this temperature, the thermodynamically stable phase is one with lowest enthalpy, i.e. $H=E+PV$ for a given pressure. The transition occurs when the enthalpy of the lower pressure phase coincides with some other phase. From Figure 6, it is clear that enthalpy for B1 phase is lower than that of B2. The transition pressure is obtained from crossing of the enthalpy $(H)$ versus pressure $(P)$ curves. We found that SrO transforms from B1 to B2 phase at 35.8 GPa. The present and earlier values of transition pressures are also listed in Table 2.

The third-order Birch-Murnaghan [43] isothermal EOS relating pressure and volume is given by

$$
P(V)=\frac{3 B_{0}}{2}\left[\left(\frac{V_{0}}{V}\right)^{7 / 3}-\left(\frac{V_{0}}{V}\right)^{5 / 3}\right]\left\{1+\frac{3}{4}\left(B_{0}^{\prime}-4\right)\left[\left(\frac{V_{0}}{V}\right)^{2 / 3}-1\right]\right\} \tag{3}
$$

Using the calculated values of $B_{0}$ and $B_{0}^{\prime}$, the pressure versus volume data for both phases were computed from the above equation. The pressure and volume curves for B1 and B2 phases are plotted in Figure 7. It indicates that volume for these structures decreases smoothly with increase in pressure and B1 phase of SrO is more compressible than B2. Our computed bulk modulus data for B1 (110 GPa) and B2 (135 GPa) structures support this finding.
