# Mechanical properties and mechanism of single crystal Cu pillar by *in-situ* TEM compression and molecular dynamics simulation

Kai-Peng Lin, Te-Hua Fang$^{*}$ and Ying-Jhin Lin

Department of Mechanical Engineering, National Kaohsiung University of Applied Sciences, Kaohsiung 807 , Taiwan

In this study, we investigate the mechanical properties of single-crystal copper (Cu) pillars. Critical deformation variations of Cu-pillared structures are estimated using in situ transmission electron microscopy compression tests and molecular dynamics simulations. The Young's moduli of Cu pillars with diameters of 2–6 nm were 90.2–124.5 GPa. The contact stiffnesses of the Cu pillars with diameters of 400 and 500 nm were 1.33 and 3.86 N/m, respectively; the Poisson's ratios for these pillars were 0.32 and 0.33, respectively. The values of the indented hardness of the Cu block were 0.27 and 1.09 GPa, respectively, while the values of Vickers hardness were 1.144 and 1.233 GPa, respectively. Through experimental work and molecular dynamics simulations, we demonstrate that Cu pillars exhibit internal stress transmission during compression. When compression reaches the maximum strain, it can be observed that Cu slips. Our results are useful for understanding the mechanical properties, contact, and local deformation of Cu pillars.

Keywords: Single crystal copper ; In-situ TEM; Molecular dynamics simulation; Vickers hardness; Young's modulus; Poisson's ratio

*Corresponding Author: Te-Hua Fang (Email: fang.tehua@msa.hinet.net)

### 1. Introduction

Due to the recent nanotechnological developments, studies on the mechanical properties of nanoscale materials have attracted significant research interest [1-3]. Tschopp et al. investigated non-Schmid parameters and described the calculated dislocation nucleation behavior for single-crystal orientations under tension, compression, and stress required for homogeneous nucleation of partial dislocations in single-crystal Cu under uniaxial loading [4]. Yang et al. conducted molecular dynamics simulations of single-crystal Cu under tension in the elastic region at a temperature of 0.1 K. The Young's moduli of single-crystal Cu nanorods are almost invariable with increasing cross-section size [5]. Li et al. investigated the mechanical properties of self-irradiated single-crystal Cu and found that the compressive Young's modulus of single-crystal Cu increases in the presence of the irradiation-induced defects [6].

Characterization of the mechanical properties of materials at the micro- and nanoscales is an active area of research in micro- and nano-systems. Further theoretical and experimental studies are necessary to understand the apparent surface stress and surface deformation, as well as their relation to surface morphology [7-13]. Stress–strain, plastic deformation, and hardening of high-symmetry single-crystal Cu have been studied by in situ micropillar compression and simulations [14]. The nanoindentation processes of single-crystal and nanocrystalline Cu have been studied using molecular dynamics simulations. Larger grain sizes and dislocations were found at the {111} close-packed plane and {100} plane [15]. The Young's modulus of nanocrystalline Cu measured by nanoindentation was found to be in the range of the reference values for polycrystalline Cu [16-17].

The deformation and hardness of the thin films and solids have been measured using nanoindentation [18-23]. More recently, tiny dynamic mechanical deformations were

experimentally measured using in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) compression tests [24].

The present study investigates the surface properties, nanomechanical properties, and deformation mechanisms of single-crystal Cu pillars and the films by molecular dynamics simulations, atomic force microscopy, in situ TEM compression, and nanoindentation. The Vickers hardness, Young's modulus, and stiffness are also presented and discussed.

## 2. Simulation and Experimental

We first simulated the compression of single-crystal Cu in the <111> direction. Figure 1(a) shows a physical model of a single-crystal Cu nanowire compression test. The colored segments of the nanowires at each end of the figure are fixed layers used to compress the nanowires. The nanowire structures have diameters (d) of 2, 3, 4, and 6 nm, length of 1.8 nm, lattice constant of 0.3639 nm and a cone angle (θ) of 2°–3°. A strain rate of $10^9/s^{-1}$ is applied. The integration time step of our simulation is 0.01 ps/step, and the temperature is 300 K. The compression was modeled using multi-body embedded atom method (EAM) potential energy. Based on the model of Cu–Cu interatomic interactions, we revised the potential. The EAM potential is expressed as [25, 26]

$$
E_{i}=F_{i}\left(\sum_{j \neq i} \rho_{i}\left(r_{i j}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j \neq i} \phi_{i j}\left(r_{i j}\right)
\tag{1}
$$

where $E_{i}$ is the total potential energy; $F_{i}$ is the embedding energy of atom ; $\phi_{ij}$ is the short-range pair potential interaction between the atoms i and j; rij is the separation distance between atoms i and j, and i is the electron density at atom i due to all of the other atoms.

Next, we investigated the mechanical properties of single-crystal Cu oriented in the <111> direction. The area and thickness of the single-crystal Cu substrate (E-Light Technology Inc.,

Taiwan) were $1\ \mathrm{cm}^2$ and $1\ \mathrm{mm}$, respectively. Nanopillars for in situ single-crystal Cu sectioning were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB, FEI, Nova 200) of Ga ions. The diameters of the single-crystal Cu nanopillars were 400 and $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ with a length of $1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

The experimental model of the single-crystal Cu nanopillar compression test is shown in Figure 1(b). Mechanical testing was performed inside a TEM system (JEOL, JEM-2010) using an in situ TEM compressive system (Hysitron, PI 95 PicoIndenter). This dynamic compressive system achieves accurate nanometer-scale positioning using a piezoceramic method. The dynamic mechanical properties of single-crystal Cu pillars were measured in diamond indenter compression tests.

The nanomechanical properties of the single-crystal Cu substrates were investigated by a nanoindentation system (Hysitron TriboScope) using a Berkovich tip with a radius of $\sim$$100\ \mathrm{nm}$. The surface roughness and morphology were observed using an atomic force microscope (AFM, Bruker Innova) in the contact mode. The spring constant and tip radius of the AFM silicon probe were $0.35\ \mathrm{N/m}$ and $2\ \mathrm{nm}$, respectively.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1 Compression process and mechanical properties during molecular dynamics simulation

In this section, we describe the simulated compression stress–strain curves of single-crystal Cu oriented in the $<111>$ direction at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ (Figure 2). The stress–strain curves in Figure 2 indicate that the compressive stress and Young's modulus increase with increasing radius of the single-crystal Cu nanopillars after compression. The stress and strain of the single-crystal Cu nanopillars also increase with increasing size. When the material reaches its maximum compressive stress, the single-crystal Cu nanopillars are destroyed and the compressive stress

sharply drops (Figure 2). The Young's moduli of the four types of single-crystal Cu nanopillars in the strain range from 0.03 to 0.05 are 90.2, 103.6, 112.4, and 124.5 GPa, respectively. Table 1 compares the Young's modulus (E) of the single-crystal Cu nanopillars in the present and previous studies. Our simulation results closely match those of previous studies [1].

Figure 3 shows the plastic deformation of single-crystal Cu in the <111> direction after compression of the nanopillars with diameters 2.0, 3.0, 4.0 and 6.0 nm. The dynamic results corresponding to the four diameters show that the nanopillars begin to slip at strain 0.06; deformation is more significant for the nanopillar with a 2.0 -nm diameter because the radius of the material is relatively small and easily deformed. The single-crystal Cu nanopillars exhibit bending fluctuation and fail catastrophically at strain 0.21. Because single-crystal Cu is a soft material, nanopillars grow in size following compression.

### 3.2. Dynamic deformation of single crystal Cu pillars during in-situ TEM compression

Figure 4 shows the deformation of single-crystal Cu nanopillars (diameters = 400 and 500 nm) under compression. The probe initially contacts the single-crystal Cu nanopillars before compression (Figures 4 a1 and b1). When the probe is compressed obliquely to the right, the diameters of Cu nanopillars increase with increasing compression displacement. Between zero-compression and maximum compression, the Cu nanopillars increase in diameter from 400 nm and 500 nm to 416 nm and 511 nm, respectively (Figures 4 a2 and b2). Figures 4 a3 and b3 show that the diameter of the single-crystal Cu nanopillars will gradually reduce to its initial value after the needle is slowly withdrawn. The single-crystal Cu exhibited slight plastic deformation after compression; single-crystal Cu nanopillars cannot be restored to their original diameter. The diameters of the nanopillars were 411 nm and 505 nm.

Figure 5a shows the normal force-displacement curves of Cu pillars (diameters = 400 and 500 nm) under a compressive load. The displacement of the pillars increased with increasing compressive force. The compressive force of the indenter induced elastic and plastic deformation of the pillars. In the initial linear elastic regime of the loading process, the slope of the force-displacement curve was nearly constant. The Poisson's ratios of the nanopillars with 400- and 500-nm diameter were 0.32 and 0.33, respectively (Figure 5a). The calculation of Poisson's ratio depends on the value of transverse strain or axial strain (in the linear elastic regime) of the material at the tip during in situ compression. At larger displacements, the slope slightly decreased, thus indicating plastic deformation. The force-displacement curve reversed during the unloading process. The values of contact stiffness for Cu nanopillars with 400- and 500-nm diameter during unloading were 1.33 and 3.86 N/m, respectively. To understand the stable behavior of the Cu nanopillars, the average critical displacement of the 400- and 500-nm diameter nanopillars were measured as 132.20 and 63.24 nm, respectively.

### 3.3. Stress-strain curves of single crystal Cu nanopillars during in-situ TEM compression

Figure 5b shows the stress-strain curves of Cu pillars (diameters = 400 and 500 nm) under a compressive load. During compression, the compressive stress of single-crystal Cu nanopillars decreased with increasing diameter (Figure 5b). Single-crystal Cu nanopillars with a small diameter displayed a uniform internal stress distribution. The critical strains for 400 and 500 nm diameter Cu nanopillars were 0.087 and 0.043, respectively. The yield strength (Y) of single nanopillars was estimated from the average compression stress and strain data. The yield strength of the nanopillars varied from 0.25 GPa at 500 nm to 0.42 GPa at 400 nm; the yield strength of single-crystal Cu nanopillars decreased with increasing diameter.

### 3.4. Mechanical properties of single-crystal Cu substrate measured using nanoindentation

Figure 6a shows the load–displacement (P–h) curves of a single-crystal Cu block substrate as measured by nanoindentation at various indentation forces. The indentation hardness (H) and the elastic modulus (E) were calculated using the Oliver–Pharr method [27, 28]. The contact area (A) was calculated from the contact depth (hc) using the precalibrated shape function (A = f(hc)) of the Berkovich indenter. The contact depth (hc) is given by [29]

$$
h_{c}=h_{\max }-\varepsilon \frac{P_{\max }}{S} \tag{2}
$$

where hmax is the maximum indenter penetration depth; $\varepsilon=0.72$ for the Berkovich indenter tip; S is the contact stiffness during unloading; and Pmax is the maximum indentation load. S ranged from 3.93 to 5.07 N/m and H ranged from 0.27 to 1.09 GPa.

Figure 6b shows an AFM micrograph of a residual local indentation on a single-crystal Cu block substrate surface subjected to a 0.25 mN indentation load. Pile-up behavior, visible in the image, occurred around the contact impression of the indentation, thus deforming the surface and thereby increasing the actual contact area and depth.

Figures 6c-d show the nanoindentation hardness and Young's modulus as a function of penetration depth for a single-crystal Cu block substrate as measured by nanoindentation at various indentation forces. The hardness and Young's modulus of the single-crystal Cu substrate decreased with increasing downforce (Figures 6c–6d). Because Cu is a soft material, its material structure changes under large compression forces, which results in the observed decrease in Young's modulus and hardness.

### 3.5. Mechanical properties of a single-crystal Cu substrate measured using the Vickers microhardness test

Figure 7a shows an optical image of an indented single-crystal Cu substrate (111); a square-pyramidal residual indent remained after the Vickers test. Then, the accumulation along the diagonal indicates that the material will have a dislocation phenomenon and instability. Vickers hardness is a characteristic property that helps researchers evaluates the plastic behavior of solid films and surfaces. The Vickers hardness function (HV) is the load divided by the surface area of the indenter, multiplied by a constant:

$$
HV = 1.8544 \frac{P}{d^2} \tag{3}
$$

where P is the load (in kg) and d is the average length of the two residual indentation diagonals (in mm). The Vickers microhardness of single-crystal Cu ranged from 1.144 to 1.233 GPa under a load ranging from 10 to 100 g (Fig. 7b). The estimated hardness (H) and Young's modulus (E) of the single-crystal Cu pillars and films evaluated in the present and previous studies are compared in Table 1. Our results closely match those of previous studies [13, 30] and confirm that single-crystal Cu is a soft material.

### 3.6. Comparison of the dynamic process and mechanical properties of single-crystal Cu nanopillars using experimental and simulation results

From the simulation and experimental results that single-crystal copper nanopillars cannot clearly see the material inside the stress transmission and slip phenomenon in the real-time dynamic compression. This is because the Cu nanopillars are too large and too thick. Therefore, this study describes the dynamic deformation of Cu nanopillars under compression using simulations. The Young's moduli of the simulated single-crystal Cu nanopillars were 90.2–124.5 GPa. These values are similar to the Young's moduli reported in the literature [1, 31]. The

experimentally derived Young's modulus was lower than the simulated Young's modulus; we attribute this difference to changes in the material due to the effect of cutting nanopillars by FIB.

## 4. Conclusion

We studied the dynamic mechanical properties and deformation of single-crystal Cu via in situ TEM compression testing and molecular dynamics simulations. The Young's moduli of the simulated Cu pillars with diameters of 2–6 nm were 90.2–124.5 GPa. The values of contact stiffness of Cu pillars with diameters of 400 and 500 nm were 1.33 and 3.86 N/m, respectively, while the Poisson's ratios were 0.32 and 0.33, respectively. The Vickers microhardness of the single-crystal Cu substrate was 1.14–1.23 GPa, indicating that single-crystal Cu is a soft material. Through experimental work and molecular dynamics simulations, we demonstrate that Cu pillars exhibit the phenomenon of internal stress transmission during compression. When compression reaches the maximum strain, we demonstrate that Cu slips. Our results are useful for understanding the mechanical properties, contact behavior, and local deformation of single-crystal Cu pillars and substrate.

## Mechanical properties and mechanism of single crystal Cu pillar by *in-situ* TEM compression and molecular dynamics simulation

Kai-Peng Lin, Te-Hua Fang, and Ying-Jhin Lin

This study explores stress transfer, mechanical properties and bending deformation of single crystal Cu nanopillar by *in-situ* TEM compression and molecular dynamics simulation. The Vickers hardness and Young's modulus are also measured and discussed.

![](./images/813060405822750722_14.jpg)
