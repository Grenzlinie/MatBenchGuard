# Young's Modulus of Helical Multi-Shell Au Nanowires by Molecular Dynamics

## Problem background
Understanding the mechanical properties of metallic nanowires is essential for their use in nanoscale devices and sensors. Gold (Au) nanowires, in particular, can adopt helical multi-shell (HMS) structures whose atomic arrangement differs from bulk fcc crystals. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations allow the study of how the atomic structure influences nanomechanical properties, such as the Young's modulus, under controlled tensile loading. This task investigates two HMS Au nanowire models, differing in the interatomic spacing of the central atom row, by computing their Young's modulus from stress–strain curves obtained from MD tensile simulations with the modified embedded-atom method (MEAM) potential. The results for a range of low temperatures provide a benchmark for nanomechanics simulation protocols.

## Model atomic structures
Both Model‑1 and Model‑2 belong to the helical multi‑shell (HMS) family. Each nanowire consists of a single central atom row running along the nanowire axis, surrounded by a single outer helical shell. The outer shell is constructed by rolling a triangular‑lattice sheet (the Au {111} plane) with the chiral vector **(7,3)** . The shell comprises **seven helical atomic rows** that spiral around the axis. The triangular lattice constant (nearest‑neighbor distance) is **2.88 Å**.

The structural differences between the two models are given in the table below; all values are taken from Tables I and II of the paper.

| Property | Model‑1 | Model‑2 |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Total number of Au atoms | 168 | 167 |
| Central row interatomic distance | 2.88 Å | 3.03 Å |
| Outer shell circumference | 26.57 Å | 26.57 Å *(same (7,3) chirality)* |
| Cross‑sectional area (target) | 56.16 Å² | 56.16 Å² |
| Length (target) | 62.75 Å | 62.75 Å |

The cross‑sectional area and length are equilibrium values after the equilibration stages; the initial constructed structure must be designed so that after the two‑stage equilibration it approaches these dimensions. The total number of atoms (168 and 167) is a known constraint from the paper and can be used to validate the generated structure.

## Key geometric parameters
For both Model‑1 and Model‑2, the cross‑sectional area of the nanowire is **56.16 Å²** and the initial length is **62.75 Å**.  
- Axial stress = applied axial force / 56.16 Å².  
- Engineering strain = elongation / 62.75 Å.  

These values are taken from the paper’s Table I and are required to convert the MD force and displacement data into stress–strain curves.

## Approach
The approach consists of three stages: building the atomic structures, performing MD tensile simulations, and extracting the Young's modulus.

- **Structure construction:** The outer shell is generated by taking a rectangular patch of the triangular lattice (nearest‑neighbor distance 2.88 Å) of width equal to the circumference (26.57 Å) and height equal to the target length (≈62.75 Å), and then rolling it into a cylinder. The rolling axis is parallel to the vertical direction of the patch, which becomes the nanowire axis. The sheet must be oriented so that the (7,3) chiral vector is parallel to the circumference; consequently there will be seven atomic rows repeating around the circumference. The central single‑atom row is then placed along the axis, with the interatomic distance of 2.88 Å (Model‑1) or 3.03 Å (Model‑2). The initial coordinates should be saved in LAMMPS data file format for a non‑periodic simulation box (e.g. boundary `f f p` or `s s p`). The generated structures must have **168 atoms for Model‑1** and **167 atoms for Model‑2**; an agent may use this atom count as a validation of the construction.  
- **Molecular dynamics:** For each model and each temperature (25 to 200 K in 25 K steps), LAMMPS runs with the MEAM potential for Au (Baskes, 1992). The MD time step is 2.0 fs. Equilibration proceeds in two stages: first, the central atom row is constrained to the axis to relax helical distortions; then, the atoms at both ends are constrained. Temperature is controlled using a Nosé‑Hoover thermostat (e.g., damping parameter 0.1 ps). After equilibration, an axial tensile force is applied. The force increases by 0.005 nN every 2.5 × 10⁴ steps until fracture, while the axial displacement of the ends is recorded.  
- **Modulus extraction:** From the force–displacement data, compute engineering stress σ = F / 56.16 Å² and engineering strain ε = ΔL / 62.75 Å. From the small‑strain linear regime of each stress–strain curve, obtain the Young's modulus as the slope dσ/dε.

The primary comparison is the temperature‑dependent modulus for the two HMS models, which reveals the effect of the central interatomic spacing on nanoscale stiffness.
