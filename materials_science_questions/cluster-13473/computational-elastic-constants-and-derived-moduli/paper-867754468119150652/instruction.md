# Computing Elastic Response Functions and Lengthscale in 2D Unstressed Spring Networks

## Problem background
Amorphous solids, such as glasses, gels, and granular materials, exhibit a breakdown of continuum elasticity at intermediate lengthscales. The spatial response to a local force dipole is not uniform but characterized by a characteristic lengthscale ℓ_c. In unstressed elastic networks, this lengthscale is expected to depend on the mean coordination excess δz = z − 2d, where z is the mean coordination and d is the spatial dimension. Understanding and quantifying ℓ_c is important for connecting elasticity to mechanical stability and vibrational anomalies (e.g., the boson peak) in disordered solids. This task investigates the relationship between ℓ_c and δz by computing the bond-response function C(r) from numerically generated two-dimensional harmonic spring networks and extracting the lengthscale.

## Approach
The task is based on the theoretical framework of harmonic spring networks at zero pressure. The networks are generated by starting from a highly coordinated lattice and randomly removing bonds while preserving mechanical rigidity and zero stress, following a protocol that ensures a prescribed mean coordination. For each network, the dynamical matrix (the Hessian of the elastic energy) and the geometric operator (relating bond-length changes to particle displacements) are constructed. The response to a unit dipolar force applied to a bond is obtained by solving the linear system M δR = S^T |α⟩, where M is the dynamical matrix, S is the geometric operator, and |α⟩ selects a single bond. The resulting displacement fields are then used to compute the matrix elements ⟨α|𝒜|β⟩ = ⟨α| S M^{-1} S^T |β⟩, which describe how the length of bond β changes under a dipole on bond α. For each pair of bonds separated by distance r, the squared matrix element is computed, and the bond-response function C(r) is obtained by averaging over all bond pairs in distance bins. In the continuum limit, C(r) is expected to scale as r^{-2d}. A crossover from this asymptotic behavior gives the lengthscale ℓ_c, which can be extracted as the position of the maximum of r^{2d} C(r) (in 2D, r^4 C(r)). The procedure is repeated for networks with different δz values, and the relation between ℓ_c and δz is inferred from the resulting data.
