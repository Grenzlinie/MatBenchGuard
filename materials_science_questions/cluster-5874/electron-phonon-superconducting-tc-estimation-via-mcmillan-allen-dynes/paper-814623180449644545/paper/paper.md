# Localization effects on superconductivity in homogeneous metallic glasses

S. J. Poon*
Ames Laboratory-U.S. Department of Energy, Iowa State University,
Ames, Iowa 50011
(Received 26 December 1984)

It is pointed out that localization effects on bulk superconductivity do exist and can be significant in homogeneous amorphous alloys (or typical metallic glasses) whose upper critical fields appear to follow the standard Werthamer-Helfand-Hohenberg-Maki (WHHM) curves. Owing to the similarity in the temperature dependence of critical fields predicted by the Fukuyama-Ebisawa-Maekawa theory of the weakly localized regime and the WHHM results over a very wide temperature range, these effects have escaped notice heretofore. Comparison of the electronic density of states obtained from specific-heat measurement (absence of field-induced delocalization effects) and the "apparent" one derived from fitting critical-field data reveals the aforementioned effects.

The diffusive motion of electrons in disordered systems has been shown to lead to quantum corrections which affect bulk superconductivity in the weakly localized regime (WLR). $^{1}$ For two-dimensional homogeneous thin films, there are now convincing experimental results $^{2,3}$ which verify the theoretical predictions of Maekawa, Ebisawa, and Fukuyama $^{4}$ in the WLR. These include the predictions of a suppression of the critical temperatures $(T_{c})$ and the appearance of positive curvatures in the upper critical field $[H_{c 2}(T)]$ curves as the thickness of the film is decreased. For bulk materials, appreciable suppression of $T_{c}$ is also indicated by the theoretical results. $^{1}$ But because this suppression depends on both the electronic parameters and interaction constants, it is difficult to perform the controlled experiments for testing the theories as in two-dimensional systems. Naturally, a more feasible approach would be to investigate the shape of $H_{c 2}(T)$ over a wide temperature range. Coffey, Muttalib, and Levin $^{5}$ have indeed predicted enhancements of critical fields above the standard Werthamer-Helfand-Hohenberg-Maki (WHHM) curves $^{6}$ as a result of magnetic field-induced delocalization effects on the Coulomb repulsion. From a systematic study of $H_{c 2}(T)$ in a large number of homogeneous disordered alloys, however, it is found that the measured $H_{c 2}(T)$ in a large number of homogeneous disordered alloys $^{7}$ are in very good agreement with the WHHM results. These findings are rather puzzling in view of the success of the weak localization (WL) theories $^{1,4,8}$ in accounting for the superconductivity of thin films $^{2,3}$ and magnetoresistance of metallic glasses. $^{9}$ On the other hand, a more recent ab initio treatment $^{1}$ of weak localization effects on bulk superconductivity predicts a somewhat different temperature dependence of $H_{c 2}$ than the one in Ref. 5. Moreover, direct comparison of experimental results with the Fukuyama-Ebisawa-Maekawa (FEM) theory $^{1}$ is possible because the $H_{c 2}(T)$ equation contains experimentally accessible parameters. It is the purpose of this paper to point out that because of the similarity in the forms of $H_{c 2}(T)$ predicted by the FEM and WHHM theories in the WLR, the possibility of localization effects has been overlooked heretofore. A critical evaluation of these effects is suggested for disordered alloys, particularly in metallic glasses where the electrical resistivity values $(\rho \geq 150 \mu \Omega \mathrm{cm})$ are quite high. Examples will be given and the implications of our findings will be discussed.

The randomness in disordered systems is treated perturbatively with respect to the parameter $(1 / 2 \pi \epsilon_{F} \tau)$, where $\epsilon_{F}$ and $\tau$ are the Fermi energy and lifetime of the electron, respectively. $^{1}$ The FEM results on the critical temperature and upper critical field are given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln \left(\frac{T}{T_{c}}\right)= & \psi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{8 \pi T}\right) \\
& +\frac{\sqrt{3} a_{0}}{16 \pi\left(\epsilon_{F} \tau\right)^{2} T} \psi^{\prime}\left[\frac{1}{2}+\left(a_{0} / 8 \pi T\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\ln \left(\frac{T_{c}}{T_{c 0}}\right)=\frac{-3 \sqrt{3}}{8 \pi\left(\epsilon_{F} \tau\right)^{2}}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{g^{*}}\right)^{2}+2 \pi\left[\frac{1}{g}-\left(\frac{\mu^{*}}{g}\right)^{2} \ln \left(\Theta_{D} \tau\right)\right]\right\},
$$

where
$$
g^{*} / g=\left[1+\mu \ln \left(\epsilon_{F} / \Theta_{D}\right)\right] /\left[1+\mu \ln \left(\epsilon_{F} \tau\right)\right].
$$

We have chosen $k_{B}=\hbar=m=1$, where $m$ is the mass of the electron. The symbols in Eq. (1) have the following meaning: $\psi$ is the digamma function. $\Theta_{D}$ is the Debye temperature.

$g=\lambda_{e-\mathrm{ph}}-\mu^{*}$, where $\lambda_{e-\mathrm{ph}}$ is the electron-phonon coupling constant which is assumed to be independent of randomness. This condition, however, is not critical for our later discussion. $\mu^{*}(\simeq 0.13)$ is the renormalized Coulomb potential and is related to the Coulomb potential $\mu(\simeq 0.5)$ in the usual way. $a=4 D e H / c$ where $D$ is the diffusivity. In the proper treatment of $H_{c 2}$, the renormalized value should be used in Eq. (1). In the "clean limit," $D=D_{0}$. Hence, disorder reduces the critical temperature from its value $T_{c 0}$ in the "clean limit" ( $\epsilon_{F} \tau$ large) according to Eq. (1b). Equation (1a) can be interpreted as the following: if the last term on the right-hand side were absent, then $H_{c 2}(T)$ would be the same as in the classical WHHM theory with critical temperature $T_{c}$ and diffusivity

$$
D=D_{0}\left[1-(3 \sqrt{3} / 4 \pi)\left(1 / \epsilon_{F} \tau\right)^{2}\right].
$$

The last term, therefore, appears to give rise to enhancement in $H_{c 2}$ due to magnetic field-induced delocalization effects.

To evaluate the "enhancement" in $H_{c 2}(T)$, numerical results are needed to compare with the WHHM curves. For typical metallic glasses ${ }^{7,10-13}$ we have chosen $\lambda_{e-\mathrm{ph}} \simeq 1$, $\Theta_{D} \simeq 0.017 \mathrm{eV}, \epsilon_{F} \simeq 5 \mathrm{eV}$. To estimate the range of $\epsilon_{F} \tau$ values for typical disordered alloys, we have surveyed a large number of metallic glasses $^{10-13}$ with resistivity greater than $\sim 150 \mu \Omega \mathrm{cm}$. Taking $D_{0}=2 \epsilon_{F} \tau / 3 m^{*}$ with $m^{*}$ being the effective mass of the electron, $\epsilon_{F} \tau$ is then given by the equation $1.3 D m^{*}=\left(\epsilon_{F} \tau\right)[1-(3 \sqrt{3} / 4 \pi)$ $\left.\times\left(1 / \epsilon_{F} \tau\right)^{2}\right]$ with $D=1 / e^{2} \rho N(0)$ and $N(0)$ is the bare density of states at the Fermi level. There are renormalization factors involved, but they cancel each other in the equations. Typical values of $D$ are in the range $\sim 0.5-1.2$ $\mathrm{cm}^{2} / \mathrm{s}$. The mean effective mass $m^{*}$ for $d$-band metals is taken as $\sim 1.5 .^{14}$ Combining all these numbers, $\epsilon_{F} \tau$ is found to be in the range $\sim 1.3-2.5$. As a result, glassy metals studied so far fall in the regime where it is difficult to identify localization effects on superconductivity by analyzing $H_{c 2}$ data alone. This is also indicated in Fig. 2.

The Pauli-paramagnetic parameter $\alpha$ obtained from curve fitting which gives the "apparent" field gradient $\left(d H_{c 2} / d T\right)$ at $T_{c}$ is actually influenced by field-induced delocalization effects. Therefore, we denote this parameter by $\alpha_{H}$. Following the conventional results, one then obtains an "apparent" renormalized density of states (states/eV atom) via the expression

$$
N_{H}(0)=-\left(9.46 × 10^{-7}\right)(M / \rho d)\left(d H_{c_{2}} / d T\right)_{T_{c}},
$$

where $M$ is the gram molecular mass, $d$ is the density in $\mathrm{gm} / \mathrm{cm}^{3}, \rho$ is in $\Omega \mathrm{cm}$, and the field gradient is given in $\mathrm{kOe} / \mathrm{K}$. On the other hand, the density of states $N_{\gamma}(0)$ determined from specific-heat measurement is free of delocalization effects. It can be related to the diffusivity (renormalized) appearing in Eq. (1a) via $1 / D \sim \rho N_{\gamma}(0)$. The latter defines a parameter $\alpha_{H=0}$, similar to $\alpha_{H}$, but in zero field. Hence, one can examine the degree to which $\alpha_{H}$ affects superconductivity by comparing the values of $N_{H}(0)$ and $N_{\gamma}$, or equivalently $\alpha_{H}$ and $\alpha_{H=0}$.

Since the phenomenological parameter $\alpha_{H}$ contains all the field-dependent localization effects, it needs some discussion. In addition to the modifications of density of states (self-energy correction) and Coulomb interaction (vertex correction), there are additional depairing effects due to the retardation in the dynamically screened Coulomb interaction (self-energy correction) according to FEM, $^{1,4}$ the fielddependent term in Eq. (1a) is due entirely to the latter effects. This is because field-induced delocalization enhances the screening of Coulomb interaction. On the other hand, all three effects contribute to the depression of $T_{c}$ [Eq. (1b)]. This is true only in the case of strong spin-orbit scattering. A qualitatively similar situation is expected even for finite values of Pauli-paramagnetic limiting parameter $\alpha$ and spin-orbit parameter $\lambda_{\text {s.o. }}{ }^{1}$ Because of the spin-splitting effects in the latter case, the interpretation of $\alpha_{H}$ will not be as simple as for the first case. A theoretical treatment of the general case is underway. $^{1}$ We have plotted in Fig. 2 the ratio $\alpha_{H} / \alpha_{H=0}$ as a function of $\epsilon_{F} \tau$ using results depicted in Fig. 1. The reduction in reduced $T_{c}$ is also shown as $\Delta t$. These results certainly depend on the various parameters being chosen, especially on $\lambda_{e-\mathrm{ph}}$. The experimental verification of the FEM results on bulk alloys can be carried out in systems where both transport ($\rho$ and $H_{c2}$) and specific-heat measurements have been made on the same sample of a given composition. For glassy alloys, it is preferable to use samples in ribbon form prepared by melt spinning. These samples tend to have rather well-defined geometry and reproducibility in their properties which minimize the uncertainties in the determination of $\rho$. If such criteria are used, then only scanty data are available in the literature. A systematic study of glassy Zr-Cu alloys was made by Samwer and Löhneysen. $^{11}$ We obtain the "apparent" density of states $N_H(0)$ by fitting their $H_{c2}$ data to the WHHM curves over the entire temperature range, down to $t \simeq 0$. The fits are found to be in excellent agreement with experimental results. Values of $\alpha_H$ and $\lambda_{\text{s.o.}}$ are listed in Table I. We have used the $\rho$ values at 4.2 K which were reported to have been overestimated by $\sim 15$ to $20\%$. This was a result of measuring the thickness of the ribbons mechanically, for example, by using a micrometer. $^{11}$ On the other hand, measuring the density and mass-per-unit length of the ribbons gives a very accurate conversion from resistance $R$ to resistivity $\rho$. This technique was utilized by the authors of Ref. 10 in their study of Zr-Cu alloy ribbons. Indeed, the disagreement between the two sets of reported values amounts of $\sim 20$ to $30\%$, with the $\rho$ values in Ref. 10 being lower. Such discrepancies are also found in several metallic glass systems recently studied by us. Strictly speaking, the $\rho$ values determined by the more accurate technique are still higher than the actual values (see comment in Ref. 10). Even if we use the overestimated $\rho$ values to determine the density of states denoted by $N_H^1(0)$, the latter are already somewhat higher than $N_\gamma(0)$ determined from specific heats (Table I). Including a minimum reduction of $\sim 15\%$ in $\rho$ (values from Ref. 11) to obtain $N_H^2(0)$ clearly increases the ratio $\alpha_H/\alpha_{H=0}$ by an appreciable amount. The uncertainties listed in Table I refer to additional corrections if one uses the $\rho$ values from Ref. 10. A qualitative comparison of these numbers with the results depicted in Fig. 2 suggests consistency in the present analysis, since the superconducting parameters in Zr-Cu alloys are similar to the ones used in obtaining the curves in Figs. 1 and 2. Other alloys with visual $(dH_{c2}/dT)_{T_c}$, $\rho$, and $N_\gamma(0)$ data on the same samples are the Mo-Ru-B glasses. $^{12}$ For these alloys, even without correcting for both the field gradients which normally amount to $\sim 10$ to $15\%$ enhancement over the visual gradients in 4d alloys $^7$ and the actual $\rho$ values, it already gives $N_H(0) \geqslant N_\gamma(0)$. Thus, one again expects $\alpha_H/\alpha_{H=0} \geqslant 1.2$ when appropriate corrections are made for these alloys. Summarizing, the present analysis based on published results suggests evidence for WL effects on superconductivity in metallic glasses.

A comment should be made on how renormalization effects due to electron-phonon and electron-electron interactions $^{15}$ can affect our results. In the presence of these effects, $N_H(0)$ is still given by the "apparent" field gradients via the usual relation. Moreover, it was found that corrections due to renormalization effects only changed the $(dH_{c2}/dT)_{T_c}$ values obtained from curve fitting by less than $2\%$. Strong coupling corrections should also be minimal since the alloys discussed above are weak-coupling superconductors.

Although we have only used metallic glasses as examples in the present analysis, similar results are expected for disordered crystalline alloys with high electrical resistivity (say $\rho \geqslant 150\ \mu\Omega$ cm) so that $\epsilon_{F\tau}$ is close to unity. An important finding is that the precursor to localization in amorphous superconductors might be the main constraint on the critical temperatures of these materials. It is interesting to note that the critical temperatures $T_{c0}$ of amorphous superconductors in the absence of WL effects can be as high as those found in crystalline alloys and intermetallic compounds with $\epsilon_{F\tau} \geqslant 5$. This implies that the electron-phonon coupling strength is rather insensitive to disorder. Certainly, a systematic study of localization effects on superconductivity is needed to unravel the relationship between these two important phenomena in bulk materials.
