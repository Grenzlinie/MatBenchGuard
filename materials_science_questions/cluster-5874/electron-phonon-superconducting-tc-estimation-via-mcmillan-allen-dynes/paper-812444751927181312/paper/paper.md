# SUPERCONDUCTIVITY AND OPTIMUM ELECTRONEGATIVITY

SHINICHI ICHIKAWA

Department of Chemical and Nuclear Engineering, University of California, Santa Barbara, CA 93106, U.S.A.

(Received 17 February 1989; accepted 19 April 1989)

Abstract—It is observed that the optimum electronegativity for superconductive metals and binary alloys is about 4 eV by Mulliken's scale. This critical value is at the midpoint in the span of metal electronegativity values. It is suggested that the electronegativity equilibration scheme in harmony with density functional formalism could be useful for the systematic understanding of superconductive materials.

Keywords: Superconductivity, Mulliken electronegativity, density functional theory.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

In a recent report by Luo and Wang [1], it was shown that superconductive elements have electronegativity values that are concentrated within a range near the center of Pauling's scale [2]. Meanwhile, among the several concepts of electronegativity suggested to this day, Mulliken's definition [3] seems to be the most physically sound and it is the one that is consistent with the density functional formalism as discussed below. In this report, it is shown that Mulliken's concept is not only useful for setting up a criterion for superconductive metal elements but also valid in establishing a standard for superconductive binary alloys in terms of the equilibrium electronegativities.

## 2. MULLIKEN ELECTRONEGATIVITY

According to Mulliken, the electronegativity of an atom $\chi$ is the arithmetic average of the first ionization potential $IP$ and the electron affinity $EA$

$$
\chi=\frac{1}{2}(IP+EA). \tag{1}
$$

Although the values of the ionization potentials are almost universally agreed upon for their exactness, the electron affinities are still not certain for many elements as the experimental measurements are difficult and lack accuracy. This is the main reason why the simple definition of eqn (1) has not been utilized more often than it should have been in the past. Nevertheless, the most recent critical review [4] suggests a set of recommended electron affinity values which are used in the analysis to follow.

The Mulliken's concept was recently justified by the density functional theory [5]. This is briefly explained in the following. In the density functional formalism due to Hohenberg and Kohn [6], the chemical potential $\mu$ is introduced as the Lagrange multiplier which ensures that the number of particles will be conserved in the minimization of the energy. That is, the chemical potential can be expressed as the derivative of the ground state energy $E$ with respect to the number of electrons $N$. On the other hand, Iczkowsky and Margrave [7] have shown that the electronegativity can be written as the negative of that derivative. Thus we have the identity

$$
\chi=-\mu=-\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial N}\right)_{V}, \tag{2}
$$

where $V$ is the nuclear potential held constant. The Mulliken electronegativity of eqn (1) is simply the finite difference approximation to the derivative of eqn (2). More detailed argument on this is given elsewhere [8]. Hence, one can refer to electronegativity and chemical potential interchangeably.

## 3. ELECTRONEGATIVITY OF THE BINARY SYSTEM

When two atoms of different electronegativities are brought together, charge transfer occurs until an equilibrium is reached, at which point the binary system attains a unique electronegativity $\chi_{\text{eq}}$. This intuitive picture was shown to be consistent with the density functional formalism [9]. Since the amount of charge donated or accepted by an atom in the formation of a diatomic molecule is, in general, small compared with the total number of electrons in the atoms involved, one could write the energy $E$ as a quadratic function of the charge $q$

$$
E=\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial q}\right)_{q=0} \cdot q+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} E}{\partial q^{2}}\right)_{q=0} \cdot q^{2}. \tag{3}
$$

Also the hardness of the atom $\eta(=IP-EA)$ can be defined [10] as

$$
\eta=\left(\frac{\partial^{2} E}{\partial N^{2}}\right)_{V}. \tag{4}
$$

Thus from eqns (2)-(4)

$$
E=\chi^{0} q+\frac{1}{2} \eta^{0} q^{2}, \tag{5}
$$

where $\chi^{0}$ and $\eta^{0}$ are for neutral atoms. Therefore,

$$
\chi=\chi^{0}+\eta^{0} q. \tag{6}
$$

From the principle of electronegativity equilibration, for a diatomic molecule AB

$$
\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}=\chi_{\mathrm{A}}^{0}+\eta_{\mathrm{A}}^{0} q_{\mathrm{A}}=\chi_{\mathrm{B}}^{0}+\eta_{\mathrm{B}}^{0} q_{\mathrm{B}} \tag{7}
$$

which leads to

$$
\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{B}}^{0} \chi_{\mathrm{A}}^{0}+\eta_{\mathrm{A}}^{0} \chi_{\mathrm{B}}^{0}}{\eta_{\mathrm{A}}^{0}+\eta_{\mathrm{B}}^{0}} \tag{8a}
$$

or

$$
\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\left(I P_{\mathrm{B}}-E A_{\mathrm{B}}\right)\left(I P_{\mathrm{A}}+E A_{\mathrm{A}}\right)+\left(I P_{\mathrm{A}}-E A_{\mathrm{A}}\right)\left(I P_{\mathrm{B}}+E A_{\mathrm{B}}\right)}{I P_{\mathrm{A}}-E A_{\mathrm{A}}+I P_{\mathrm{B}}-E A_{\mathrm{B}}}\right]. \tag{8b}
$$

On the other hand, it was shown through the density function theory [11] that the chemical potential of a diatomic molecule becomes the following as the temperature approaches absolute zero,

$$
\mu=-\frac{1}{2}\left(I P_{\min }+E A_{\max }\right), \tag{9}
$$

where $IP_{min}$ is the lesser of the ionization potentials and $EA_{max}$ is the greater of the electron affinities of the two atoms. If we assume that at low temperatures where superconductivity occurs the equilibrium electronegativity is dictated by $IP_{min}$ and $EA_{max}$, we find that eqn (8b) reduces to eqn (9) for all cases (i.e. $IP_{\mathrm{A}}<I P_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $E A_{\mathrm{A}}>E A_{\mathrm{B}}$; $I P_{\mathrm{A}}<I P_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $E A_{\mathrm{A}}<E A_{\mathrm{B}}$; $I P_{\mathrm{A}}>I P_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $E A_{\mathrm{A}}>E A_{\mathrm{B}}$; $I P_{\mathrm{A}}>I P_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $E A_{\mathrm{A}}<E A_{\mathrm{B}}$).

The overall average of all 51 alloys ($\Sigma T_{ci}\chi_{eq_{i}}/\Sigma T_{ci}$) is 3.98 eV. By comparison, this value is 4.43 eV if we use eqn (8b).

Other definitions for $\chi_{eq}$ are conceivable such as the geometric mean $(\chi_{\text{A}}\chi_{\text{B}})^{1/2}$ due to Pauling [12] and the harmonic mean $2\chi_{\text{A}}\chi_{\text{B}}/(\chi_{\text{A}}+\chi_{\text{B}})$ which is actually a simplified form of eqn (8a) when the ratio $\chi_{i}/\eta_{i}$ is constant for a certain group of compounds; however, these values are consistently much higher than that calculated by eqn (8a) and more so with the geometric mean. It was shown recently [13] that the geometric mean is less general and lacks physical meaning compared with the harmonic mean.

The physical picture behind eqn (9) is that the atoms are non-interacting in the sense that electrostatic influence between them can be neglected, as if their internuclear distance is sufficiently long. In other words, according to classical electrostatics, the ionization potential increases and the electron affinity decreases as the distance becomes shorter. Such consideration becomes more important as we deviate more from absolute zero temperature.

Equations similar to eqn (8a) can be derived for somewhat more complex systems. For example, in the case of $\text{A}_{x}\text{B}_{y}$ type alloys

$$
\chi_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{x \eta_{\mathrm{B}}^{0} \chi_{\mathrm{A}}^{0}+y \eta_{\mathrm{A}}^{0} \chi_{\mathrm{B}}^{0}}{y \eta_{\mathrm{A}}^{0}+x \eta_{\mathrm{B}}^{0}},\qquad(10)
$$

where $x$ and $y$ could be fractional numbers; however, it becomes important to incorporate the geometrical structure for each case and that requires a more sophisticated treatment than above. This is under study at the moment.

It is noted that during the review of this paper, one of the referees has brought to our attention a very recent publication by Asokamani and Manjula [15] in which they show that the oxide superconductors have electronegativities between 2.5 and 2.65 by Pauling's scale. This is somewhat higher than the range for elements as reported by Luo and Wang [1] on the same scale. In our case also, the electronegativities for the metal oxides tend to have higher than average values for the binaries [e.g. NbO (4.18 eV), TiO (4.14 eV)].

## 5. CONCLUSIONS

Superconductivity occurs when the Mulliken electronegativity is neither too high or too low but when it has a medium value. The overall trends observed for single and binary systems indicate that there is an optimum value of electronegativity corresponding to the highest $T_{c}$ value.

It is rather surprising that such a crude argument presented here does indicate the above systematic trends. Due to the increasing interests in the understanding of electronegativity in the light of density functional formalism, further refinement of the argument as well as studies of more complex high temperature superconductors may contribute to the physical understanding of superconductivity phenomena in general.
