## 1. INTRODUCTION

We have constructed and analyzed 51 symmetric coincidence tilt boundary models between hard-sphere f.c.c. crystals. Three tilt axes, [100], [110] and [111], were considered. Models were constructed for all coincidence orientations up to $\Sigma 53$ for the [100] axis, up to $\Sigma 43$ for the [110] axis and $\Sigma 39 a$ for the [111] axis. For each boundary we have determined the sizes of the interstitial and substitutional spheres, the excess volume and the polyhedral stackings that make up the boundary.

## 2. PROCEDURE

### 2.1 Construction of the Boundaries

The grain boundary models were constructed graphically. Two symmetrical half-crystals, projected onto a plane perpendicular to the tilt axis, were translated with respect to each other until a mechanically stable position which maximized the density without atom overlap was found; then the coordinates (in three dimensions) of all the atoms were calculated. We frequently found that there were several mechanically stable configurations which were very close in density, so that the selection of the densest one was only possible by precise calculation, using the atom coordinates.

For about half the boundaries the densest configuration was obtained only after a relative displacement of the half-crystals *in the direction of the tilt axis* (the Z-direction) so that the planes perpendicular to the tilt axis did not continue through the boundary plane. Such a displacement has not been reported in previous grain boundary models.

### 2.2 Calculation of the Interstitial and Substitutional Hole Size

Each possible interstitial hole was characterized by the diameter of the largest sphere that would fit into it. The center of each of these spheres was found by constructing, numerically, spheres passing through all sets of four atom centers which contained no other atom centre. If two potential interstitial spheres overlapped, the larger one was selected. The substitutional holes were found by sequentially removing each atom from the boundary and determining the size of the resulting hole by the procedure described above.

### 2.3 Calculation of the Excess Volume

The difference between the volume of a bicrystal and that of a perfect single crystal is the "excess volume" associated with the grain boundary. All the boundaries we studied had a periodic structure. We found the excess volume associated with one period by constructing a parallellepiped with vertices at atom centers and with two rectangular faces (one inside each half-crystal) with edge-lengths equal to the boundary periods parallel and perpendicular to the tilt axis. If this parallelepiped has a volume V and contains N spheres, its excess volume is $V-N\Omega$, where $\Omega$ is the atomic volume (in f.c.c. $\Omega=0.70711$, for a unit sphere diameter). This excess volume per period can be normalized with respect to the atomic volume:

$$V^* = (V-N\ \Omega)/\Omega$$

The area of A of one boundary period is that of the rectangle with edge-lengths equal to the boundary periods. It can also be normalized with respect to the area per atom $(\Omega^{2/3})$.

$$A^* = A/\Omega^{2/3}$$

The excess volume per atom-area in the boundary is then defined as

$$V^{EX} = V^*/A^*$$

It can be thought of as the number of atoms "missing" per atom-area in the grain boundary

### 2.4 Polyhedral Hole Description
The structure of the boundaries was analyzed in terms of stackings of polyhedral figures. This is an extension of our earlier work (1,2). In many cases, the polyhedra could be identified by inspection. Sometimes, however, it was useful to examine the number of neighbors of the largest interstitials in order to make an unambiguous identification, as described in the earlier paper (2).

## 3. RESULTS
### 3.1 Boundary Structure
A very common figure in the [100] and [110] tilt boundaries is the capped trigonal prism, more or less distorted. Fig. 1 shows a typical example. The capped trigonal prism is formed by the neighbors of the largest interstitial (A), and its distortion is reflected in the larger size of the interstitial (0.818) compared to that in the ideal trigonal prism (0.528). The rest of the boundary consists of tetrahedra and octahedra.

The atom positions are shown as full and open circles. The additional symbols show interstitial sites (left) and sub- stitutional sites (right); the atom-diameters associated with each of the sites are listed beneath (in units of the crystal atom diameter).

Figure 2 shows two examples of boundary structures containing two capped trigonal prisms, as- sociated with the largest (A) and second largest (B) interstitial. Comparison of the two figures shows that this combination of trigonal prisms can be considered as a "unit of tilt": as the tilt angle increases, the figures move more closely together, thus producing more "units of tilt" per unit length along the boundary. This trend continues for angles above and below the ones shown here, and similar sequences have been observed for other tilt axes.

In some cases the boundary structure cannot conveniently be described by deltahedra (2). Fig. 3 shows two such examples. In both cases, the capped trigonal prism around the largest interstitial (A) is readily identifiable. In principle, the second largest interstitial (B) could be considered as the centre of another, though very distorted, trigonal prism. However, it is more convenient to describe the structure as a five-sided channel parallel to the tilt axis, containing both the second and third largest (C) interstitial positions, which form a zig-zag line down the channel. The difference between the two examples of Fig. 3 is the different staggering order of the five peripheral atoms of the channel.

As pointed out above (Section 2.1) the densest mechanically stable structure was often obtained only after a translation ($\Delta Z$) in the direction of the tilt axis. In Fig. 4 this configuration is compared with that of the densest structure with Z-translation, for a [100] $\Sigma 5$ boundary. The increase in density resulting from the Z-translation can be seen from the decrease of the excess volume (0.39 to 0.32) and of the maximum interstitial size (0.753 to 0.567). There are no substitutionals with diameter larger than 1.0 in the densest boundary. Since the Z-translation in this case is very large, the relation between the boundary figures in the two structures in not easily established. Usually, however, $\Delta Z$ is small enough that the figures still correspond to distorted, but still recognizable, versions of the corresponding boundary with $\Delta Z = 0$.

The [111] tilt boundaries have not been analyzed in terms of polyhedral units because the problem became too complicated. Fig. 5 shows an example of such a boundary. The triangles represent configurations of hard-sphere contact. All [111] boundaries we constructed had a Z-translation.

### 3.2 Excess Volume
We found the excess volume to vary between 0.5 and zero (for the coherent twin). No boundaries with higher excess volume were found. It seems plausible that the excess volume would have an upper limit, but we cannot as yet offer an explanation of the empirical value of 0.5.

Fig. 6 illustrates the correlation of the excess volume with the tilt axis orientation. In general, for a given boundary repeat distance, the [100] tilt boundaries are the densest (lowest curve), the [111] tilt boundaries are the least dense (uppermost curve) and the [100] tilt boundaries fall in between (middle curve).

### 3.3 Interstitial and Substitutional Sphere Sizes
Almost all the boundaries we studied contain one type of interstitial hole with diameter of at least 0.6 atom diameters (Fig. 6). In many cases (e.g. $\Sigma 17$ on Figs. 2, and 3) the biggest interstitial was larger than 0.8. No interstitials larger than 0.92 have been found. These interstitials are much larger than the largest ones found in either the perfect crystal (0.414) or in the dense random packing of hard spheres (0.7) (3).

Fig. 6 illustrates how the correlation between the density of the boundary and the type of tilt axis which we noted in Section 3.2 on the excess volume, also holds for the size of the largest interstitial. We have found many dense boundaries which contain no substitutionals with a diameter larger than the atom diameter (the denser structure of Fig. 5, for instance). In general, the substitutionals proved to be surprisingly small, rarely exceeding a diameter of 1.2. There is only a very weak correlation between the size of the largest substitutional and the type of tilt axis (Fig. 6).

## 4. CORRELATIONS

### 4.1 Correlation Between Boundary Density and Repeat Distance

In Fig. 6 the excess volume and the diameter of the largest interstitials and substitutionals are correlated with the repeat distance of the boundary. If the boundary figures are repeated by a simple translation in the boundary (obvious in Fig. 3), the choice of the repeat distance is unique. If the repeating translation includes a Z direction displacement causing the boundary figures to alternate between the two different layers (as in the "staggered" configuration of $\Sigma 17 a$, Fig. 2), we used the component of repeat translation perpendicular to the tilt axis. However, if the boundary figures repeat by a translation with a reflection, (as in the $\Sigma 17 b$ boundary of Fig. 1) we neglected the reflected occurance and used the repeat distance for the full translation.

![](./images/812402449376608257_8.jpg)

All three correlations show a general trend of decreasing boundary density with increasing repeat distance. Only in the case of the excess volume and the largest interstitials could a clear correlation with tilt axis be discerned (dashed lines).

### 4.2 Correlation Between Excess Volume and Boundary Energy

Fig. 7 shows a correlation between the excess volume of a number of our [100] tilt boundaries and the grain boundary energy calculated by Smith et al. (4) for the same boundaries after computer relaxation with a soft potential. We used the values for boundaries without Z-translation, since those corresponded to the ones used for relaxation. However, in two cases, the densest hard-sphere configuration had a non-zero Z-translation.

## 5. CONCLUDING REMARKS

We expect that the hard-sphere models we have constructed will be useful for the study of grain boundary segregation, decohesion, diffusion and motion; and they are the logical starting configurations for computer relaxation. We intend to present some more of our results in a later paper. At the present time, the catalog of all our boundaries is available as a Harvard Technical Report from the authors.
