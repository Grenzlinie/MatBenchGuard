# AMORPHOUS STRUCTURE OF GRAIN BOUNDARIES AND GRAIN JUNCTIONS IN NANOCRYSTALLINE SILICON BY MOLECULAR-DYNAMICS SIMULATION

P. K EBLINSKI, $^{1}$ S. R. PHILLPOT, $^{1}$ D. WOLF $^{1}$ and H. GLEITER $^{2}$

¹Materials Science Division, Argonne National Laboratory, Argonne, IL 60439, U.S.A. and
²Forschungszentrum Karlsruhe, 76021 Karlsruhe, Germany

(Received 21 May 1996; accepted 26 June 1996)

Abstract—Molecular-dynamics simulations using the Stillinger-Weber three-body potential are used to synthesize fully dense nanocrystalline silicon with a grain size up to 7.3 nm by crystallization from the melt. The structures of the highly-constrained grain boundaries, triple lines and point grain junctions are found to be highly disordered and similar to the structure of amorphous silicon. These results suggest that nanocrystalline silicon may be treated as a two-phase system, namely, an ordered crystalline phase in the grain interiors connected by an amorphous, intergranular glue-like phase. ## 1. INTRODUCTION

By design the severely constrained microstructures in, for example, epitaxially stabilized thin-film phases, multilayers and in nanocrystalline or partially crystalline materials, exploit the to-date often unknown local structural environments experienced by a significant fraction of atoms situated in the interfaces. Given the complexity of these artificial microstructures, it is commonly recognized that two important conceptual challenges are (a) the development of atomic-structure models and (b) the establishment of a thermodynamics-based framework for the description of these novel ‘phases’. Here we use atomic-level simulations to address both of these issues for the model case of nanocrystalline silicon.

In spite of much experimental work on the structure and properties of nanocrystalline materials (NCMs) performed to date, a structural model for the grain boundaries (GBs) and a thermodynamic description consistent with the observations have not evolved. Of particular interest has been the relationship of the structures of the GBs to amorphous structures and the possibility of a (sharp or continuous) transition between them. For example, Shen et al. [1] demonstrated that ball milling can be used to produce two-phase nanocrystalline and amorphous silicon; for an average grain size of 8 nm, about 15% of the material was identified as being amorphous [1]. Extensive earlier work by Veprek et al. [2] on nanocrystalline silicon produced by chemical transport in a hydrogen plasma had also identified a strongly grain-size dependent amorphous component. A limitation of these studies is their inability to determine the spatial distribution of the amorphous component within these highly inhomo- geneous microstructures; i.e. to answer the question as to whether entire grains are amorphous (perhaps only those below some critical size [3]) or whether perhaps only the GBs and grain junctions are amorphous. Here we address this question by molecular-dynamics simulations.

Our simulation approach draws heavily on a new molecular-dynamics simulation method developed recently [4] for the synthesis of fully dense NCMs. In this method, small crystalline seeds with controllable orientations are inserted into the melt; subsequent cooling below the melting point results in the growth of the crystallites to form reasonably well equili- brated grain boundaries. The final result is a fully dense, void-free polycrystalline microstructure which can subsequently be characterized at zero tempera- ture. The first application of this method to grow an eight-grain polycrystalline fcc model material de- scribed via a Lennard-Jones potential revealed heavily disordered GBs and grain junctions [4,5]. This has lead us to propose that Rosenhain’s historic ‘amorphous-cement’ model [6] may be a more appropriate description of the two-phase microstruc- ture of this simple model material than GB models based on the coincident-site lattice [7].

Apart from the fact that the distributions in the grain shapes and grain sizes present in polycrystalline materials were not considered, this first study [4] suffers from two limitations which will be overcome here. Firstly, the simple-cubic arrangement of eight grains in a three-dimensionally (3D) periodic simulation cell results in a cubic grain shape and in rather unrealistic grain junctions, namely four-grain line junctions and eight-grain point junctions. By contrast, in the present simulations the grains will be

arranged on an f.c.c. lattice instead. The microstruc- ture thus generated contains triple (line) junctions and four-grain point junctions, as is the case in real polycrystalline materials. Also, the grain shape is nowrhombic dodecahedral (i.e. closer to spherical) instead of cubic (for details see Section 2.1). Secondly, for a comparison of the structure and properties of disordered GBs with the amorphous phase [5], the choice of the Lennard-Jones potential and, in fact, of an f.c.c. material, is not a good one because of the instability of the amorphous structure. Here we will, instead, investigate silicon which is known to have a reasonably stable, well understood, approximately four-coordinated bulk amorphous phase [8-10]; the latter will serve as a useful reference basis for the analysis of the disorder present in GBs.

For several reasons silicon represents an ideal choice of a one-component covalent model material for this study. Firstly, a well-tested interatomic potential due to Stillinger and Weber [11] (SW) is available for Si which provides not only a good description of the diamond and amorphous phases,but also of the melting temperature (of $1690 \pm 20 ~K$ [12], remarkably similar to the experimental value1683K) and the six-coordinated structure of the liquid [12]. Secondly, the structure and excess energy of the bulk amorphous phase are well reproduced by the SW potential [13-15]. Thirdly, the SW potential has been used successfully to describe crystalline silicon in a wide variety of defective environments ranging from small clusters [16] and grain boundaries[17], to dislocation structures and dynamics [18]. Finally, the SW potential has also been used to describe the faceting of the (100) crystal-liquid interface [19] and to simulate epitaxial crystal growth from the melt [20]. We believe this combination of relevant phase information reproduced at least semi-quantitatively by the SW potential makes $Si$ an ideal model material for this simulation study.

For this model material we demonstrate that for the three grain sizes investigated (of 3.8, 5.4 and7.3 nm) the structures of the highly-constrained grain boundaries, triple lines and point grain junctions are highly disordered and very similar to the structure of amorphous silicon. These results suggest that nanocrystalline silicon may be treated as a two-phase system, namely an ordered crystalline phase in the grain interiors connected by an amorphous, inter- granular glue-like phase. Moreover, the high degree of isotropy of key GB parameters suggests that historic models viewing the grain boundaries as a cement-like phase with a more or less uniform width, density and energy density appear to describe the interfaces more aptly than models derived from bicrystal studies.

The highly inhomogeneous structure of the fully dense NCM obtained from the crystal-growth simulation (by a procedure described in Section 3.3) will be analyzed both globally, involving system averages, and locally, providing more detailed information on the different types of point, line and planar defects present in the microstructure. The global characteristics include the average mass and energy densities, the volume percentage of the amorphous phase and the total excess energy as a function of the grain size.

More detailed analysis of the NCM on both global and local levels is provided by radial and angular distribution functions. The radial distribution function (RDF), $G(r)$, characterizing the degree of long-range order, measures the probability of finding an atom at a given distance $r$, from a central atom. In the construction of the overall $G(r)$ all the atoms are taken into account, thus representing an average over whole system. More suitable for the characterization of localized disorder is a local RDF constructed such that only atoms from a given part of the material are taken to be the central atoms. Whereas in a homogeneous system, such as a perfect crystal, the local and global RDFs are identical, the local RDF associated, for example, with GB atoms in a nanocrystalline material can be expected to differ significantly from that for atoms in the grain interiors and from the global average.

A measure of the short-range order within a given region is provided by the angular distribution function, $P_{jik}(\cos\theta)$ (simply denoted by $P(\cos\theta)$), which measures the probability that the angle between two nearest-neighbor vectors $\mathbf{r}_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{ik}$ is $\theta$. (In a disordered system at zero temperature, the maximum nearest-neighbor separation is defined as the half-way distance between the perfect-crystal nearest- and second-nearest-neighbor distances, $0.570\,a_0$, where $a_0$ is the zero-temperature lattice parameter.) In evaluating the angular distribution function, only central atoms $i$ from a given part of the system are considered while $j$ and $k$ may be from anywhere. This measure of the short-range order is particularly useful for comparing locally disordered regions with the bond orientations in the perfect crystal in which the angular distribution function is simply a $\delta$-function at $\cos\theta=-1/3$, i.e. at the tetrahedral angle $\theta=109.48^\circ$.

To further characterize the NCM at a local level, we monitor the plane-by-plane energy per atom averaged over a cylindrical ('local-probe') area when moving across the GBs. If the cylinder radius is chosen such as to avoid triple lines and higher-fold junctions, the integral over such energy profiles can be used to determine the GB energy in a local region of the interface. Such energy profiles also provide a convenient measure of the GB width and they are very useful for a direct comparison with bicrystalline GBs.

## 3. SYNTHESIS AND CHARACTERIZATION

### 3.1. Simulation of single-crystal growth

In order to assess whether the growth of nanocrystalline Si from the melt by means of our recently developed MD method [4] is indeed feasible, we must first establish that within the time scale accessible to MD simulations $(10^{-10}-10^{-9}\,\text{s})$ it is possible to grow a single crystal from the melt. Furthermore, given that the seed crystallites are delimited by several crystallographically distinct growth faces, it would be useful to know the degree of anisotropy in the growth process; this would enable us to assess whether we can, indeed, expect to achieve the 'ideal' dodecahedral grain shape sketched in Fig. 2. Finally, since all computational resources are finite, it is helpful to know the optimum growth conditions. To address these questions, here we first discuss the simulation of epitaxial growth from the melt on different crystallographic planes followed by the growth of a single seed embedded in the melt. Throughout, we used a time step of $2.75\times10^{-15}\,\text{s}$ for which energy was found to be conserved to four significant figures in micro-canonical-ensemble, constant-volume simulations of several thousand time steps.

Kluge and Ray [20] studied homo-epitaxial growth on the (100) surface of silicon using the SW potential. They found that growth of the supercooled melt in contact with the crystal occurs between $T\cong1000\text{K}$ and $T\cong1680\text{K}$, the upper limit corresponding to the melting point whereas the lower limit originating from the fact that, on an MD time scale, diffusion in the supercooled liquid becomes extremely slow below 1000K. The maximum growth velocity for this substrate orientation, $V_{(100)}\cong20\,\text{m/s}$, was obtained for $T=1350\text{K}$. In our investigation of the anisotropy of homo-epitaxial growth of Si we were able to reproduce this result (our growth velocity was $V_{(100)}=19.8\pm2.0\,\text{m/s}$).

In their simulations of bicrystal and polycrystal growth from the melt using a Lennard-Jones potential, Phillpot et al. [4] had observed that application of external pressure accelerated the growth, supposedly because it facilitated densification of the liquid upon freezing. Given that silicon expands on freezing we tested whether application of negative pressure would accelerate crystal growth. We found that neither negative nor positive external pressure increases the growth velocity. The combination of a supercooling temperature of $T=1350\text{K}$ with zero external pressure therefore represents the optimum condition for crystal growth. Apparently

the thermodynamic driving force for crystal growth, $T_{\mathrm{m}}-T$, is then relatively large while self-diffusion is still fast enough to ensure rapid crystallization.

To study the anisotropy of homo-epitaxial growth under these optimized conditions, we performed similar simulations for the (110) and (111) crystalliquid interfaces; the growth velocities thus determined are $V_{(110)}=17.5 \pm 2 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}$ and $V_{(111)}=13.0 \pm$ $3 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}$. The relatively large variation of these growth velocities for the three principal directions suggests that the three-dimensional, approximately spherical crystalline seeds embedded in the supercooled melt might change their shape somewhat during growth. In agreement with previous simulations and experiments [19] we also found, however, that the fastest growing, (100) interface facets into (111) segments, presumably because it can lower its energy by lying on the densest rather than second-densest plane in the cubic diamond lattice. Once faceted, the growth velocity decreases, thus significantly improving the overall isotropy of crystal growth needed to grow practically spherical crystallites.

In order to assess the quality of these homoepitaxially grown single crystals we cooled our samples down to zero temperature and analyzed their structure. In all cases, the average energy per atom in each crystallographic plane was $-4.335 \mathrm{eV}$, which is the same as the cohesive energy of perfect crystal SW Si. Indeed, detailed structural analysis reveals virtually perfect, dislocation and point-defect free silicon in which each atom has four nearest neighbors, at distance $0.433 a_{0}$, and eight second neighbors, at distance $0.707 a_{0}$ (where $a_{0}=5.43 \AA$ ).

In the growth studies described above we investigated flat, infinitely extended, planar interfaces, i.e. with no curvature. By contrast, the grains in a polycrystal are 3-D objects delimited not only by more or less well-defined, relatively small planar facets but also lines and corners where these facets meet; these can be expected to slow down the crystal-growth kinetics.

A more important effect causing a reduced growth velocity arises from the considerably reduced melting point of the seed grains due to their finite size. From melting studies on gold clusters, for example, it is well established that the melting temperature decreases with decreasing cluster size [21]. In a recent systematic study [22] we have verified that small Si grains embedded in molten Si exhibit a similarly reduced melting point. (We note, however, that these grains are delimited by crystal/liquid interfaces instead of free surfaces.) For example, an approximately spherical 1000-atom Si grain, with a diameter of about $3.5 \mathrm{~nm}$, was found to dissolve above $T_{\mathrm{m}}=1450 \pm 20 \mathrm{~K}$ and to grow below this temperature [22]. For the same thermodynamic driving force, $T_{\mathrm{m}}-T$, to be present as in the bulk crystal-growth simulations one would therefore have to lower the growth temperature to about $1130 \mathrm{~K}$, a value greatly reducing the diffusion constant and, hence, the net growth velocity. Our study gave an optimum growth velocity at about $1250 \mathrm{~K}$, i.e. $100 \mathrm{~K}$ below the optimal temperature for the epitaxial growth from the melt. One should keep in mind, however, that as the grain size increases during growth, so does the melting point and hence the optimal growth temperature until, in the limit of infinite grain size, it reaches the value for bulk planar growth.

In order to study the influence of all these factors we simulated the growth of a single, dodecahedral seed from its melt at $1250 \mathrm{~K}$. The initial configuration consisted of a cubic, $5 \mathrm{~nm}$ large simulation cell containing approximately 6000 atoms on the perfect diamond lattice. As in the case of planar growth, the system was melted at $3000 \mathrm{~K}$ while the 800 atoms forming the seed (corresponding to a seed diameter of about $2.5 \mathrm{~nm}$ ) were kept fixed. The melt was then cooled down to $1250 \mathrm{~K}$ and the whole system (melt plus seed) was permitted to evolve freely with no further constraints imposed on the seed atoms. A constant-pressure Andersen algorithm [23] was used to assure zero external pressure during the growth process. The seed was observed to grow more or less isotropically thus retaining its roughly spherical shape. As expected, the average growth rate of $\sim 7 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}$ is considerably slower than that for the planar homo-epitaxial growth.

### 3.2. Synthesis and microstructural characterization

In the preceding section we established optimal conditions for the growth of 3-D single-crystal grains from the melt and found that the initially spherical seeds grow roughly isotropically. Analogous to these simulations, the growth of a four-grain nanocrystalline microstructure with seeds initially arranged on an f.c.c. lattice starts with a perfect Si crystal in a 3-D periodic cubic simulation box. The crystal is meltedby heating to $T=3000 \mathrm{~K}$ at constant volume for 1000 steps while the seed atoms are kept fixed at their perfect-crystal positions. In the next 1000 MD steps the four seeds are gradually rotated by randomly chosen rotation angles and axes. After further equilibration at high temperature the system is cooled down to $T=1250 \mathrm{~K}$ and a constant-pressure algorithm is applied to relax the system to zero external pressure. From then on the system is allowed to evolve freely with no constraints imposed on the seed atoms. The growth continues until the internal energy of the system stops decreasing; depending on the system size, this growth phase requires typically $150000-300000$ time steps. Finally the system is cooled down under zero external pressure via a stepwise decrease of the temperature to $T=0 \mathrm{~K}$; this process requires typically about 20,000 time steps.

The eventual formation of triple lines and finally point junctions occurs under the ever increasing constraints of an already existing solid matrix, giving the atoms in these regions less and less freedom to homo-epitaxially attach them- selves to already crystallized material and to relax the local excess volume stored in these highly disordered regions.

Figure 6(a) shows that even for the smallest grain size $(\sim 3.8$ nm) the overall radial distribution function exhibits sharp crystalline peaks originating from the ordered grain interiors. However, between the crystalline peaks a non-vanishing background is clearly visible which, as we shall show, originates from disordered regions along the GBs and grain junctions. Also the angular distribution function in Fig. 6(b) exhibits a sharp crystalline peak at $\cos\theta=-1/3$, which is slightly broadened due to the contribution from the disordered regions.

The average excess energy per atom is plotted in Fig. 7 as a function of the inverse grain size, $1/d$; the latter is a more natural length scale than $d$ as it corresponds roughly to the ratio of the total GB area to the volume of the material. In the large-grain limit in which the effects of grain junctions can be ignored, only the GBs contribute to the excess energy, giving a linear variation in $1/d$ (see Fig. 7). Deviations from a straight line are particularly pronounced for the two smallest grain sizes; these are supposedly due to the contributions from the grain junctions [24].

The system-averaged density of our model NCMs represents another simple global characteristic. Surprisingly, the density was found to increase with decreasing grain size, by up to $0.5 \%$ over that of the perfect crystal for the smallest grain size. Experimentally the density of nanocrystalline silicon films has been observed to be essentially the same (within $\pm 2 \%$ ) as that of perfect-crystalline Si [25]. By contrast, our earlier simulations for an f.c.c. metal described by a Lennard-Jones potential showed a significant decrease in the theoretical density [4]. This distinct behavior of Si could have at least two causes.

Firstly, in a covalent material some GBs can actually contract rather than expand as is typical for GBs in metals [26]. Also, X-ray diffraction experiments on (100) twist GBs in Ge indicate a small volume contraction [27].

The similarities among these profiles indicate relatively little structural variation from one GB to another, particularly a rather uniform GB width (of typically about $1.5$-$2\ a_0$, or about 0.8-1.1 nm) as already suggested from a visual inspection of the structures in Figs 3 and 4. Moreover, these energy profiles are practically independent of the grain size; this is demonstrated in Fig. 10(b) showing three profiles of crystallographically the same GB, however in the three samples with different grain sizes.

The peak excess energies in the GB distribution functions in Fig. 10 of $\sim0.20$ eV/atom are slightly above the value of 0.185 eV/atom for bulk amorphous Si (dashed lines). Presumably this increase is due to the severe confinement of the disordered intergranular material between misoriented crystalline grains [28]. We have also determined the peak excess energies of the atoms in the various types of grain junctions. We found that atoms in the centers of the triple lines and point grain junctions have typical excess energy of about $\sim0.22$ eV/atom; i.e. even slightly higher than the atoms in the GB cores. This difference is probably due to the even greater degree of confinement of these atoms since, trapped in pockets of liquid well after the GBs have already been formed, these regions are the last to grow from the melt.

The GB energy, which is given by the integral over these energy profiles, varies relatively little from one boundary to another, although for any given boundary there is some variation within the GB plane [4]. The average GB energy was found to be essentially independent of the grain size and equal to $1300$-$1350\ \text{erg/cm}^2$, with none of the 24 GBs having an energy lower than about $900\ \text{erg/cm}^2$ or higher than about $1500\ \text{erg/cm}^2$. As expected, these values are higher than the values obtained in Section 3.3 by dividing the entire excess energy of the system by the (systematically too large) GB areas in Table 1, which ignore the presence of the grain junctions.

The absence of low-energy GBs probably has two causes. Firstly, the random misorientations between grains effectively avoids the formation of low-angle and of 'special' GBs, with virtually all boundaries in the system being 'general' boundaries; i.e. with both tilt and twist components and hence a relatively high energy [26]. Secondly, whereas in a bicrystal rigid-body translations parallel to the GB are known to provide a powerful relaxation mechanism, in the severely constrained NCMs these translations cannot be optimized simultaneously for all the grains and hence all the GBs; this results in higher GB energies [4].

## 5. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

In the preceding section we have demonstrated that the GBs and grain junctions in our highly idealized model of nanocrystalline silicon described by the Stillinger-Weber potential are, indeed, amorphous. The relatively minor differences in their structures and energies with respect to bulk amorphous Si (the latter with a cohesive energy of 0.185 eV/atom for the SW potential, by comparison with the values of 0.20-0.22 eV/atom for the GBs and grain junctions) are due to the severe confinement of the amorphous intergranular phase between the grains. These results suggest that the detailed atomic structures of the GBs and grain junctions may not be of great importance in such a highly constrained microstructure. In fact, a two-phase model in which the inverse grain size (i.e. ratio of the GB area to the system volume) is a natural variable describes our simulation results rather well. That in nanocrystalline Si a strongly grain-size dependent fraction of the material is, indeed, amorphous is consistent with experiments [1,2].

This leads to our first conclusion, namely, that Rosenhain's historic model [6] describes the GBs in our idealized form of nanocrystalline silicon more aptly than GB models derived from bicrystal studies. Derived from extensive investigations of the mechanical properties of polycrystalline materials, in this model the grain boundaries are viewed as a cement-like phase with a more or less uniform width, density and energy density. Our simulations have shown that in spite of its severe confinement, the structure and properties of the amorphous intergranular phase are remarkably similar to those of bulk amorphous silicon. Moreover, apart from a slightly higher degree of confinement, the structures of the triple lines and the point grain junctions are virtually

indistinguishable from that of the GBs and of bulk amorphous silicon.

An important question concerns the relationship of the atomic structures of the highly constrained GBs in the nanocrystalline materials with those of entirely unconstrained bicrystalline GBs. A recent systematic study of the effect of the GB energy on the structure of twist grain boundaries in Si bicrystals [28] revealed a universal, 'confined amorphous' structure of all the high-energy boundaries, with a width, energy profile and total excess energy per unit GB area very similar to those determined above for nanocrystalline Si; by contrast, low-energy boundaries were found to be crystalline. These bicrystal simulations also revealed that introduction of the amorphous film into the bicrystal, either by high-temperature annealing or via bicrystal growth from the melt, results in a lowering of the GB energy with respect to that obtained from zero-temperature lattice-statics relaxation. We therefore concluded that the formation of an amorphous intergranular 'phase' is a thermodynamic-equilibrium phenomenon [4] which is governed by the relative magnitudes of the excess energies of the atoms in the GBs and in the bulk amorphous phase (compare Fig. 10 with Fig. 27 in Ref. [28]).

Our second conclusion is therefore that, based on this comparison with bicrystal simulations using also the SW potential, the atomic structures and energies of the grain boundaries in nanocrystalline Si are essentially the same as the 'constrained amorphous' structures of high-energy, bicrystalline GBs.

Whereas here we concluded that the structures of GBs in NCMs and bicrystals are essentially the same, in our earlier work on a nanocrystalline f.c.c. model material [4] we reached the opposite conclusion: in spite of the well-known fact that the GBs in f.c.c. bicrystals or coarse-grained polycrystals are ordered, the GBs in our nanocrystalline microstructure were found to be highly disordered. These opposite conclusions can possibly be reconciled by recognizing a tendency of severe microstructural constraints to induce disorder: in f.c.c. materials such microstructural constraints are essential to produce intergranular disorder, whereas in the case of Si no further disorder can be added to the already disordered bicrystalline GBs.
