# Ab Initio Prediction of Dopant Segregation at Elemental Semiconductor Grain Boundaries without Coordination Defects

T. A. Arias and J. D. Joannopoulos

Physics Department, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02139
(Received 29 June 1992)

We present results of an extensive state-of-the-art $ab$ $initio$ study of the segregation of dopants to the $\Sigma=5$ tilt (310) grain boundary in germanium. Despite the lack of reactive bond coordination defects in this boundary, we predict a significant tendency for $n$-type dopants to segregate to the boundary. Our results lead to a general theory of dopant segregation at tetrahedrally bonded semiconductor defects, where lattice relaxation effects are irrelevant and segregation is driven by the mixing of localized carrier states of the defect with the hydrogenic states of the dopant.

PACS numbers: 61.70.Ng, 61.70.Sk, 64.75.+g

Of the extended defects in solids, grain boundaries in particular are important to understand from a practical point of view, as most modern electronic devices employ polycrystalline semiconductor materials [1]. Moreover, grain boundaries represent a significant challenge to physicists, as many common dopants tend to segregate, collect, at grain boundaries, resulting in a complex system interacting through several mechanisms at similar energy scales. Though much experimental progress has been made in the study of the structure of semiconducting grain boundaries in recent years [especially in the imaging of structures through high-resolution electron microscopy [2,3] (HREM)], many important problems remain unsettled due to the inherent difficulties of the experimental isolation and study of defect phenomena deep in the bulk of materials. Such problems include both understanding whether the distribution in the gap of interface states associated with the boundary is due to the intrinsic structure of the boundary or its associated extrinsic defects, as is currently speculated [4,5], and also understanding the specific mechanisms driving segregation of impurities to the boundary (especially of dopants).

$Ab$ $initio$ study provides both the ability to access the detailed information required to sort these issues out and an unprecedented degree of control over the physical system under study. Because of the importance in the grain boundary problem of understanding extrinsic defects and of the unique capabilities which the total-energy pseudopotential technique brings to address this problem, we have chosen to focus our study on the mechanisms driving segregation, particularly that of electrically active dopants. High computational costs generally restrict $ab$ $initio$ studies to a single system which is then best chosen to be as representative of the class of systems of interest as possible. For this reason, for our material we choose the simple elemental semiconductor germanium, with its well-known homology to silicon, and for the boundary we take the $\Sigma=5$ tilt [310] boundary, a member of the large class of low-energy grain boundaries preserving the local tetrahedral bonding network of the host crystal. Such boundaries are particularly relevant in cast polycrystalline materials, for example, where most grain boundaries are low-energy symmetric tilt boundaries of this local tetrahedral order-preserving type [6]. Finally, for our dopants we study arsenic for $n$ type and gallium for $p$ type.

We perform our calculations in a supercell of 68 atoms with the minimal dimensions in the boundary plane required by the periodicity of the boundary (5.65 $\mathring{\mathrm{A}} \times 8.94$ $\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$) and extended 30.4 $\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$ in the perpendicular direction. This cell contains 34 (310) planes and is sufficiently long that the two boundaries imposed by the periodicity of the cell are separated by over 15 $\mathring{\mathrm{A}}$. We expand the electronic wave functions in the cell in a plane-wave basis with plane-wave cutoff of 14.7 Ry and use three points in the irreducible zone to perform the Brillouin integrations. We describe germanium and the dopant arsenic with Starkloff-Joannopoulos pseudopotentials [7] to make direct comparisons possible with our group's previous work on $twist$ boundaries in germanium [8], and for gallium we use the Kleinmann-Bylander form [9] for the Hamann-Schluter-Chiang potential [10]. To gauge the order of the aggregate error of all of our approximations in the small total-energy differences we are calculating, we directly compare our calculations with $experiment$ in the most closely related type of energy difference where such a comparison is possible, the energy in a frozen-phonon lattice distortion with energy comparable to the small ($\approx 50$ meV) energy differences we are calculating. The experimental zone-center optic phonon frequency differs from the result of a frozen-phonon calculation performed at the same plane wave cutoff using the same germanium pseudopotential and a comparable $k$-point integration scheme by only about 3%, putting our estimate of the error in our calculations at several meV. Finally, to relax the electronic wave functions to the Born-Oppenheimer (BO) surface, we use the preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) procedure of Teter, Payne, and Allan [11].

The atomic coordinates of this relaxed configuration agree handsomely in all three dimensions with high-resolution electron microscopy measurements [2,3]. Two structural features of this boundary are important in our discussion here. First, the crystal has completely restored its tetrahedral bonding network, with all bond lengths and bond angles close to their bulk values. Because the local environment of each possible substitutional site on the boundary is very similar to that of the bulk, we can expect, as will be borne out in our calculations below, that the energy of a long-length-scale excitation, such as the lattice relaxation associated with an extrinsic defect, will be nearly independent of the location of the excitation relative to the boundary. Under these circumstances, such lattice effects then are unlikely to contribute significantly to any net defect-boundary interaction. The second important feature is that the manner in which the boundary does differ from the bulk is through the presence of subtle topological differences, particularly when we get out to fifth nearest neighbors-our reconstruction has both five- and seven-member rings in addition to the sixfold rings of which the bulk consists entirely. As we shall see immediately below, these differences can significantly affect excitations, such as carriers, which are sensitive to shorter-length-scale features of the lattice because they remain coherent over several bond hops. These topological differences then can serve to drive the segregation of electrically active extrinsic defects (such as donors) by distinguishing the boundary from the bulk in terms of its electronic properties. These two observations on the boundary structure form the basis of understanding a new dopant segregation mechanism which may operate even in the absence of bond coordination defects.

The aforementioned interaction of the $\Sigma$=5 tilt (310) boundary with carrier excitations is confirmed directly in the electronic structure of our undoped system. In particular, the distribution of the electronic eigenstates of our supercell near the bulk gap points to the presence of an interface band for electrons on the order of 10 meV below the conduction band and gives no indication of boundary states for holes derived from the valence band. Further examination of the interfacial electronic states by projection of the density of the lowest empty band in our cell along the [310] axis confirms the localization of these states on the boundary (Fig. 2). Though the experimental situation is unclear due to the influences of segregating species and the tendency of even our simple boundary to form "coherent" steps in experiment [3], current-voltage characterizations of bicrystal [12] and polycrystalline samples [13] have shown that grain boundaries in germanium, without observed exception, provide effective traps for electrons but not holes. Though our calculations complement this picture, with our boundary following the rule, it should be stressed that various experiments place the boundary electron trap states in germanium much deeper in the gap than we calculate, almost reaching the top of the valence band [14,15]. The depth of the experimental states is most likely related to the presence of defects in the boundary reconstructions (such as steps) and not related to the intrinsic structure of the symmetric tilt boundaries.

To gain insight into the impurity-boundary system and learn whether and to what extent the presence of the interface electron states affect the segregation of dopants to the boundary, we have performed calculations with various dopants (gallium, arsenic, and ${\rm As^+}$) at various sub-

stitutional sites (the grain-center site labeled "bk," the intermediate site "i," and the boundary site "gb;" see Fig. 1). With the impurities at these locations we first calculate the energy of the system with all atomic positions held fixed at their equilibrium values in the undoped state and then allow the positions of the dopants and all surrounding atoms out to third nearest neighbors to relax. This device helps to separate lattice relaxation from electronic effects by determining the relaxation energy as the lattice responds to the impurity, a measure of the elastic energy associated with the incorporation of the impurity into the lattice. Concentrating at present on the results for the neutral arsenic dopant (Fig. 3, left), we note that although lattice relaxation represents a significant energy (nearly one-half of the net boundary interaction), the differences among the elastic incorporation energies of the different substitutional sites are relatively small compared to the final interaction with the boundary. This bears out our previous expectations and shows that in this case lattice effects do not contribute significantly to the boundary-dopant interaction, which therefore must be dominated by electronic effects.

Despite the lack of blatant electronic defects like dangling bonds or sub- or extra-coordinated sites to interact with the arsenic dopant, we still find an appreciable (0.1 eV, see Fig. 3, left) tendency for arsenic to segregate to the boundary. The segregation is due to a more subtle electronic mechanism. In the dopant-boundary system there is a natural mixing between the low-energy interfacial states of the boundary and the hydrogenic orbital of the dopant. Figure 2 illustrates that placing the impurity on the boundary maximizes this mixing, which accompanies a 90-meV reduction in the energy of this state in our calculation. We propose that the reduction of the energy of this filled state is responsible for most of the 0.1-eV binding of the arsenic to the boundary. In the figure we also see that when the impurity is far from the boundary, the donor electron prefers to stay with the dopant, demonstrating that the interface band must lay between the conduction band and the hydrogenic state of an isolated arsenic in germanium (13 meV), consistent with our band structure results. Although both the isolated hydrogenic state and the isolated interface state ionize very readily at room temperature, the drop of the donor state nearly an order of magnitude deeper into gap when the dopant is on the boundary ensures that donors in the vicinity of the boundary will not ionize, even at several times room temperature. This continuing occupation of the donor states not only guarantees that the mixing-induced attraction of the dopant to the interface will persist at elevated temperatures but also will result in the loss of the room-temperature electrical activity of arsenic dopants near the boundary, as is observed experimentally [16].

To confirm that this electronic effect is indeed responsible for the binding to the boundary and to provide an independent check that the interaction with the boundary does push the donor state deeper into the gap, we have removed the donor electron and studied the segregation of $\text{As}^{+}$. As expected, the interaction with the boundary is dramatically reduced (by a factor of 3; see Fig. 3, center). Additionally, we find that the now empty donor eigenstate in these calculations also lowers by 90 meV when the donor is placed directly on the boundary. These eigenenergy shifts are significantly less reliable than quantities derived from total energies because the total energies are variational in the various convergence parameters of our calculation, whereas the Kohn-Sham eigenenergies are not. To obtain a more reliable determination of the increase in ionization energy of the donor, we compute the change in the total energy differences between our neutral and ionized systems (with the ions held fixed during the ionization process) as we move the arsenic from the bulk to the boundary. We then find directly that the ionization potential is increased by roughly 80 meV, verifying the results from the eigenenergy shifts.

Finally, we turn to the segregation of a $p$-type dopant, gallium (see Fig. 3, right). Again, lattice relaxation around the impurity is a significant source of energy but one that remains insensitive to the location of the dopant and thus does not contribute significantly to the boundary interaction. Moreover, because of the lack of boundary hole states with which to mix, there is now no lowering of a filled electronic state as the dopant approaches the boundary and hence very little net interaction with the boundary. Note that although the gallium donor state may mix somewhat with the electron interface state, this interaction is between empty states only and thus does not affect the total energy of the system.

Although the experimental results on dopant segrega-

tion are enigmatic, it has been observed that when care is taken to ensure an equilibrium distribution, arsenic and phosphorus tend to segregate to elemental semiconductor grain boundaries, whereas boron does not [16]. Our calculations on dopant segregation to the $\Sigma=5$ tilt (310) boundary complement this experimental picture, and our proposed mechanism now suggests a more general picture: $n$-type but not $p$-type dopants segregate in germanium. Quantitatively, the experimentally determined dopant-boundary binding energies are significantly larger $(\sim 0.5 \mathrm{eV})$ than what we have calculated; however, this may be attributable to the depth of the experimental electron-trap states, which increases their localization on the boundary and thus enhances their interaction with the donor ionic charge. Although the depth of the experimental trap states in the gap introduces two new complications, the basic physics observed in our calculation continues to apply. First, the interface states are now typically lower in energy than the donor states, but still the total energy of the system is reduced as the lower, filled state (in this case, the interface state) has its energy reduced by the mixing between the states. Second, the electron-trap states deep in the gap tend to pin the Fermi level and bend the bands near the interface, but still the system prefers to place $n$-type donors near the interface where they lower the total energy by lowering the energy of the filled interface states at the pinned Fermi level.

We have studied just one particular grain boundary, but the behavior of our undoped boundary is representative of many aspects of the behavior of the ensemble of boundaries found in polycrystalline samples. Not surprisingly then, very general conclusions may be drawn from our study. We expect tetrahedrally reconstructed boundaries to respond to long-length-scale excitations, such as the elastic effects around an impurity, very much in the same way as does the bulk, so that lattice relaxations are relatively unimportant in the energetics of the segregation process. Excitations such as electrons which are more sensitive to short-range modifications to the lattice, on the other hand, sense the subtle topological differences (e.g., fivefold and sevenfold rings) between the bulk and boundary, and may even bind in bona fide interface states to boundaries without bond-coordination defects. These states then drive dopant segregation through mixing with the hydrogenic states of the donors. From this general picture we predict that if one were to divide tetrahedrally reconstructed grain boundaries into classes based on segregation behavior, one would find just four simple classes: those boundaries which attract either $n$-type but not $p$-type impurities, $p$ type but not $n$ type, both types, or neither type, depending on whether the particular boundary localizes either electrons but not holes, holes but not electrons, both types of carriers, or neither type, respectively. This expectation is borne out not only for our boundary calculations but also in germanium in general, where the boundaries, almost without exception, both trap electrons but not holes and attract $n$-type but not $p$-type impurities. As a final observation, our general arguments about tetrahedrally coordinated grain boundaries make no reference to the dimensionality of the boundary, so that dislocations or local defects which retain tetrahedral coordination should also exhibit the same behaviors. They also may have localized electron or hole states and so attract $n$-type or $p$-type dopants through the same mechanism we have outlined here.
