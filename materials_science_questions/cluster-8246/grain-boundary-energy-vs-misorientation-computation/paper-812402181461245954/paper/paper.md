# Energy-minimizing Structures for Interfacial Dislocation Arrays: Non-planar Configurations in Small-angle Grain Boundaries

A. H. KING and FU-RONG CHEN

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, State University of New York at Stony Brook, Stony Brook, NY 11794 (U.S.A.)

(Received August 30, 1985)

## ABSTRACT

Electron microscope observations of small- angle grain boundaries in zinc reveal that the boundaries are generally not planar but rather take on a puckered form. A linear isotropic elastic analysis of the interactions between the individual dislocation segments making up the boundaries reveals that the interaction forces are consistent with the observed behavior which is therefore expected to be general to dislocation-containing boundaries in all materials.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The net dislocation content of a small-angle grain boundary can be determined, if the misorientation and boundary plane are known, from Frank's formula [1] or by using Bollmann's O lattice approach [2], which amounts to a generalization of Frank's formula. The actual configuration of the dislocations, however, cannot be predicted on the basis of these geometrical formulations unless certain assumptions are made, and these usually include the supposition that the dislocation array is planar and may therefore be described by a single plane normal. Formulae for line directions such as those given by Amelinckx and Dekeyser [3], for example, yield only planar dislocation arrays. We shall demonstrate in this paper that the assumption of planarity is not valid for general small-angle grain boundaries and that a "puckering" of the boundary plane is required to minimize the grain boundary energy.

Earlier electron microscope observations [4] have revealed non-planar boundaries in aluminum alloys, when the average boundary plane was neither parallel nor perpendicular to the rotation axis, and so the boundaries were neither pure tilt nor pure twist in character; such boundaries will be referred to as having a mixed character. In the present work we have observed small-angle grain boundaries of mixed character in zinc and have also developed a theoretical understanding of the puckering behavior.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

High purity zinc (99.999 wt.%) was prepared for transmission electron microscopy by cold rolling and then annealing at $85\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ in vacuo. Transmission electron microscopy specimens were electrochemically thinned in a 10% solution of nitric acid in methanol. Images of small-angle grain boundaries and diffraction patterns from the crystals were obtained at various orientations, and so the crystal misorientations and the dislocation line directions were overdetermined. The determinations were then optimized using a technique developed from that suggested by Ball [5], to provide line directions to an accuracy of better than $1^{\circ}$ of arc.

It is seen that the dislocation lines are not coplanar in any of the boundaries; in general, two of the three lines are rotated out of the "average boundary plane" toward the screw orientation while the third is rotated away from it. In each case, there appeared to be some deviation from the pure screw orientation, although for some of the dislocations this deviation was within the experimental error of the trace analysis.

In order to verify that these dislocation arrays correctly accommodate the misorientation between the two grains, we require a slight modification of the usual formalisms. The O lattice technique [2] provides a convenient framework; the method is represented schematically in Fig. 2. The positions of O lattice elements are determined from the basic equation
$$\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)^{-1} \boldsymbol{b}^{\mathbf{L}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathbf{O}}$$
where $\boldsymbol{b}^{\mathbf{L}}$ is a crystal lattice vector. For a grain boundary, where the transformation $\mathbf{A}$ is a rotation, the O elements are continuous lines parallel to the rotation axis. To determine the dislocation positions, Wigner-Seitz cells are constructed around the O elements and, for the basal plane rotations considered here, the resulting O cells are tubes of regular hexagonal cross section with their axes parallel to the $c$ axis of the two grains. The conventional practice at this stage is to construct a dislocation network by placing a dislocation at each intersection of the boundary plane with an O cell wall. The correct Burgers vectors are found from the basic equation since the vector $\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathbf{O}}$ that specifies the cell wall is defined by a lattice vector $\boldsymbol{b}^{\mathbf{L}}$, which is the Burgers vector for the dislocation.

The usual construction for boundaries of mixed character is shown schematically in Fig. 3(a), and a corresponding schematic diagram of our observed structures is shown in Fig. 3(b). In each analyzable case the dislocations still lie in or close to the O cell walls so that the dislocations do, in fact, correctly accommodate the misorientation. The rotations of the individual dislocation segments appear to occur mainly within the O cell walls, although for boundary 1 there is also a slight rotation out of the cell wall for the dislocation rotated away from its Burgers vector, as shown in the stereographic projection given in Fig. 4.

### 4. EXPLANATIONS FOR THE OBSERVATIONS

The reasons that the boundaries become puckered onto two boundary planes are not immediately obvious; certainly the total dislocations line length is increased by this phenomenon. (We choose to use the term "puckering" as distinct from "faceting" because facets are usually associated with low index or high symmetry boundary planes; the boundaries observed here fall into neither category.) Explanations based on the anisotropy of the dislocation line energy appear to be insufficient because the energy change is minimal; those segments which rotate into lower energy orientations also reduce their lengths, while the segments which rotate into higher energy orientations increase their lengths. A refinement to the computation is achieved by determining the forces acting on each of the three dislocation lines close to the nodes at either end of the central dislocation segment. These forces are then summed to give an estimate of the total force acting on the node rather than simply on the central dislocation. Alternatively, it may be considered that this procedure has the effect of adding the torques on the second and third types of dislocation segment, rotating in the opposite sense to the torque on the first; this is because (in an infinite array) the test points on segments B and C at one end of the central A segment are entirely equivalent to test points at the far ends of the B and C segments at the other end of the central A segment. Thus, for an infinite array where all the segments of a given type are equivalent, the summation used here measures the total torque on A segments in one sense, and the equivalent torque on B and C segments (which must rotate in the opposite direction to maintain the contiguity of the array) in the opposite sense.

Again, it is seen that the pure twist boundary is stable in its planar form, but all boundaries of mixed character tend to become puckered by the interaction forces acting on the individual dislocation segments. The maximum puckering occurs at about $\phi=45^{\circ}$, where the value of $P/D$ is approximately 0.7. In contrast, the flat boundary exhibits a single narrow peak of energy density, which extends to a much greater height. The actual heights of the vari- ous peaks in these energy distributions de- pend sensitively on the choice of inner cut-off radius, since they asymptotically approach infinity. The effective cut-off radius used in plotting Fig. 11 is $0.035D$, corresponding to about 3.5 nm in the experimental boundaries, which is quite large; this value was chosen to keep the peaks of manageable height for plot- ting. Smaller cut-off radii tend to increase the height of the flat boundary peak more than those associated with the puckered boundary, and so we are confident in stating that the puckered boundary has the lower energy.

Whether the zero-torque configurations ac- tually minimize the boundary energy remains unclear, but the fact that they represent low energy forms of the boundary structure rela- tive to the flat boundaries has apparently been established.

## 5. DISCUSSION

In Section 4 we have shown that the self- interaction forces of a dislocation array tend to distort the network into a puckered con- figuration. This result is true for any array corresponding to an asymmetrical grain boundary, where the Burgers vectors of the dislocations are not symmetrically disposed with respect to the boundary plane and was derived using only linear isotropic elasticity. The effect is not therefore one of anisotropic elastic response, but it will no doubt be af- fected by the isotropicity of the crystals. In the experimental cases that we have studied, the dislocation array was the sole constituent of the interface in question, and the pucker- ing response to the self-interaction forces was relatively simple; in other cases, such as large- angle grain boundaries, epitaxial interfaces and semicoherent interphase boundaries, dis- location arrays are embedded in the core structure of the interface. For these cases the interaction forces between individual disloca- tion segments will be similar to those de- scribed here, but the response to these forces may be complicated by the need to distort the boundary plane in order to pucker the dis- location array. In particular, when the inter- facial core energy is large or strongly orienta- tion dependent, puckering may be suppressed at the expense of a periodic interfacial stress modulation with wavelength equal to the dis- location array spacing. These stresses may provide incipient instabilities which help to nucleate boundary migration.

The calculations which we have performed have all maintained an element of symmetry perpendicular to the boundary plane; this configuration was adopted as a computational convenience, but it leads inevitably to the re- sult that the individual dislocation segments remain within the O cell walls. In cases where the rotation axis lies in a general direction with respect to the boundary plane rather than in the $x_{b}-z_{b}$ plane, it is conceivable (and even likely) that the forces on the dislocation segments will tend to rotate them out of the O cell walls as observed, for example, in Fig. 4.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

Small-angle grain boundaries of mixed char- acter in zinc have been observed to adopt puckered configurations which deviatesignifi- cantly from the usually assumed planar dislo- cation array. The interaction forces exerted between the individual segments of the dislo- cation arrays act in a manner consistent with the observed behavior, and the puckered forms of the boundaries are of lower energy than the corresponding planar forms. It ap- pears that the puckering should be a general effect for small-angle grain boundaries and that the puckering forces described here may affect the behavior of other dislocation-con- taining interfaces.
