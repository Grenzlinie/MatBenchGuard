# High-resolution study of [011] low-angle tilt boundaries in aluminium

By J. M. PENISSON and A. BOURRET

Centre D' Etudes Nucléaires de Grenoble,
Département de Recherche Fondamentale,
Section de Physique du Solide, 85 X-38041 Grenoble Cedex, France

[Received 7 March 1979 and accepted 13 April 1979]

## ABSTRACT

The structure of [011] low-angle tilt boundaries in aluminium has been studied in bicrystals with different grain boundary planes (GBP). High-resolution electron microscopy using lattice-plane imaging and tilted illumination at 100 kV gave the fine structure of this type of boundary. Edge and $60^{\circ}$ dislocations were always found: the (011) GBP is stable and has the lowest energy. The edge sessile dislocations showed no tendency to rotate in a thin foil and the elastic relaxation field was very well reproduced in the high resolution image. By contrast, a $60^{\circ}$ dislocation tended to rotate away from the [011] direction in a thin foil as shown by the weak-beam technique: this rotation is limited by interaction with other dislocation segments. Splitting of the $60^{\circ}$ dislocation, if it exists at all, is less than $8 \AA$.

## § 1. INTRODUCTION

New aspects of the dislocation structure in low-angle grain boundaries have been observed recently in germanium (Bourret and Desseaux 1979). The geometrical approach given by the Frank rule (Frank 1950) and systematically applied in the 0-lattice construction (Bollmann 1970) enables one to deduce the possible Burgers vectors when both the rotation between adjacent grain and the habit plane of the interface are specified. However the possibility of introducing partial dislocations and consequently stacking faults leads to great complexity in the dislocation structure. No such effect was expected in aluminium whose stacking fault energy is high and a comparison with german- ium appears to be necessary. Relatively few observations of dislocations in aluminium at high resolution have been made (Parsons and Hoelke 1969); the main difficulty arises from the easy rearrangement of dislocations during thinning in this material and from the small atomic plane distance to be imaged $(d_{111}=2 \cdot 3 \AA)$ when compared to semiconductors. The use of aluminium bicrystals (Levy 1969) is a great advantage in producing dislocations in a given orientation ; however, dislocation rotation may occur for glissile dis- locations and special care is necessary when interpreting lattice-fringe images.
Therefore the main purposes of this study are :

(i) to determine the Burgers vector of the dislocation arrays when the boundary orientation is close to a pure [011] tilt configuration,

(ii) to analyse the atomic-plane relaxation outside the core region in terms of elastic energy and relate it to the high-resolution images,

## § 2. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

Aluminium bicrystals were grown by a zone-melting method from two disorientated seeds at the ENSM de St Etienne. The main disorientation component is a pure tilt with [011] common axis and a tilt angle $\theta \sim 2.5$ to $3^{\circ}$; the residual twist component is $\leqslant 30'$ as measured by X-rays. Two grain boundary planes (GBP) are made, $(01\overline{1})$ and (100). Specimens are cut in a plane perpendicular to the tilt axis with an electrolytic saw in order to avoid any mechanical damage (Levy 1969). Thin slices 0.5 mm thick are then electrolytically thinned in a 90:10 mixture of ethanol and perchloric acid. A JEM 100 C electron microscope fitted with a high-resolution goniometer stage is used at a direct magnification of 650 000. Two sets of $\{111\}$ lattice planes are observable at 100 keV in the tilted illumination mode and two images are taken, one for each $\{111\}$ set on the same area. The specimen orientation is initially set to align the [011] zone axis along the optic axis. The incident beam is then inclined for a particular (111) diffraction vector and is therefore at the exact Bragg condition for the corresponding planes. This procedure

## § 4. Discussion
### 4.1. Grain boundary geometry
The general description of the subgrain boundary is well represented by the 0-lattice theory. The 0-lattice corresponding to a rotation around a [011] axis is represented in fig. 6. Dislocation lines are given by intersecting the

cell walls (separating regions of good coincidence) by the GBP. For (01$\overline{1}$) GBP, pure edge dislocations are obtained (path A); for (100) GBP, arrays of alternate $60^{\circ}$ dislocations (path B'), and the (21$\overline{1}$) GBP is also composed of another combination of $60^{\circ}$ dislocations (path B). The steps contained in the (01$\overline{1}$) GBP cannot be described on the hypothesis of a pure rotation around the [011] axis; in this case the $60^{\circ}$ dislocations would have alternating signs for their screw component and this is not the case. In fact $60^{\circ}$ dislocations accommodate a twist component of the grain boundary. The residual twist component is equal to $0 \cdot 2^{\circ}$ around a [01$\overline{1}$] axis: this gives a mean distance between $60^{\circ}$ dislocations equal to $400 \mathring{A}$, as deduced from the Frank formula, and this is very close to the mean experimental value. It is worth noticing that these parallel $60^{\circ}$ dislocations incorporated in the A-type dislocations are only part of a honeycomb network: the dislocations not perpendicular to the foil have disappeared during the thinning process. In fact an array of parallel identical $60^{\circ}$ dislocations in bulk material would give non-zero stress at large distance and therefore is not stable; however the remaining parallel segments in the thin foil are stable because their screw component gives stresses decreasing as $1/r^{2}$ (Eshelby and Stroh 1951).

0-lattice of a pure [011] tilt low-angle boundary. Path A corresponds to a (01$\overline{1}$) GBP ; path B' to a (100) GBP and B to a (21$\overline{1}$) GBP.

Apart from the geometrical description it is also necessary to consider the energies involved. Three types of walls will be described here: (01$\overline{1}$), (100) and (21$\overline{1}$) GBP. The energy per unit area of a pure (01$\overline{1}$) grain boundary composed of edge dislocations is given (Bourret and Desseaux 1979) by
$$
W_{\mathrm{A}}=[\mu b \theta /(4 \pi(1-\nu))] \log \left(e b /\left(2 \pi r_{0} \theta\right)\right)
$$
where $\mu$ and $\nu$ are respectively the shear modulus and the Poisson ratio $b$ is the length of the Burgers vector $1 / 2$ [110], $\theta$ is the rotation angle between the two crystals, and $r_{0}$ is the cut off radius (supposed to be small compared with the distance between two dislocations).

Using the Rey and Saada method (Rey and Saada 1976) it is relatively easy to derive, for a (100) GBP composed of alternate $60^\circ$ dislocations (path B') the energy
$$
W_{\mathrm{B}^{\prime}}=[\mu b \sqrt{ } 2(4-\nu) \theta /(16 \pi(1-\nu))] \log \left(u b /\left(\sqrt{ } 2 \pi r_{0} \theta\right)\right)
$$
with
$$
u=\exp \{3 /(4-\nu)-\nu \log 2 /(4-\nu)\}.
$$

Thus $W_{\mathrm{B}^{\prime}} / W_{\mathrm{A}} \simeq \sqrt{ } 2(4-\nu) / 4$, showing that an A-type wall has a much lower energy than a B'-type. Likewise for a $(21 \overline{1})$ GBP composed of $60^{\circ}$ dislocations (B-type wall) the energy is given by :
$$
W_{\mathrm{B}}=[\mu b(4-\nu) \theta /(8 \pi \sqrt{ } 3(1-\nu))] \log \left(v b \sqrt{ } 3 /\left(2 \pi r_{0} \theta\right)\right)
$$
with
$$
v=\exp \{3 /(4-\nu)-(2+\nu) \log 2 /(4-\nu)\}.
$$

$W_{\mathrm{B}}$ is of the same order of magnitude as $W_{\mathrm{A}}$ and slightly larger. Thus contrary to the germanium case (Bourret and Desseaux 1979), the A-type wall is stable and shows no tendency to rotate as illustrated on fig. 7 for $\nu=0 \cdot 33$, $r_{0}=b / 4$ and $b=2 \cdot 86 \mathring{A}$. Rotation would have formed a faceted GBP with a B-type wall without a decrease in energy. This fact is experimentally confirmed

Interesting features of the elastic field are very well reproduced experimentally, the long-range rotation corresponding to the subgrain boundary and the asymmetry of the elastic relaxation relative to the (111) extra half-plane. The exact correspondence between fringes and atomic planes is defined in a somewhat arbitrary manner ; in fact in the tilted illumination imaging mode, a lateral shift of the fringe pattern relative to the atomic position is introduced. Thus the good fit between experimental and

calculated positions is rather suprising at first sight. A definite answer to image interpretation can be given only by complete dynamical calculation in a defected crystal (Cowley 1978). This approach is very cumbersome and no useful results are yet available. However qualitative arguments could give a fruitful approach. Considering first the perfect crystal, the intensity of the fringe system when the undeflected beam and only one diffracted beam G are admitted to the objective aperture is given by :

$$
I(\mathbf{r})=A_{0}{ }^{2}+A_{\mathbf{g}}{ }^{2}+2 A_{0} A_{\mathbf{g}} \cos \left(2 \pi \mathbf{g r}+\psi_{\mathbf{g}}-\psi_{0}+\phi_{\mathbf{g}}-\phi_{0}\right)
$$

where $A_{0}, \psi_{0}, A_{\mathbf{g}}, \psi_{\mathbf{g}}$, are respectively the amplitudes and phases of the undeflected and G beams, and $\phi_{\mathbf{g}}-\phi_{0}$ is the optical phase shift between undeflected and G beams. Thus the fringe pattern is parallel to the atomic planes and laterally shifted by a quantity

$$
\Delta r=\left(\psi_{\mathbf{g}}-\psi_{0}+\phi_{\mathbf{g}}-\phi_{0}\right) / 2 \pi g.
$$

When the crystal is deformed near an edge dislocation, the structure will be properly reproduced by the optical system if :

(i) the dynamical conditions are not modified,
(ii) the optical phase shift is constant.

These two conditions are, to a first approximation, fulfilled for rotations of the structure but not for large dilatations. For an edge dislocation with a planar strain, the dilatation $e$ and the rotation angle $w$, are given in polar coordinates by (Hirth and Lothe 1968)

$$
e=-b(1-2 v) \sin [\theta / 2 \pi r(1-v)]
$$
$$
w=b \cos [\theta / \pi r].
$$

Atomic displacements will be given by partial integrals of $(e+w)$ so that the atomic pattern will be fairly well reproduced in regions where $w>e$. Where the dilation is larger than the rotation, the image will also look like the atomic arrangement for very small values of dilatation. In fact the major part of the elastic field of the dislocation is well reproduced as illustrated on fig. 8, even in an imaging mode using tilted illumination, apart from an unknown lateral shift smaller than $b / 2$.

### 4.3. Influence of foil surface
Correct imaging of the displacement field near an edge dislocation is an indirect justification for neglecting the surface effects. It means essentially that no rigid rotation occurs, as was experimentally verified by weak-beam experiments. Since the A-type dislocation is sessile, it is likely that no rotation occurs during the thinning process. It also indicates that the surface relaxation is not detectable at the actual level of resolution. Inspection of the elastic field near an edge dislocation at a free surface (Yoffe 1961) shows that displacements perpendicular to the Burgers vector are mainly affected by the 'image' dislocation. The order of magnitude of such a displacement is less than $b / 2 \pi$ for $r<10 b$, which is actually in the error bar.

Looking now at the $60^\circ$ dislocation (B-type) the situation is completely different. The line of discontinuity in the fringe pattern (fig. 2) along the glide plane could be interpreted either as a small splitting into two partial dislocations or as a rotation of the dislocation during the thinning. The first interpretation proposed previously (Bourret and Penisson 1978) is questionable in view of the weak-beam experiment as described above.

The equilibrium position of a dislocation segment in a thin foil could be found approximately by a method developed by Hazzledine (Hazzledine, Karnthaller and Winter 1975): the dislocation is supposed to be rigid and the equilibrium position is found by minimizing the energy using isotropic elasticity ; in this condition any mixed dislocation tends to rotate to increase its screw character. An isolated $60^\circ$ dislocation would rotate through an angle $\psi_{\mathrm{m}}$ given by minimizing $E_{\mathrm{B}}$ :

$$
E_{\mathrm{B}}=\left(\mu b^{2} t / 4 \pi \cos \psi\right)\left[\cos ^{2}(\pi / 3-\psi)+\sin ^{2}(\pi / 3-\psi) /(1-\nu)\right] \log \left(R / r_{0}\right)
$$

with $\psi$ equal to the rotation angle from the $Oz$ direction, positive towards the screw orientation. $R$ is the outer cut-off radius and $t$ is the foil thickness.

In the aluminium case the equilibrium angle $\psi_{\mathrm{m}}$ is equal to $22^\circ$. This angle is independent of the foil thickness. This result is in contradiction with the observations and it is necessary to introduce the interactions within the grain

$$
E_{\mathrm{T}}=E_{\mathrm{B}}+E_{\mathrm{A}_{1} \mathrm{~B}}+E_{\mathrm{A}_{2} \mathrm{~B}}

where $E_{\mathrm{A}_{1} \mathrm{~B}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{A}_{2} \mathrm{~B}}$ are the interaction energies between $\mathrm{A}_{1}, \mathrm{~B}$ and $\mathrm{A}_{2}, \mathrm{~B}$. If interaction image stresses are neglected and A-type dislocations are supposed to be fixed, $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ is given by :

$$

+\mu b^{2} / 4 \pi(1-\nu) & {\left[-\frac{z \sqrt{ } 6}{3} \sin (\pi / 3-\psi) A(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta}) / \sin ^{2} \psi\right.} \\
& \left.+\frac{\sqrt{ } 3}{3} \sin (\pi / 3-\psi) B(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta})\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

with

$$
A(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta})=K(y_{\alpha}, x_{\beta})+\cos \psi K(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta})
$$

$$
B(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta})=I(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta})-z^{2} J(x_{\alpha}, y_{\beta}).
$$

Explicit expressions for $I, J, K$, which are geometrical factors, are given by Hirth and Lothe (1968). They can be expressed as a function of $\psi$ and the geometrical parameters $z$ and $h$ defined in fig. 9.

The energy is minimized numerically with respect to $\psi$ and the following constants are used : $R=1 \mu \mathrm{m}, b=2.86 \mathring{A}, \nu=0.33, h=10 \mathring{A}, z=40 \mathring{A} ; h$ and $z$ are directly measured on the micrographs and are independent of thickness. The equilibrium rotation angle $\psi_{\mathrm{m}}$ is represented in table 2 as a function of $t$ and compared to the experimental values. Down to $100 \mathring{A}$ thickness the agreement is correct and gives some confidence to the calculated values under this limit where no experimental measurements are possible.

The projected length $l$ of a $60^{\circ}$ dislocation, as deduced from $l=t \operatorname{tg} \psi_{\mathrm{m}}$, is a maximum for $t \sim 100 \mathring{A}$. However for $t \sim 200 \mathring{A}, l$ is limited to $7 \mathring{A}$ showing that, for a large thickness, the sessile dislocations of the grain boundary are very effective in aligning glissile dislocations. The best lattice image of a $60^{\circ}$ dislocation showing a minimum extension of $8 \mathring{A}$ (fig. 2) was obtained in this thickness range. However, even in these conditions the projected length of the inclined dislocation is of the order of the dissociation width. A stacking fault energy of $120 \mathrm{erg} \mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (Blandin, Friedel and Saada 1966) would give a splitting distance of $7 \mathring{A}$. Therefore no conclusion on the splitting of a $60^{\circ}$ dislocation is possible, except that it must be less than $8 \mathring{A}$. It should however be possible to make further progress by using a higher grain-boundary angle. The equilibrium conditions do not depend on the scale of the geometrical arrangements so that, by dividing the distance between defects by a factor $M$, the same $\psi_{\mathrm{m}}$ is obtained for a thickness divided by the same factor. For instance with a tilt angle $\theta=6^{\circ}$, the projected length will be equal to $3.5 \mathring{A}$ in a $100 \mathring{A}$-thick foil.

## § 5. CONCLUSIONS

In aluminium—and this result is extendable to all high-energy stacking-fault materials—the structure of a low-angle grain boundary is relatively simple. Only edge and $60^{\circ}$ dislocations are present when the tilt axis is [011] in contrast to germanium whose stacking fault energy is lower. The value of the stacking fault energy will also directly control the stability of different GBPs. In aluminium the lowest energy GBP is (011) instead of (112) when splitting into partials can occur. High resolution images obtained on each individual edge sessile dislocation show a remarkable accordance with the elastic theory outside the core ; it is concluded that, even in the tilted imaging mode, valuable information is obtained on the elastic distortions in the case of planar strain especially in regions where these are mainly rotations. However for glissile dislocations, rotation of dislocations occurs during thinning and the projected image may not look like the projected structure. This rotation appears to be the main limitation of the study of end-on glissile dislocation. This difficulty can be overcome by stabilizing the dislocations in a relatively high-angle grain boundary, or in substances which present a high lattice friction such as those with the diamond structure.
