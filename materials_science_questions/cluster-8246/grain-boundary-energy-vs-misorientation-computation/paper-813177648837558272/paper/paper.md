# Systematic study of grain boundary atomistic structures and related properties in cubic zirconia bicrystals

*Dedicated to Professor Dr. Duk Yong Yoon on the occasion of his 65th birthday*

Systematic grain boundary study of cubic zirconia has been conducted by using bicrystals. It is clearly demonstrated that grain boundary atomistic structures dramatically change according to the misorientations and plane orientations of the boundaries, resulting in a dramatic change of excess energies and solute segregation behaviors. Combining with theoretical calculations, it is found that grain boundaries possess unique coordination-deficient cation sites at the cores, and their densities have a clear correlation with these properties in high-angle grain boundaries. This result indicates that grain boundary properties in ceramics are possibly determined by the accumulation of coordination-deficient sites. Thus, systematic grain boundary study using bicrystal offers fundamental understandings of the relationship between atomistic structures and properties in ceramic grain boundaries.

**Keywords:** Yttria-stabilized cubic zirconia; Bicrystal; Grain boundary atomistic structure; High-resolution transmission electron microscopy; Solute segregation

## 1. Introduction

The grain boundary atomistic structure has been the subject of intense interest for a wide variety of materials [1, 2]. It is expected that grain boundary phenomena such as grain boundary diffusion, sliding, fracture, corrosion, etc., which may affect overall materials properties, are determined by the grain boundary structure (e. g., [3]). Therefore, understanding the nature of grain boundary structure and related property is of great importance for designing better or novel materials. However, the grain boundary structure is a function of five macroscopic geometrical degrees of freedom [3], in addition to temperature, pressure, and compositions, which extremely complicates the understanding of geometry-structure-property relations in grain boundaries. Systematic study for a well-defined grain boundary is, thus, very important in order to clarify such relationships. In contrast to metals, a systematic grain boundary study is still limited in ceramic materials. Recently, however, grain boundary studies using bicrystals have been applied to many kinds of ceramic oxides [4-24]. Bicrystal experiments have the advantage that the grain boundary geometries can be precisely controlled. Systematic bicrystal experiments, thus, have the potential to relate geometry with structure and property in ceramic materials. This is required for the clear understanding of the grain boundary phenomena and resulting materials properties.

We have studied cubic zirconia bicrystals to investigate the relationship between geometry, atomistic structure, excess energy, and solute segregation behavior. Zirconia ceramics are one of the promising oxide ceramics, which are utilized as oxygen sensors, solid electrolyte for fuel cells, coating materials, and so on [25-28]. Their mechanical and functional properties are considered to be related to grain boundary factors, such as structure and chemistry [29-31]. However, the fundamental understanding of the effect of the individual grain boundaries on the properties still remain unresolved.

We systematically fabricated nine types of symmetric tilt grain boundaries with the same rotation axis of [110]. The bicrystals include low-angle, random, and some low $\Sigma$ grain boundaries, according to the coincidence site lattice (CSL) theory [32], to cover all types of grain boundaries. Combing high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) with theoretical atomistic calculations, the nature of local atomistic structures at the grain boundary can be revealed and, therefore, the origin of the properties can be well understood. The aim of the present paper is to review our results obtained for zirconia bicrystals and to extract the factors to determine the grain boundary properties from the view point of the atomistic structures.

## 2. Experimental Procedure

Commercially available high-purity $\mathrm{ZrO}_{2}-9.6\ \text{mol}\%\ \mathrm{Y}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}$ single crystals (Dowa Minning Co., Ltd.) are used for preparing the bicrystals. The single crystals contain a certain amount of $\mathrm{Y}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}$ for stabilizing the high-temperature cubic phase at room temperature.

The precisely oriented single crystals were successively polished using 9 $\mu$m, 3–6 $\mu$m and 1 $\mu$m diamond suspensions to achieve a mirror finish on the bonding side of the surfaces. The size of each single crystal was selected to be $5 \times 10 \times 10$ mm³, so as to obtain 10 mm cube bicrystals.

Grain boundary energies were evaluated by the thermal grooving technique [e. g., 33]. The surfaces of the cut plates from the cube bicrystals were mechanically polished with diamond suspension to achieve a mirror state. Then, the specimens were annealed at 1673 K for 2 h in air to groove the boundaries. The groove profiles formed at the boundaries were analysed directly from the surface images obtained by the atomic force microscope (AFM, SHIMADZU, SPM–9500E). The details for evaluation of the grain boundary energy are reported elsewhere [20, 34].

The atomistic structures of the grain boundaries are examined by HRTEM. Foils for HRTEM observations were prepared by a standard procedure using an ion thinning method. HRTEM observations were mainly carried out by a TOPCON EM-002B (200 kV) and a HITACHI H–9000NAR (300 kV) transmission electron microscope. For the detailed observation of the $\Sigma=9$, $\{221\}$ grain boundary, a JEOL ARM–1250 (1250 kV) transmission electron microscope was used. TEM-EDS analyses were carried out on a Topcon EM-002BF field-emission-type high-resolution electron microscope using a Noran Voyager EDS system with a focused probe size of less than 1 nm. HRTEM image simulations were made using the Tempas programs based on the multi-slice method, developed by Kilaas [35].

To predict the grain boundary atomistic structures theoretically, systematic lattice statics calculations were performed using the GULP program code [36]. It has been well demonstrated that the lattice statics calculations are an effective method for predicting the stable grain boundary structures in many kinds of ceramic materials [37–42]. The potential parameters used in this study were taken from the literature reported by Lewis and Catlow [43]. All the calculations were performed on the assumption of pure cubic zirconia in this study. Although a more precise theoretical model for predicting pure zirconia polymorphs has been reported [44], we used the present model for simplicity in predicting the grain boundary structures. Ions were treated as rigid spheres to facilitate the convergence of the calculations for the grain boundary cells with complicated atomic coordinates. The effects of electronic polarization can be neglected in the present calculation because the shell model was confirmed to make minor effects for the present grain boundaries.

The initial cell configurations are systematically varied with different translation states between adjoining grains. The lattice energy for each cell configuration was iteratively minimized to achieve the optimum atomic positions at 0 K using the quasi-Newton method with the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno Hessian update scheme [45]. The calculated energies were then evaluated as a function of translation states, and the atomic configurations with local energy minima were subsequently selected as the equilibrium structures. Details of the calculation methods are reported elsewhere [20].

The lattice energies were calculated by summing all the potentials of constituent ions in the calculation cells. The grain boundary excess energies were estimated by subtracting the calculated lattice energy of the single-crystal cell from the calculated lattice energy of the cell including the grain boundary.

## 3. Results

The misorientation angles, $2\theta$, and subsequently the grain boundary planes are systematically varied from the top of the figure to the bottom. Atomic-level bonding of crystals is confirmed in these images. It is demonstrated that the grain boundary structures dramatically change according to their misorientation angles. It is expected that the changes in grain boundary atomistic structure may affect the grain boundary properties such as excess energies and impurity/solute segregations. To investigate the relationships, the experimental measurements of grain boundary energy and solute ($Y^{3+}$) segregation behavior were performed for these systematic bicrystals.

### 3.2. High-angle grain boundary
At higher misorientations, the grain boundary structure can hardly be recognized as an array of dislocations. The grain boundary structures are rather considered to be composed of an array of locally accommodated structure segments, usually called structure units [48]. The concept of the structure units was firstly proposed in metal grain boundaries, but the concept is also applicable for other types of materials. In the present high-angle grain boundaries, however, the situation is rather complicated because zirconia is composed of two kinds of ions, $Zr^{4+}$ and $O^{2-}$, which results in a crystal structure with cation and anion sublattices, respectively. It is very difficult to determine the structure of the anion sublattice directly from the HRTEM images because the images only correspond to the position of cation sublattices in the present conditions. In this study, therefore, the grain boundary structure determination was assisted by lattice statics calculations including the anion sublattice. Here, we focus on the three high-angle grain boundaries, i. e. $\Sigma=3,\{111\}$, $\Sigma=11,\{113\}$ and $\Sigma=9,\{221\}$ boundaries, respectively, which showed very unique characteristics in energy and solute segregation behavior as already shown in Fig. 3.

The stable atomic structure of the grain boundary is also theoretically predicted, and the lowest-energy grain boundary structure is shown in Fig. 7c. The grain boundary excess energy was calculated to be $3.01\ \text{J/m}^2$ [20], which is the highest among the three grain boundaries studied here. The stable structure is composed of asymmetric structure units and has a rather large boundary expansion of $1.00\ \text{\AA}$, resulting in the formation of slightly large free volumes at the core of the boundary.

The simulated HRTEM image based on the predicted structure model is shown in Fig. 7b. The simulated image agrees with the experimental image, as for the periodical array of asymmetrical structure units. However, the calculated image shows more expanded features at each structure unit than the experimental image. The difference may be derived from the slight difference in the boundary expansion. In the real grain boundary, $\text{Y}^{3+}$ segregation causes the introduction of oxygen vacancies for compensating the charge differences associated with the substitution of $\text{Y}^{3+}$ to the $\text{Zr}^{4+}$ sites. The accumulation of oxygen vacancies at the grain boundary core has been experimentally suggested in another type of grain boundary in yttria-stabilized cubic zirconia [50]. The introduction of oxygen vacancies is considered to relax the interactions between the neighboring oxygen ions inside the structure units, and allow the structure units to slightly shrink with the cation frame structures being preserved. This effect is expected to be larger in this boundary than in the other grain boundaries because the $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=9$, $\{221\}$ grain boundary had the highest $\text{Y}^{3+}$ segregation among all the grain boundaries fabricated here.

Similar to the other two low-energy grain boundaries, cation sites with different coordination state are found along the calculated structure, as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 7c. These cation sites have seven-fold coordination. However, the density of the coordination-deficient sites appears to be much higher than that of the other two grain boundaries. The coordination-deficient cation sites must be the characteristic boundary sites in cubic zirconia grain boundaries.

### 3.3. Grain boundary faceting

Despite the same bonding condition for the bicrystal fabrication, only the $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=3,\{112\}$ grain boundary exhibited a faceted feature. This indicates the importance of grain boundary orientation even for the larger-scale grain boundary morphology. Figure 8a shows a HRTEM image of the boundary. This plane orientation was also observed in the $\text{NiSi}_2$ system which has the same cubic fluorite symmetry [51], suggesting that the present $\{111\}/\{115\}$ boundary is energetically favorable. It is clearly seen that the faceting has a constant periodicity and exhibits a unique shape, as schematically illustrated in the inset in Fig. 8b; i. e., the corners of the facets were very sharp on one side and blunt on the other side. ## 4. Discussion

### (a) Relationship between atomistic structures and properties in zirconia grain boundaries.

The atomistic calculations of high-angle grain boundaries clearly showed that the relaxed grain boundary structures tend to possess cation sites with coordination deficiencies. The tendency of creating miscoordination sites at the core of the boundaries is similar to fcc metal grain boundaries [52]. It is interesting to point out that the coordination-defi- cient sites still keep the cubic coordination polyhedra after the effective atomistic relaxations. This tendency may indi- cate that grain boundary structures are relaxed so as to re- tain the bond length between the respective ions similar to those in the bulk.

To evaluate the effect of the coordination-deficient cation sites, the densities of the sites are estimated from the calcu- lated grain boundary models. The densities are simply esti- mated by dividing the number of the coordination-deficient cation sites around the boundary by the boundary area. As a first approximation, in this estimation, the bond distortions and the difference in coordination numbers are not taken into account. The allowance of the bond lengths between $Zr^{4+}$ ion sites and coordinating $O^{2-}$ ion sites are assumed within a deviation of 25 % from those in the grain interior. ### (b) The mechanism of grain boundary faceting

The experimental observations indicate that the periodicity of the facets must be related to the periodic array of DSC dislocations. These periodical DSC dislocations should reflect the slight misalignment from the perfect $\Sigma=3$ orientation during the fabrication of the bicrystal. A schematic model of the grain boundary faceting is shown in Fig. 13. We assumed atomically flat bonding surface before bonding (Fig. 13a), and the bonding begins by forming the transient {112} boundary structure as in Fig. 13b. After the transient {112} boundary is formed, the DSC dislocations are periodically introduced to compensate the slight misorientation from the perfect $\Sigma=3$ orientation. The density of the dislocations should be determined by the degree of the misalignment from the perfect $\Sigma=3$ orientation, and the periodicity is a result of the mutual elastic interaction between the DSC dislocations. In-between the dislocations, the faceting will fully progress, with leaving the only one sharp corner at the middle, as shown in Fig. 13c, to reduce the total grain boundary energy. The atomistic mechanism of faceting transition is proposed elsewhere [19]. Thus, the periodicity of the facet must be determined by the periodicity of the grain boundary dislocations. It is possible that the periodicity of the facets can be altered by changing the slight misorientation between the adjacent crystals, introducing the DSC dislocations with different periodicity.

## 5. Conclusions

Systematic grain boundary study using cubic zirconia bicrystals has been conducted. The following conclusions are obtained in this study.
(1) Cubic zirconia bicrystals can be fabricated by diffusion bonding of two $\mathrm{ZrO}_{2}-9.6 \mathrm{~mol} \% \mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ single crystals at 1873 K for 15 h in air. HRTEM observations revealed that the two single crystals were well bonded on an atomistic level to form grain boundaries without any secondary phases such as amorphous phases.
(2) The grain boundary atomistic structures, excess energies, and solute segregation behaviors were strongly dependent on the grain boundary geometries.
(3) Low-angle grain boundaries can be described by a periodical array of dislocations which compensate the slight misorientation from the perfect crystal.
(4) The atomistic structures of $\Sigma=3,\{111\}, \Sigma=11$, $\{113\}$, and $\Sigma=9,\{221\}$ grain boundaries have been determined by HRTEM observations combined with lattice statics calculations. It was found that stable grain boundary structures possess cation sites with coordination deficiencies. Grain-boundary energy and solute segregation behaviors showed strong correlation with the density of the coordination-deficient cation sites at the grain boundary cores. The presence and accumulation of such deficient sites at the grain boundaries must be the main origin of the excess grain boundary energy and solute segregation phenomena in yttria-stabilized cubic zirconia grain boundaries.
(5) The $\Sigma=3,\{112\}$ grain boundary showed a faceted feature. The periodicity of the facet is related to the introduction of grain boundary dislocations.
