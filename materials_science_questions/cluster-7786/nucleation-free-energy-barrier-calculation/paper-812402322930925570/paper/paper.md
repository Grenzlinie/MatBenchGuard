# A TEST OF CLASSICAL NUCLEATION THEORY: CRYSTAL NUCLEATION OF LITHIUM DISILICATE GLASS

G.F. NEILSON and M.C. WEINBERG

Jet Propulsion Laboratory, California Institute of Technology, 4800 Oak Grove Drive, Pasa- dena, California, 91103 USA

Received 23 January 1979

For several inorganic glasses predictions have been made of the homogeneous crystal nuclea- tion rate using classical nucleation theory. However, in none of these cases were comparisons made with experiment, due primarily to the inability of being able to guarantee homogeneous conditions. Evidence has been provided that crystallite formation in $Li_{2} O \cdot 2 SiO_{2}$ glass may ini tiate by a homogeneous mechanism. Thus, we have computed the nucleation rate curve of lithi- um disilicate crystals in this glass. It is found that not only do all forms of the classical theory predict nucleation rates many orders of magnitude smaller than those observed, but also that the temperature dependence of the theoretical rate is quite different from that observed.

## 1. Introduction

Currently, we are witnessing a burgeoning interest in nucleation phenomena. Although the majority of the theoretical [1], experimental [2-4] and computer studies [5-7], have been aimed at the elucidation of liquid droplet condensation from supersaturated vapors, a wide variety of nucleation phenomena have been studied recently [6-8].

Herein, we shall be concerned with the nucleation of crystals from a viscous melt. Much of the theoretical framework developed for the description of droplet condensation is applicable to the problem of crystal nucleation. However, since in practice it is of greater difficulty to obtain explicit expressions for crystal nuclea- tion rates from fundamental principles, the development of nucleation theory for condensed systems has relied more heavily upon semi-phenomenological argu- ments. More specifically, the standard classical nucleation rate expression derived by Turnbull and Fisher [9] employed the Becker-Döring-Volmer-Weber [10] theory in conjunction with the absolute reaction rate theory of Eyring [11].

Several investigators have compared the predictions of classical nucleation the- ory with the results of homogeneous nucleation experiments in liquid-metal systems

[12,13]. They generally observed that although the temperature dependence of the nucleation rate was well described by the classical theory, the theoretically pre- dicted nucleation rates were about a factor of $10^6$ smaller than those obtained experimentally. Similar comparisons have been made for the crystal nucleation of organic compounds [14]. Once again, it was observed that the temperature depen- dence of the nucleation rate followed closely the theoretical prediction, and fur- thermore in some cases reasonable agreement was obtained between the theoreti- cally and experimentally determined prefactor.

On the other hand, the extent of knowledge available concerning homogeneous nucleation in inorganic glass-forming systems is much more limited. One reason for this state of affairs is due to the fact that most glasses crystallize heterogeneously. Nevertheless, a number of calculations have appeared in the literature which predict the rate of homogeneous nucleation in several inorganic glass forming systems [15-17]. It is important to note, however, that in all of the latter systems either no experimental nucleation results are available or nucleation has been observed to occur heterogeneously or on the surface. Recently, James et al. [18] measured the nucleation rate of $Li_2O \cdot 2 SiO_2$ crystals under various conditions and concluded that the crystallization behavior supported the view that it occurs homogeneously. Nucleation measurements in this system have also been made by Matusita and Tashiro [19]. However, no nucleation calculations have been performed to date to compare with these experiments.

The $Li_2O \cdot 2 SiO_2$ composition is particularly attractive for the study of crystal nucleation for a variety of reasons. It is a simple system for which phase diagram information and a variety of thermodynamic data is available, including heat capac- ities and enthalpies of fusion [19]. The structures of both glass and crystal phases have been determined through X-ray studies [20]. Also a considerable amount of experimental information for this system concerning glass-in-glass [21] and crystal- lite nucleation behavior is available [18], [19] and [22], including the effect of initial amorphous phase separation on subsequent crystallite nucleation processes [23]. Finally, through an extended composition range in the vicinity of the disili- cate composition, the glass viscosity is of suitable magnitude to allow convenient and detailed kinetic study of internal (volume) crystallite nucleation over a range of temperatures. At the disilicate composition, there is no evidence of metastable amorphous phase separation, and only $Li_2O \cdot 2 SiO_2$ crystals have been observed to internally nucleate and grow.

Thus, we have computed the nucleation rate of lithium disilicate crystals as a function of temperature, employing several variations of the standard classical nu- cleation rate expression (see sects. 2 and 3). We have found that not only does the classical theory predict nucleation rates many orders of magnitude smaller than those observed, but also that the temperature dependence of the nucleation rate predicted by theory is not in agreement with the temperature variation of the rate found experimentally when the usual procedure of associating the activation energy for motion across the nucleus-matrix interface with that for viscous flow or diffu-

sion is followed. We have considered a number of possible explanations for these discrepancies and found them all inadequate to explain the lack of agreement between the classical theory and experiments.

## 2. Classical nucleation theory

To date, all calculations of the theoretical rate of homogeneous nucleation of crystallites in glasses have employed some form of the rate equation derived by Turnbull and Fisher [9]. For nuclei of spherical shape, their equation reads

$$
I=K_{v} \exp \left(-\Delta G_{\mathrm{A}} / k T\right) \exp \left(-\Delta G^{*} / k T\right), \tag{1}
$$

where

$$
K_{v}=n^{*} N(\sigma / k T)^{1 / 2}(2 v / 9 \pi)^{1 / 3}(k T / h), \tag{2}
$$

and

$$
\Delta G^{*}=16 \pi \sigma^{3} / 3\left(\Delta G_{v}\right)^{2} \tag{3}
$$

is the free energy required to form a nucleus of critical size at the temperature $T$. $\Delta G_{\mathrm{A}}$ is the free energy of activation required to move a basic molecular unit or mobile molecule across the glass-crystal interface. The quantity $\sigma$ is the interfacial crystal-glass surface free energy per unit area, $n^{*}$ is the number of molecular units in the surface of a critical nucleus, $N=\rho_{\mathrm{g}} A / M$ is the number of molecular units per unit volume of the glass phase where $\rho_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the density of the glass and $M$ is the molecular weight of the mobile species, $v$ is the volume per molecular unit in the crystal nucleus, $h$ is Planck's constant and $k$ the Boltzmann constant, and $\Delta G_{v}$ is the standard free energy difference berween glass and crystal per unit volume. For evaluation of eq. (2), we set

$$
v=M / \rho_{\mathrm{c}} A \tag{4}
$$

$$
=(4 \pi / 3)\left(r^{* 3} / i^{*}\right), \tag{5}
$$

where $\rho_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the crystal density, $A$ is Avogadro's number, $i^{*}$ is the number of molecular units in a critical nucleus and $r^{*}$ is its radius given by

$$
r^{*}=-2 \sigma / \Delta G_{v}. \tag{6}
$$

Also

$$
n^{*} \approx 4 i^{* 2 / 3}. \tag{7}
$$

Thus

$$
K_{v}=(8 N / 3 h)(k T \sigma)^{1 / 2}\left(4 \pi \rho_{\mathrm{c}} A / M\right)^{1 / 3} r^{* 2}. \tag{8}
$$

In eq. (1) we define the product $K_{v} \exp \left(\Delta G_{\mathrm{A}} / k T\right)$ as the pre-exponential factor,

$K'$, of the nucleation rate. For nucleation in glass-forming systems, this factor has been expressed as [24]

$$
K^{\prime}=N D^{\prime} / a_{0}^{2}, \tag{9}
$$

where $D'$ is the diffusion coefficient for molecular transport across the nucleus-matrix interface and $a_{0}$ is an intermolecular diameter. Since $D'$ is not easily determined by experimental measurement, $D'$ is approximated by the self diffusion coefficient, $D$. Uhlmann has further related $D'$ to the viscosity $\eta$ through the Stokes-Einstein equation to obtain

$$
K^{\prime}=N k T / 3 \pi a_{0}^{3} \eta. \tag{10}
$$

Uhlmann notes [25], however, that the Stokes-Einstein relation is stictly applicable only for simple molecular liquids, and for complex silicate glasses even the form of the relationship between $D$ and $\eta$, as well as the magnitude of the coefficient relating them, is open to question. Furthermore, the uncertainty of the approximation of setting $D^{\prime} \approx D$ is not known. The $\Delta G_{v}$ in eq. (3) is often approximated by [25,26]

$$
\Delta G_{v}=\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}} \Delta T_{\mathrm{R}} / V \tag{11}
$$

or

$$
\Delta G_{v}=\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}} T_{\mathrm{R}} \Delta T_{\mathrm{R}} / V, \tag{12}
$$

where $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the molar heat of fusion, $V$ is the molar volume, $T_{\mathrm{R}}=T / T_{\mathrm{F}}$ is the reduced temperature of the absolute melting temperature $T_{\mathrm{E}}$, and $\Delta T_{\mathrm{R}}=1-T_{\mathrm{R}}$. For large undercoolings, eq. (12) is considered to be more reliable than eq. (11), since the difference in heat capacities between crystal and glass is taken into account in eq. (12). Thus for the case of eq. (11) or (12), respectively,

$$
\Delta G^{*} / k T=16 \pi \sigma^{3} V^{2} /\left(3 k T_{\mathrm{E}} \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{2} T_{\mathrm{R}} \Delta T_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}\right), \tag{13}
$$

$$
\Delta G^{*} / k T=16 \pi \sigma^{3} V^{2} /\left(3 k T_{\mathrm{E}} \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{2} T_{\mathrm{R}}^{3} \Delta T_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}\right). \tag{14}
$$

The liquid (glass)-crystal surface tension may be related to the enthalpy of fusion via the empirical expression used by Turnbull [27] for liquid-metal systems.

$$
\sigma=\beta \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}} /\left(A^{1 / 3} V^{2 / 3}\right), \tag{15}
$$

$\beta$ is a parameter having a value of about 0.33 for non-metals and about 0.5 for metals. Thus, with eq. 14 one obtains

$$
\Delta G^{*} / k T=B / T_{\mathrm{R}}^{3} \Delta T_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}, \tag{16}
$$

where

$$
B=16 \pi \beta^{3} \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}} / 3 R T_{\mathrm{E}}. \tag{17}
$$

It has been found that for certain materials [24] $\Delta G^{*} / k T \approx 50$ at $\Delta T_{\mathrm{R}} \approx 0.2$. Thus, with these values one obtains from eq. (16), $B=1.024$.

## 3. Results

In this section we will compare the nucleation rates of lithium disilicate crystals predicted by various forms of the classical homogeneous nucleation rate equation with those determined experimentally. Both the temperature dependence and the magnitudes of the theoretical rates will be compared with experimental values. It will be shown that the theoretical values are very sensitive to the approximations employed for the pre-exponential factor as well as the form of the exponential in the classical equation. Also, the importance of employing a very accurate form for $\Delta G_{v}$ for nucleation rate calculations will be demonstrated.

The experimental crystallite number density data from which the corrected nucleation rates shown in fig. 1 were obtained, are very similar to those obtained recently by the same author [18]. In ref. [18], James provides evidence which suggests that this glass composition nucleates homogeneously. Thus, the experimental curve shown in fig. 1 should also correspond to the condition of homogeneous nucleation.

## 4. Discussion

Several possible explanations readily come to mind which could account for the discrepancies between the theoretical and experimental nucleation rates in this system. However, we shall argue that the obvious objections apparently do not suffice to rationalize the observed disparities.

Transient nucleation effects are known to be important in crystal nucleation from viscous liquids [10]. Furthermore, since transient nucleation will tend to increasingly suppress nucleation as the temperature is decreased, one might speculate that the deviation from linearity of $\log (\eta I)$ at low temperatures, shown in fig. 2, could be attributed to this phenomenon. However, this apparently is not the case. Transient nucleation has been observed and studied in this composition [22]. Furthermore, the steady state nucleation rates were established in ref. [22], and these are the ones which were employed herein. Also, it should be noted that inclusion of transient nucleation effects would only increase the discrepancy between the magnitudes of the experimental and theoretical nucleation rates since this would imply that the true steady state nucleation rates are even larger than those shown.

It is well known that metastable crystalline species can initially nucleate and transform to the stable crystalline form at later times. In fact, such metastable crystallite formation has been observed in the lithia-silica system [28]. However, it is considered unlikely that the formation of metastable species is the origin of the disagreements between experiment and theory. First of all, it was observed that in the temperature region below $520^{\circ} C$ and in the composition region close to 66.7 mol $\% SiO_{2}$ , the only crystalline species formed was $Li_{2} O \cdot 2 SiO_{2}$ . At higher tem peratures the formation of small amounts of $Li_{2} O \cdot SiO_{2}$ was observed in the slightly $Li_{2} O$ rich region ( $63-66 mol \% SiO_{2}$ ) while traces of crystoballite were observed on the somewhat high $SiO_{2}$ side of the $Li_{2} O \cdot 2 SiO_{2}$ composition glass. However, several studies have shown that the observed nucleation rates of composi- tions on either side of the $Li_{2} O \cdot 2 SiO_{2}$ glass composition are comparable. Thus, if metastable crystallite formation were responsible for the disagreement between theoretical and experimental rates, one would expect the observed experimen- tal rates of composition on either side of the $Li_{2} O \cdot 2 SiO_{2}$ composition to widely differ. As we have mentioned, nevertheless, this is not observed to occur.

Amorphous phase separation is believed to be capable of abetting crystal nuclea- tion [23]. Furthermore, as we have mentioned, phase separation does occur in the low $Li_{2} O$ region of this system. However, the crystal nucleation rates observed of glass composition within and outside of the immiscibility region do not differ greatly. Thus, although the immiscibility boundary is not believed to extend to the disilicate composition, the occurrence of phase separation could still not account for the magnitude of the discrepancy.

Finally, the fact that the experimental nucleation rates are many orders of mag- nitude larger than the theoretical rates suggests that the crystal nucleation may not be taking place homogeneously. However, it is unlikely that the disagreement canbe attributed to heterogeneous crystal nucleation. First of all, James et al., [18] have presented fairly convincing experimental evidence demonstrating the insensi- tivity of nucleation rates in this system to the purity of raw materials employed and the type of crucible utilized for melting. Furthermore, although the nucleation rates measured by James and by Matusita and Tashiro differ somewhat from each other, this divergence is totally insignificant compared to the differences between theoretical and experimental nucleation rates. In addition, the fact that the half- width of the experimental nucleation curves are much narrower than those pre- dicted by theory cannot be accounted for by arguments appealing to heterogeneous nucleation. According to heterogeneous nucleation theory [10], the only modifica- tion of the temperature dependent portion of the nucleation expression, eq. (3), needed to account for heterogeneous crystal nucleation is the introduction of a factor $f(\theta)$ into the exponential term, i.e.

$$\Delta G^{*}=\left[16 \pi \sigma^{3} / 3\left(\Delta G_{v}\right)^{2}\right] f(\theta),\qquad(20)$$

where $\theta$ is the substrate-nucleus contact angle, and $f(\theta)$ accounts for the change in

surface free energy associated with nucleus formation on a foreign surface. If het- erogeneous nucleation were occurring in this system, then the value of $\sigma$ which we determined from the experimental slope would be in error, but the prediction of the temperature dependence of $I$ would remain unaltered since $f(\theta)$ would "auto matically" be incorporated. In the event that several types of foreign nucleating species were present, then the overall nucleation rate would reflect the sum of the contributions from each type. In this case one would expect the experimentally determined $I$ versus $T$ curve to be broader than the one ascertained from homoge neous nucleation theory, rather than the reverse as was found.

James [18] has noted that the water content of lithium disilicate glass can alter the crystal nucleation rate by a factor of 5. Although such a small difference in nu- cleation rate is inadequate to explain the large discrepancies noted herein, the effect of water on nucleation rates in glasses has not been well explored either from the experimental or theoretical point of view. If the presence of water in the glass could somehow be responsible for the unusually large divergence between the theoretical and observed rate, then a modification of the standard nucleation theory would be required to account for such circumstances.

In summary, we reiterate that without marked revision of the standard theory, none of the possible explanations which we can envision seem to be adequate to account for the divergence between theory and experiment. We conclude that either there is a basic flaw in the classical nucleation theory as employed for describing crystal nucleation in glasses, or else there is a serious misinterpretation regarding the selection of the parameters than enter into this theory.
