Abstract—The heterogeneous nucleation of martensite at a dislocation is considered using an elasticity model. The dislocation is allowed to have an arbitrary Burgers vector representing, in an approximate manner, the stress field of a pile-up. It is found that an array of roughly 10 dislocations is required to nucleate martensite in the Fe-29 Ni system. The model shows that, depending on temperature, martensite can nucleate in either an isothermal or athermal manner. Any transition which shows athermal characteristics below a given start temperature should also show, according to the model, an isothermal transformation above this temperature. The role of diffusing interstitial atoms which could cause an impending isothermal martensite transformation to become bainitic in character is not considered.

## INTRODUCTION

The methods by which martensitic transformations nucleate and grow are not yet understood. One unanswered question is the nature of the defect sites at which heterogeneous nucleation occurs [1]. Another is the origin of the nucleation barrier. Does it reflect the need to overcome the interfacial energy in the usual manner or does it result from the thermally activated motion of the dislocations responsible for the lattice invariant shear within the embryo [2]? The reasons why some martensites nucleate in an athermal manner and other martensites nucleate in an isothermal manner are also not clear [3]. These questions remain unanswered because the required experiments are laborious and difficult. A relatively few small defects must be found [2,4,5] and studied during the transformation.

It is helpful in understanding phase transformations, particularly when experimental efforts are inconclusive, to develop thermodynamic models and study their characteristics. The calculation by Kaufman and Cohen [3], for example, which showed that homogeneous nucleation of martensite in steels was untenable is a good example of a useful model calculation.

Since their study, several models have been developed for heterogeneous nucleation of martensite. Olsen and Cohen [6] have studied a model in which a portion of a wall of dislocations underwent faulting on close-packed planes to produce the close-packed phase in the f.c.c. to h.c.p. transformation. A second shearing is required to produce the f.c.c. to b.c.c. transformation. No activation barrier occurs in their thermodynamic equations, but a barrier is postulated to result, however, from the thermally activated motion of the dislocations associated with the internal slip as first suggested by Magee [2]. # FREE ENERGY FOR HETEROGENEOUS NUCLEATION

In examining the nucleation problem, we will separate the total free energy change $(F_{T})$ during the formation of a martensite embryo containing (n) atoms into chemical $(F_{c})$ and elastic $(F_{E})$ contributions:

$$
F_{T}=F_{c}+F_{E}. \tag{1}
$$

The chemical free energy change is given in the usual manner by:

$$
F_{c}=g n+\gamma S, \tag{2}
$$

in which $g$ is the change in free energy per atom when an unconstrained volume of parent phase transforms at a constant temperature $(T)$ to the product phase, $\gamma$ is the specific interfacial free energy per unit area between parent and product and $S$ is the interfacial area of the embryo.

The elastic free energy change is made up of two terms:

$$
F_{E}=F^{D B}+F^{B B}, \tag{3}
$$

in which $F_{D B}$ represents the elastic interaction between the fixed dislocation and the Bain strains (transformation strains) of the embryo and $F^{B B}$ represents the self elastic energy of the embryo when formed within the constraints of the surrounding parent phase. The embryo is assumed to contain two twin-related variants noted as the [100] and [001] types according to the direction of the compressive strain $(e_{1})$ for the f.c.c. to b.c.c. Bain strain [10]. The strains associated with the two variants are conveniently described using a general transformation strain matrix given by:

$$
\mathbf{e}[H K L]=\left(\begin{array}{lll}
e_{11} & e_{12} & e_{13} \\
e_{21} & e_{22} & e_{23} \\
e_{31} & e_{32} & e_{33}
\end{array}\right) \tag{4}
$$

in which the strains $\mathbf{e}[100]$ for the [100] variant are given by:

$$
\mathbf{e}[100] \equiv\left(\begin{array}{ccc}
e_{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & e_{2} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & e_{2}
\end{array}\right), \tag{5}
$$

and by

$$
\mathbf{e}[001]=\left(\begin{array}{ccc}
e_{2} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & e_{2} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & e_{1}
\end{array}\right), \tag{6}
$$

for the [001] variant in which the Cartesian coordinates 1, 2, 3 in equation (4) are taken along the [100], [010] and [001] directions, respectively.

It can be shown [11,12] that the free energy of a twinned embryo in an otherwise defect-free crystal is minimized when the twins are regularly distributed with a period $(\lambda)$ having a volume fraction $(f)$ of the dominant variant as given by the crystallographic theories [13,14]. Thus the embryo shown schematically in Fig. 1a, having a periodic distribution of variants, is taken to be representative of the critical nucleus for a martensite plate. If the elastic free energy is properly computed for the discrete arrangement of twin-related variants in Fig. 1a, then the specific interfacial free energy of the embryo can be taken to be that for a coherent interface, i.e., between 10 and $40 \mathrm{ergs} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$. However, a detailed elasticity calculation for the discrete twin model is tedious. A less demanding alternative is to model the embryo as a continuous distribution of the two variants in the manner of

Easterling and Thölen [7], as shown schematically in Fig. 1b, having an averaged Bain strain given by:

$$
\mathbf{e} \equiv f \mathbf{e}[100]+(1-f) \mathbf{e}[001]. \tag{7}

On using this expedient, the strain energy in the interfacial regions associated with the alternating, discrete twins is no longer included in the elasticity calculation and must be accounted for by increasing the specific interfacial free energy to the value for a semi-coherent interface [8].

The details of the calculations have been carried out using the transformation defect and dislocation defect formalism described elsewhere [15], except that continuum elasticity theory was used instead of lattice statics. The analysis utilizes Fourier methods which since the pioneering work of Mura [16] have been applied to a variety of elasticity problems involving defects and solid solutions. The straight dislocation line was modeled by a half-plane of dislocation defects, $\tau^{D}(\mathbf{x})$, each having an effective force couple as shown schematically in Fig. 2. The forces are computed from the stress free strains which are chosen such that the relative displacements across the slip plane were equal to the Burgers vector of the dislocation. For convenience, the system modeled was Fe-29 at. $\%$ Ni as this system had been studied earlier in considering the problem of streaming [17,18]. The elastic constants and Bain strains are given in Table 1 of reference [17]. The edge of the half-plane was oriented so that the dislocation would be in the pure screw orientation, lying along the $[\overline{1} 10]$ direction. The screw dislocation was used because the interaction term, $F^{B D}$ is larger than for the edge dislocation.

The embryo was chosen to be an oblate ellipsoid with the orientation of the minor axis given by the angles $\theta$ and $\phi$, as shown in Fig. 3. This ellipsoid is filled with transformation defects, $\tau^{B}(\mathbf{x})$, having stress free strains equal to the average transformation strains given by equation (7). Their force fields, shown schematically in Fig. 2b, are determined from the defect stress free strain which is equal to the transformation Bain strain.

The self elastic energy of the constrained embryo can be written in the form

$$
F^{B B}=\frac{N}{2} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} E^{B B}(\mathbf{k}) T^{B}(\mathbf{k}) T^{B}(-\mathbf{k}), \tag{8}
$$

in which $N$ is the number of atoms in the crystal, $E^{B B}(\mathbf{k})$ is the Fourier transform over (d) of the elastic interaction energy between transformation defects at $(\mathbf{x})$ and $(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d})$ and $T^{B}(\mathbf{k})$ is the defect amplitude given by:

$$
T^{B}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{N} \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \tau^{B}(\mathbf{x}) \exp (-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}). \tag{9}
$$

Following Brailsford [19], a spherical Debye cut-off has been used to provide bounds for the summations over $\mathbf{k}$ space.

### Self Energy
On carrying out the Fourier transform of an ellipsoid within which $\tau^{B}(\mathbf{x})=1$, substituting the result into equation (8) and summing over $\mathbf{k}$-space, we find the self elastic energy can be written in the form

$$
F_{E}^{B B}=E_{0}^{B B} n^{\alpha}, \tag{10}
$$

in which the exponent $\alpha$ is approximately unity, as expected, making the self energy proportional to the embryo volume. The energy coefficient $E_{0}^{B B}$ is a function of the axial ratio $t=a / b$ in which $a$ is the major axis and $b$ is the minor axis, the volume fraction of major variant $f$ and the orientation of the embryo given by $\theta$ and $\phi$.

### Interaction energy
Although the elastic self energy of the plate is zero at infinite axial ratio, surface energy constrains the embryo to a finite axial ratio during nucleation and the resulting sizeable elastic energy prevents homogeneous nucleation from occurring. It is necessary, therefore, that the embryo interact with an existing defect to reduce the elastic energy for the transformation to take place. The general form of the elastic interaction between the embryo and a dislocation described by the defects, $\tau^{D}(\mathbf{x})$ is given by:
$$
F^{D B}=\frac{N}{2} \sum_{\mathbf{k}} E^{D B}(\mathbf{k}) T^{D}(\mathbf{k}) T^{B}(-\mathbf{k}),\qquad(11)
$$
in which $E^{D B}(\mathbf{k})$ is the Fourier transform over $(\mathbf{d})$ of the interaction energy between a dislocation defect at $(\mathbf{x})$ and a transformation defect at $(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d})$ and $T^{D}(\mathbf{k})$ is the dislocation defect amplitude given by:
$$
T^{D}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{N} \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \tau^{D}(\mathbf{x}) \exp (-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}).\qquad(12)
$$

In calculating $T^{D}(\mathbf{k})$ and $T^{B}(\mathbf{k})$, it is necessary to specify the orientation of the ellipsoid described by $\theta$ and $\phi$ in Fig. 3, with respect to the dislocation line. This relationship is shown in Fig. 6, where the major axis of the ellipsoid is taken to be in the [001] direction, i.e. for $\theta=90$ in Fig. 3. With the major axis in this direction, the net elastic energy of the plate, which includes the positive contribution from the self energy, and a negative contribution from the interaction energy, is a minimum. The habit plane is then determined by minimizing the elastic energy with respect to the rotational angle $\phi$.

Christian [21] noted some time ago that the interaction would act in this manner as the dislocation stress field falls off as $(1 / r)$ and, when multiplied by an embryo of volume $r^{3}$, a negative interaction proportional to $r^{2}$ results.

## HETEROGENEOUS NUCLEATION

On adding the chemical free energy in equation (2) to the self elastic energy in equation (10) and the interaction energy in equation (13), we find that the total free energy can be written in the form
$$F_{T}=A n+B n^{2 / 3} \quad(14)$$
in which
$$A=g+E_{0}^{B B}(t) \quad(15)$$
and
$$B=s(t) \bar{a}^{2}\left(\gamma+N_{B} \gamma_{I}(t)\right). \quad(16)$$

The geometrical parameter $s(t)$ is equal to the surface area of the ellipsoid, $S$, divided by the square of the lattice parameter $(\bar{a})$, and the number of atoms in the ellipsoid to the (2/3) power. It can be expressed as:
$$s(t)=2 \pi\left(\frac{3 t}{4 \pi}\right)^{2 / 3}\left[1+\frac{\ln \left(t \sqrt{t^{2}-1}\right)}{t \sqrt{t^{2}-1}}\right]. \quad(17)$$

The number of dislocations in the pile-up is approximated by $N_{B}$ and
$$\gamma_{I}(t)=f E_{0}^{B D}(t) / s(t) \bar{a}^{2}, \quad(18)$$
represents the effective negative surface energy for the interaction with a single dislocation, $N_{B}=1$. A computation for $\gamma_{I}$ for the Fe-29 Ni system is shown in Fig. 8.† The free energy barrier for nucleation is given by:
$$\Delta F^{*}=F\left(n^{*}, t^{*}\right)-F_{m} \quad(19)$$
in which $F_{m}$ is the metastable minimum value of $F$ as a function of $n$ and $t$. The minimum in $F$ is not necessarily zero because the coefficient $B$ in equation (14) can be negative for certain values of axial ratio $t$ which allows $\gamma+N_{B} \gamma_{I}(t)<0$. The parameters $n^{*}$ and $t^{*}$ are the number of atoms and the axial ratio, respectively, of the critical nucleus.

The surface energy parameter which influences $\Delta F^{*}$ is the effective interfacial free energy
$$\gamma_{e}\left(t^{*}\right) \equiv \gamma+{ }_{B} \gamma_{I}\left(t^{*}\right). \quad(20)$$

For embryos that are internally slipped or twinned, we expect the interface to be semi-coherent with $\gamma$ relatively large compared to $\gamma_{e}(t^{*})$. For this case, the number of dislocations required to nucleate martensite can be estimated from $\gamma_{I}(t^{*})$, i.e., calculated from the interaction of the embryo with a single dislocation, provided that $t^{*}$, the critical axial ratio for nucleation is known:
$$N_{B}=\frac{\gamma_{e}\left(t^{*}\right)-\gamma}{\gamma_{I}\left(t^{*}\right)} \quad(21)$$

$$\approx \frac{-\gamma}{\gamma_{I}\left(t^{*}\right)}. \quad(22)$$

The relations which are most useful in this respect are equations (14), (15) and (16). There are a number of steels, nevertheless, where it is believed martensite forms only in an athermal manner. Magee [2], however, has made the important observation that steels in which martensite is athermally nucleated have two things in common: 1. The presence of interstitials, such as carbon or nitrogen and 2. $M_{s}$ temperatures above room temperature. Carbon diffusion apparently prevents thermally activated martensite nucleation, either by changing the driving force or by pinning the embryo, resulting in a lower bainite transformation product instead of isothermal martensite as predicted by our model.

In regard to the questions raised at the outset, our model, which does not treat the influence of mobile interstitials, says the following:

A pile-up of dislocations can initiate heterogeneous nucleation of martensite.

The classical free energy barrier to nucleation can, nevertheless, exist for a martensite embryo at a pile-up.

With sufficient supercooling, martensite can also nucleate in the absence of a barrier at a pile-up in systems with favorable thermodynamic parameters. This feature of the model is interpreted as athermal martensite.

All systems that have athermal martensitic transfor- mations should have an isothermal martensitic trans- formation at some higher temperature.
