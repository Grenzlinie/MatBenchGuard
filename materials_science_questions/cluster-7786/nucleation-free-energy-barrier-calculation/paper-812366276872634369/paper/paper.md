# Binary homogeneous nucleation as a mechanism for the formation of aerosols*

Philippe Mirabel† and Joseph L. Katz

Department of Chemical Engineering and Institute of Colloid and Surface Science, Clarkson College of Technology, Potsdam, N. Y. 13676
(Received 27 September 1973)

The rates of nucleation of liquid aerosols from the gaseous mixtures $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{HNO}_{3}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ at $25^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for various relative humidities (10% to 100%) and various activities of acid vapor are calculated using the Flood-Neumann-Döring-Reiss-Doyle theory of binary homogeneous necleation. The activities of acid vapor needed for nucleation are 25 to 300 times smaller for $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ than for $\mathrm{HNO}_{3}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$. This is due to the much larger free energy of mixing in the liquid phase for $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$. Conversion from activities to actual pressures leads to condentrations of nitric acid which are much too high to be found under normal atmospheric conditions. On the other hand, the concentrations of sulfuric acid vapor needed to nucleate droplets in the $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ system are in the range $4(10^{-5})$ to $1.3(10^{-2})$ ppm, a concentration which can result from photo-oxidation of $\mathrm{SO}_{2}$ in the atmosphere. Calculations are made of the growth curves for $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ droplets (radius versus composition) at various relative humidities from the critical size radius up to a $1000 \mathring{A}$ radius, corresponding to nuclei large enough to serve as condensation centers for heterogeneous nucleation.
A generalized version of the Kelvin equation which includes the composition dependence of the surface tension for a binary mixture is derived and applied in calculating these growth curves. Sign errors in the second derivatives of the free energy in Doyle's paper are corrected. The limitations of the binary homogeneous nucleation theory at extremely low concentrations of one of the components are discussed and it is shown that this theory becomes inapplicable if the actual vapor pressure of one component is below $10^{-6}$ torr.

Condensation of water vapor leading to cloud formation in the atmosphere occurs when the supersaturation of water is at most 1% to 2%, supersaturations which are far smaller than those required for droplet formation by homogeneous nucleation (about 300%). In the atmo- sphere, large supersaturations are prevented by the presence of abundant condensation nuclei. The major sources of such nuclei are insoluble particles coming from smokes and dust, soluble particles such as sodium chloride coming from oceans or ammonium sulfate formed by chemical reaction in the atmosphere, and preexisting suspended liquid particles (liquid aerosols). Sulfuric acid + water liquid aerosols are of special importance since they are known to be one of the major constituents of photochemical smogs.

The purpose of this paper is to examine how certain kinds of these liquid aerosols can be generated in the at- mosphere by the binary homogeneous nucleation of water vapor and a polluting reactant such as sulfuric acid (or nitric acid). Binary homogeneous nucleation is such an efficient nucleation mechanism that nucleation can occur even when both of the components are greatly undersat- urated. $^{1}$ As will be shown later, large rates of nuclea tion are predicted even at relative humidities as low as 10% in the presence of $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$. This is quite unlike nor- mal heterogeneous nucleation where nucleation occurs only if the relative humidity is greater than 100% (or al- most 100% in the case of soluble nuclei).

Once nucleated, the particles grow to larger size by adding water and acid molecules as long as there is a high enough concentration of pollutant. If there is a continuous generation of pollutant, they will eventually grow large enough to become visible. Even when such is not the case, they can serve as condensation nuclei on moving into a region of higher relative humidity.

We speculate that the generation rates of pollutants are affected by the presence of such aerosols. For ex- ample, if some of the interesting chemical reactions, such as, reactions of hydrocarbons, transformation of $\mathrm{SO}_{2}$ into $\mathrm{SO}_{3}$, oxidation by ozone, etc., proceed much faster in liquid acid solution then the existence of a sub- microscopic aerosol composed of an enormous number of concentrated sulfuric acid + water droplets would pro- vide the necessary reaction sites. Thus, instead of re- stricting oneself to homogeneous gas phase reactions in examining the various scenarios for the fate of atmo- spheric pollutants, one would also need to include acid catalyzed liquid phase reactions if their reaction rates are sufficiently high.

## THEORY OF BINARY NUCLEATION

The rate (i. e. number of nuclei formed in one cubic centimeter in one second) of homogeneous nucleation of a binary (two components, e. g., $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$) system is predicted by the equation

$$I=C \exp \left(-\Delta G^{*} / k T\right), \tag{1}$$

where $C$ is a frequency factor, $k$ is Boltzmann's con- stant, $T$ is the temperature and $\Delta G^{*}$ is the free energy required to form an embryo (droplet of liquid) having a size and composition such that it is in (unstable) equilib- rium with the surrounding gaseous phase. This equa- tion, except for the value of $C$ was derived by Flood² in 1934. In 1950 Reiss³ showed how to fully generalize sin- gle component nucleation theory and was thus able to provide a unique prescription for obtaining the frequency factor $C$. In 1961 Doyle⁴ applied Reiss' theory to sulfu- ric acid but unfortunately made an error in calculating the second derivative of $\Delta G$.

The free energy of formation of an embryo of arbi-

trary size and composition is
$$\Delta G=n_{1}\left(\mu_{1 l}-\mu_{1 g}\right)+n_{2}\left(\mu_{2 l}-\mu_{2 g}\right)+4 \pi r^{2} \gamma,\qquad(2)$$
where $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ are the number of moles of components1 and 2 in the embryo, $\mu_{1 l}$ and $\mu_{2 l}$ are the chemical potentials of components 1 and 2 taken for a macroscopicamount of a liquid phase of the same composition, $\mu_{1 g}$  and $\mu_{2 g}$ are the chemical potentials of components 1 and2 in the gas phase, r is the radius of the embryo and $\gamma$  is the surface tension of the binary mixture. The maximumof $\Delta G$ (i.e., $\Delta G^{*}$ ) for a fixed number of molecules of 1 and 2 in the gas phase (i.e., for fixed vapor pressures of constituents) can be found by solving the two equations
$$\left(\frac{\partial \Delta G}{\partial n_{1}}\right)_{n_{2}}=0, \quad\left(\frac{\partial \Delta G}{\partial n_{2}}\right)_{n_{1}}=0.\qquad(3)$$

As shown by Reiss $^{3}$ for ideal mixtures and by Doyle $^{4}$  for $H_{2} SO_{4}+H_{2} O$ at $50 \%$ relative humidity, these two equations lead to the existence of a saddle point in the three dimensional system, free energy surface versus $n_{1}$ and n2, the saddle point being the barrier that the embryos(droplets) have to overcome in order to grow and become stable. Solving Eqs. (3) one obtains
$$\left(\frac{\partial \Delta G}{\partial n_{1}}\right)_{n_{2}}=\mu_{1 l}-\mu_{1 g}+\frac{2 \gamma \bar{V}_{1}}{r^{*}}-\frac{3 X^{*} V}{r^{*}} \left.\frac{d \gamma}{d X}\right|_{X=X^{*}}=0,\qquad(4a)$$

$$\left(\frac{\partial \Delta G}{\partial n_{2}}\right)_{n_{1}}=\mu_{2 l}-\mu_{2 g}+\frac{2 \gamma \bar{V}_{2}}{r^{*}}+\frac{3 V\left(1-X^{*}\right)}{r^{*}} \left.\frac{d \gamma}{d X}\right|_{X=X^{*}}=0, \quad(4 b)$$

$$X=\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}},\qquad(4c)$$
where $\bar{V}_{1}$ and $\bar{V}_{2}$ are the partial molar volumes andV is the molar volume of solution, $V=(1-x) \bar{V}_{1}$  $+x \bar{V}_{2} .$ 

Equations (4a) and (4b) allow one to calculate the val- ues of $X^{*}$ and $r^{*}$ (i.e., the composition and radius of the droplets) at the saddle point. $\Delta G^{*}$ is then obtained by solving Equation (4c) and the following equation,
$$\frac{4 \pi r^{* 3}}{3}=n_{1}^{*} \bar{V}_{1}+n_{2}^{*} \bar{V}_{2}=\left(n_{1}^{*}+n_{2}^{*}\right) V,\qquad(5)$$
for $n_{1}^{*}$ and $n_{2}^{*}$ , and substituting these (and $r^{*}$ ) into Equation (2).

Reiss $^{2}$ derived the frequency factor C, by assuming, as in single component nucleation theory, that each embryo is able to grow or shrink by acquiring or losing single molecules and obtained the following expression for C, which we have rearranged to explicitly show the symmetry in interchanging substances 1 and 2,
$$C=\frac{\beta_{1} \beta_{2}}{\beta_{1} \sin ^{2} \phi+\beta_{2} \cos ^{2} \phi}\left[N_{1}+N_{2}\right] S\left(-\frac{\mathrm{P}}{\mathrm{Q}}\right)^{1 / 2}\qquad(6)$$
where $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ are the impingement rates (i.e. number of molecules that arrive at the embryo per unit area per unit time) of species 1 and 2, i.e.
$$\beta_{i}=\frac{P_{i}}{\left(2 \pi m_{i} k T\right)^{1 / 2}},\qquad(7)$$
 $N_{1}$ and $N_{2}$ are the number densities of species 1 and 2, S is the surface area of the embryo, $\phi$ is the angle between the original axis $n_{1}$ and the direction of the pass through the saddle point $^{3}$ and P and Q are second derivatives of $\Delta G^{*}$ with respect to these new rotated axis $n_{1}'$  and $n_{2}'$ . In terms of the unrotated second derivatives and the angle of rotation $\phi, P$ and Q are given by the equations
$$\mathrm{P}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1}^{2}}\right) \cos ^{2} \phi+2\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1} \partial n_{2}}\right) \cos \phi \sin \phi+\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{2}^{2}}\right) \sin ^{2} \phi \quad(8 a)$$

$$\mathrm{Q}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1}^{2}}\right) \sin ^{2} \phi-2\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1} \partial n_{2}}\right) \sin \phi \cos \phi+\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{2}^{2}}\right) \cos ^{2} \phi \quad(8 b)$$

The angle of rotation $\phi$ is given by the equation $^{5}$ 
$$\tan 2 \phi=2\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1} \partial n_{2}}\right) /\left[\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1}^{2}}\right)-\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{2}^{2}}\right)\right].\qquad(8c)$$

The unrotated second derivatives of $\Delta G$ are given by thefollowing equations:
$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{4 \pi r^{3}}{3}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1}^{2}}\right)_{n_{2}}= & \frac{V X^{2}}{1-X}\left(\frac{d \mu_{2 l}}{d X}\right)+\left(\mu_{1 l}-\mu_{1 g}\right) \frac{\bar{V}_{1}}{3} \\
& +\frac{V X}{r}\left\{\frac{2 \gamma X}{1-X}\left(\frac{d \bar{V}_{2}}{d X}\right)+\left[6 X\left(\bar{V}_{2}-\bar{V}_{1}\right)\right.\right. \\
& \left.\left.+\bar{V}_{1}\right] \frac{d \gamma}{d X}+3 X V \frac{d^{2} \gamma}{d X^{2}}\right\}.
\end{aligned}\qquad(9a)$$

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{4 \pi r^{3}}{3}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{1} \partial n_{2}}\right)= & -V X\left(\frac{d \mu_{2 l}}{d X}\right)+\left(\mu_{2 l}-\mu_{2 g}\right) \frac{\bar{V}_{1}}{3}-\frac{V X}{r} \\
& \times\left\{2 \gamma\left(\frac{d \bar{V}_{2}}{d X}\right)+\left[6(1-X)\left(\bar{V}_{2}-\bar{V}_{1}\right)-\bar{V}_{2}\right]\right. \\
& \left.\times \frac{d \gamma}{d X}+3(1-X) V \frac{d^{2} \gamma}{d X^{2}}\right\}
\end{aligned}\qquad(9b)$$

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{4 \pi r^{3}}{3}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \Delta G}{\partial n_{2}^{2}}\right)_{n_{1}}= & V(1-X)\left(\frac{d \mu_{2 l}}{d X}\right)+\left(\mu_{2 l}-\mu_{2 g}\right) \frac{\bar{V}_{2}}{3} \\
& +\frac{V(1-X)}{r}\left\{2 \gamma\left(\frac{d V_{2}}{d X}\right)+[6(1-X)\right. \\
& \left.\times\left(\bar{V}_{2}-\bar{V}_{1}\right)-\bar{V}_{2}\right] \frac{d \gamma}{d X}+3(1-X) V \frac{d^{2} \gamma}{d X^{2}}. \\
& \text { (9c) }
\end{aligned}$$

These equations for the second derivatives of $\Delta G$ with respect to the $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ axes differ somewhat from those given by Doyle. $^{4}$ They correct two sign mistakes in his equation (8) [our Equation (9a)] and a printer's error in his equation (10) [our Equation (9c)].

## RESULTS
Calculation of the rate of nucleation at $25^{\circ} C$ in $H_{2} SO_{4}$  $+H_{2} O$ and $HNO_{3}+H_{2} O$ systems have been made at relative humidities of water of $10 \%, 25 \%, 50 \%, 75 \%$ and $100 \%$ and at activities of acids varying from $10^{0}$ to $10^{-5}$ . Since we are primarily interested in systems at atmospheric pressures, we shall approximate the activities (or relative humidities for water) by their ideal gas value, i.e., the ratio of the actual partial pressure to the equilibrium vapor pressure; $a_{i}=P_{i} / P_{i}^{\circ}$ .

The location of the saddle point was obtained by iteratively numerically solving Equations (4a) and (4b). In all cases, we found that the condition for the existence of a saddle point was satisfied, i.e., $(\partial^{2} \Delta G / \partial n_{1} \partial n_{2})^{2}$ $- (\partial^{2} \Delta G / \partial n_{1}^{2})(\partial^{2} \Delta G / \partial n_{2}^{2})>0$. The results of our calculations for the rates of nucleation at various activities and relative humidities are given in Figs. 1 and 2. Fig. 3 shows the activities needed to achieve a rate of nucleation of 1 droplet per $\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ per sec. Comparison with Doyle's results for $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ at $50 \%$ relative humidity shows that our rates of nucleation are higher by a factor of $10^{2}$ to $10^{3}$. This difference is almost entirely due to Doyle's error in his equations for the second derivatives and to his use of a too small value for the vapor pressure of sulfuric acid, both of these errors affecting only the preexponential factor $C$ in Equation (1). Our exponential factor $\exp(-\Delta G / kT)$ differs only negligibly from Doyle's, the differences being entirely attributable to the accuracy of the fit to the experimental data.

For the rest of the curve ($X = 0$ to $X = 0.30$) we used the values of the surface tension of pure water at $25\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ (71.97 dyne/cm) and the increment of the surface tension of the mixture over that of pure water up to $X = 0.30$ given by Whatmough. $^{12}$ These data were then fitted with an accuracy of $0.1\%$ by a sixth degree polynomial.

The uncertainties in the literature data (not to be confused with the accuracy with which our polynomials were fit to the literature data) is an important source of error since the rate of nucleation has a very strong dependence upon most of these variables.

Partial molar free energies are generally determined with a claimed accuracy of $1\%$. A $1\%$ change in partial molar free energy would change the rate of nucleation by a factor of 2 to 3 for $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ and by a factor of 2 to 6 for $\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ depending on the relative humidity. Unfortunately the different compilations for a given mixture are not always in agreement to within $1\%$. For example the data given by Davis *et al.* $^{13}$ for $\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ differ from those in the compilation by Redlich *et al.* by as much as $8\%$. Their use changes the rate of nucleation by as much as a factor of $10^4$ at $10\%$ relative humidity.

Surface tensions are usually known with an accuracy better than $1\%$, which affects the rate of nucleation by a factor of at most 6. For $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, use of the older values given by the International Critical Tables$^{14}$ which differ by less than $0.7\%$ from those of Sabinina and Terpugow changes the rate of nucleation by a factor less than 3 and is therefore not a major source of uncertainty. We have no way of knowing the accuracy of the $\text{HNO}_3$ $+ \text{H}_2\text{O}$ surface tension data other than to note that the two sets of data go smoothly into each other, so we presume there is no major uncertainty due to it.

Molar volumes of solution and partial molar volumes are not critical since they are known with a high accuracy (better than $0.05\%$). Such a small uncertainty has no effect on the rate. However, care must be taken not to compute the molar volume of a solution by a linear relation between the volume of the pure components, i.e., zero excess volume of solution, (as has been done$^{15}$), but directly from the density. Doing so leads to values which can be $10\%$ too high and therefore decrease the rate of nucleation by a factor of $10^4$.

Vapor pressures over pure sulfuric acid have not been determined directly, but calculations using Van't Hoff's equation have been made by Abel. $^{16}$ He obtained a vapor pressure of $5(10^{-4})$ torr. More recently, Vermeulen and Gmitro$^{17}$ repeated these calculations using better data and found $3.6(10^{-4})$ torr. These two values, which are very close to each other, are *much* larger than the value used by Doyle, $10^{-6}$ torr, in his calculation. Direct determination of the vapor pressure of sulfuric acid would be helpful, as the rate of nucleation is directly proportional to it. No such uncertainty exists for vapor pressure of pure $\text{HNO}_3$ which is 62.2 torr at $25\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. $^{18}$

Table I gives, for a rate $J = 1$, the values of several quantities of interest such as the composition $X^*$, the radius $r^*$, the number of molecules in the droplet $n^*$, and the value for the free energy of formation $\Delta G^*$, all these quantities being determined at the saddle point. As can be seen from this table, the critical sized nuclei have roughly a radius of $7\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ and contain 42 molecules for $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ system, and have a radius of 10 to $11\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ and contain 105 to 120 molecules for $\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ mixtures.

One may question the applicability of macroscopic surface tension to such a small system (this is known as the capillarity assumption in the nucleation literature), but predictions made using it are in excellent agreement with experiments on nonane$^{19}$ for clusters as small as 26 molecules and having a radius of $12\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, and with recent experiments on $n$-butylbenzene$^{20}$ for clusters as small as 17 molecules and having a radius of $9\ \mathring{\text{A}}$.

## DISCUSSION

The activities needed for the nucleation of $\text{H}_2\text{SO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ are 25 to 300 times smaller than those needed for $\text{HNO}_3$ $+ \text{H}_2\text{O}$. This is due to the much larger heat of solution, and therefore much larger free energy of the liquid phase, for $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ than for $\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. However, because of the very large differences in the values of the vapor pressure over pure sulfuric acid and nitric acid, the nucleating effect of sulfuric acid relative to that of nitric acid appears even larger when expressed as concentrations or partial pressures. Sulfuric acid will nucleate water at 6 to 8 orders of magnitude lower concentration than will nitric acid at the same relative humidity. Binary homogeneous nucleation will occur with nitric acid only if the partial pressure of $\text{HNO}_3$ is above 2 torr. Such a value is of course much too high to be found under normal atmospheric conditions. Thus the direct combination of nitric acid and water vapor to form liquid aerosols will not occur in the atmosphere. A similar result will be found with other compounds having a high vapor pressure at normal temperature such as $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{NO}$, $\text{NO}_2\ldots$. For aerosols containing nitrogen compounds, another kind of reaction must exist for their formation such as chemical reactions in the liquid phase of preexisting droplets, or binary nucleation between water and a compound of low vapor pressure as proposed by Kiang *et al.* $^{21}$ for aerosols involving $\text{NH}_3$.

On the other hand, the partial pressures of sulfuric acid required to nucleate droplets in the $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ system are in the range of $3(10^{-8})$ to $10^{-5}$ torr, corresponding roughly to concentrations of $4(10^{-5})$ to $1.3(10^{-2})$ ppm for one atmosphere total pressure. Such concentrations of sulfuric acid probably occur frequently near heavily populated areas due to the transformation of $\text{SO}_2$ produced mostly by combustion of fuel, to $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ by photochemical reactions. As shown by Cox and Penkeet, $^{22}$ the photooxidation of atmospheric $\text{SO}_2$ will occur under favorable conditions (presence of ozone, hydrocarbons and water vapor) leading to the formation of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$. At such a rate, a concentration large enough to cause binary nucleation in presence of water can be rapidly built up. To understand what are the influences of the free energy of mixing and of the magnitude of the vapor pressure we have calculated (see Fig. 4) the rate of nucleation of a mixture composed of water at $100\%$ relative humidity and a hypothetical compound (referred to later as component 2) for which all of its values of mixing (i.e., $\gamma, V, \overline{V}_i$) are those of the nitric acid + water system except for its vapor pressure $P$ and its free energy of mixing. The vapor pressure $P$ is varied from that of pure nitric acid, $P_0$, to one $10^{-25}$ times smaller [i.e., from $6.22(10^{1})$ to $6.22(10^{-24})$ torr]. The free energy of mixing is varied from that of an ideal solution (i.e. $\Delta G_{1\ \text{deal}} = RT [X \ln X + (1 - X) \ln(1 - X)]$) to one 3 and to one 6 times that of an ideal solution. These $\Delta G$'s have been chosen because, at $X = 0.5$, they correspond approximately ($-1232$ cal $\text{mole}^{-1}$ and $-2464$ cal $\text{mole}^{-1}$) to the free energy of mixing of $\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($-1290$ cal $\text{mole}^{-1}$ and $-2780$ cal $\text{mole}^{-1}$).

![](./images/812366276872634369_6.jpg)

FIG. 4. Activity of acid in the vapor phase needed to achieve a rate of nucleation $J$=1 nuclei $\text{cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ as a function of the equilibrium vapor pressure of the pure acid, $P$. $\Delta G_1$, $\Delta G_3$ and $\Delta G_6$ refer to the value of the free energy of mixing assumed for the corresponding theoretical mixture, i.e. 1, 3 and 6 times the free energy of mixing of an ideal mixture and $P_0$ refers to the equilibrium pressure of $\text{HNO}_3$.

With an ideal free energy of mixing, $\Delta G_1$, (systems such as $\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CH}_3\text{OH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ have approximately such a free energy of mixing) the activity needed to nucleate saturated water vapor (i.e., $100\%$ relative humidity) is always greater than unity. However for larger free energies of mixing we see that it is possible to nucleate saturated water vapor with activities of the second component much smaller than unity even if the vapor pressure of that second component is absurdly small. We see that binary homogeneous nucleation is predicted to occur even for partial pressure of the second component as low as $10^{-24}$ torr at $100\%$ relative humidity. Even at $50\%$ relative humidity, partial pressure as low as $10^{-20}$ torr are predicted to cause homogeneous binary nucleation. If this were really the case, it would be extremely difficult to get homogeneous nucleation of pure water; any kind of impurity should therefore be able to induce binary nucleation without requiring a supersaturation for water greater than 1.

Obviously nucleation theory breaks down at these extreme conditions. One must remember that the theory is based on the existence of a steady rate of flow of embryos through the saddle point. Nucleation occurs by impingement of molecules of both species on nuclei of critical size. The number of molecules of each specie striking a critical nucleus is $4\pi r^{*2}\beta_i$ per second. Assuming a minimum rate of nucleation of one droplet $\text{cm}^{-3}\ \text{sec}^{-1}$, $4\pi r^{*2}\beta_i$ must then have a minimum value of unity.

Such a condition is always satisfied for pure water (or pure nitric acid), but this condition limits the actual pressure of the second component to a minimum value of $10^{-6}$ torr. In Table I we see that for relative humidities higher than $25 \%$ the concentrations of $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ needed for an appreciable rate of nucleation in the steady state are smaller than $10^{-6}$ torr. What this means is that, since one can never obtain a rate of nucleation of 1 nuclei $\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ $\sec ^{-1}$ at smaller concentrations, the predicted $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ concentrations in Table I should be raised to $10^{-6}$ torr if such a rate is actually desired.

While changing the equilibrium vapor pressure does not affect $\Delta G^{*}$ (which depends on the activities of the gases) it does affect the frequency factor. Assuming that the two vapors behave as perfect gases, the number density $N_{i}$ can then be expressed in terms of the partial pressure $P_{1}$, by $N_{1}=P_{1} / k T$. If one of the components is present in low concentration (becuase its vapor pressure is small), the frequency factor $C$ then simplifies to

$$
C=\frac{P_{1} \beta_{2}}{k T \sin ^{2} \phi} S^{*}\left(-\frac{\mathrm{P}^{*}}{\mathrm{Q}^{*}}\right)^{1 / 2}. \tag{10}
$$

As the variable $S^{*}, \sin \phi, \mathrm{P}^{*}$ and $\mathrm{Q}^{*}$ have only a slight effect on the factor $C$, the rate of nucleation for a given relative humidity will primarily depend upon the variables $\beta_{2}$ and $\Delta G^{*}$. Therefore, as suggested by Equation (10), the growth rate of nuclei at the saddle point is determined by the smaller $\beta_{i}$, i.e., by the specie of lower concentrations.

It is interesting to note that while the equations for binary nucleation, Eqs. (1-9), appear very complicated certain simple expressions which provide insight into the process are obtainable. One can calculate $\Delta G^{*}$ in terms of the critical radius $r^{*}$, or the critical composition $X^{*}$ and obtain equations which are identical to, or are the obvious generalization of, the single component nucleation equation, i.e.,

$$
\Delta G^{*}=\frac{4}{3} \pi \gamma r^{* 2} \tag{11a}
$$

and

$$
\Delta G^{*}=\frac{16}{3} \pi \gamma^{3} V^{2} /\left[R T \ln S^{*}\right]^{2}, \tag{11b}
$$

where

$$
S^{*} \equiv S_{1}^{\left(1-X^{*}\right)} S_{2}^{X^{*}}, \tag{11c}
$$

where $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ are the supersaturation of component 1 and 2 in the vapor phase (not to be confused with the activity of the vapor phase), i.e., the ratio between the actual partial pressure, $P_{i}$, of the water or pollutant, and the equilibrium partial pressure, $P_{i}^{\text {sol }}$, over a large solution of composition $X^{*}$. These equations were first derived by Flood. $^{2}$ (In his derivation, he did not take into account the terms involving $d \gamma / d X$ in our Equations (4a) and (4b), but these terms cancel in the equations for $\Delta G^{*}$. They must however be included for the accurate determination of $X^{*}$ and $r^{*}$ and therefore $\Delta G^{*}$.) We also note that $\Delta G^{*}$ depends only upon the values of the supersaturation but not upon the order of magnitude of the equilibrium vapor pressure of pure components. It is also interesting to note that Equations (4a) and (4b) are the generalization of the Kelvin equation for the critical size droplets to include the effect of the composition dependence of the surface tension. Since

$$
\mu_{i l}-\mu_{i g}=-R T \ln \frac{P_{i}}{P_{i}^{\text {sol }}}, \tag{12a}
$$

these equations can be written

$$
R T \ln \frac{P_{1}}{P_{2}^{\text {sol }}}=\frac{2 \gamma \bar{V}_{1}}{r^{*}}-\left.\frac{3 X^{*} V}{r^{*}} \frac{d \gamma}{d X}\right|_{X=X^{*}} \tag{12b}
$$

$$
R T \ln \frac{P_{2}}{P_{2}^{\text {sol }}}=\frac{2 \gamma \bar{V}_{2}}{r^{*}}+\left.\frac{3\left(1-X^{*}\right)}{r^{*}} V \frac{d \gamma}{d X}\right|_{X=X^{*}}. \tag{12c}
$$

This generalization of the Kelvin equations can be derived directly from Laplace's equation by realizing that if the composition is size dependent and the surface tension depends on composition, then the surface tension is size dependent. Laplace's equation is then

$$
\Delta \mathrm{P}=\frac{2 \gamma}{r}+\frac{d \gamma}{d r}. \tag{13}
$$

Using the equation for the pressure dependence of the chemical potential $\left(\partial \mu_{i} / \partial P\right)_{T}=\bar{V}_{i}$ and neglecting the compressibility of the liquids, one obtains

$$
R T \ln \frac{P_{i}}{P_{i}^{\text {sol }}}=\bar{V}_{i}\left[\frac{2 \gamma}{r}+\frac{d \gamma}{d r}\right]. \tag{14}
$$

From equation (5), we can derive the following equations

$$
\left(\frac{d r}{d X}\right)_{n_{1}}=\frac{r \bar{V}_{2}}{3 V(1-X)} \tag{15a}
$$

![](./images/812366276872634369_7.jpg)

FIG. 5. Growth of the droplet from the saddle point to a radius of $1000 \AA$. The curves give the relation between the composition of a droplet (mole fraction) and its size at various relative humidities for $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ at $25^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$.

$$
\left(\frac{d r}{d X}\right)_{n_{2}}=-\frac{r \bar{V}_{1}}{3 V X}. \tag{15b}
$$

Combining equations (14), (15a) and (15b) one obtains equations (12b) and (12c).

Some insight into the growth of the droplets after they have just passed the saddle point can be obtained from nucleation ideas. Due to the very large value of the ratio $\beta_{1} / \beta_{2}$, about $10^{5}$ to $10^{9}$ water molecules will impinge upon a droplet in the period between the incorporation of two acid molecules, depending on the relative humidity. (We suppose that all the acid molecules which impinge upon a droplet will be incorporated.) For a critical cluster containing roughly 50 molecules, the incorporation of such a large number of water molecules is not possible and most of the water molecules must evaporate. Then, between the incorporation of two acid molecules, (i.e., for $n_{2}$ constant), an equilibrium relating the size and the composition of a droplet is established such that $(\partial \Delta G / \partial n_{1})_{n_{2}}=0$. This condition leads to equation (4a) which is now valid for all $r>r^{*}$. This equation and equation (4c) determine the growth of the droplets. Figure 5 shows the variation of composition of droplets at constant relative humidity from the saddle point radius to $1000 \mathring{A}$ radius, a size large enough to easily serve as condensation centers for heterogeneous nucleation. Equation (4c) also allows one to calculate the ratio $n_{1} / n_{2}$ representing the number of water molecules which can be incorporated into a droplet for every acid molecule. This number is respectively 2.3 and 3.0 for droplets of $10 \mathring{A}$ and $1000 \mathring{A}$ at $10\%$ relative humidity; 7.0 and 8.4 for droplets of the same size at $100\%$ relative humidity. All the other water molecules which strike the droplet $(10^{5}$ to $10^{9}/\text{sec})$ will have to evaporate. Thus, the rate of growth of droplets, as well as the rate of nucleation, is controlled by the specie of lower concentration, i.e., of lower vapor pressure.
