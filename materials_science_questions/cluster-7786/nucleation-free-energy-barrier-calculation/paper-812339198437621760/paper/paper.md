# Growth of Polar Crystal Surfaces on Ionized Organic Substrates

Dorothy M. Duffy and John H. Harding*

Department of Physics and Astronomy, University College London, Gower Street,
London, WC1E 6BT, United Kingdom

Received February 20, 2004. In Final Form: June 4, 2004

Calcite crystals nucleate on the (01.2) face on a diverse range of organic substrates, including self-assembled monolayers, hydrogen-bonded ribbons, and polymer rafts. The (01.2) face of calcite is a polar surface. Therefore macroscopic crystal growth can only occur if the dipole moment is quenched. We demonstrate that the dipole moment can be quenched for a given polar direction by adsorption onto an organic substrate with arbitrary charge density. The density of ions in the outer calcium plane must be modified, by introducing rows of vacancies, to fulfill the condition of zero net dipole moment. Interfacial energies are calculated for interfaces between the polar (01.2) and (00.1) faces of calcite and stearic acid monolayers with a range of densities. It was found that, contrary to the experimental evidence, the (00.1) face has lower interfacial energy than the (01.2) face with monolayers with equivalent densities. We give an explanation for this discrepancy based on kinetic models.

## Introduction

The experimental field of crystal growth control by organic substrates has grown considerably in recent years. $^{1}$ Most workers have studied calcium carbonate, because of its importance in biomineralization. Early experiments showed that Langmuir monolayers of long-chain carboxylic acids favored nucleation of calcite on the (10.0) face, $^{2}$ in contrast to the (10.4) faces that dominate the morphology of crystals grown from pure solution. The 3-index notation for the hexagonal lattice is used throughout this paper. More recently, a range of substrates, including self-assembled monolayers, $^{3-6}$ polymers, $^{7,8}$ and hydrogen-bonded ribbons, $^{9}$ have all been shown to modify the growth of mineral phases.

Some control over crystal nucleation is currently attainable. Perhaps the most striking example is the demonstration that the length of the hydrocarbon chain (whether it contains an odd or even number of carbon atoms) $^{10}$ can determine the nucleation behavior. It has long been known that changing the headgroups of the chain strongly affects the morphology. Sulfate and phosphate headgroups favor the (00.1) face. $^{11}$ All this suggests the importance of the local geometry in determining which face nucleates on the organic substrate, an idea known as templating. However, the nucleation of the (00.1) face raises another important issue: the electrostatics of polar interfaces, since a crystal oriented in this direction consists of a stack of equally spaced charged planes of alternating sign.

Surfaces of ionic (and partially ionic) crystals can be classified according to the electrostatic properties of the repeat unit perpendicular to the surface. $^{12}$ Surfaces that have a repeat unit with a net dipole moment are known as polar surfaces, and the dipole moment diverges with the thickness of the crystal slab. It follows that the surface energy is infinite. In order for such surfaces to be stable, it is necessary to quench the macroscopic dipole moment in some way. Kummer and Yao $^{13}$ demonstrated that the dipole moment of a stack of $n$ planes with charge density $\sigma$ and spacing $a$ would be canceled by a dipole layer with charge density $\sigma/2$ and spacing $na$. Formally, this layer can be produced by moving half a plane of charge from the top of the slab to the bottom. Harding $^{14}$ extended this procedure to the case of a general crystal structure. There are a number of methods of producing this dipole layer in practice. The simplest is to modify the density of ions in the outer layers of the crystal by introducing vacancies or by surface reconstruction. These modifications (and therefore the surfaces stabilized) are often of high energy. In the systems under discussion here, there are two alternative mechanisms that can stabilize polar directions. The first is to ionize the acid groups attached to the organic layer. This produces a partially charged layer attached to one end of the ionic crystal. The other is to adsorb charged ions onto the crystal surface from solution. We will discuss these possibilities later in the paper.

The polar (00.1) direction is the most commonly found orientation in biomineralized calcite. Addadi et al. $^{15}$ succeeded in growing (00.1) oriented calcite on substrates of sulfonated polystyrene and $\beta$-sheet polyaspartate, and (00.1) oriented calcite has been grown on self-assembled monolayers of alkanethiols. $^{3}$ Nevertheless, the (00.1)

In this paper, we address the question as to how the(01.2) and (00.1) surfaces can match a wide range of organic structures, given the constraints of charge neutrality and zero net dipole moment for the crystal as a whole and on the ability of the crystal surface and the organic monolayer to find such a match. The introduction of steps does not solve this problem unless an array of steps can be found that both overcomes the mismatch and tilts the crystal so that the vicinal surface produced does not have a macroscopic dipole moment. ### Methods and Procedures
In previous work, we used molecular modeling tech-niques to calculate the interfacial energies between calcite crystals and Langmuir monolayers of stearic acid. $^{17,18}$ Similar methods were used in these calculations and are summarized here. The calculations are carried out using the general-purpose molecular dynamics program DL_POLY. $^{19}$ In all simulations, an NVT ensemble was employed with a 1 fs time step. A united atom model is used for the tails of the organic molecules, $^{20}$ and the CHARMM22 $^{21}$ potentials are used for the headgroups. The TIP3P potential is used for water. $^{22}$ Calcite is modeled using the potentials of Pavese et al. $^{23}$ This potential gives good agreement with the experimental properties of calcium carbonate. $^{24}$ The lattice parameters of the mini mized rhombohedral structure are $a=b=4.79$ Å, $c=$ 17.48 Å, $\alpha=\beta=90^{\circ}$, $\gamma=120^{\circ}$. The small differences between the model and experimental parameters ($a=b$ $=4.99$ Å, $c=17.06$ Å, $\alpha=\beta=90^{\circ}$, $\gamma=120^{\circ}$) are not significant for our purposes. The interaction potentials between water and the crystal ions were derived by de Leeuw and Parker. $^{25}$ The potentials between the stearate and crystal ions were derived from the calcium carbonate potential and the CHARMM potentials using the scheme suggested by Schroder et al. $^{26}$ for zeolite interactions. The water/crystal potentials and the monolayer crystal po- tentials were tested against ab initio calculations $^{18}$ and found to give good agreement between the equilibrium separations and the interaction energies.

Most computer simulations simulate the dipole layer discussed above by terminating the crystal with half-layers of ions. More generally, the macroscopic dipole moment can be quenched by modifying the ionic density, and thus the charge density, on $m$ outer planes with the conditionthat $^{27}$

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{m} \sigma_{j}=(-1) \frac{\sigma_{m+1}}{2} \qquad(1)
$$

Here $\sigma_{j}$ is the charge density of the $j$th plane. When two outer planes are modified, the sum of the charge density of the outer two planes must equal half of the chargedensity of the third crystal plane $(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=-\sigma_{3} / 2)$. If $\sigma_{1}$  $=0.25 \sigma_{3}$, then $\sigma_{2}=-0.75 \sigma_{3}$ to cancel the dipole moment. This corresponds to a $75 \%$ reduction in the ionic density of the outer plane and a $25 \%$ reduction in the ionic density of the second plane. This procedure may be used to form interfaces between monolayers of ionized stearic acid (stearate) molecules and polar crystal directions of calcite with quenched dipole moments. The monolayers have a centered rectangular unit cell with sides $a$ and $b$, giving a charge density of $-2 e / a b$. The monolayer forms the first charged plane, and the outer Ca plane of crystal ions forms the second plane. For a given monolayer density, the charge density of the Ca layer is constrained by eq 1. The dipole moment of a crystal terminated by a full layer of $Ca^{2+}$ ions is canceled by a monolayer with density equal to the surface density of $Ca^{2+}$ ions and a half $CO_{3}^{2-}$ layer on the lower surface. This is illustrated in Figure 1a. For lower density monolayers, it is necessary to reduce the density of ions in the terminating Ca plane in order to quench the dipole. The condition of eq 1 is fulfilled if one Ca2+ ion is removed for every two "missing" monolayer molecules. The lower surface of the crystal is terminated by a $Ca^{2+}$ plane with the same density as that of the upper $Ca^{2+}$ plane and a $CO_{3}^{2-}$ plane with the same charge density as the monolayer. An example is given in Figure 1b. The missing $Ca^{2+}$ ions and monolayer molecules resemble the cores of misfit dislocations introduced to match the structural parameters and to quench the dipole moment.

The energies of four simulation blocks are required to obtain the interfacial energy: a pure crystal slab $(E_{c})$, a pure water slab $(E_{w})$, a monolayer on a half water slab $(E_{mw})$, and a monolayer on a half crystal slab $(E_{mc})$. The simulation cells have three-dimensional periodicity, but a vacuum slab is introduced in one direction. The half- crystal of the monolayer/crystal interface is terminated on the lower surface with modified crystal planes, as in Figure 1. The upper and lower surfaces of the pure crystal slab are modified accordingly. The charge on the monolayer on water is neutralized by a Ca layer with half the monolayer density. The interfacial energy $(\gamma_{mc})$ is calcu lated from

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{mc}}=\left(\left(E_{\mathrm{mc}}-(1 / 2) E_{\mathrm{c}}\right)-\left(E_{\mathrm{mw}}-(1 / 2) E_{\mathrm{w}}\right)\right) / A \quad(2)
$$

where $A$ is the surface area of the simulation cell.

## Results and Discussion

Given the composition of the solution and the relevant chemical constants, the degree of ionization of the organic monolayer can be calculated by solving the Poisson−Boltzmann equation self-consistently with the surface charge on the monolayer using the full nonlinear Grahame equation. This has been performed previously for mixed solutions of $\text{CaCl}_2$ and $\text{NaCl}^{28}$ We have adapted these methods for calcium carbonate solutions by including the relevant species for the bulk as obtained in geochemical calculations.²⁹ Calculations show that a bulk pH of 6.0 (typical of many experiments) corresponds to a local pH of about 5.25, close to the $\text{p}K_{\text{a}}$ value of octadecanoic acid of 5.4 that is usually assumed³¹ (some authors claim that the $\text{p}K_{\text{a}}$ value is much higher than this³²). From this, we predict that the acid groups of the monolayer are about 60% ionized. This supports the idea that the dipole layer needed to stabilize the polar surface comes from the ionized organic monolayer.

## Conclusions

Previous¹⁷,¹⁸ calculations demonstrated that polar surfaces of ionic crystals could grow on ionized organic substrates but that a well-defined substrate charge density was required to cancel the surface dipole moment. This seemed to be inconsistent with the experimental evidence that polar (01.2) surfaces of calcite nucleate on a range of organic substrates with diverse properties. Self-assembled monolayers of alkanethiols on Ag⁴ and Au⁵,⁶ with carboxylic headgroups have a hexagonal structure with lattice parameters of 4.77 and 4.97 Å, respectively. The carboxylate groups of the hydrogen-bonded ribbons have a centered rectangular structure with lattice parameters of 4.5 and 9.5 Å.⁹ The polydiacetylene films have a centered rectangular structure with lattice parameters of 4.9 and 8.8 Å.⁸

In this paper, we have shown that the dipole moment of polar surfaces can be quenched by a substrate with an arbitrary charge density by modifying the charge density of the terminating cation plane. We have modeled interfaces between polar (01.2) and (00.1) surfaces and stearate monolayers with a range of molecular densities. We found that the interfaces could be stabilized by reducing the surface density of Ca ions. Rows of ions were removed from the surface at regular intervals, and this served both to accommodate the lattice mismatch between the monolayer and the crystal surface and to quench the dipole moment. Away from these lines of missing ions or defects, there was very good matching between the crystal and the monolayer for both types of interface. The interfacial energies increased linearly with increasing monolayer lattice parameter, and the interfacial energy was slightly lower for the (00.1) interfaces than the (01.2) interfaces for the same monolayer density. For both interfaces, the interfacial energy was found to be low for a range of monolayer densities, and therefore we expect enhanced nucleation of both faces on charged organic substrates. These results are consistent with experiments, which found that the (01.2) crystals nucleated on carboxylic acid terminated alkanethiols on gold were elongated along

the direction of good lattice match.⁶ We would expect to find a reduced growth rate in the direction of poor matching because of the necessity of incorporating defects into the interfacial structure.

One question remains, however. Why does the (01.2) surface nucleate in preference to the (00.1) surface when our calculations find that the interfacial energy of the (00.1) surface is slightly lower? Our current calculations suggest two possible answers. First, we have shown that a nucleus oriented in the (01.2) direction is more stable than one oriented in the (00.1) direction for thin enough nuclei (of the order of a few calcium carbonate units wide). Also, simulations of $Ca^{2+}$ and $HCO_3^-$ ions in solution above ionized monolayers found that a bilayer structure evolved with the $Ca^{2+}$ ions above the monolayer and $HCO_3^-$ ions above the $Ca^{2+}$ ions. The average orientation of the $HCO_3^-$ ions in solution was close to the orientation of the $CO_3^{2-}$ ions of the (01.2) surface and very different from the flat conformation of the $CO_3^{2-}$ ions of the (00.1) surface. These results suggest that if a bilayer configuration of ions forms in solution the (01.2) surface would be kinetically favored. Both of these simulations point in the same direction: the common appearance of the (01.2) direction is kinetic in origin.

Most discussions of these interfaces use geometrical arguments to explain why the calcite crystals grow in specific directions. The results reported here suggest two modifications to this view. First, they show that geometrical templating arguments must allow for considerable disordering in the organic functional groups on which the crystal is going to nucleate. The ordered interface, if it exists, is self-assembled as part of the nucleation and growth process; it does not exist preformed on the organic monolayer, waiting for the mineral to find it. Second, the effects of long-range electrostatics constrain the possible outcomes. If a crystal is to grow in a polar direction, then both the constraints of local geometrical matching and of global electrostatics (i.e., the quenching of macroscopic dipoles) must be met.
