Growth studies of ultrahigh vacuum deposited thin films are often carried out ex situ, assuming the total film mass reached at the end of the deposition is preserved in the subsequent stages of film preparation. Many kinetic models commonly adopted to analyze quantitatively the mechanism of growth take into account the role of the deposition rate of molecules on the substrate surface, their diffusion, and their possible desorption. Within this framework, a strong simplification (and approximation) of the model is achieved when considering a regime of complete condensation (i.e., neglecting the possibility of re-evaporation of the deposited molecules, both during the deposition and the postdeposition stages of growth). Here, we demonstrate that, for molecular materials of relatively small organic molecules physisorbed on inert surfaces, this phenomenon may strongly affect not only the surface dynamics during deposition but also the postdeposition stage of thin film preparation. Some examples showing clearly its effects on the surface of single crystals and the thin film phase are reported and discussed. Finally, a quantitative description of desorption is provided by comparing the prediction of thermodynamics for the quaterthiophene/silica system with the experimental observation of the growth dynamics of the film and the results of approximate kinetic models. The thermodynamic model employs the surface free energies of a quaterthiophene crystal, which are evaluated by molecular simulation using a newly developed force field.

## I. Introduction

The preparation of a highly controlled thin films of organic materials is becoming a challenging but rewarding goal in view of obtaining technological devices with performances close to those of traditional semiconductors, but at considerably lower costs. Several other advantages motivate the study of this class of materials. Above all are the virtually unlimited possibilities in terms of the tunability of their physical properties, both by selecting different molecules and by adopting different growth techniques. Within this framework, important results have been obtained in the field of electronic and optoelectronic devices using conjugated oligomers such as oligothiophenes and oligocenes. $^{1}$ In the so-called "oligomer approach" to the study of organic semiconductors, small conjugated molecules are a natural starting point for the development of "all-plastic" technology based on highly processable polymers. Nevertheless, oligomers generally show a better capability of assembling in highly crystalline phases than polymers, which is a very desirable requisite when high carrier mobilities are required. Furthermore, the ease of sublimation of these materials makes them the best candidates for the study of growth processes from a fundamental point of view adopting vapor-phase growth techniques, such as organic molecular beam deposition (OMBD). $^{2}$

There are many reports in the literature dealing with the growth process of ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) sublimated organic materials, adopting both crystalline and amorphous substrates. In the latter case, the most widely used substrate is silica because of its relevance in the field of integrated electronics. With exception of the case of self-assembled monolayers, the deposited molecules do not "react" with the substrate-forming chemical bonds but are solely physisorbed on its surface, thanks to weak van der Waals interactions. Under this condition, re-evaporation (desorption) of molecules from the substrate is expected to play an important role in the microscopic growth processes leading to film growth. Nonetheless, in most quantitative studies of the growth mechanism of organic thin films, a regime of complete condensation is assumed, which is a rather strong approximation.

The present work demonstrates that for small organic molecules desorption is a process that is active both during a deposition experiment in UHV and after all molecules have thermalized to form the crystalline solid film, even when the substrate is at room temperature. As a prototypical example of a small oligomer, we chose quaterthiophene (4T) because of its relevance in the field of organic semiconductors. The availability of many thermodynamic data for this molecule has allowed an estimate of the vapor pressure of the solid at room temperature by a comparison between the growth shape of the crystal investigated experimentally and the equilibrium shape predicted by a thermodynamic model for the heterogeneous nucleation of the material from the vapor phase based on the calculation of the surface energies of the crystal. The predicted vapor pressure turns out to be significantly higher than the residual pressure in a UHV chamber, suggesting a tendency toward re-evaporation. This prediction is proven by monitoring via atomic force microscopy (AFM) the erosion of a molecularly flat surface of a 4T single crystal as a function of the time spent in UHV. ## II. Experimental Methods
4T ($\text{C}_{16}\text{H}_{10}\text{S}_{4}$, molecule mass $5.5 \times 10^{-25}$ kg) was synthesized in accordance with a procedure reported elsewhere. $^{3}$

4T single crystals were grown from the vapor phase with the procedure described in ref 4 for the low-temperature polymorph $\alpha$4T/LT belonging to the monoclinic system (space group $P2_{1}/c$) with cell parameters $a = 6.09$ Å, $b = 7.86$ Å, $c = 30.48$ Å, $\beta = 91.8^{\circ}$, and $Z = 4$. The crystal was then placed on a glass plate contacting the (001) face and inserted into the growth chamber.

UHV deposition of 4T has been carried out using a Knudsen effusion cell with an orifice area of $7.9 \times 10^{-3}$ cm$^{2}$ and an orifice transmission factor of 0.6 (Clausing's transmission factor for a straight tubular orifice with a length-to-diameter ratio of 0.7);$^{5}$ the source−substrate distance was 14 cm. The residual pressure in the growth chamber was $5 \times 10^{-10}$ Torr. Silica substrates were cleaned using various solvents and blown dried with dry nitrogen before introduction into the growth chamber. Each sample after deposition remains in the growth chamber for a period of 3 h. The structure of 4T films grown on silica is reported in ref 6, where it is demonstrated to be the same as the $\alpha$4T/LT polymorph, with crystalline domains exhibiting the (001) plane as the face in contact with the substrate.

AFM images were collected with a Nanoscope IIIa (Digital Instruments) in tapping mode with silicon cantilevers within a few hours after sample removal from the growth chamber. Island density number and island size distributions of 4T thin films were evaluated, averaging among at least 10 images a size of $100\ \mu\text{m}^{2}$ each.

## III. Theoretical Calculations
The energies of the surfaces of the 4T/LT crystals enter the thermodynamic model to be described below, and they were calculated by atomistic computer simulation. Following the approach of Tasker,$^{7}$ the crystal is modeled by a series of planes parallel to the surface of interest and periodic in two dimensions. This slab is divided into two blocks. Each block comprises two regions, regions 1 and 2. The atoms belonging to region 1 are allowed to move, whereas those belonging to region 2 are kept fixed at their bulk equilibrium positions (see below). The bulk of the crystal is simulated by the two blocks joined together ("bulklike slab": vacuum/region 2/region 1/region 1/region 2/vacuum), whereas the surface is represented by a single block ("surfacelike slab": vacuum/region 2/region 1/vacuum). Thus, region 1 represents the molecules near the surface, which is exposed upon cleaving the crystal along a certain plane. Region 2 contains those molecules further away from the surface that represent the underlying crystal (Figure 1). The surface energy $\gamma$ is then given by

$$
\gamma = \frac{2E_{\text{s}} - E_{\text{b}}}{2A} \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{\text{s}}$ and $E_{\text{b}}$ are the energies of the bulklike and surfacelike slabs and $A$ is the surface area.

The energies of the bulk and surface models were minimized with the NEWTON program of the TINKER 4.1 molecular modeling package. $^{8}$ The simulations were based on the MM3 force field, $^{9}$ modified by inclusion of an ab initio inter-ring torsion potential, $^{10}$ potential-derived charges on all atoms from gas-phase B3LYP/6-31G** calculations, and an additional rescaling of all coefficients of the $R^{-6}$ dispersion terms. $^{11}$ van der Waals and electrostatic interactions were smoothly reduced to zero beyond a cutoff value. We carried out several calcula- tions with cutoffs of 9.0, 12.0, 15.0, 17.5, and 19.9 Å. The simulation boxes were obtained by first running the $NPT$ dynamics (temperature 300 K) of a 3D supercell generated from the unit cell of the LT polymorph of 4T. This was done to include the effect of the crystal's thermal expansion. The final configuration was minimized to bring the atoms to their equilibrium position (at fixed lattice parameters). The surfaces were obtained by cutting this supercell according to the following crystallographic planes: (001), (100), (110), and (010). The dimensions of the simulation cells (slab thickness and periodicity in lateral dimensions) were chosen such that they were at least twice the greatest cutoff value (i.e., $\geq$40 Å). The energies of the slabs were then reminimized with respect to the coordinates of the atoms within region(s) 1. The rms gradient at convergence was set to $0.0001$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ Å$^{-1}$.

The final energy values are shown in Table 1. The cutoff distance of 19.9 Å was eventually chosen for the subsequent calculations. With this choice of the cutoff, the energy values are basically converged. We also observe that, for a given cutoff, the energies are ordered as follows: (001) < (010) < (110) < (100).

## IV. Results and Discussion
### IV.1. Nucleation Theory and Growth Modeling of 4T with OMBD
In the Knudsen mode of evaporation, the total number of molecules effusing per unit time along the axis of the Knudsen cell into the vacuum (deposition rate $I_{\text{A}}$) can be readily calculated with the following equation$^{12}$

$$
I_{\text{A}} = \frac{(W_{\text{a}}A_{\text{e}}/\pi L^{2})p_{\text{H}}}{(2\pi mk_{\text{B}}T_{\text{H}})^{1/2}} \tag{2}
$$

where $A_{\text{e}}$ is the orifice area, $W_{\text{a}}$ is the orifice transmission factor, $L$ is the source−substrate distance, $p_{\text{H}}$ is the source vapor

The vapor pressure of 4T at various temperatures (between 90 and 160 °C) has been measured by Kloc et al.¹³ Therefore, with eq 2 we are able to predict the deposition rate of 4T molecules at the source temperatures employed. Given the rate of effusion of the molecular beam onto the substrate, kept at temperature $T_{\text{C}}$, the pressure that the molecular beam applies to the substrate surface ($p_{\text{C}}$) can be calculated with the equation¹²

$$
p_{\text{c}}=(2\pi mk_{\text{B}}T_{\text{c}})^{1/2}I_{\text{A}}=\frac{W_{\text{a}}A_{\text{e}}}{\pi L^{2}}p_{\text{H}}\left(\frac{T_{\text{c}}}{T_{\text{H}}}\right)^{1/2} \tag{3}
$$

which is a slight modification of the Knudsen law,¹⁴ taking into account the geometry of the effusion experiment. Table 2 reports the calculated deposition rate at the employed source temperatures and the corresponding calculated pressures for the experiment described in section IV.3.

A knowledge of $p_{\text{C}}$ is of fundamental importance for quantitatively estimating the level of supersaturation of the vapor during 4T nucleation and growth at the substrate level and then for predicting the size of the critical nucleus, nucleation rate, and several other thermodynamic parameters. The thermodynamic supersaturation $\Delta\mu$ is defined as

$$
\Delta\mu = k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{c}}\ln\left(\frac{p_{\text{c}}}{p_{\text{eq}}}\right) \tag{4}
$$

where $p_{\text{eq}}$ is the vapor pressure of the material at the substrate temperature.

When the heterogeneous nucleation of 4T is considered, the free-energy variation $\Delta G$ related to this transition can be expressed as a first attempt in the form

$$
\begin{align*}
\Delta G=-n_{k}\Delta\mu + 2\gamma_{100}S_{100} + 2\gamma_{010}S_{010} + \\
4\gamma_{110}S_{110} + (2\gamma_{001}-\sigma)S_{001} \tag{5}
\end{align*}
$$

where $n_{k}$ is the number of molecules in the crystal, $\gamma_{hkl}$ and $S_{hkl}$ are the surface energy and area of the $(hkl)$ crystal face of the $\alpha$4T/LT polymorph, respectively, and $\sigma$ is the specific energy of adhesion of 4T nuclei on silica. Equation 5 assumes that only the {001}, {100}, {110}, and {010} faces contribute to the equilibrium shape of the 4T crystal that will result as illustrated in Figure 2. The {001} face is also known to be the face in contact with the substrate. Although this last observation has been demonstrated experimentally,⁶ the presence of the other faces in the equilibrium shape of the crystal must be verified. As a general rule, highly corrugated (high-energy) surfaces do not contribute to the equilibrium shape of the crystal because their area, calculated by minimizing eq 5, may be equal to zero or even negative. Equation 5 also assumes that the microscopic structure of a critical nucleus or small crystal is identical to that of the final (infinite) crystal. This is not always the case,¹⁵ but it is nonetheless a reasonable working hypothesis when this nucleus is relatively large (see below).

As illustrated in Figure 2, the areas $S_{hkl}$ can be expressed as a function of the number of unit cells $n_{a}$, $n_{b}$, $n_{c}$, and $n_{d}$ along the edges of the faceted nucleus parallel to the crystallographic direction indicated by the subscript:

$$
S_{001}=(n_{a}n_{b}+2n_{a}n_{d}+2n_{b}n_{d}+2n_{d}^{2})ab \tag{6a}
$$

$$
S_{100}=n_{b}n_{c}bc \tag{6b}
$$

$$
S_{010}=n_{b}n_{c}ac \tag{6c}
$$

$$
S_{110}=n_{c}n_{d}c\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}} \tag{6d}
$$

$$
n_{k}=(n_{a}n_{b}+2n_{a}n_{d}+2n_{b}n_{d}+2n_{d}^{2})n_{c}Z \tag{6e}
$$

The simultaneous search for a stationary point in eq 5 with respect to $n_{a}$, $n_{b}$, $n_{c}$, and $n_{d}$ allows us to calculate the critical dimensions $n_{a}{}^{*}$, $n_{b}{}^{*}$, $n_{c}{}^{*}$, and $n_{d}{}^{*}$:

$$
n_{a}{}^{*}=\frac{4c(\gamma_{110}\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-a\gamma_{010})}{Z\Delta\mu} \tag{7a}
$$

$$
n_{b}{}^{*}=\frac{4c(\gamma_{110}\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-b\gamma_{100})}{Z\Delta\mu} \tag{7b}
$$

$$
n_{c}{}^{*}=\frac{2ab(2\gamma_{001}-\sigma)}{Z\Delta\mu} \tag{7c}
$$

$$
n_{d}{}^{*}=-\frac{2c(\gamma_{110}\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-a\gamma_{010}-b\gamma_{100})}{Z\Delta\mu} \tag{7d}
$$

This set of equations relates the dimension of the critical nucleus (and then the thermodynamically stable shape of the crystal) to the supersaturation of the system and the surface energies of the crystal. Equation 7c is of particular importance because it allows us to predict the growth conditions leading to 3D ($n_{c}{}^{*} > 0.5$) or 2D ($n_{c}{}^{*} = 0.5$) nucleation of 4T. In the former case, the film will be constituted by separated or ill-connected grains, depending on coverage, whereas in the latter the material will form a continuous flat surface.

In our case, with all depositions carried out with the growth chamber and substrate at room temperature, a knowledge of the vapor pressure of 4T molecules at room temperature is mandatory for calculating the supersaturation and then the equilibrium shape of the crystal. The extrapolation of this datum from the Arrhenius plot reported in ref 13 (see graph in Figure 5 and data in Table 1) gives a vapor pressure of $4 \times 10^{-10}$ Torr. Equation 7c allows us to calculate the critical dimension $n_c^*$ expected at these supersaturations, when the surface and adhesion energies are known. For 4T, the adhesion energy $\sigma$ is unknown for any substrate, but it has been estimated in the case of pentacene deposited on UV-ozone-treated silicon oxide.¹⁶ The (001) plane of 4T and pentacene is lined up with C−H bonds of the extremities of molecules packed in a herringbone motif, with one molecule per 23.9 and 24.3 Å², respectively. ### IV.2. Effect of UHV on the Surface of 4T Single Crystals.
To verify directly the predicted sublimation tendency of 4T crystals, we have monitored ex situ with AFM the morphology evolution of a molecularly flat surface of a single crystal of α4T/LT after placing it in the UHV growth chamber for a certain time. The results are reported in Figure 3. Figure 3a shows the (001) surface of the as-grown crystal exhibiting two terraces separated by a step of about 1.5 nm, as can be seen from the cross-sectional profile. This height corresponds to half the length of the *c* axis of the crystal; therefore, on the basis of the crystal structure,⁴ this step is an elemental step. Another feature of the morphology of this surface is represented by the white spot located at the center of the images. It is part of the crystal itself and consists of a mound about 3 *µ*m wide and 15 nm high, probably formed during the growth process of the single crystal or during the transfer onto the substrate. Figure 3b shows the same region of Figure 3a after 1 day in UHV at room temperature. It is observed that the step profile becomes rough and approaches the mound. Finally, Figure 3c shows the same region after 2.5 days in UHV at room temperature. The step edge is now extremely indented and has overcome the mound position, leaving behind a multitude of islands. It is particularly impressive that in Figure 3c one can recognize the shape of the native step-edge shown in Figure 3a, indicating that erosion also starts in the second layer of the crystal surface in a region right beneath the step edge. All previous observations lead to clear evidence that the process of sublimation takes place spontane- ously at room temperature and in UHV conditions on the surface of 4T crystals. Furthermore, this process is rather fast and is observed to influence the morphology of the crystals after a few hours.

### IV.3. Effect of UHV on the Surface of 4T Thin Films.
The previous example shows that, as expected, sublimation of the crystals occurs more likely at defects. Therefore, polycrystalline thin films exhibiting a high density of defects such as grain boundaries are expected to be more prone to re-evaporate. This is observed, for example, in the preparation of polycrystalline 4T thin films deposited on potassium acid phthalate (KAP) (Figure 4).⁶ The presence of two different crystalline domains, namely, needles and flat layers, characterized by the same structure (α4T/LT) but different contact planes gives rise to the formation of grain boundaries that are responsible for the presence of concave voids over all the film surface and in particular in the region near the needles.

The morphology of 4T thin films deposited on KAP is particularly meaningful because post-growth nucleated holes can be easily recognized. The crystalline domains belonging to the flat phase are characterized by upright molecules,⁶ which prevent the $\pi-\pi$ interaction of 4T molecules with the substrate, and are coherently oriented (as demonstrated from optical analysis reported in ref 6); this brings about the coalescence of the islands when the film reaches a sufficient coverage and then leads to the final even surface covering almost all of the substrate surface.

In the case of 4T thin films deposited on amorphous substrates such as silica, the random azimuthal orientation of islands is responsible for an aggregation mechanism characterized only by island impingement with the formation of grain boundaries.¹⁷ In this case, the morphology of the film is too complex for us to observe the effects of post-growth desorption directly.

For this reason, we report below a quantitative analysis of the island size distribution of 4T films grown on silica with an identical thickness ($I_{\text{A}} \times \Omega t = 1$ nm, where $\Omega$ is the volume occupied by one molecule in the α4T/LT crystal and *t* is the deposition time) but a different deposition rate, as calculated with the procedure described in section IV.1. For any value of the deposition rate, within the range explored, the films present island morphology, as can be seen in the AFM images reported in Figure 5. When the deposition rate increases, the island density number is observed to increase, but beyond a certain value of the deposition rate, it saturates at a value of about 1.4 $\mu\text{m}^{-2}$. Further details on the film morphology are provided by the graph in Figure 5, where the ratio between the island density number of islands with area *a* ($N_{a}$) and the total island density (*N*) is reported as a function of the island area for the three films grown at different deposition rates. For a deposition rate of 7 Å/min, the island size distribution is relatively broad and centered at about 0.05 $\mu\text{m}^{2}$. For a deposition rate of 16 Å/min, the island size distribution changes abruptly, becoming narrower and being centered at about 0.03 $\mu\text{m}^{2}$. However, a further increase of deposition rate up to 52 Å/min does not give rise to substantial changes of the ex situ appearance of the film. Indeed, as shown in Figure 3, the island size distribution is very similar to that of the film deposited at 16 Å/min, being only slightly shifted toward lower island areas.

The evolution of the film morphology with deposition rate in the nucleation regime of growth has been extensively studied both in inorganic and organic systems.¹⁸ When films are grown under the same conditions, the island density number is observed to increase with the deposition rate, with a law depending primarily on the size of the critical nucleus.¹⁹ In the case of molecular materials deposited on inert substrates, nucleation is typically 3D, as in our case, and the critical nucleus is composed of a large number of molecules.¹⁶ A large critical nucleus and a regime of complete condensation give growth dynamics in which the island density is expected to increase *linearly* with the deposition rate.¹⁹ Furthermore, the island size distribution is expected to scale dynamically with the deposition rate, appearing progressively narrower and centered at a smaller island size with increasing deposition rate.²⁰ This behavior is actually observed in our system for deposition rates lower than 16 Å/min, as shown in Figure 3. However, the total island density apparently does not scale linearly with the deposition rate but saturates at 1.4 $\mu\text{m}^{-2}$. These observations can be explained by considering that the regime of complete condensa- tion is in general a strong approximation in the case of the deposition of organic molecules on inert substrates. Neverthe- less, the critical phenomenon responsible for the saturation of the island density and for the shape of the island size distribution at a deposition rate higher than 16 Å/min is the post-growth desorption of the film. At a deposition rate of 52 Å/min, the morphology of the film right at the end of the deposition is expected to be characterized by a huge number of small islands with a narrow size distribution that is far from those of the other films. Because the desorption of molecules is always active, islands keep on reducing their size during the permanence of the film in UHV after deposition. This process is faster for smaller islands because they possess a higher surface-to-volume ratio and vapor pressure. In this way, the smallest islands can even completely re-evaporate, and then they will not contribute to the ex-situ evaluation of the total number of islands and their size distribution.

Coarsening phenomena driving molecular migration from smaller islands to larger ones, without changing the total film mass, are assumed to play only a marginal role in the evolution of the island size distribution of the considered samples. Indeed, samples are deposited and imaged at room temperature to reduce the thermal activation of molecular and cluster diffusion. Indeed,

once the samples are exposed to air, these are the mechanisms that may be responsible for coarsening. However, these processes are generally very slow. Moreover, cluster diffusion is rather unlikely because of the large size of the islands.

Narrow island size distributions with a maximum at small sizes can be obtained not only at high deposition rates but also at low substrate temperatures. For the 4T/silica system, below a critical temperature of about 200 K the nucleation of 4T islands occurs via a post-growth process, giving rise to a narrow island size distribution centered at about $0.02\ \mu m^2$. $^{21}$ Under this condition, the effect of desorption is expected to influence the film morphology, in particular because the film must undergo an annealing treatment up to room temperature before extraction from the growth chamber for ex-situ analysis. Therefore, the scaling of the total island density with temperature, for temperatures below 200 K, which is observed not to follow exactly the behavior expected for postdeposition nucleation, can be explained by the occurrence of desorption.

### V. Conclusions

The strong desorption tendency of 4T thin films has been demonstrated, and it has important consequences on the study of the growth process of organic materials and on the performances of devices based on these systems. When the submonolayer regime of growth is considered, desorption may cause an abrupt decrease of the density of nuclei that cannot be taken into account in an ex-situ analysis of the sample. This would produce wrong estimates of, for example, the nucleation rate of the film, diffusion coefficients of molecules, spatial correlations among nuclei, and so forth. For thicker films, this phenomenon is expected to be less effective but can in any case substantially affect the ultimate morphology of the film. In this latter case, the density of defects, in particular, grain boundaries, determines the intensity of desorption because it occurs preferably at those sites. The evolution of this process can lead to the nucleation of holes on the surface of the film, hindering the possibility of completing the coverage of the substrate and therefore negatively affecting the film performance in terms of carrier mobility.
