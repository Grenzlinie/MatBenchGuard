## ABSTRACT

We present a theory of quantum switching of polarization in hydrogen-bonded order-disorder ferroelectrics, and examine the macroscopic quantum-tunneling rate of the polarization reversal for nano-samples induced by microscopic tunneling of protons between the two wells in potentials of hydrogen bonds. We compute the rate of the electric polarization tunneling through the barrier between two minima of the free energy density of a system. By using the instanton approach, we calculate a barrier factor and a prefactor multiplying the exponential of the action integral for the tunneling splitting at zero applied electric field. We compute the tunneling rate of domain walls. We obtain the double tunneling effect, i.e., the polarization tunneling related to the proton tunneling between the two minima in potentials of hydrogen bonds.

In recent years, there has been considerable interest in the phenomenon of the macroscopic quantum tunneling [1-5]. It corresponds to the tunneling of a macroscopic variable through the barrier between two minima of the effective potential of a macroscopic system. The possibility of the quantum tunneling of the magnetic moment in small particles has been considered in ferromagnets and antiferromagnets [2-4]. Effects of macroscopic tunneling of polarization may play an important role in ferroelectrics due to the reduction in sample size. Such samples may be considered as permanent (nonvolatile) memory systems [6]. The concept of permanent (nonvolatile) memory is intrinsically related to memories that do not lose, in classical terms, the information stored in them. With the development of nanotechnology, it is possible to reduce the size of domains that can store a single bit of information. A single domain of a uniaxial ferroelectric grain may be thought of as a classical permanent memory. At the mesoscopic level, this system may experience considerable quantum fluctuations due to tunneling between two possible memory states, thus destroying the classical permanent memory effect. To study these quantum effects the concrete example of a mesoscopic uniaxial ferroelectric grain should be discussed where the orientation of the electric polarization determines two possible memory states. It is of importance to consider the possibility of macroscopic tunneling of polarization at very low temperatures in nanoscale samples in the case of ferroelectric ordering.

A special group of ferroelectrics is the family of hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics, in which quantum effects of proton and deuteron tunneling can occur even at high temperatures and in large samples. An additional, and special problem of polarization switching, is the polarization reversal in hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics induced by the proton tunneling. Hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics undergo an order-disorder phase transition [7]. Though all atoms in the unit cell are involved in the ferroelectric process, the phase transition nevertheless is triggered by an ordering of hydrogens in one of the two possible equilibrium sites in the hydrogen bonds. An investigation of the mechanism of mobility of domain walls and polarization reversal is required since it is necessary to clarify the influence of the proton transfer on the domain wall dynamics and on the polarization switching [8-10]. The problem stems from the striking difference in domain-wall dynamics of proton and deuteron contained substances (for instance, $KH_2PO_4$ and $KD_2PO_4$) [11]. It is known that the isotope effect in the Curie temperature and spontaneous polarization is the case [11]. The substitution of deuterons for protons in the $KH_2PO_4$-group nearly double their Curie or Neel temperatures, which indicates the involvement of the hydrogen bonds in the mechanism of their corresponding phase transitions. Traditionally, the tunneling model, in which the marked increase in the phase transition temperature is attributed to a change in the tunneling frequency caused by the mass change, explained the proton-deuteron isotope effect and the decrease in the phase transition with increasing pressure is attributed to an increase in the tunneling integral [7]. At very low temperatures, and because of reducing the sample size, the quantum tunneling of protons or deuterons can govern the switching of polarization. To our best knowledge, the quantum switching was theoretically considered in ferroelectrics in [12, 13]. However, the tunneling effect has been calculated only in ferroelectrics, which undergo a phase transition of the displacive type. The polarization tunneling in ferroelectrics, which undergo an order-disorder phase transition, has not been studied. This group of ferroelectrics comprises hydrogen-bonded crystals, in which protons or deuterons may tunnel between two possible equilibrium sites in the hydrogen bonds. The linkage between the proton tunneling and the tunneling of polarization has not been discussed. We investigate here the double quantum tunneling taking place in the switching of polarization in order-disorder hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics. Our goal is to examine the effect of hydrogen tunneling on the polarization tunneling. To determine the degree of memory loss, the tunneling rate between the two polarization states should be calculated in the absence of an applied static electric field. Therefore, we examine the macroscopic quantum-tunneling rate of polarization reversal for nano-samples induced by microscopic tunneling of protons between the two wells in potentials of hydrogen bonds. We will compute the rate of tunneling of the polarization as quantum decay of the metastable state through the barrier between two minima of the effective potential of a system. By using the instanton approach, we will calculate a barrier factor and a prefactor multiplying the exponential of the action integral for the tunneling splitting. We will also compute the tunneling rate of domain walls. To our knowledge, the tunneling effect of domain walls in ferroelectrics is considered here for the first time.

The free energy density of a single domain ferroelectric grain made of a uniaxial ferroelectric material is given by
$$
F=-\frac{A}{2} P^{2}+\frac{B}{4} P^{4}, \quad(1)
$$
where $A, B$ are positive coefficients of the Landau expansion in the case of second-order phase transitions [14-16].

In the semi-classical limit, the tunnel splitting $\Delta$ can be calculated using standard methods like WKB [17] or the instanton technique [18] to obtain analytical results. In the latter, the problem of finding $\Delta$ is essentially reduced to determine the classical path, which connects the two maxima of the inverted potential $-F(P)$ (the minima of $F(P)$ ) and calculate the corresponding action. Actually, the tunneling of polarization should be described as a sum over all possible paths of transitions from the initial state to the final one, including those that are classically forbidden. Each path is weighed by the action of the path. Among the paths, one dominates. It is the saddle point path, which passes directly through the valley of the energy, i.e., the free energy density relief. For a quartic double-well potential of the form (1) these calculations can be performed analytically. Using [18], we obtain
$$
\Delta=4 \sqrt{3} \omega\left(\frac{S}{2 \pi \hbar}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \exp \left(-\frac{S}{\hbar}\right), \quad(2)
$$
$$
S=\frac{16 V_{0}}{3 \omega}, \quad(3)
$$
where $V_{0}=\frac{A^{2}}{4 B} V$ and
$$
\omega=\frac{\Gamma}{2 \hbar \cosh \left(\frac{\Gamma}{2 k_{B} T_{c}}\right)}\left[k_{B} T_{c}\left(\frac{T_{c}-T}{T_{c}}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}. \quad(4)
$$

$S$ is the so-called instanton action obtained from the sum over paths. $V$ is the volume of the ferroelectric single-domain grain. The prefactor $\omega$ ("attempt frequency") is determined by a classical attempt time, i.e., by the pseudo-spin mode frequency calculated within the framework of the Ising model in a transverse field [14] in the long-wave approximation. It is associated with small harmonic oscillations around the up or down orientations of the polarization. $T_{c}$ is the phase transition temperature. $J, \Gamma$ are the interaction constant and the proton tunneling integral correspondingly taken from the Hamiltonian for the Ising model in a transverse field [14]
$$
H=-\Gamma \sum_{i} S_{i}^{x}-\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i j} J_{i j} S_{i}^{z} S_{j}^{z}. \quad(5)
$$

Thus, we derive the quantum tunneling transition rate to switch polarization from $\vec{P}$ to $-\vec{P}$ at zero electric field. At very low temperatures the frequency is given by
$$
\omega=\frac{\Gamma}{2 \hbar \cosh \left(\frac{\Gamma}{2 k_{B} T_{c}}\right)}\left(\frac{J}{k_{B} T_{c}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}. \quad(6)
$$

The complete set of data required for the estimation of the macroscopic tunneling of polarization is available for $\mathrm{KH}_{2} \mathrm{PO}_{4}$. In contrast with majority of hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics, a much better description of the properties of $\mathrm{KH}_{2} \mathrm{PO}_{4}$ can be received within the framework of the four-particle cluster approximation [11]. The pseudo-spin mode frequency in this model is given by [14]
$$
\omega=\frac{\Gamma}{\hbar}\left(1-\frac{4\left\langle S^{x}\right\rangle w}{\Gamma}+\frac{4\left\langle S^{x}\right\rangle \varepsilon}{\Gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}, \quad(7)
$$
$$
\left\langle S^{x}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2} \tanh \left(\frac{\Gamma}{2 k_{B} T}\right). \quad(8)
$$

$w$ is the energy associated with a singly ionized (Takagi) $\mathrm{HPO}_{4}$ or $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{PO}_{4}$ group [11], $\varepsilon$ is the energy of a non-polar non-ionized (Slater) $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{PO}_{4}$ [11]. Using $\Gamma=295 \mathrm{~K}, w=1100 \mathrm{~K}, \varepsilon=$ $115 \mathrm{~K}[15], A=0.46$ [16] at $T_{c}=123 \mathrm{~K}[14], T \approx 0.1 \mathrm{~K}, B=11.9 \times$ $10^{-11}$ esu [16], for the grain of one nanometer and substituting these data into Eq. (2) we obtain the polarization tunneling frequency is $7.5 \times 10^{3} \mathrm{~Hz}$. The result of the calculation depends on the volume of the grain and hence on the number of ferroactive atoms, i.e., the coefficients depend on the volume involved in the tunneling process. The appearance of the number of atoms in the exponent means that the tunneling rate becomes unobservable small if the ferroelectric grains contain a large number of atoms. The tunneling rate of the electrical polarization of a single domain and single bit grain in a hydrogen-bonded ferroelectric deuterated sample is presented in Fig. 1 in dimensionless units
$$
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta /\left[\frac{4 D}{\left(\frac{2 \pi}{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right], \quad D=\frac{16 V_{0}}{3 \hbar},
$$
as a function of the value of the reciprocal dimensionless hydrogen tunneling integral in dimensionless units of $\frac{D}{\Gamma}$ depending on the degree of deuteration at zero external electric field and in the approximation of strong deuteration $\Gamma \ll k_{B} T_{c}$ leading to $\Delta^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\Gamma}{D}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \exp \left(-\frac{D}{\Gamma}\right)$. The logarithm (to the base ten) of the dimensionless tunneling integral is presented as a function of $\frac{D}{\Gamma}$. It is seen that the threefold increase in the microscopic tunneling of protons (deuterons) ( $\frac{D}{\Gamma}$ changes from 10 to 30 ) leads to a decrease in the rate of macroscopic tunneling of polarization in nine orders of magnitude. Since the value of the proton tunneling integral for the hydrogen-bond double well $\Gamma$ strongly depends on the hydrogen mass, this frequency should reveal a large isotope effect taking place as a result of the replacement of protons by deuterons. However, there are no data to estimate the rate of the polarization tunneling in the deuterated $KH_2PO_4$. The considerable rate of quantum tunneling of the electric polarization leads to destroying the classical memory effect of the single domain ferroelectric grain of the $KH_2PO_4$ crystal. In view of the large effect of the deuteron tunneling on the rate of the electric polarization it is clear that the tunneling rate of polarization in a strongly deuterated sample is very small. Then the speed of the quantum switching of polarization due to the deuteron tunneling is sufficiently low to avoid loss of the classic memory ability. Thus, it is clear that deuteration can serve as a method of governing the value of polarization tunneling. Note that in this approach, the effect of all higher lying states on the tunneling dynamics is ignored.

The result of our calculation in Eqs. (2) and (3) is different from that obtained in [13]. The barrier in [13] is determined by the saturation polarization, by the electric susceptibility with zero static electric field and the plasma frequency, while our approach deals with the coefficients of the Landau expansion found from measurements. The second feature of our calculation is emphasizing the difference between ferroelectrics of the displacement type and hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics belonging to the order-disorder type. In addition, it should be pointed out that there is no estimation of the tunneling rate of the electric polarization in [13]. The microscopic tunneling of ferroactive atoms was also not considered in [13]. The tunneling rate of polarization was calculated in [12] in terms of the model of the displacement type. It is impossible to make a comparison of our result with the result of [12] due to the distinction in the types of ferroelectric ordering and the presence of the microscopic tunneling of protons in our case. The influence of dissipation has been studied in [12]. It has been shown that the phonon modes are not significant sources of dissipation. For low impurity concentrations, less than 1% (clean samples), there is no significant dissipation. It should be noted that the macroscopic quantum tunneling of polarization was considered in the hydrogen-bonded chain, being a structural unit of the proton channel in proton-conductive bio-membranes [19]. The hydrogen-bonded chain is not a ferroelectric system. It consists of many hydrogen bonds. Authors [19] obtained the tunneling rate, which is much larger than our frequency. It turned out larger than $10^{12}$ Hz. Such a frequency is closer to the tunneling frequency of one proton than to vibrations of the long chain, which comprises many hydrogen bonds.

Further, we consider the quantum tunneling of domain walls in hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics. According to [11], the motion of a domain wall in $KH_2PO_4$-type crystals is connected with transfer of protons between the two sites in the hydrogen bonds. Thus, the hydrogen transfer between the two sites in double-minimum potentials of hydrogen bonds possibly triggers the motion of the domain wall, i.e., this intra-bond hydrogen transfer process is rate determining for the domain-wall motion and polarization switching. The striking isotope effect in the mobility of domain walls can be a quantum phenomenon caused by tunneling of protons and deuterons in double-well potentials of hydrogen bonds. It turned out that the measured ratio between the proton and deuteron mobility is about 5-6 orders [11]. The same ratio was detected in measurements of the ratio between the correlation frequencies of proton and deuteron [20]. All the mentioned facts indicate a linkage between the mobility of domain walls and polarization reversal and hydrogen tunneling between the sites in double-well potentials of hydrogen bonds. Thus, a sequence of proton "jumps" in the double-well potentials of hydrogen bonds may cause the domain wall motion. It is of importance to investigate the linkage between the proton tunneling and the domain wall dynamics and polarization reversal in light of so strong isotope effect and to clarify the mechanism of the mobility of domain walls in hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics [8].

In such small ferroelectric grains, the dynamics of domain walls or the nucleation of ferroelectric domains at low temperatures may be also governed by quantum effects. In this case, quantum switching of polarization can be determined by tunneling of protons, i.e., the double quantum effect occurs in hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics. Our consideration of the quantum tunneling of domain walls is based on the instanton approach presented in this paper. We add the gradient term $rac{1}{2}K(\nabla P)^2$ to Eq. (1), $K$ is the inhomogeneity coefficient, which is estimated by using the following equation: $K=rac{\delta^2}{4}$, $\delta$ is the distance between the two minima of the double-well potential for a proton in the hydrogen bond. We calculate the energy $V_0$ determining the action $S$ in (2) and (3). The calculation leads to the following equation

$$
V_0 = rac{(2)^{\frac{3}{2}} A^{\frac{3}{2}} K^{\frac{1}{2}} V}{3Bd}, \tag{9}
$$

where $d$ is the width of the domain wall. Substituting $A$, $B$, and $\omega$ used above and taking $\delta=0.34\times10^{-8}$ cm [21], $d=8\times10^{-8}$ cm [22] and $V=(2.1\times10^{-9}$ m$)^3$, we obtain $\Delta=8.8\times10^{2}$ Hz. This means that the tunneling of the domain walls may lead to the low-frequency resonance at the calculated frequency.

Tunneling of polarization between two opposite orientations leads to the splitting of the non-tunneling ground-state energy level into two levels separated by $\hbar\Delta$. Correspondingly, a very weak ac field will induce transitions between the two levels. This should result in the resonant absorption of the energy of the electro-magnetic field. If the energy minima of the two macroscopic states are equivalent, which occurs when the external electric field is negligibly small, the polarization performs a simple oscillation between them. The oscillation frequency is the resonant frequency, which is equal to the tunneling rate. The imaginary part of the frequency-dependent magnetic susceptibility describing the effect of resonance absorption due to the macroscopic tunneling was calculated in [23]. This tunneling can also lead to a peak at the calculated frequency in the frequency-dependent dielectric susceptibility. The peak should become

independent of temperature below some temperature. With decreasing temperature, thermal effects become less important indicating the presence of quantum tunneling of the electric polarization. The rate of polarization relaxation should also become independent of temperature as temperature goes down and reaches the temperature of the crossover from the classical regime of polarization switching to the quantum one. The described resonance method may be used for observation of the tunneling of polarization and of domain walls.
