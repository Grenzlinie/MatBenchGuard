**ABSTRACT:** The increasing interest and research on two-dimensional (2D) materials has not yet translated into a reality of diverse materials applications. To go beyond graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides for several applications, suitable candidates with desirable properties must be proposed. Here we use machine learning techniques to identify thermodynamically stable 2D materials, which is the first essential requirement for any application. According to the formation energy and energy above the convex hull, we classify materials as having low, medium, or high stability. The proposed approach enables the stability evaluation of novel 2D compounds for further detailed investigation of promising candidates, using only composition properties and structural symmetry, without the need for information about atomic positions. We demonstrate the usefulness of the model generating more than a thousand novel compounds, corroborating with DFT calculations the classification for five of these materials. To illustrate the applicability of the stable materials, we then perform a screening of electronic materials suitable for photoelectrocatalytic water splitting, identifying the potential candidate Sn₂SeTe generated by our model, and also PbTe, both not yet reported for this application.

**KEYWORDS:** two-dimensional materials, machine learning, high throughput screening, big data, density functional theory (DFT)

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Ever since the acclaimed history of graphene realization leading to the 2010 Nobel prize in physics,¹² there has been increasing research efforts to discover and employ novel two-dimensional (2D) materials for several potential applications. These are related to the remarkable mechanical, thermal, electric, electronic, magnetic, optical, and even exotic superconducting and topological properties these systems have.³ This endeavor led to the synthesis and properties evaluation of many 2D materials families such as hBN, silicene, germanene, stanene, phosphorene, transition metal dichacogenides (TMDs), MXenes, borophene,⁴⁻⁶ bismuthene,⁷ Janus structures of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs),⁸ and, more recently, even non-van der Waals 2D materials such as gallenene⁹ and hematene.¹⁰ Even beyond, with the potential of forming different heterostructures, the combinatorial possibilities for novel materials and the associated mechanisms for the control of specific properties are immense.

Although there have been a huge number of 2D materials proposals, coming mainly from density functional theory (DFT) calculations, in real-life applications, the 2D materials' space has hardly been explored. These are usually selected from a restricted group of a few among possible ones. Commonly, a structural prototype is selected on the basis of the existence of a successful material for the interest property, and then all atomic combinations forming isoelectronic systems in this prototype will constitute the set of materials candidates. The last step of this trial-and-error process is the DFT verification of the property for the material candidates, identifying the promising ones. This high-throughput materials screening usually results in few selected materials, which in turn could in principle also exhibit detrimental characteristics from the point of view of applications. For instance, the stability is frequently a relevant problem for prediction of materials' properties. In almost every possible application, the first essential requirement is that the material must be thermodynamically stable. Besides stability, each application will demand different property requirements, furthering the screening process.

Even though the thermodynamic stability is the first essential requirement, its evaluation is expensive to do exhaustively for every possible material combination.¹¹

In the ideal scenario, one wishes to determine the set of stable structures for a given combination of atoms with a specific stoichiometry without the DFT formation energy ($\Delta H_f$) evaluation for every possible combination. This

Interestingly, despite the importance of thermodynamic stability, there is no general rule that allows knowing a priori which structure is the most stable one. In recent years, much effort has been invested in the systematic prediction of two-dimensional systems. For instance, a topology-scaling algorithm was used to determine the materials possessing layered motifs in their crystal structures, predicting 680 monolayers with exfoliation energies below those of the existent two-dimensional materials.¹² Later, on the basis of the criterion of the difference between experimental and DFT lattice constants, Choudhary et al. identified 438 monolayers (as of the time of this work) with low exfoliation energies.¹³ In the same spirit, Mounet et al. computed the binding energies of 2,662 identified layered structures and classified those that are easily (1036 materials) or potentially (789 materials) exfoliable.¹⁴ Combining a top-down exfoliation algorithm and bottom-up chemical substitution, more than 6,000 2D materials were generated by Zhou et al.¹⁵ The recently created computational 2D materials database (C2DB)¹⁶ uses a slightly different approach. The database has DFT calculations of 2D materials distributed among common prototypes, which are combinatorially decorated with a different atomic basis from the whole periodic table, resulting in 3,712 2D materials (version 2018-12). The DFT high-throughput identification and characterization¹⁷ of two-dimensional materials has been the cornerstone of these recently available 2D materials' databases, which are expected to bridge the gap between data from simulations and real-world applications. Although these are a good platform for the prediction of properties, 2D compounds are still few in relation to the large number of three-dimensional materials, mainly because the so far explored material space has been concentrated on van der Waals type compounds. In this way, the thermodynamic stability continues to require calculations of the total energy and the energy above the convex hull. Machine learning techniques are one promising strategy to accelerate this process, by using available data to identify its patterns and then make predictions of unknown data.¹⁸

Here we propose a data-driven strategy to explore 2D materials' thermodynamic stability. Namely, using the recently created C2DB 2D materials database, we apply machine learning techniques to obtain descriptors which are capable of classifying materials as stable or unstable in a given structural group, based on the formation and convex hull energies. For the stable materials, we can predict its formation energy with a mean accuracy better than 0.2 eV/atom. Finally, illustrating the application of the selected stable materials, we perform a further screening for materials candidates with suitable electronic properties for photoelectrocatalytic (PEC) water splitting. ## 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Thermodynamic Stability. Despite the intense development of machine learning techniques applied to materials science in recent years,¹⁸ there is always a fundamental problem that precedes the use of a specific algorithm, the choice of property to be learned. For example, the learning of the DFT-LDA total energy for binary systems stabilizing both zinc blende and rock salt structures can also be achieved by predicting the difference between the total energy of these structures. The more stable structure could also be learned via classification techniques only by labeling the two structural classes, i.e., without any numerical specification of the total energy,¹⁹⁻²¹ showing that the mapping of the regression into a classification problem can in some cases lead to a more adequate description of the target property. In this work, since the thermodynamic stability is defined in terms of both the total energy and the energy above the convex hull, we have defined a global property characterizing the thermodynamic stability in terms of these variables, instead of using a different regression model for each of these properties.

First, we classify compounds according to the thermodynamic stability into three classes: low, medium, and high stability. We consider that all materials with positive formation energies are thermodynamically unstable, which we refer to hereinafter as low stability. Materials with negative formation energies are divided in terms of the energy above the convex hull, i.e., for $\Delta H_{hull} < 0.2$ eV/atom, compounds are considered highly stable, whereas for $\Delta H_{hull} > 0.2$ eV/atom, compounds could potentially be dissociated into other compounds, i.e., medium stability. Therefore, our work focuses on the prediction of materials' classes in which compounds are divided into low, medium, and high stability, as represented in Figure 2. To produce a machine learning model for materials, three components are needed: available data, a suitable numerical representation of materials or descriptor, and learning algorithms and their optimization.¹⁸ We now describe each of these components, essential to the obtained results. Our training data source comes from the recently created computational 2D materials database (C2DB).¹⁶ The database has DFT calculations of 3,712 2D materials for the accessed version (2018-12), distributed among prototypes that are combinatorially decorated with different basis atoms from the periodic table. The prototypes included in the database that we used are AgBr₃, AuSe, Bil₃, BiTeI, BN, CdI₂, CH₂Si, CH, CrW₃S₈, CrWS₄, FeOCl, FeSe, GaSe, GaS, GeS₂, GeSe, HfBrS, MnS₂, MnPSe₃, MoS₂, MoSSe, NiSe, PbSe, PbS, PdS₂, ReS₂, RhO, ScPSe₃, SnS, Ti₂CO₂, Ti₃C₂, Ti₃C₂O₂, Ti₄C₃, Ti₄C₃O₂, TiCl₃, TiS₃, and WTe₂. Since the magnetic order is an additional degree of freedom that can influence the learning of the structure stability, we restricted our study to nonmagnetic materials. This additional filter reduces the database to 2,685 compounds. Finally, in the training process we consider compounds with extreme behavior, that is, those that are clearly defined within the limits of the low-, medium-, and high-stability classes. Thus, we separate compounds with formation energies between −0.1 and 0.1 eV/atom and with energies above the convex hull between 0.1 and 0.3 eV/atom. These boundary cases are always included in the training sets.

In ML, given that sufficient data are available to sample the interest relationship, the task is then how to map the material-to-attribute connection. To this end, a set of materials' variables called features are needed, which will represent materials in a computer-friendly way. The representation of materials is a subject of intense and complex development,²²ˣ²³ in which the accurate description presents a greater challenge than the recent successes achieved for molecular representations.²⁴ˣ²⁵ In a generalized representation, both elemental and structural features²⁶ must be considered, while, with increasing complexity, electronic²⁷ or other features can also be included.

In order to find trends in the chemical space that do not depend on complex features that require a priori knowledge of the structure, we instead search for features associated with the properties of the compounds' constituent atoms. Furthermore, the SISSO method consists of two steps: feature space construction and feature selection. The feature space is incrementally constructed by combinatorially applying analytical operations between the initial set of primary input features $\Phi_0$, i.e., the previously constructed primary feature space from Tables 2 and 1. These combinations are made using the following operators: $\{I, +, -, \times, \div, \exp, \log, | |, \sin, \cos, \sqrt{}, ^{-1}, ^2, ^3, ^6\}$. The descriptor is selected as the feature that minimizes the overlap region between materials with low, medium, and high stability. We employ the SISSO method to perform a novel process of automated feature engineering and selection. This is done by training a SISSO classifier, now in a multitask approach, $^{31}$ where each prototype is considered as a learning task, and the machine learning model is trained for all tasks simultaneously. This process arrives at the best nonlinear features employed in linear models than can differentiate between classes simultaneously for every prototype. These linear models of up to three dimensions achieve poor classification results to our problem. Nonetheless, descriptors selected are low-dimensional representations accounting for variance in the data. Therefore, we selected 10 combined features from the top-ranked list, and testing them in the stochastic gradient boosting model, we select those indicated as important features for the proposed classification model.

In Figure 3a, we present the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the thermodynamic stability classification model, showing the model true positive rate (TPR = (true positive)/(true positive + false negative)) against false positive rate (FPR = (false positive)/(false positive + true negative)) with changing threshold. The ROC curve can be used as an evaluation metric of the classification model performance, where the area under the curve (AUC) should range between 1 for perfect classification and 0.5 for no discrimination capacity (dashed line). For our multiclass problem, we have three ROC curves, one for each class, calculated with one-vs-all methodology. The curves for the low- (red), medium- (orange), and high-stability (green) classes achieve AUC scores of 0.93, 0.89, and 0.94, respectively, showing a good degree of classification capacity. As expected, the classification score for the medium-stability class is lower due to the class definition by construction, where it has overlapping boundaries both with the low-stability class for the formation energy criteria and with the high-stability class for the energy above convex hull criteria. Adding to this are the DFT uncertainties on the calculated energies, which can act as noise on the data.

Having classified the 2D materials, as a second step we construct machine learning regression models to predict the formation energies and energy above convex hull of different systems. The accuracy for formation energy regression models in materials science literature is usually in the range we achieved, $^{35-39}$ even though a direct comparison cannot be made due to the nature of each data set. As discussed earlier, even with detailed structural information, the accurate representation of compounds is still a challenge, in contrast to the success achieved in the reduced chemical space of molecules. Currently, increased accuracy is achieved for extended materials' formation energies prediction by using different forms of detailed structural information such as the generalized RDF or angular distributions. $^{23,38}$ However, descriptors with a large number of input features increase the complexity of the machine learning model, consequently demanding more training data. Besides, for novel predictions and materials' discovery, atomic positions are not known. Strategies for representing materials with atomic-position-independent descriptors such as the one we presented here is currently a challenge. $^{37,39-42}$

Finally, the usefulness and test of a machine learning model is its predictive ability of novel data. Our machine learning model can be employed for creating a vast number of novel materials: for each chosen prototype, one can generate every possible elemental combination while satisfying valence rules. In a real-case scenario, the stability model would be one step of a multistep high-throughput materials screening process. As such, one would find promising materials candidates with desired properties such as electronic or magnetic properties and, as a final screening filter, select those materials that are stable and potentially synthesizable. Therefore, we proceeded to generate 100 novel atomic combinations outside the database whose formulas can fit 10 different prototypes. Specifically, the novel compound family $ABC_2$ (where A, B, and C are atoms from groups III, V, and VI, respectively) can be found in the prototypes RhO, PbSe, GaSe, AuSe, NiSe, and FeSe. Applications: Electronic Properties Screening. We now present an illustration of the potential use of the stable 2D materials previously selected to applied problems. For screening electronic materials suitable for photoelectrocatalytic water splitting; besides the stability as the first condition for any application, compounds are also required to have a sufficient band gap between the free energy of water splitting of 1.23 eV and about 3.0 eV and adequate electronic band edges alignment.$^{48}$ Other additional requirements are solvent stability, visible optical absorption, small excitonic binding energies and low recombination rate, low rate of backward and side reactions, corrosion resistance, environmental friendliness, and cost efficiency.$^{48}$

We examined the first requirement in the previous section and selected suitable materials for the further screening process of its electronic properties. From the total set of 3,712 materials, those predicted to be stable and presenting formation energies better than $-0.1$ eV/atom are 1,487 candidates. Besides the thermodynamic stability, another criterion to be satisfied should be the dynamical stability, measured theoretically by the absence of significant imaginary phonon frequencies, which indicate materials instabilities. Applying the filter of presenting the minimum eigenvalue of the Hessian $|\tilde{\omega}_{min}^2| < 10^{-5}$ eV $\mathring{A}^{-2}$, the set of candidates reduces to 581 stable materials. Among the stable 2D materials, we then selected those that present an electronic band gap value in the range between 1.2 and 3.5 eV, calculated in the accurate $G_0W_0$ level of theory, promoting reduced deviation to experimental values. The larger range for the selected gap values can in principle account for materials that could have their electronic gaps tuned by reasonable strain or electric field effects, for instance. The band gap screening filter reduced the set of 2D materials to 90 candidates (the complete list is presented in the Supporting Information).

The last screening filter is applied to ensure that the materials' band edges straddle with the water splitting redox potentials. For pH-dependent reactions, the redox potentials vary according to the Nernst equation:

$$
E^{\text{pH}} = E^{\text{pH=0}} + 0.059 \times \text{pH} \tag{1}
$$

Therefore, for the water splitting reactions, using values in reference to vacuum, the VBM energy must be lower than the $H^+/H_2$ reduction potential of $-4.44$ eV for pH = 0 and $-3.614$ eV for pH = 14, while the CBM energy must be higher than the $O_2/H_2O$ oxidation potential of $-5.67$ eV for pH = 0 and $-4.844$ eV for pH = 14.

Finally, in Figure 4 we show the candidate materials that satisfy the requirements of the screening process of stability, suitable gap, and aligned band edges. The materials satisfying the requirements are $MoS_2$, $WS_2$, $MoSSe$, $WSSe$, $WSTe$, $GeTe$, $PbTe$, phosphorene, and $PtSe_2$. The first two are well-known 2D materials for this specific application,$^3$ and two of the most studied TMDs materials. The next three are related to them, the Janus monolayers of TMDs.$^{49}$ Additionally to the suitable properties screened for, these Janus TMDs materials have intrinsic dipole moments and electrostatic potentials, which aid in electron−hole separation, consequently resulting in higher-efficiency photocatalytic materials.$^{50}$ GeTe has been recently proposed as a promising 2D material for PEC water splitting,$^{51}$ while PbTe in this same GeSe prototype structure as of the time of this work, to our knowledge, has not been reported yet for this application in the literature. Phosphorene and its derived nanomaterials have also recently been proposed as metal-free photocatalysts.$^{52}$ Finally, $PtSe_2$ was recently synthesized by epitaxial growth$^{53}$ and proposed as a photocatalyst.$^{54,55}$

The success of the ML model lies in the accurate thermodynamic stability prediction for two-dimensional systems as a necessary requirement for any other application. A large number of atomic combinations can be quickly examined, which, on the basis of knowledge and intuition, allows a rapid subsequent evaluation of other properties. For instance, we evaluate the stability of 40 ordered alloys with the structural prototype GeSe, i.e., $A_2BB'$ with A = Si, Ge, Sn, and Pb and B = O, S, Se, and Te. Besides confirming the stability of previously known compounds, e.g., GeTe and PbTe, we find novel 2D compounds, such as $Sn_2SeTe$. According to our DFT results, this compound has a formation energy of $-0.16$ eV/atom. As represented in Figure 4, the valence band maximum in $-5.81$ eV and the conduction band minimum in $-3.39$ eV (at the HSE06$^{56}$ hybrid functional level of theory) are strong evidence that $Sn_2SeTe$ is also a potential candidate for photoelectrocatalytic water splitting.

### 3. CONCLUSIONS
In the present work, we employ a data-driven approach which combines the use of materials repositories and databases, machine learning techniques applied to the discovery of

functional materials, and the application of a high-throughput screening methodology for a targeted application. Specifically, from a recently created database and repository of 2D materials, we employ a machine learning technique capable of predicting the thermodynamic stability of these materials without explicit information about the atomic positions, relying only on the prototype structure. With the obtained model, we can then predict the stability of materials beyond the original database, for which this property is unknown. This can lead to accelerated discovery and design of novel materials. Finally, as an applied problem using the selected stable materials, we perform a high-throughput screening of 2D materials suitable for photoelectrocatalytic water splitting. We find 10 potential candidates, of which two are yet unreported for this application.

As perspectives for future work, there is great potential to explore and use 2D databases, as evidenced by the realization of a few works on machine learning for 2D materials, namely, exploring the formation of heterostructures⁵⁷ and layered materials,⁵⁸ predicting the band gap of functionalized MXenes,⁵⁹ band gap, and exciton binding energies,⁶⁰ and predicting the exfoliation energies³⁸ of 2D materials from the bulk parent structures. A promising strategy we are investing in is to generate and combine several machine learning models for different properties, so that one could screen for a great number of materials combinations for specific applications, and then evaluate with DFT calculations or experiments those few selected candidates that are most promising. In the short term, properties such as the dynamical and thermal stability, electronic, optical, magnetic, topological and superconducting properties, and even the further investigation of materials representation, both as detailed complex features or simpler scalar features, are potential routes for development.
