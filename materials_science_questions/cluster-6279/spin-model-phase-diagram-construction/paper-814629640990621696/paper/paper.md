# Theory of the magnetic ordering and lattice internal rearrangement transition in $\text{UO}_2$

Robert Siemann and Bernard R. Cooper
Department of Physics, West Virginia University, Morgantown, West Virginia 26506
(Received 23 April 1979)

The first-order antiferromagnetic transition in fluorite structure $\text{UO}_2$ coincides with a lattice transition to a transverse internal rearrangement (TIR). In the TIR, the oxygen planes have a periodic internal shear relative to the undistorted fcc uranium sublattice. We have investigated the selection mechanism that causes the TIR to occur in preference to other distortions. The driving mechanism that causes some distortion to occur is the splitting of the $\Gamma_5$ cubic crystal-field ground state and consequent lowering of crystal-field energy. Our detailed calculations, involving consideration of a number of "candidate" distortional modes, verify that the TIR occurs because the lattice is relatively soft for such a distortion. This elastic energy advantage more than makes up for the advantage of a competing conventional (homogeneous) internal distortion in lowering the crystal-field energy more for a given size relative distortion. Our calculations incorporate experimental elastic effects by using parameters from a rigid-ion model fit to the phonon behavior. We find a low-temperature oxygen displacement of approximately 0.021 $\mathring{A}$ in close agreement with the experimental 0.014 $\mathring{A}$.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The nature of the first-order antiferromagnetic transition $^{1,2}$ in $\text{UO}_2$ and the associated lattice distortional behavior $^{3-5}$ have been a matter of considerable interest for some years. Special interest was attracted to this material by the fact that there is no measurable macroscopic lattice distortion accompanying the first-order magnetic transition. This lack of any appreciable macroscopic distortion has been well verified in recent years by low-temperature x-ray experiments on both polycrystalline $^6$ and single-crystal $^7$ $\text{UO}_2$.

Ordinarily one expects a lattice distortion in association with magnetic ordering. Moreover the behavior of $\text{UO}_2$ is characteristic $^{8,9}$ of strong magnetoelastic interactions in association with the magnetic ordering transition behavior. To resolve this apparent contradiction, Allen $^{4,5}$ proposed the existence of an internal strain occurring at the magnetic ordering in $\text{UO}_2$. The fluorite structure for $\text{UO}_2$, as shown in Fig. 1, is an fcc structure with a basis of one uranium and two oxygens (denoted $A$ and $B$). In the particular conventional (homogeneous) internal strain suggested by Allen, $^4$ shown in Fig. 2, the $A$ and $B$ sublattices move rigidly relative to one another and to the uranium sublattice. Thus all sublattices individually remain fcc. Hereafter we refer to this type of distortion as "the Allen mode."

About four years ago, Faber and Lander performed detailed neutron elastic cross-section measure- ments $^{10-12}$ in $UO_{2}$. An internal strain of the sort suggested by Allen could not explain the experimentally observed anisotropy of the neutron scattering. The question then was whether one could retain the attractive feature of Allen's picture that the uranium sublattice remained undistorted, yet find some distor- tional mode for the oxygen sublattices that would explain the experimental data. For this reason, Faber et al. $^{10}$ considered the possibility of internal rear rangement distortions where both the $A$ and $B$ oxy gen sublattices individually have periodic distortions, i.e., a periodic inhomogeneous internal distortion, while the uranium ions retain an undistorted fcc lattice. They considered two kinds of internal rear- rangements as shown in Figs. 3 and 4. (In these figures, the oxygen sites are at the corners of the cube. In the fluorite structure, each uranium is at the body center of a cube of oxygens, where alternating body centers along $\langle 100\rangle$ are vacant. The figures illustrate the situation for oxygen cubes with body centers occupied by uranium ions.) The experimental results $^{10-12}$ required that the movement of the oxy gens was within the planes transverse to the direction of propagation of the magnetic structure.

In the transverse internal rearrangement (TIR), shown in Fig. 3, alternate oxygen planes slide parallel to each other (along the $y$ axis) by equal and opposite amounts across the direction of propagation of the periodic distortion ( $x$ axis), which is transverse to the direction of propagation of the magnetic structure( $z$ axis). In the longitudinal internal rearrangement(LIR), shown in Fig. 4, alternate oxygen planes move toward or away from each other along the direction of propagation of the periodic distortion( $x$ axis), which again is transverse to the direction of propagation of the magnetic structure ( $z$ axis). There are two types of LIR possible, either with the dis- placement of the oxygens parallel to the uranium moment [shown in Fig. 4 (a)] or perpendicular to the uranium moment [shown in Fig. 4 (b)]. A detailed fitting of the experimental results $^{10}$ required the presence of a transverse internal rearrangement with a relative shift of the oxygen atoms of $2.6 \times 10^{-3}$ , and excluded the possibility of a longitudinal rearrangement mode.

The question then arises, what is the selection mechanism that causes the transverse internal rearrangement, i.e., internal shear deformation, to occur in association with the first-order magnetic ordering in $UO_{2}$? It is clear enough why some distortion occurs. As discussed by Allen for his model of the distortion behavior, the resulting splitting of the $\Gamma_{5}$ cubic crystal-field triplet ground state for the $U^{4+}$ ion allows a lowering of the ground-state energy (cooperative Jahn-Teller effect). The question is what mechanism causes the selection of the particular type of lattice distortion which occurs. The present paper answers that question. The basic physical idea involved is that suggested by Cooper. $^{13} He$ has suggested that the transverse internal rearrangement mode occurs in practice because it is less unfavorable with respect to raising the elastic energy than competing modes, such as the Allen mode, which may actually be more favorable than the TIR for lowering the crystal-field energy for a given size relative distortion. We have shown $^{14}$ that this relative elastic softness criterion also selects the macroscopic (external homogeneous) distortional mode that occurs in practice in

cerium monopnictides. In the present paper, we verify through detailed calculations that indeed the TIR occurs because the lattice is relatively soft for such a distortion. We do this through performing energy comparisons with the other physically reasonable "candidate" lattice distortional modes. Our calculations predict an amplitude close to that observed for the TIR.

In Sec. II, we discuss the development of the model on which we base our calculations. In Sec. III, we treat the comparison of ground-state energies for the various "candidate" lattice distortions, and show why the transverse internal rearrangement gives the lowest energy. In the concluding Sec. IV, we treat the phase diagram for the occurrence of the transverse internal rearrangement mode with varying relative strength of effective quadrupolar and exchange energies, as defined within our model. We discuss the pertinence of the model behavior to the actual experimental behavior of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$, particularly with respect to the occurrence of the first-order transition and the amplitude of the observed TIR. We also discuss the importance of understanding the elastic interactions at a microscopic level as the key element controlling the detailed behavior.

## II. DEVELOPMENT OF MODEL HAMILTONIAN

The interactions that determine the magnetic ordering and lattice distortional behavior of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ are the crystal-field interaction of the $\mathrm{U}^{4+}$ ions with the surrounding crystalline environment, the exchange energy giving magnetic ordering, and the elastic energy. In this section we discuss each of these energies in turn, and how they contribute to make up our overall model Hamiltonian.

We consider these interactions for each of several "candidate" distortional modes, to find the ground-state energy of the modes with respect to each other and with respect to the undistorted lattice. The candidate modes considered are selected on the basis of experimental plausibility. As already discussed, we consider the Allen internal mode shown in Fig. 2, the transverse internal rearrangement (TIR) mode shown in Fig. 3, and the two types of longitudinal internal rearrangement (LIR) modes, with oxygen displacement parallel and perpendicular to the $\mathrm{U}^{4+}$ momentum direction, shown in Fig. 4(a) and (b), respectively. In addition, we consider the two physically plausible homogeneous external distortions and associated magnetic structures. These are a tetragonal distortion with magnetic ordering along $\langle 100\rangle$ and an orthorhombic distortion with magnetic ordering along $\langle 110\rangle$, shown in Fig. 5(a) and (b), respectively.

### A. Crystal-field interaction

As already discussed, each $\mathrm{U}^{4+}$ ion in the fluorite structure is at the body center of a cube formed by the eight surrounding oxygen ions. For purposes of treating the distortional effects on the crystal-field phenomenologically in a tractable and physically reasonable way, we adopt a point-charge model. That is, we attribute the crystal electric field at each $\mathrm{U}^{4+}$ ion to the net effect of point charges located at each oxygen site. We allow for adjustment of the size of the effective charge entering into the gradient of the crystal field, $^{14}$ i.e., giving the magnitude of crystal-field distortional effects. So we do not require this charge to be the same as that giving the static cubic crystal-field splitting. As discussed in Sec. IV, our model behavior requires an effective charge of about one electron determining the crystal-field gradient.

The $\mathrm{U}^{4+}$ ion has a $5 f^{2}$ configuration. For $L$-$S$ coupling the free-ion ground-state multiplet would be

$^{3} H_{4}$ , as in $Pr^{3+}$ . When intermediate coupling effects are considered, $^{15}$ there is an admixture of $J=4$ multiplets, such as $^{1} G_{4}$ , belonging to other $L-S$ terms, but the $^{3} H_{4}$ component of the ground state remains predominant. The only effect of these admixtures,pertinent to our calculations, is to change the $g_{J}$  value to make it somewhat greater than the value of $\frac{4}{5}$ pertinent to $^{3} H_{4}$ , i.e., tending to increase the mo ment.
A cubic crystal field splits the $J=4$ ground-state multiplet. If one associates the crystal field with a negative charge as the source, the ground state is ei- ther a $\Gamma_{1}$ singlet or a $\Gamma_{5}$ triplet depending on the rela tive sizes of the fourth- and sixth-order contribution to the crystal fields. $^{16}$ Within the context of thepoint-charge model, use of calculated $\langle r^{4}\rangle$ and $\langle r^{6}\rangle$  values $^{17}$ and the experimental lattice parameter $^{1}$ lead one to expect a $\Gamma_{5}$ crystal-field ground state, $^{15}$ and this is also indicated by the experimental behavior. $^{4,5,8,10-12}$ When, in addition, one considers relatively strong crystal-field mixing effects pertinent to the crystalline environment in $UO_{2}$ , there are Jmixing effects giving additional components to the $\Gamma_{5}$  cubic crystal-field ground state such as $^{3} F_{2}$ . Again, however, the $^{3} H_{4}$ component is predominant in the $\Gamma_{5}$ crystal-field ground state. The only pertinent effect of this J mixing is to somewhat lower the $U^{4+}$ moment. $^{18}$ 
For our modeling purposes we consider a free-ion $^{3} H_{4}$ ground state and neglect J-mixing effects caused by the crystal field. The net effect of intermediate coupling and / mixing is probably to somewhat lower the net moment of the $\Gamma_{5}$ ground state relative to that of the $\Gamma_{5}$ state coming wholly from $^{3} H_{4}$ . The only effect this would have in our considerations is to give an overall scaling factor somewhat less than uni- ty to our calculated sublattice magnetization behavior.
The crystal-field Hamiltonian has the followingform $^{19}$ :
$$\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{0}+\sum_{m, n} C_{m n} \delta O_{m}^{n},\qquad(2.1)$$
 where $H C_{0}$ is the cubic crystal-field Hamiltonian, and the second term represents the effect of a small dis- tortion (of size characterized by $\delta$ ) from the cubic symmetry. The $C_{m n}$ are coefficients determined by the symmetry of the particular distortion. $O_{m}^{n}$ areStevens operator equivalents. $^{20}$ 
The particular form of $H C$ depends on the particular direction in the crystal chosen as the axis of quantiza- tion. The two choices of interest in this paper are the<100) and <110) directions. We then have for thecubic crystal-field Hamiltonian that $^{19}$ 
$$\mathcal{K}_{0}^{\langle 100\rangle}=B_{4}^{0}\left(O_{4}^{0}+5 O_{4}^{4}\right)+B_{6}^{0}\left(O_{6}^{0}-21 O_{6}^{4}\right), \quad(2.2)$$

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{K}_{0}^{\langle 110\rangle}= & -\frac{1}{4} B_{4}^{0}\left(O_{4}^{0}-20 O_{4}^{2}-15 O_{4}^{4}\right) \\
& -\frac{13}{8} B_{6}^{0}\left(O_{6}^{0}+\frac{105}{26} O_{6}^{2}-\frac{105}{13} O_{6}^{4}+\frac{231}{26} O_{6}^{6}\right). \\

\end{aligned}$$

The point-charge model values for the coefficientsare given by $^{19}$ 
$$B_{4}^{0}=\frac{7}{18} \frac{|e| q}{d^{5}} \beta\left\langle r^{4}\right\rangle,\qquad(2.4)$$

$$B_{6}^{0}=-\frac{1}{9} \frac{|e| q}{d^{7}} \gamma\left\langle r^{6}\right\rangle, \quad(2.5)$$
 where $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are Stevens $^{20}$ multiplicative factors, $\langle r^{4}\rangle$ and $\langle r^{6}\rangle$ are $5 f$ radial integrals, and $d$ is the uranium-oxygen separation $[d=\frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{3} a)]. q$ is the effective charge on the oxygen atom and $|e|$ is the magnitude of the electronic charge.
Actually the cubic crystal field as such plays a very minor role in our considerations. It is important that the cubic crystal field be such as to give a $\Gamma_{5}$ triplet ground state. As already mentioned use of the calcu- lated $\langle r^{4}\rangle$ and $\langle r^{6}\rangle$ values and the experimental lat tice parameter in expressions (2.4) and (2.5) lead to a value of the crystal-field x parameter, $^{16}$ determined by the ratio of the fourth-to-sixth crystal field, viz., $x=0.9$ , giving a $\Gamma_{5}$ ground state. Beyond supplying a I's ground state, the role of the cubic crystal field is quite minor. Specifically it comes in only in deter- mining the role of excited crystal-field states. So long as the splitting to these states is large, they change the predicted behavior only in a minor way from that predicted considering only the $\Gamma_{5}$ cubic crystal-field ground state; and the behavior is insensitive to the particular value of cubic crystal-field splitting.
To roughly estimate the cubic crystal-field splitting, we refer to the calculations of Rahman and Runci- man. $^{15}$ As yet, there is no experimental measure ment of this splitting. Rahman and Runciman calcu-lated a value of $2086 ~K$ for the splitting from the $\Gamma_{5}$  state to the first excited $\Gamma_{3}$ state. Satten and Mar golis $^{21}$ did point-charge calculations for the $f^{2}$ confi guration with a range of crystal-field parameters and obtained splittings in the range of about 1500 to2200 K. We simply adopt the Rahman and Runci- man value for this splitting. We note that this corresponds to an effective charge on the oxygen atoms of $-3.4|e|$ . This charge actually plays no role in our considerations. The effective charge of impor- tance is that entering the distortional crystal-field terms which we determine independently.

### B. Exchange-interaction magnetic ordering

We treat the exchange interaction in the molecular-field approximation. So the magnetic term in the Hamiltonian is given by
$$\mathcal{K}=-\lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle J_{z}+\frac{1}{2} \lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle^{2}, \quad(2.7)$$
where the brackets denote the self-consistently determined thermal average. The direction of magnetic ordering for each of the modes considered is shown in Figs. 2-5.

For the tetragonal distortion we take the direction of ordering to be the $\langle 100\rangle$ direction parallel to the displacement. For the orthorhombic distortion we choose the ordering direction to be $\langle 110\rangle$, again parallel to the distortion. For the Allen $^{4}$ internal distortion mode, the direction of ordering is also $\langle 110\rangle$. The tetragonal, orthorhombic, and Allen modes are all homogeneous. The effects of the distortion, and the nature of ordered moment, are the same at each uranium site.

The internal rearrangement modes are inhomogeneous. The longitudinal internal rearrangement (LIR) mode creates two kinds of sites, corresponding to an expansion or a compression of the surrounding oxygen atoms. Thus it is not sufficient to investigate the LIR by examining its effects at one site. We cannot write down a single-site Hamiltonian, as can be done for the homogeneous distortions.

We assume a simple two-sublattice model for magnetic ordering with the LIR. We consider the ordered moment to be within the $\langle 001\rangle$ plane perpendicular to the propagation vector of the magnetic structure. The moment is either parallel to, or perpendicular to, the direction of the oxygen displacements.

We can consider the surroundings of a compression site in the LIR, and take exchange interactions between nearest neighbors and next-nearest neighbors. In the molecular-field approximation the magnetic interaction at the central (compression) site may be written

$$
\mathcal{H}_{\text {Ex } C}=\left(4 \mathcal{J}_{1}\left\langle J_{z E}\right\rangle-4 \mathcal{J}_{1}\left\langle J_{z E}\right\rangle-4 \mathcal{J}_{1}\left\langle J_{z C}\right\rangle+6 \mathcal{J}_{2}\left\langle J_{z C}\right\rangle\right) J_{z},
$$

where $\mathcal{J}_{1}$ and $\mathcal{J}_{2}$ are nearest- and next-nearest-neighbor exchange constants, and the subscripts $C$ and $E$ denote compression and expansion sites. Here the three terms in $\mathcal{J}_{1}$ come from considering exchange to the four nearest-neighbor $E$ sites in the same plane perpendicular to the propagation vector as the central $C$ site, and to the four $E$ and four $C$ sites belonging to the plane above and plane below, going along that propagation vector.

Note that the net coupling to the expansion sites vanishes. Similarly, a central $E$ site has no coupling to nearest- and next-nearest neighbor $C$ sites. Thus, considering nearest- and next-nearest-neighbor interactions, we find that the magnetic behavior at the two kinds of sites for the LIR is independent.

The transverse internal rearrangement (TIR) mode also creates two kinds of uranium sites. This can be seen in Fig. 6 where we are looking down the axis of magnetic propagation. For uranium sites such as the two at the upper part of Fig. 6, the neighboring oxygen plane toward the top of the figure moves to the left and that below, to the right. For the lower uranium sites in the figure, these left and right movements of oxygen planes are interchanged. So, for the two types of uranium sites, the short and long diagonals of the distorted oxygen cube faces are interchanged.

In the absence of magnetic ordering, these two types of uranium site in the TIR are physically indistinguishable, i.e., with regard to crystal-field and elastic effects associated with the distortion of the surrounding oxygens. This leads us to follow the suggestion of Allen, $^{22}$ as adopted by Faber et al., $^{10}$ and take a four-sublattice magnetic structure in association with the TIR. In this magnetic structure, the moment at each uranium site is aligned along the short diagonal of the projection, onto the (001) plane, of the top face of the surrounding distorted oxygen cube. (In Fig. 6, the open and closed arrows denote $\mathrm{U}^{4+}$ ordered moments in two successive planes going down the $\langle 001\rangle$ axis.) The two uranium sites created by the TIR then remain physically equivalent, and all effects of this mode are completely specified by the behavior at a single uranium site. For the small distortions of interest to us, the direction specified by the short diagonal is essentially $\langle 110\rangle$.

### C. Elastic energy

We treat the elastic energy microscopically by using a rigid-ion model. We choose the microscopic elastic interaction parameters within this model by adopting the values used by Dolling et al. $^{23}$ to fit the phonon spectrum of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$. (Note that the tabulated parameter values given in Ref. 23 were subsequently corrected by Dolling et al. as quoted by Cochran $^{24}$ in his Table I.) A shell model does provide a somewhat better fit $^{23,25}$ to the phonon spectrum; however, we follow Allen $^{4}$ in not choosing to deal with the complications introduced by such a model. This seems warranted since, as Cochran $^{24}$ has pointed out, the two different sets of parameters for the shell model used by Dolling et al. $^{23}$ in their models 3 and 4 yield fittings of comparable quality. Thus it is difficult to take a particular choice of microscopic model for the elastic energy, or of parameter values with such a model, too literally. As discussed in Sec. IV, this unavoidable degree of arbitrariness in specifying the microscopic elastic interactions represents the main source of any uncertainty remaining in having a completely quantitative model for the magnetic and associated lattice behavior of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$. As will be shown, the predicted behavior for the nature of distortion found in $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ changes within the range of microscopic elastic parameters allowed by the experimental uncertainties in the phonon fitting obtained by Dolling et al. $^{23}$

In the rigid-ion model, the lattice energy between

two ions is taken to have the form²⁴
$$
V_{i j}=\frac{q_{i} q_{j}}{r_{i j}}+\Phi_{i j}\left(r_{i j}\right), \tag{2.9}
$$
where $q_{i}$ is the charge on ion $i$, and $r_{i j}$ is the separation between the ions. The first term is the Coulomb interaction, extending to all the atoms in the crystal. The second term represents a repulsive radial interaction, usually restricted to nearest neighbors of a given type.

In the fluorite structure there are three atoms in the unit cell. The space lattice consists of an fcc lattice with a basis of one cation $(C)$ and two inequivalent anions (denoted $A$ and $B$). Each ionic species separately forms an fcc lattice. Each cation is surrounded by 12 nearest-neighbor cations and 8 nearest anions (4 $A$ and 4 $B$). Each anion, $A$ or $B$, is surrounded by 6 anions of the opposite type.

To specify the rigid-ion model, we consider the energy per molecule of the static lattice to be given by
$$
U=-\frac{\alpha_{M} Z_{A}^{2} e^{2}}{a}+8 \Phi_{1}\left(r_{C A}\right)+6 \Phi_{2}\left(r_{A B}\right)+6 \Phi_{3}\left(r_{C C}\right), \tag{2.10}
$$
where $Z_{A} e$ is the anion charge, $a$ is the lattice constant, and $\alpha_{M}$ is the Madelung constant. (The cation charge is $Z_{C}|e|=-2 Z_{A}|e|$). Here $\Phi_{1}\left(r_{C A}\right)=\Phi_{1}\left(r_{C B}\right)$, $\Phi_{2}\left(r_{A B}\right)$, and $\Phi_{3}\left(r_{C C}\right)$ are repulsive potentials between nearest-neighbor cation-anion (C-$A$ or $C$-$B$), nearest-neighbor anion-anion, and nearest-neighbor cation-cation pairs, respectively.

The rigid-ion model is specified by adopting values for the ionic charges determining the Coulomb (Madelung) energy, $Z_{C}|e|$ for the cations and $-\frac{1}{2} Z_{C}|e|$ for the anions; and by adopting values for the short-range interaction parameters. These short-range interaction parameters are defined with respect to the derivatives of the repulsive potentials as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{d^{2} \Phi_{1}(r)}{d r^{2}}\right|_{r=r_{C A}} & \equiv \frac{e^{2} A_{1}}{2 v}, \\
\left.\frac{1}{r} \frac{d \Phi_{1}(r)}{d r}\right|_{r=r_{C A}} & \equiv \frac{e^{2} B_{1}}{2 v},
\end{aligned}\tag{2.11a}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{d^{2} \Phi_{2}(r)}{d r^{2}}\right|_{r=r_{A B}} & \equiv \frac{e^{2} A_{2}}{2 v}, \\
\left.\frac{1}{r} \frac{d \Phi_{2}(r)}{d r}\right|_{r=r_{A B}} & \equiv \frac{e^{2} B_{2}}{2 v},
\end{aligned}\tag{2.11b}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{d^{2} \Phi_{3}(r)}{d r^{2}}\right|_{r=r_{C C}} & \equiv \frac{e^{2} A_{3}}{2 v}, \\
\left.\frac{1}{r} \frac{d \Phi_{3}(r)}{d r}\right|_{r=r_{C C}} & \equiv \frac{e^{2} B_{3}}{2 v},
\end{aligned}\tag{2.11c}
$$
where $v=\frac{1}{4} a^{3}$ is the volume of the primitive unit cell.

The contribution of the repulsive interaction to the elastic energy of a particular mode is found in the conventional manner,²⁶ by expanding the potential to second order in the Cartesian coordinates of the displacements. This second-order term is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{1}{4} \sum_{l \kappa} \sum_{l^{\prime} \kappa^{\prime}} \sum_{\alpha \beta} \frac{\partial^{2} \Phi_{\kappa \kappa^{\prime}}(r)}{\partial x_{\alpha} \partial x_{\beta}}\right|_{r=r_{0}} u_{\alpha}\left(l \kappa, l^{\prime} \kappa^{\prime}\right) u_{\beta}\left(l \kappa, l^{\prime} \kappa^{\prime}\right) \\
=\left.\frac{1}{4} \sum_{l \kappa} \sum_{l^{\prime} \kappa^{\prime}} \sum_{\alpha \beta}\left[\frac{x_{\alpha} x_{\beta}}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{d^{2} \Phi_{\kappa \kappa^{\prime}}(r)}{d r^{2}}-\frac{1}{r} \frac{d \Phi_{\kappa \kappa^{\prime}}(r)}{d r}\right)+\delta_{\alpha \beta} \frac{1}{r} \frac{d \Phi_{\kappa \kappa^{\prime}}(r)}{d r}\right]\right|_{r=r_{0}} u_{\alpha}\left(l \kappa, l^{\prime} \kappa^{\prime}\right) u_{\beta}\left(l \kappa, l^{\prime} \kappa^{\prime}\right). \quad (2.12)
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $l$ and $l^{\prime}$ label the unit cells, $\kappa$ and $\kappa^{\prime}$ label the ions within the cell, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ label the Cartesian coordinates. On examining the right-hand side of Eq. (2.12), we see that the $u_{\alpha}$ can be put in terms of the amplitude of a specified distortion mode, and the coefficients involving the derivatives of the interaction potential can be put in terms of the $A_{\kappa}$ and $B_{\kappa}$ repulsive interaction parameters through use of the definitions in Eqs. (2.11). This then gives the elastic energy quadratic in the amplitude of distortion, with magnitude determined by the $A_{\kappa}$ and $B_{\kappa}$ parameters.

The contribution to the elastic energy from the Coulomb interaction is more complicated, since it involves summing over all the ions in the crystal. This is the same problem encountered in determining the electrostatic Madelung energy of an ionic crystal.

To evaluate the lattice sums entering into the electrostatic energy, we divide up the fluorite structure into sublattices. The fluorite structure is usually regarded as an fcc lattice with a basis of three atoms. In discussing the internal modes, it is more convenient to divide each fcc lattice into four simple cubic lattices, and to consider the fluorite structure to consist of an sc space lattice with a basis of 12 atoms (4 cations and 8 anions). In the three internal modes, the 8 anion sublattices are rigidly displaced.

In determining the electrostatic energy we must evaluate two kinds of lattice sums. The first sum involves the interaction of an atom in the unit cell with all of the ions on a different sc sublattice. We thus must evaluate the sum
$$
E=\sum_{\overline{l} \neq \overline{l}^{\prime}} \frac{q_{i} q_{j}}{\left|\overline{r}_{i j}-\overline{l}\right|}, \tag{2.13}
$$

where $q_{i}$ and $q_{j}$ are the two charges associated with the two sublattices, $\vec{\tau}_{i j}$ is the relative displacement of the two sublattices, and $\vec{l}$ is a sc lattice vector. The other sum encountered is of the form

$$
E=\sum_{\vec{l} \neq 0} \frac{q_{i}^{2}}{|\vec{l}|}.
$$

This sum corresponds to the interaction of a given atom with all of the rest of the ions on the same lattice.

We evaluate these sums using Ewald's method $^{27}$ to rewrite the lattice sum as a term involving a sum over the direct lattice and a term involving the sum over the reciprocal lattice. We thus have for the first type of sum

$$
\sum_{\vec{l}} \frac{1}{\left|\vec{\tau}_{i j}-\vec{l}\right|}=\frac{\pi}{v_{c}} \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\vec{g}} e^{i \vec{g} \cdot \vec{\tau}_{i j}} \frac{\exp \left(-g^{2} / 4 p\right)}{g^{2} / 4 p}+p \sum_{\vec{l}} \frac{\operatorname{erfc}\left(\sqrt{p}\left|\vec{\tau}_{i j}-\vec{l}\right|\right)}{\sqrt{p}\left|\vec{\tau}_{i j}-\vec{l}\right|},
$$

where $\vec{g}$ is a reciprocal-lattice vector, and $v_{c}$ is the volume of the sc unit cell, i.e., $v_{c}=a^{3}$. $p$ is a number chosen so that both sums converge rapidly. (In the numerical computations we have taken $\sqrt{p}=1 / a$.) We also have, for the second type of sum,

$$
\sum_{\vec{l} \neq 0} \frac{1}{|\vec{l}|}=\frac{\pi}{v_{c}} \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\vec{g}} \frac{\exp \left(-g^{2} / 4 p\right)}{g^{2} / 4 p}+\sqrt{p} \sum_{\vec{l} \neq 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{p}|\vec{l}|} \operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{p}|\vec{l}|)-\frac{2 \sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{\pi}}.
$$

For $\vec{g}=0$, both of these expressions diverge, reflecting the infinite interaction energy of a single sublattice. However, when calculating the total energy of the lattice, all of the $\vec{g}=0$ terms sum to zero, because the overall charge of the crystal is zero. The Madelung energy, per molecule, of the structure is then given by combining Eq. (2.16) and Eq. (2.15) as

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{c}=3 Z_{A}^{2} e^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{v_{c}} \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\vec{g} \neq 0} \frac{\exp \left(-g^{2} / 4 p\right)}{g^{2} / 4 p}\right. & \left.+\sqrt{p} \sum_{\vec{l} \neq 0} \frac{\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{p}|\vec{l}|)}{\sqrt{p}|\vec{l}|}-\frac{2 \sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right) \\
& +\frac{1}{8} \sum_{i \neq j} q_{i} q_{j}\left(\frac{\pi}{v_{c}} \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\vec{g} \neq 0} e^{i \vec{g} \cdot \vec{\tau}_{i j}} \frac{\exp \left(-g^{2} / 4 p\right)}{g^{2} / 4 p}+\sqrt{p} \sum_{\vec{l}} \frac{\operatorname{erfc}\left(\sqrt{p}\left|\vec{\tau}_{i j}-\vec{l}\right|\right)}{\sqrt{p}\left|\vec{\tau}_{i j}-\vec{l}\right|}\right), \quad(2.17)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $i$ and $j$ label the 12 sublattices.

We determine the Coulomb contribution to the elastic energy by expanding the Madelung energy to second order in the displacements. For example, if the anion sublattices are displaced along the $\langle 001\rangle$ direction (as in the Allen mode), the change in electrostatic energy is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E_{c}=\frac{1}{8} \sum_{i \neq j} q_{i} q_{j} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\left.\frac{\pi}{v_{c}} \frac{1}{p} \sum_{\vec{g} \neq 0} g_{z}^{2} e^{i \vec{g} \cdot \vec{\tau}_{i j}} \frac{\exp \left(-g^{2} / 4 p\right)}{g^{2} / 4 p}\right|_{0}\right. & +p^{3 / 2} \sum_{\vec{l}}\left\{\frac{Z_{i j}^{2}}{R_{i j}^{2}}\left[\frac{3 \operatorname{erfc}\left(R_{i j}\right)}{R_{i j}}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{3}{R_{i j}^{2}}+2\right) \exp \left(-R_{i j}^{2}\right)\right]\right. \\
& \left.\left.-\left.\left(\frac{\operatorname{erfc}\left(R_{i j}\right)}{R_{i j}^{3}}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \frac{\exp \left(-R_{i j}^{2}\right)}{R_{i j}^{2}}\right)\right\}\right|_{0}\right)\left(d_{i j}\right)^{2}, \quad(2.18)
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
R_{i j}=\sqrt{p}\left|\vec{\tau}_{i j}-\vec{l}\right|,
$$

$$
Z_{i j}=\sqrt{p}\left[\left(r_{i j}\right)_{z}-l_{z}\right]
$$

and $\vec{d}_{i j}$ is the relative distortion (change in $\vec{\tau}_{i j}$ due to distortion) of sublattices $i$ and $j$. In Eq. (2.20), the subscript $z$ denotes components of vectors along the $\langle 001\rangle$ direction of lattice displacement. The subscript 0 in Eq. (2.18) means that the expression is to be evaluated at the undistorted value of $\vec{\tau}_{i j}$.

Combining the Coulomb and short-range repulsive interactions, we obtain the following expressions for the elastic energies of the various internal modes:

$$
E=0+\frac{e^{2}}{2 v}\left[\frac{4}{3}\left(A_{1}+2 B_{1}\right)+8 B_{2}+4 A_{2}\right] \delta^{2}
$$

for the Allen mode,

$$
E=\left(\frac{77.50 Z_{A}^{2} e^{2}}{a^{3}}+\frac{e^{2}}{2 v}\left[\frac{4}{3}\left(A_{1}+2 B_{1}\right)+4 A_{2}\right]\right) \delta^{2}
$$

for the longitudinal rearrangement mode,

$$
E=\left(\frac{-38.75 Z_{A}^{2} e^{2}}{a^{3}}+\frac{e^{2}}{2 v}\left[\frac{4}{3}\left(A_{1}+2 B_{1}\right)+4 B_{2}\right]\right) \delta^{2}
$$

for the transverse rearrangement mode. We express the elastic energies of the tetragonal and orthorhombic distortions, respectively, as

$$
E=2 C_{11} a \delta^{2},
$$

$$
E=\left(\frac{1}{2} C_{11}+C_{44}+\frac{1}{2} C_{12}\right) a \delta^{2},
$$

where$^{28}$

$$
C_{11}=\frac{e^{2}}{2 v a}\left[\frac{2}{3}\left(A_{1}+2 B_{1}\right)+2 A_{2}+2 A_{3}+2 B_{3}+12.20 Z_{A}^{2}\right],\qquad(2.26)
$$

$$
C_{12}=\frac{e^{2}}{2 v a}\left[\frac{2}{3}\left(A_{1}-4 B_{1}\right)-2 B_{2}+A_{3}-5 B_{3}-21.62 Z_{A}^{2}\right],\qquad(2.27)
$$

$$
C_{44}=\frac{e^{2}}{2 v a}\left[\frac{2}{3}\left(A_{1}+2 B_{1}\right)+2 B_{2}+A_{3}+3 B_{3}-6.10 Z_{A}^{2}\right]-\frac{e^{2}}{2 v a}\left(\frac{\left[\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\left(A_{1}-B_{1}\right)-20.12 Z_{A}^{2}\right]^{2}}{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\left(A_{1}+2 B_{1}\right)+2\left(A_{2}+2 B_{2}\right)}\right).\qquad(2.28)
$$

### D. Model Hamiltonian

The model Hamiltonian assumes the following form:
$$
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{0}+\sum_{m n} C_{m n} \delta O_{m}^{n}-\lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle J_{z}+\frac{1}{2} \lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle^{2}+\kappa \delta^{2},\qquad(2.29)
$$
where $\kappa$ represents an effective elastic constant for a particular distortion mode. The other terms are as previously discussed.

As discussed earlier, the cubic crystal-field ground state is the $\Gamma_{5}$ triplet, and the excited states have energies about 2000 K, or more, above the $\Gamma_{5}$. Because of this large separation, we have essentially a three-level system. The primary effect of a distortion, or a molecular field, will be to mix the states within the $\Gamma_{5}$ manifold. Appreciable mixing between the $\Gamma_{5}$ states and the higher states is not expected.

We have determined the energy levels by diagonalizing the crystal field and magnetic Hamiltonian using both the full $J=4$ manifold and only the $\Gamma_{5}$ states. We find only small differences in the results. To obtain the same behavior of a particular mode (such as fixing the Néel temperature) requires different parameters depending on which manifold is used. However, once the parameter adjustments are made, the observable quantities, such as the moment and distortion size, are not greatly different. We therefore use the $\Gamma_{5}$ manifold to explain the behavior of the various modes. However, in the discussion below, we point out any differences that occur by using the full $J=4$ manifold.

## III. GROUND STATE

### A. Crystal-field and exchange energy

We first consider the self-consistently determined ground-state energy for each of the distortional modes shown in Figs. 2-5, considering only the crystal-field and exchange contributions to the Hamiltonian of Eq. (2.29). That is, for the time being, we do not consider the effects of the final, elastic, term in Eq. (2.29).

The ground-state energy of the various modes, as a function of $\rho|\delta|$, is shown in Fig. 7. The product $\rho|\delta|$ corresponds to the product of the effective charge entering the distortional crystal-field terms times the amplitude of the distortion. Thus the behavior shown in Fig. 7 gives the decrease in combined crystal-field and exchange energy with increasing strength of crystal-field distortional terms. For each of the distortional modes, the expectation value $\langle J_{z}\rangle$, corresponding to the ordered magnetic moment, has been determined self-consistently at each value of $\rho \delta$. The same value of the molecular-field parameter $\lambda$ has been used for each mode, and this $\lambda$ is kept constant as the size of distortion varies.

![](./images/814629640990621696_7.jpg)

FIG. 7. Self-consistent ground-state crystal-field plus exchange energies for "candidate" distortional modes in $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$.

Figure 7 is intended to show the relative behavior of plausible candidates for the actual distortion behavior in $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$. There are some additional possibilities involving increases of energy with distortion or nonmagnetic behavior, which we have investigated, but which are not shown.

For the tetragonal distortion, we show the behavior giving a decrease in energy of the magnetically ordered state with expansion of the surrounding cube of oxygens. As can be seen, this mode is relatively disadvantageous for lowering the crystal-field energy. In fact, for sufficiently large $\rho|\delta|$ ($\geq 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$) a nonmagnetic tetragonal compression will have a lower ground state, for a given $\rho|\delta|$, than the magnetic expansion state. However, this compressive nonmagnetic tetragonal mode loses out in the energy competition to others of the modes shown, and does not apply to the actual behavior of $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$.

Besides the compressive orthorhombic mode having magnetic ordering, an expansive mode can be considered. This does give magnetic ordering, but the ground-state energy is raised with expansive orthorhombic distortion.

For the transverse internal rearrangement, the behavior is shown for the moments aligned parallel to the short diagonals of the distorted cube faces. We have also considered alignment parallel to the long diagonal. In that case, the mode is nonmagnetic for $\rho|\delta| \geq 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$. For smaller values of $\rho|\delta|$, the state with alignment parallel to the long diagonal is magnetic, but the energy is raised by the distortion. In any case, for any $\rho|\delta|$, the state with moment alignment along the short diagonals has lower energy than that with moment along the long diagonals.

For the Allen mode, the moment prefers to align itself in the plane containing the four anions which approach each other, and the energy behavior is shown in Fig. 7 for that case. There is an alternative possibility, where the moment is aligned in the plane containing the four anions moving away from each other. This gives a situation analogous to that for the transverse internal rearrangement mode with moment aligned along the long diagonal. For small values of $\rho|\delta|$, the state is magnetic, but the energy is raised. For larger values of $\rho|\delta|$, the energy is lowered, but the moment is zero. In any case, for specified $\rho|\delta|$, the Allen mode with moment aligned in the contractive anion plane has lower energy than that with moment aligned in the expansive anion plane.

The behavior of the two versions of the longitudinal internal rearrangement mode are shown in Fig. 7. As discussed above, the LIR's create two physically different kinds of uranium sites, corresponding to the expansion or compression of the surrounding oxygens. We find that one site is favorable for magnetic ordering, while the other is not. Thus for the LIR with moment aligned parallel to the displacement, we find that the energy is lowered at the expansion site, but raised at the compression site. In Fig. 7, we show the average energy of the two sites. For small values of $\rho|\delta|$, the net change in energy is essentially zero. For larger values of $\rho|\delta|$, the moment vanishes at the compression site, and both sites now lower their energy with distortion. However, half of the sites are not ordered. For the LIR with moment aligned perpendicular to the displacement, we find that both sites remain ordered with increasing $\rho|\delta|$; but the energy is lowered at the compression sites, and raised at the expansion sites. At large enough $\rho|\delta|$, there is a net lowering of the average energy of the two sites.

For the orthorhombic contraction, the Allen mode, and the transverse internal rearrangement mode, the moment of the magnetic state decreases continuously to zero with increasing distortion. This decrease of the moment is a consequence of these low-symmetry distortions giving a singlet level as the crystal-field-only ground state. The energy splitting from this singlet to the first excited crystal-field state increases with size of the distortion. Thus as the distortion increases in size for fixed exchange, one goes below the threshold in the ratio of exchange to crystal-field splitting necessary for singlet-ground-state magnetic ordering. $^{29}$ The tetragonal expansion, on the other hand, does retain a saturated moment with increasing distortion. This occurs because for a tetragonal distortion the splitting of the $\Gamma_{5}$ state is into a singlet and a doublet, rather than into all singlets. For a tetragonal expansion, the doublet is low; and one has an ordered moment for all strengths of exchange relative to distortional splitting. However, for a tetragonal contraction the singlet is low, and for sufficiently large distortion one has a nonmagnetic state.

All of the mode behaviors shown in Fig. 7 display the considerable strength of the distortive crystal-field interaction. The magnetic undistorted ground-state energy is that of a system with a Néel temperature close to the $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$ transition temperature. Even rather small values of $\rho|\delta|$, especially for the Allen mode, can easily double or triple the ground-state energy. Thus the distortive crystal-field interaction is quite significant compared to the undistorted magnetic interaction.

If the ground state is determined using the full $J=4$ manifold, we find essentially the same behavior for all modes as that shown in Fig. 7. The actual value of the energy for a particular mode may be different by several percent for large $\rho|\delta|$, but the same relative energies of the modes is found.

As can be seen from Fig. 7, for the combined crystal-field and exchange energies, the orthorhombic contraction and the transverse internal rearrangement give approximately equal ground-state energies, with the orthorhombic mode having a small advantage at large $\rho \delta$. The Allen mode is most advantageous with

respect to lowering the crystal-field energy. The en- ergy of this mode, for the same $\rho|\delta|$, is significantly lower than the others. To find which mode corresponds to the ground state for given parameters in our model Hamiltonian involves including the elastic energy, and finding where along the $\rho|\delta|$ axis one is for the stable state for each mode.

### B. Elastic energy
In Sec. III A we examined the distortive crystal- field and exchange energies of the various modes. We now examine the elastic energies. This is done most easily by finding the effective elastic contant, $\kappa$ of Eq. (2.29). This depends both on the particular distortional mode considered and on the values of microscopic elastic parameters.

The values of microscopic parameters, as deter- mined by the rigid-ion model fittings of Dolling et al. $^{23}$ to their measured phonon spectrum, have significant experimental uncertainty. (This is also true of the shell model fittings.) These parameters are $^{23,24}$ (in units of $e^{2}/2v$):
$$A_{1}=23.0 \pm 3.6, \quad B_{1}=3.9 \pm 1.1, \tag{3.1a}$$
$$A_{2}=7.9 \pm 0.9, \quad B_{2}=-1.1 \pm 0.2, \tag{3.1b}$$
$$A_{3}=13.5 \pm 1.6, \quad B_{3}=-3.8 \pm 0.4, \tag{3.1c}$$
$$Z_{c}(\text{charge on cation})=(2.015 \pm 0.127)|e|. \tag{3.1d}$$

This range in the values of the microscopic parameter fitting in turn implies a significant range in values of $\kappa$, the elastic constant, and hence of the elastic energy for each of the possible modes. The value of $\kappa$ for the transverse internal rearrangement mode shows a particularly substantial variation as the microscopic elastic parameters vary over the permitted range.

This experimentally permitted range in $\kappa$ values for the various modes is illustrated in Table II. This shows $\kappa$ for the various modes for several sets of microscopic elastic parameters within the permitted range. The sets of parameters used in Table II start with the midpoint values of the parameters, i.e., the nominal experimental values, and show the changes in $\kappa$ on going toward the "soft" part of the allowed range. Although the listing of possible sets of microscopic parameters in Table II is not exhaustive, the general trends are clear. The transverse internal re- arrangement consistently has a much smaller elastic constant, i.e., is "softer," than the other modes. This softness, and the considerable variation shown for $\kappa$ in the TIR, occurs because the TIR the contribu- tions to the elastic constant from the Coulomb and repulsive interactions are comparable in magnitude, but opposite in sign. It is interesting to contrast the roles of the Coulomb and repulsive interaction contributions to the elastic energy in the Allen mode and the transverse internal rearrangement, respectively. In contrast to the TIR, for the Allen mode the Coulomb interaction, as shown in Eq. (2.21), makes no contri- bution to the elastic energy. This is because the ratio of oxygen-oxygen to oxygen-uranium displacements in the Allen mode is two to one, providing exact can- cellation in the Coulomb contribution to the elastic energy. On the other hand, for the Allen mode the repulsive interaction contribution is larger than that for the TIR. The anion-cation contribution is the same for both modes. This is so because the dis- placement of each surrounding oxygen with respect to the uranium atom is of the same kind in the two modes. However, the oxygen-oxygen repulsive in- teraction, as exhibited by the parameter $A_{2}$ , is large for the Allen mode; while the $A_{2}$ contribution van ishes for the TIR. Again this behavior is easily un- derstood on the basis of the nature of the relative motions, now oxygen-oxygen, for the two modes. In the rigid-ion model, the $A$ and $B$ parameters correspond to radial and tangential motion, respec- tively, of the interacting ions with respect to the un- distorted position vector connecting them. In the TIR, the only oxygen-oxygen motion is a relative shear. Thus $B_{2}$ is nonzero, but $A_{2}$ vanishes. For theAllen mode, in addition to such a shear giving a $B_{2}$  contribution, there is also a movement of some oxy- gens directly toward or away from each other. This gives a large $A_{2}$ contribution to the elastic energy.
Combining the elastic energy behavior with that previously discussed, and shown in Fig. 7, for the combined crystal-field and exchange energies, allows us to consider which modes are apt to be favored on considering all contributions, crystal field, exchange, and elastic, to the ground-state energy. The tetra- gonal distortion displays the largest effective elastic constant. This mode is thus unfavorable with respect to both the elastic energy and the crystal-field energy. The longitudinal internal rearrangement also possesses a rather large elastic energy. In Sec. III A, it was shown that the longitudinal modes are also disadvantageous with respect to the crystal-field ener- gy. We found that the orthorhombic distortion was slightly favored over the transverse internal re- arrangement mode with respect to the combined crystal-field and exchange energy. However, we now find that the orthorhombic elastic energy is typically three or four times larger than the elastic energy of the TIR. The Allen internal distortion mode has an elastic constant typically four to eight times that of the TIR; so it is disadvantageous for elastic energy. On the other hand, as discussed in connection with Fig. 7, the Allen mode is most advantageous for lowering the combined crystal-field and exchange en- ergy.
We are thus left with two favored modes. The com- bined crystal-field and exchange interaction clearly favors the Allen internal distortion. However, the elastic in- teraction clearly favors the transverse internal rearrange- ment.

### C. Determination of ground state
The equilibrium ground-state energy depends both on the combined crystal field plus exchange interac- tion and on the elastic interaction. The crystal-field interaction favors the Allen mode, while the elastic interaction favors the transverse internal rearrange- ment. The relative strengths of these two interac- tions will determine which mode occurs.
We first treat the determination of the equilibriumground state by considering effects only within the $\Gamma_{5}$  crystal-field ground-state manifold. If we include only the second-order terms in the crystal-field Ham- iltonian, Eq. (2.1), then the Hamiltonian for both the Allen mode and the TIR may be written in the form,
$$H=C \rho \delta\left(O_{2}^{0}-O_{2}^{2}\right)-\lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle J_{z}+\frac{1}{2} \lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle^{2}+\kappa \delta^{2},(3.2)$$
 where, for the same crystal-field gradient charge and the same microscopic elastic parameters, $C$ and $\kappa$ are different for the two modes. The axis of quantiza- tion in both cases is taken along $\langle 110\rangle$ . The crystal field distortional terms in Eq. (3.2) have a symmetry that, in the absence of magnetic ordering, satisfies the requirement that both modes possess energy lev- els which do not depend on the sign of $\delta$ , i.e., on changing the direction of all anion displacements in Figs. 2 and 3, while keeping the same axis of quanti- zation. The fourth- and sixth-order terms in the Hamiltonians for the two modes also display identical symmetries, and again reflect this requirement. We consider, however, only the effects of the second- order terms, as these are dominant.
The free energy of a system described by Eq. (3.2) is given by,
$$G\left(T, \delta,\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle\right)=-k T \ln Z+\frac{1}{2} \lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle^{2}+\kappa \delta^{2}, \quad(3.3)$$
 where $Z$ is the partition function determined using the eigenvalues of the operator parts of Eq. (3.2), $T$ is the temperature, and $k$ is the Boltzmann constant.
The equilibrium distortion and moment are foundby minimizing the free energy with respect to both $\delta$  and $\langle J_{z}\rangle$ . Minimizing with respect to $\langle J_{z}\rangle$ leads to the usual self-consistency condition on the moment.
The minimization condition on $\delta$ may be written
$$\left[\partial G\left(T, \delta,\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle\right) / \partial \delta\right]=C \rho\left\langle O_{2}^{0}-O_{2}^{2}\right\rangle+2 \kappa \delta=0, \quad(3.4)$$
 so that the equilibrium distortion may be expressed as a thermal average of quadrupole operators. The system Hamiltonian may thus be expressed in the

equivalent form
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{K}= & -Q\left\langle O_{2}^{0}-O_{2}^{z}\right\rangle\left(O_{2}^{0}-O_{2}^{z}\right)+\frac{1}{2} Q\left\langle O_{2}^{0}-O_{2}^{z}\right\rangle^{2} \\
& -\lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle J_{z}+\frac{1}{2} \lambda\left\langle J_{z}\right\rangle^{2},
\end{aligned}\qquad(3.5)
$$
where
$$
Q \equiv \frac{C^{2} \rho^{2}}{2 \kappa}.\qquad(3.6)
$$

Equation (3.5) has the form of a system with both dipolar and quadrupolar ordering in the molecular- field approximation. The behavior of such a system is determined by the ratio $Q / \lambda$. That is, the behavior is determined by the relative strengths of the quadru- pole and magnetic interactions.

The equilibrium (self-consistently determined) ground-state energy and magnetic moment of a sys- tem described by Eq. (3.5) are shown in Fig. 8 as functions of $Q / \lambda$. The ground-state energy is con tinually lowered with increasing $Q / \lambda$. Thus in com paring two systems with the same $\lambda$, the one with the larger $Q / \lambda$ ratio will have the lower ground-state en ergy. In addition, the magnetic moment is continual- ly decreased. For $Q / \lambda \geq 0.074$ there is no magnetic ordering even at zero temperature. There is, howev- er, an equilibrium distortion. The vanishing of mag- netic ordering for sufficiently large $Q / \lambda$ has its cause in the splitting of the $\Gamma_{5}$ level into singlets by the TIR or Allen mode. Large $Q / \lambda$ corresponds to large ratio of distortional splitting to exchange. Thus, as already mentioned in Sec. III A, for sufficiently large $Q / \lambda$ the threshold condition for singlet-ground-state magnetic ordering is no longer satisfied.

We find the same results if the full $J=4$ manifold is used. In this case we include the cubic crystal-field operator in Eq. (3.5). The complication is that the results do not depend entirely on the ratio of $Q / \lambda$, but also depend on the relative sizes of $Q$ and $\lambda$ with respect to the cubic crystal-field interaction. Thus, depending on the choices of $Q$ or $\lambda$, the moment may be made to vanish at $Q / \lambda \approx 0.07$ or $Q / \lambda \approx 0.08$ for example. The basic results, that the moment de- creases and the ground state is continually lowered with increasing $Q / \lambda$, remain unchanged.

FIG. 8. Self-consistently determined ground-state energy and magnetic moment as functions of $Q / \lambda$ for the system described by Eq. (3.5).

We now return to the question of determining when the Allen mode, and when the transverse inter- nal rearrangement mode, occurs as the equilibrium state of the system. Comparing the second-order dis- tortion coefficients of the two modes gives that [in the notation suggested by Eq. (3.2)]
$$
C_{\text {Allen }}=-2.5 C_{\text {TIRM }}.\qquad(3.7)
$$

That is, as observed earlier, the crystal-field interac- tion of the Allen mode is considerably stronger than that of the transverse internal rearrangement mode. Moreover, the coefficient $C$ enters the expression for $Q$ as $C^{2}$. Thus, in effect, the distortive crystal-field interaction of the Allen mode is $(2.5)^{2}$ times the in teraction in the TIR. We assume the same value of $\rho$ for both modes. Thus the elastic constant, $\kappa$, of the TIR must be less than $\frac{1}{6.25}$ of the elastic constant for the Allen mode, if the TIR is to have a larger $Q$. Such a relative softness of $\kappa$ for the TIR can occur within the allowed range of fitting parameters for the rigid-ion model (as seen, for example, in the last two columns of Table I). Then if we choose the micro- scopic elastic constants to be in that part of the al- lowed range, the transverse internal rearrangement mode elastic energy will be sufficiently smaller than that of the Allen mode so that the overall energy preference will be for the TIR. In doing this, we take the same molecular-field coefficient, $\lambda$, for both modes. (In fact, for a nearest- and next-nearest neighbor exchange model, $\lambda \sim-4 \vartheta_{1}+6 \vartheta_{2}$ both for the Allen mode with type 1 ordering and for the TIR with the four-sublattice magnetic structure of Fig. 6.)

In summary, the experimentally allowed range of microscopic elastic parameter values for the rigid-ion model fitting of the phonon spectrum permits either the Allen mode, or the transverse internal rearrange- ment to be the preferred equilibrium state, depending on the relative sizes of the two elastic energies. This demonstrates the sensitivity of the equilibrium behavior to the elastic interaction.

## IV. PHASE DIAGRAM FOR OCCURRENCE OF THE TRANSVERSE INTERNAL REARRANGEMENT AND THE FIRST-ORDER TRANSITION IN UO₂

We now discuss the detailed nature of the behavior within our model, including the possibility both of a transverse internal rearrangement lattice distortion and of antiferromagnetic ordering of the four-

sublattice-type shown in Fig. 6. For this discussion, we assume that microscopic elastic parameters have been chosen from within the allowed experimental range such as to yield the TIR as the preferred equili- brium state. We will show that the mechanism for the occurrence of the TIR discussed in Sec. III can yield a first-order transition with amplitude of the TIR close to that observed in $\mathrm{UO}_{2}$.

First, we consider the phase diagram for the transverse internal rearrangement mode, considering crystal-field effects acting only within the $\Gamma_{5}$ manifold. This gives the behavior of the TIR, as a func- tion of temperature, for various values of the ratio $Q / \lambda$. We include the effects of the fourth- and sixth-order terms in the crystal-field Hamiltonian, although their effect is small compared to that of the second-order terms.

The phase diagram for the TIR, as a function of $Q / \lambda$ and temperature, is shown in Fig. 9. We deter- mine this diagram by finding, for each value of $Q / \lambda$ and $T / \lambda$, the equilibrium (self-consistent) distortion and magnetic moment.

The phase diagram shows considerable structure. For small values of $Q / \lambda$ the ordering transition is second order, to a state with both magnetic ordering and a TIR distortion. For large values of $Q / \lambda$, the transition is also second order, and is to a distorted state with no magnetic ordering. If $Q / \lambda$ is not too high, this is followed by a second transition at lower temperature to a magnetically ordered state. Of par- ticular interest is an intermediate region, where the transition is first order, to a state having both a TIR distortion and magnetic ordering.

Operationally, the order of the transition, on cool- ing from the paramagnetic regime, was determined by examining the behavior of the free-energy curve for the ordered solution in the high-temperature part of the ordered regime. The transition was identified as second order if the free-energy curve for the or- dered state smoothly merged from below with the free-energy curve for the paramagnetic state as tem- perature increased. This gave order parameters, i.e., distortion or ordered magnetic moment, that continu- ously went to zero with increasing temperature. The transition was identified as first order if the ordered free energy cut across, from below to above, the paramagnetic free energy, with increasing tempera- ture. In this case the self-consistent ordered solu- tions abruptly terminate with increasing temperature (typically with fairly large value of the order parame- ters) slightly beyond the crossing. In the moderately large $Q / \lambda$ regime, the appearance of an additional, lower temperature, second-order transition was recog- nized by the continuous development, in the self- consistent solutions, of an ordered moment below that second transition temperature. The distortion was continuous through that temperature. The second-order nature of the lower temperature transi- tion could also be seen through the smooth merging of ordered moment and paramagnetic free-energy curves, when one calculated such curves for distor- tion fixed at the value pertaining to the transition.

The phase diagram in Fig. 9 is almost identical to that found by Allen. $^{5}$ (Of course, the distortion in the present case is the TIR.) Allen, in effect, considered only the second-order terms in the Hamiltonian. This again illustrates that the second-order terms are dominant.

We obtain the same type of phase diagram if the full $J=4$ manifold is used. The difference, as with the discussion involving Fig. 8 in Sec. III, is that the behavior of the system is not uniquely specified by the ratio $Q / \lambda$. Instead we must describe the system by the ratio $Q / \lambda$ and, for example, a particular fixed $\lambda$. However, we find the same kinds of transition behavior.

The discontinuity in the magnetization, and in the size of the distortion, in the first-order transition re- gion, depends weakly on the particular value of $Q / \lambda$. The discontinuity in the moment ranges from about 0.4 to 0.52 of the zero-temperature value. Experi- mentally $^{1}$ the discontinuity is about 0.5 of the zero temperature moment. This size of discontinuity corresponds to $Q / \lambda \approx 0.033$.

We now estimate the size of the distortion. We use a value of the transverse internal rearrangement mode elastic constant, $\kappa$, which corresponds to values of microscopic elastic constants, in the allowed exper- imental range, slightly beyond the threshold of soft- ness required to stabilize the TIR mode relative to the Allen mode, viz., we used the elastic parameters listed in column 5 of Table II. We take $Q / \lambda=0.033$ to fit the experimental moment discontinuity at $T_{N}$. From the phase diagram, $\lambda$ must be selected to be

The distortion and $\langle J_{z}\rangle$, for these parameters, as a function of temperature are shown in Fig. 10. Both the $\langle J_{z}\rangle$ and the distortion curves remain rather flat for a substantial part of the ordered temperature range.

The $T=0$ value of $\langle J_{z}\rangle$ is reduced from the saturated $\Gamma_{5}$ value of 2.5. Using the $J=4$ value of $g=0.8$, the $T=0$ value of $\langle J_{z}\rangle$ corresponds to a moment $M=1.9\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$. Experimentally$^{10,12}$ the value of ordered moment at low $T$ is $1.74\ \mu_{\mathrm{B}}$. However, $J$-mixing effects of the crystal field are not included here. In fact, in the Rahman and Runciman calculation, crystal-field parameters were chosen to give a $J$ mixing reducing the value of the $\Gamma$ moment from the value of $2.0\ \mu_{\mathrm{B}}$ pertaining to a $^{3}H_{4}$ multiplet to a value of $1.8\ \mu_{\mathrm{B}}$.

Experimentally, $^{10,12}$ the TIR distortion gives an oxygen displacement at low temperature of $2.6\times 10^{-3}a$. The model behavior, shown in Fig. 10, gives a low-temperature oxygen displacement of $3.9\times 10^{-3}a$. Within our model, with the particular constraints used for comparing the energies of the TIR and Allen modes, this value is close to the lower limit on the size of TIR distortion obtainable. This is so since the elastic energy has been chosen to be almost as hard as possible without the Allen mode having a lower energy. However, the size of distortion is rather insensitive to further softening of the elastic parameters. If $Q/\lambda$ and $\lambda$ are held fixed to keep the magnetic behavior unchanged, and $\kappa$ is reduced by some factor, the distortion will increase only by the square root of that factor. [From Eq. (3.4) the equilibrium $\delta\sim\rho/\kappa$; while from Eq. (3.6) for fixed $Q$, $\rho^{2}/\kappa$ is constant.] For example, if we used the smaller value of $\kappa$ given in column 6 of Table II, rather than that already used from column 5, the distortion would increase by only $8\%$. Thus the typical size of the TIR distortion is comparable to that observed experimentally. From Fig. 10, we see that the predicted discontinuity in the TIR distortion at the transition is about 0.5 of the zero-temperature value, i.e., the distortion discontinuity closely "mimics" the moment discontinuity. There are no experimental results available giving the distortion behavior at the first-order transition.

We obtain similar results using the full $J=4$ manifold. Again fitting the Néel temperature and the discontinuity in the moment, we find that the distortion is about $10\%$ larger than that shown in Fig. 10. Also, the low-temperature moment in the presence of the TIR distortion is slightly larger ($\langle J_{z}\rangle\approx 2.45$) than that shown in Fig. 10. However, the moment at $T=0$ with no distortion, implied by the value of exchange constant, $\lambda$, necessary to match $T_{N}$, is also larger ($\langle J_{z}\rangle\approx 2.55$) than the corresponding value ($\langle J_{z}\rangle\approx 2.50$) considering only the $\Gamma_{5}$ manifold. Thus, the distortion reduces the ordered moment in about the same proportion whether one considers only the $\Gamma_{5}$ manifold or the full $J=4$ manifold.

The balance of energy competition between the transverse internal rearrangement and the Allen mode, determining which actually occurs, is quite sensitive to the details of the microscopic modeling of the crystal-field and elastic interactions. We have noted that using values for the microscopic elastic constants given in column 5 of Table II, we obtain a $T=0$ amplitude of $3.9\times 10^{-3}a$ for the TIR compared to the experimental value$^{10}$ of $2.6\times 10^{-3}a$. If the same values of microscopic elastic constants are used to find the high-temperature value of $C_{44}$, specified through use of Eq. (2.28), we obtain a value that is 0.45 of the experimental value. $^{8}$ (The fact that the theoretical $\delta$ is larger than experiment is consistent with the theoretical $C_{44}$ being smaller than experiment. The departures from experiment being in these directions is not surprising, since to stabilize the TIR relative to the Allen mode, we have used microscopic elastic parameters from the "soft" part of the experimentally allowed range.)

We could choose to increase the microscopic elastic constants to match the experimental $C_{44}$. In fact, use of the nominal experimental values, given in column 1 of Table II, essentially does this. Then the elastic constant $\kappa$ used in determining the TIR behavior

would increase correspondingly as shown by the final entry in column 1 of Table II. (The $\kappa$ value for the TIR changes in a way almost exactly proportional to that in $C_{44}$. This comes about because these elastic constants correspond to physically similar shear mo- tions. The $C_{11}$ and $C_{12}$ elastic constants are much less sensitive to modest changes in the microscopic elastic constants, and the experimental values are well fitted using the microscopic parameters from ei- ther column 5 or column 1 of Table II.) Since the amplitude of the TIR goes as $\kappa^{-1 / 2}$ , this would give a decrease in the amplitude of the TIR to $2.9 ×10^{-3} a$ in very close agreement with the experimental ampli- tude.

The reason we do not choose to do such a fitting is that under the constraints of our procedure, dis- cussed following Eqs. (3.5) and (3.6) in connection with Fig. 8, for deciding whether the Allen mode or the TIR occurs for a given set of parameters, the parameters of column 1 do not give a soft enough $\kappa$ to stabilize the TIR relative to the Allen mode. Clearly the energy balance between the Allen mode and the TIR is too delicate to take literally all de- tailed results of a particular choice of microscopic models for the crystal-field, exchange, and elastic en- ergies. For example, the arguments developed fol- lowing Eqs. (3.5) and (3.6) for the relative energies of the TIR and the Allen mode involved assuming that the parameter $\rho$ giving the effective charge entering the crystal-field distortional terms is the same in both cases. For the covalent electronic struc- ture effects present in $UO_{2}$ , this is not necessarily so. Also, a different microscopic model for the elastic behavior could change the detailed behavior of the energy comparison between the Allen mode and the TIR. However, it does not seem warranted to go into the complications of dealing with another model, such as one of the shell models. This is so, because as already pointed out, in Sec. IIC, shell models with different sets of parameters give phonon spectrum fittings of comparable quality.

What we can and do point out is the essentially simple and definitive result of our survey of the en- ergetics of possible distortional modes for $UO_{2}$ . There are two primary factors in selecting out the "candidate" modes that actually occur physically. These two factors are that for the same size of rela- tive distortion, a mode should decrease the crystal- field energy more and increase the elastic energy less than other candidate modes. The maximum crystal- field energy decrease favors the Allen mode; the minimum elastic energy increase favors the transverse internal rearrangement; and so these two modes are the only serious contenders for the distor- tion that actually occurs. If the elastic parameters are soft enough, the TIR wins out; and when it does so, we predict an amplitude for the TIR quite close to the experimental value.
