# Edge effects on low temperature surface adsorption phases and phase transitions on finite-width square terraces

Alain J. Phares $^{*}$, Francis J. Wunderlich

Department of Physics, Villanova University, Mendel Hall, Villanova, PA 19085-1699, USA

Accepted 11 February 2003

## Abstract

A transfer matrix approach is used to conduct a thorough investigation of all possible adsorbate configurations or phases on terraces of various widths $M$ including edge effects, and limited to first- and second-neighbor adsorbate-adsorbate interactions. Figures showing the configurations that occur within this model are presented for $M=3$ and 4, and detailed tables provide the phase sequences corresponding to all possible ranges of values for the interaction energies. Higher width terrace results have also been completed and are available upon request. As such, the model calculations provide a useful reference for experimentalists leading to predictions of the values of the interaction energies from the observed phase sequence and the conditions prevailing at the transition between phases. A few experimental results are interpreted using the model. One suggests specific experiments, which would make the observations more amenable to direct comparison with the calculations.

## 1. Introduction

In a recently proposed model of low temperature preferential adsorption on the steps of long finite-width square terraces [1], lattice–adsorbate interactions on edge-sites were assumed to be different from that in the interior or bulk of the surface. The substrate consists of long terraces with the edge-sites being first-neighbors, forming an array parallel to the bulk-sites. Agreement was obtained with the experimental data satisfying the constraints of the model, such as adsorption of cobalt on silver(1 0 0) [2] and bromine on Cu(11 1 0) [3], which in Lang et al.’s notation for high Miller index surfaces [4] corresponds to Cu (s)-[10(1 0 0) × 1(0 0 1)]. Here, the model is applied to study the effect on the adsorption phases and phase transitions by changing the orientation of the edges, so that adjacent edge-sites are second-neighbors, for which experimental data is available. We have completed the study of terraces of width $M$ up to six atomic sites. We were also able to find a number of analytic extensions of these results for any $M$, which were numerically verified up to $M=11$. Here we are reporting the complete results for the first non-trivial terraces with $M=3$ and 4, and only provide the configurations of all possible phases for $M=5$. The discussion of higher width terraces is very brief and just sufficient to exhibit a sample of analytic

No a priori assumptions are made as to whether forces are attractive or repulsive, or as to their relative strength. By changing the orientation of the steps, many more structural orderings are observed, including clustering on the edges, domain walls, staircase patterns, square, and hexagonal patterns. The patterns are also found to depend on whether the width of the terrace is even or odd. As in previous investigations, we will show that the occupational characteristics at the transitions between phases are often found to fit exact analytic expressions in terms of the golden ratio. A method of conducting adsorption experiments is again suggested. (1) Record the structural ordering of the phases, as the pressure of the gas is gradually increased. (2) Determine the pressures at which transitions between phases occur, starting from empty to full coverage. When there is no surface reconstruction, and if first- and second-neighbor interactions are predominant, our model predicts a number if not all the interaction energies, adsorbate-adsorbate, and adsorbate-substrate at the edge-sites and in the bulk. Our results are compared with the available experimental observations.

## 2. The model

The general features of our lattice model and comparison with others have been described at length in a series of articles published in the past 9 years [5-13]. For completeness, the following summary intends to make the article self-contained, providing the appropriate references for those interested in pursuing the details. The long terraces considered are made of a square lattice, with the arrays of edge-sites oriented at $45^\circ$ with the arrays of sites in the bulk, as shown in Fig. 1. Adsorption takes place at sites forming a lattice commensurate with that of the substrate; for this reason we do not make any distinction between these two lattices. The notation used here is the same as that of [1]. The chemical potential per particle in the gas phase, $\mu'$, depends on the gas pressure. The adsorbate-substrate interaction energy at edge-sites is $V_\text{e}$, and $V_0$ is the adsorbate-substrate interaction energy at bulk-sites. Lateral interactions are limited to first- and second-neighbors with energies $V$ and $W$, respectively.

![](./images/812399721602613249_4.jpg)

Fig. 1. Square lattice terrace with edge-sites being second-neighbors.

Positive energies correspond to attraction and negative energies to repulsion. At thermodynamic equilibrium, $\theta_0$ is the coverage of the lattice, $\theta$ and $\beta$ are the numbers per site of first- and second-neighbor monomers, and finally $\gamma$ is the number per site of monomers at the edges of the terrace. Therefore the number of monomers per site in the bulk is $(\theta_0-\gamma)$, and, at thermodynamic equilibrium, the energy per site $E_\text{site}$ is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\text{site} &= \mu'\theta_0 + V_0(\theta_0-\gamma) + V_\text{e}\gamma + V\theta + W\beta \\
&= (\mu'+V_0)\theta_0 + (V_\text{e}-V_0)\gamma + V\theta + W\beta.
\end{aligned} \tag{1}
$$

It is then convenient to define the quantities,

$$
\mu = \mu' + V_0, \quad U = V_\text{e} - V_0, \tag{2}
$$

where $U$ is zero when the adsorbate-substrate interaction is the same at the edges as in the bulk. For convenience, $U$ is referred to as the "edge-interaction" and to $\mu$ simply as the "chemical potential." With $T$ the absolute temperature and $k_\text{B}$, Boltzmann's constant, the absolute activities associated with $\mu$, $V$, $W$, and $U$ are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&x = \exp\left(\frac{\mu}{k_\text{B}T}\right); \quad y = \exp\left(\frac{V}{k_\text{B}T}\right); \\
&z = \exp\left(\frac{W}{k_\text{B}T}\right); \quad u = \exp\left(\frac{U}{k_\text{B}T}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{3}
$$

Unlike the case for which edge-sites are first-neighbors [10], here the recursive construction of the transfer matrix, $T_M^1$, for a terrace of $M$ sites in width, and with $U=0$, involves eight block-matrices, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_M^1 &= \begin{pmatrix}
T_{M-1}^1 & xP_{M-1}^1 \\
T_{M-1}^2 & xzP_{M-1}^2
\end{pmatrix}; \\
T_M^2 &= \begin{pmatrix}
T_{M-1}^3 & xyP_{M-1}^3 \\
T_{M-1}^4 & xyzP_{M-1}^4
\end{pmatrix};
\end{aligned} \tag{4a}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
& T_{M}^{3}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
T_{M-1}^{1} & x z P_{M-1}^{1} \\
T_{M-1}^{2} & x z^{2} P_{M-1}^{2}
\end{array}\right) ; \\
& T_{M}^{4}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
T_{M-1}^{3} & x y z P_{M-1}^{3} \\
T_{M-1}^{4} & x y z^{2} P_{M-1}^{4}
\end{array}\right) ;
\end{aligned}
\tag{4b}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
& P_{M}^{1}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
T_{M-1}^{1} & x y P_{M-1}^{1} \\
T_{M-1}^{2} & x y z P_{M-1}^{2}
\end{array}\right) ; \\
& P_{M}^{2}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
T_{M-1}^{3} & x y^{2} P_{M-1}^{3} \\
T_{M-1}^{4} & x y^{2} z P_{M-1}^{4}
\end{array}\right) ;
\end{aligned}
\tag{4c}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
& P_{M}^{3}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
T_{M-1}^{1} & x y z P_{M-1}^{1} \\
T_{M-1}^{2} & x y z^{2} P_{M-1}^{2}
\end{array}\right) ; \\
& T_{M}^{4}=\left(\begin{array}{cc}
T_{M-1}^{3} & x y^{2} z P_{M-1}^{3} \\
T_{M-1}^{4} & x y^{2} z^{2} P_{M-1}^{4}
\end{array}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4d}
$$

The initial conditions are $T_{0}^{1}=T_{0}^{2}=T_{0}^{3}=T_{0}^{4}=$ $P_{0}^{1}=P_{0}^{2}=P_{0}^{3}=P_{0}^{4}=1$, and the rank of any of the block-matrices with lower index $M$ is $2^{M}$. Once matrix $T_{M}^{1}$ is constructed for $U=0$ (or $u=1$ ), it is generalized for any $u$ in exactly the same manner it is done in [10], where edge-sites are first-neighbors. For example, for the non-trivial case $M=3$, the transfer matrix is:

$$
T_{3}^{1}=\left(\begin{array}{cccccccc}
1 & x u & x & x^{2} y u & x u & x^{2} u^{2} & x^{2} y u & x^{3} y^{2} u^{2} \\
1 & x z u & x & x^{2} y z u & x u & x^{2} z u^{2} & x^{2} y u & x^{3} y^{2} z u^{2} \\
1 & x y u & x z & x^{2} y^{2} z u & x u & x^{2} y u^{2} & x y z u & x^{3} y^{3} z u^{2} \\
1 & x y z u & x z & x^{2} y^{2} z^{2} u & x u & x^{2} y z u^{2} & x^{2} y z u & x^{3} y^{3} z^{2} u^{2} \\
1 & x z u & x y & x^{2} y^{2} z u & x z u & x^{2} z^{2} u^{2} & x^{2} y^{2} z u & x^{3} y^{3} z^{2} u^{2} \\
1 & x z^{2} u & x y & x^{2} y^{2} z^{2} u & x z u & x^{2} z^{3} u^{2} & x^{2} y^{2} z u & x^{3} y^{3} z^{3} u^{2} \\
1 & x y z u & x y z & x^{2} y^{3} z^{2} u & x z u & x^{2} y z^{2} u^{2} & x^{2} y^{2} z^{2} u & x^{3} y^{4} z^{3} u^{2} \\
1 & x y z^{2} u & x y z & x^{2} y^{3} z^{3} u & x z u & x^{2} y z^{3} u^{2} & x^{2} y^{2} z^{2} u & x^{3} y^{4} z^{4} u^{2}
\end{array}\right).
\tag{5}
$$

In general, all elements in even-numbered columns are multiplied by $u$. An additional factor of $u$ is added to all elements in even-numbered columns in the second half of the matrix.

The partition function, $Z$, of the adsorption system on a terrace of width $M$ and finite length $N$, at thermal equilibrium, is computed from the knowledge of all the eigenvalues of the transfer matrix. The Frobenius-Perron theorem states that the eigenvalue of largest modulus of a matrix, whose elements are real and non-negative, is real and positive. This is the case of our transfer matrix. Let $R_{\mathrm{le}}$ be this largest eigenvalue. In the thermodynamic limit as the length of the terrace becomes infinite, $R_{\mathrm{le}}$ becomes the only contribution to $Z$, and $Z=R_{\mathrm{le}}^{1 / M}$ [14]. In this limit, the coverage $\theta_{0}$, the numbers per site of first- and second-neighbors, $\theta$ and $\beta$, and the number per site of monomers on the edges, $\gamma$, are obtained as:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\theta_{0}=\frac{x}{M R_{\mathrm{le}}} \frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{le}}}{\partial x} ; & \theta=\frac{y}{M R_{\mathrm{le}}} \frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{le}}}{\partial y} ; \\
\beta=\frac{z}{M R_{\mathrm{le}}} \frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{le}}}{\partial z} ; & \gamma=\frac{u}{M R_{\mathrm{le}}} \frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{le}}}{\partial u}.
\end{array}
\tag{6}
$$

The occupational characteristics of the terrace are specified by the set $\{\theta_{0}, \theta, \beta, \gamma\}$. Referring to phases, zero coverage or empty phase, $\{0,0,0,0\}$, will be called " $E$." Full coverage will be denoted " $F$ " and corresponds to $\{1,2(M-1) / M, 2(M-1) / M, 2 / M\}$. The entropy per site divided by Boltzmann's constant, $S_{\text {ent }}$, will be called entropy, and it is given by:

$$
S_{\mathrm{ent}}=\left(\frac{1}{M}\right) \ln R_{\mathrm{le}}-\left(\frac{E_{\text {site }}}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}\right).
\tag{7}
$$

All numerical calculations were done on the Cray J90 of the Pittsburgh Supercomputing Center using the EISPACK software for determining the largest eigenvalue $R_{\mathrm{le}}$ and its partial derivatives with respect to the various activities. The numerical accuracy achieved in computing the occupational characteristics is better than eight figures, and that in computing the entropy is better than six figures. Keeping the temperature relatively low, and for a given set of values of $V, W$, and $U$, all adsorption phases are determined by varying $\mu$. At low chemical potential, or low gas pressure, there is no adsorption and the terrace is empty, $\{0,0,0,0\}$. As $\mu$ is

gradually increased, adsorption begins to take place, the entropy increases to a maximum, then decreases and reaches a plateau, which lasts for a relatively wide range of values of $\mu$. If the plateau is at $S=0$, we have reached a perfectly ordered phase; if $S$ is not zero we have reached a partially ordered phase. After a series of peaks and plateaus, and beyond a certain value of $\mu$, the terrace is completely covered. The transition between phases is the point at which the entropy reaches a local maximum. As has always been the case in the past, the value of $\mu$ at which a transition occurs is numerically found to satisfy the condition:

$$
\mu=-V\left(\frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta \theta_{0}}\right)-W\left(\frac{\Delta \beta}{\Delta \theta_{0}}\right)-U\left(\frac{\Delta \gamma}{\Delta \theta_{0}}\right),\qquad(8)
$$

where $\Delta \theta_{0}, \Delta \theta, \Delta \beta$ and $\Delta \gamma$ are the changes in the characteristics between the phases on either side of the transition. Equivalently stated, throughout the transition region between phases, there is no change in the total energy per site $(\Delta E_{\text {site }}=0)$. In the boundary region between any two phases, the quantities $\theta, \beta$ and $\gamma$ are each linearly related to the coverage $\theta_{0}$, with the end-points of the line matching the occupational characteristics of the phases on either side, as verified in all previous studies. Therefore, these linear relations and Eq. (8) are additional checks of the validity of our numerical results.

The results obtained for terraces of width $M=3$ and the corresponding analysis of the available experimental data, are presented in Section 3. We are not aware of experimental data for terraces of higher order widths, which could be analyzed using our model.

The results for terraces with $M=4$ are fully presented in Section 4. Section 5 provides all the phases with their occupational characteristics and configurations for $M=5$, but without the detailed energy regions and sequences in which these phases occur. A brief discussion is also included on higher order width terraces. Section 6 is the summary and conclusion.

one knows whether this phase is the only one observed.

Then, the model indicates that it occurs if and only if $V < 0 < W$ and $U > -W/2$, which corresponds to the last row of Table 1. The characteristics of the transitions $E \to \bar{S}_2$ and $\bar{S}_2 \to F$ are listed in Table 3, which occur at $\mu_1 = -3W/2 - U$ and $\mu_2 = -4V - W$, respectively. As mentioned in previous publications, when the entropy at a transition is zero, this expresses the fact that at the transition there is a mixture of the phases appearing on either side. This is the case in the example being considered. Thus, at the first transition one should observe a mixture of empty patches and patches of $\bar{S}_2$. Similarly, at the second transition, one should observe a mixture of patches with full coverage and patches of $\bar{S}_2$. If it is possible to determine experimentally the pressures at which the transitions occur, this in turn will provide the values of $\mu_1'$ and $\mu_2'$, leading to the relationships: $\mu_1' = -3W/2 - V_\text{e}$ and $\mu_2' = -V_0 - 4V - W$. If in addition, the adsorbate-substrate interactions, $V_\text{e}$ and $V_0$, at the edge and in the bulk of the terrace are also known, one may then deduce the values of first- and second-neighbor interaction energies as $V = (1/6)(\mu_1' + V_\text{e}) - V_0/4$, and $W = -(2/3)(\mu_1' + V_\text{e})$. Since $W$ is expected to be positive and $V$ negative, one predicts $V_\text{e} < -\mu_1'$ and $V_0 > (2/3)(\mu_1' + V_\text{e})$.

Another example of adsorption on reconstructed Cu(1 0 0) leading to terraces of $M=3$ is the "Surfactant effect of oxygen in epitaxial $\gamma$-Fe/Cu(0 0 1)-O$(2\sqrt{2}\times\sqrt{2})$R45$^\circ$" [16]. This experiment shows that oxygen can be used to prepare the surface, which then stabilizes the epitaxial growth of the $\gamma$-Fe film. Still another example of preparing the Cu(1 0 0) surface is by adsorption of lithium followed by magnesium leading to Mg/Cu(1 0 0)-Li$(2\sqrt{2}\times\sqrt{2})$R45$^\circ$ [17]. There, the authors use a tensor LEED analysis to conclude that their "model 9" provides the best interpretation of the data. This "model 9" correspond to our phase $S_2$. Without additional information, one cannot set limits on the possible interaction energies as this phase occurs in a large number of energy regions.

The deposition of molecular oxygen on Ag(4 1 0) corresponds to adsorption on terraces of three atomic sites in width [18]. This is a molecular beam experiment, where the authors show that adsorption takes place first at the steps, and gradually in the bulk at 105 K. This is interpreted as the sequence, $E\rightarrow S_3\rightarrow \bar{S}_2\rightarrow \bar{S}_1\rightarrow F$. From Table 1, there are several energy regions for which this occurs, corresponding to repulsive first- and second-neighbors ($V<0$, $W<0$), and $U>0$; that is, adsorbate-substrate interaction at the step is larger than that in the bulk of the terrace. This is in agreement with [18] which reports a barrier activation energy of 0.25 eV on the step, and 0.60 eV in the bulk.

We have found references on structural changes of fcc(1 0 0) surfaces occurring during the adsorption process, creating grooves and domain walls with periodicity of 5 and 6 molecular units wide, such as ethanethiol on Au(1 0 0) [19]. While such a system may be treated as made of terraces with $M=5$ and 6, the experimental data could not be interpreted using our model. The remaining sections summarize the theoretical results for higher width terraces, showing a very rich variety of phases including domain walls.

## 5. Terraces of width $M>4$

We have completed the compilation of all the energy regions and phases for $M=5$ and 6, and obtained partial results for higher width terraces up to and including $M=11$. For $M=5$, there are 10 pairs of phases $(Q_{i}, \bar{Q}_{i})$, one self-dual phase $Q_{12}$, and five phases appearing without their dual, $Q_{5}, Q_{10}, Q_{14}$, $Q_{15}$ and $Q_{16}$, for a total of 26 phases, as in the case $M=4$. The occupational characteristics and configurations are shown in Fig. 5a and b. For repulsive first-neighbors, there are 8 energy regions for $W$, ranging from $W<V$ to $W>0$, with 98 sub-regions for $U$. For attractive first-neighbors, there are 16 energy regions for $W$ ranging from $W<-7 V / 3$ to $W>0$, with 178 sub-regions for $U$.

For $M=6$, there are 13 pairs of phases, 2 self-dual phases, and 26 phases occurring without their dual, for a total of 54 phases. For repulsive first-neighbors, there are 24 energy regions for $W$, with 462 sub-regions for $U$. For attractive first-neighbors, there are two energy regions for $W$, $W<0$ and $W>0$, with nine sub-regions for $U$. All of this information is available upon request. However, the given diagrams for $M=5$ should be a sufficient guide for the experimentalists, and gives some hints on how we were able to achieve the analytical extension for any $M$ of a number of phases.

Generalization of the results for higher width terraces have been obtained. Namely, preferential adsorption on the edges correspond to two phases: (1) every other edge-site is occupied, $\{1 / M, \quad 0, \quad 0, \quad 1 / M\}$, generalizing $S_{3}, R_{2}$, and $Q_{2}$; and (2) all edge-sites are occupied, $\{2 / M, \quad 0, \quad 0, \quad 2 / M\}$, generalizing $R_{11}$ and $Q_{6}$. These phases also appear with their duals. Cluster formation on the edges has also been found, namely $\{2 / M, \quad 4 / 3 M, \quad 2 / 3 M, \quad 4 / 3 M\}$, which is the generalization of phases $R_{14}$ and $Q_{9}$, as well as a number of phases with domain walls. For example, phase $\{(M+2) / 4(M+1), \quad 0, \quad 0, \quad 1 /(M+1)\}$ occurring on even width terraces is the generalization of $R_{5}$. It consists of a staircase of vacancies separating triangular patches of $p(2 \times 2)$. Phase $\left\{\left(3 M^{2}-6 M-\right.\right.$ $8) / 4 M(M-3),\left(M^{2}-2 M-2\right) / M(M-3),\left(M^{2}-2 M-\right.$ $2) / M(M-3), 2 / M\}$ for even-width terraces, $M>4$, is another example of a domain wall pattern. Fig. 6 makes the domain wall more explicit with $M=10$. For any even $M>4$, the periodicity of the domain walls is $M-3$. To give a flavor of some of the generalizations we have achieved, the following is a sequence of phases generalized for $M>4$ and

corresponding to repulsive first- and second-neighbors with $U<2V+3W$:

There are a number of phases appearing without their dual partners, and rarely does the sequence of phases in a given energy region exhibit particle-hole symmetry, normally expected for pair-wise interactions.

## 6. Conclusion

The model is applicable at thermal equilibrium and relatively low temperature (may be as high as 1000 K in some cases). The surface structure is assumed to remain unchanged during adsorption. However, in some cases, surface reconstruction occurs during the adsorption of certain elements, such as oxygen and alkali metals, adsorption on the reconstructed surface may then be analyzed using this model. The interaction energies are coverage independent and the only ones considered are: adsorbate–substrate in the bulk being different from that at the edges; and adsorbate–adsorbate limited to first- and second-neighbors.

Since no a priori assumptions are made as to whether forces are attractive or repulsive, or as to their relative strength, we have obtained all possible phases and the complete set of energy regions in which a given phase sequence is observed. This work has shown the effect of the orientation of the edge- sites of square lattice terraces. Unlike the previous study [1] of square lattice terraces with edge-sites being first-neighbors, when edge-sites are second- neighbors, a richer collection of structural orderings are observed. With the edge-sites being first-neigh- bors we only found square and rectangular adsorption patterns, and their various combinations. Here, we highlighted clustering on the edges and domain walls, and for the first time, we have observed conditions of certain transitions between two phases to depend on temperature. The changes in these conditions occur at a critical temperature, which depends on the energy region.

For a given adsorption system, while keeping a relatively low temperature, we suggest one gradually increases the external gas pressure and records, at thermodynamic equilibrium, the phases and condi- tions at which the transition between phases occur. The phases and the order in which they appear depend on the interaction energies. Thus, with this informa- tion, one can deduce the various interaction energies in the manner described in Section 3 for $M=3$. If there are experimental data available for square terraces with $M=4$, we have provided Tables 4 and 5 to allow for the analysis of the data and obtain some, if not all of the interaction energies, provided first- and second-neighbor interactions are predomi- nant. Should experimental data become available

for terraces with $M=5$ and 6, the equivalent of Table 4 and parts (a)-(b) of Table 5 will be provided upon request.
