# Autocatalytic degradation of white phosphorus with silylenes†

Wolfgang W. Schoeller*ab

Received 17th October 2008, Accepted 19th March 2009
First published as an Advance Article on the web 20th April 2009
DOI: 10.1039/b818396a

White phosphorus ($P_4$) is prone to undergo degradation by nucleophiles and is reluctant to do so with electrophiles. Silylenes possess a strong singlet character but at the same time bear a largely inert lone pair orbital at the silicon atom. Thus they predominantly react in a similar way to electrophilic carbenes. Due to the poor $\pi$-character of the P–P bonds in white phosphorus, the overlap with the empty orbital for the electrophilic silylene is less facile and results in a relatively large barrier for the addition reaction. The electrophilic approach of the silylene to white phosphorus is catalyzed by addition of a second $P_4$, forming a trigonal bipyramidal transition state geometry. Its stability towards fragmentation is essentially lower than that of the silyl cation. The entropy contributions for bimolecular *versus* termolecular reactions are discussed.

## Introduction

Strong nucleophilic anionic bases are able to degrade white phosphorus$^{1,2}$ as has been shown originally around the turn of the $19^{\text{th}}$ century by Michaelis and Pitsch$^3$ and Burgess and Chapman$^4$ in the reaction of white phosphorus with sodium hydroxide and sodium alkoxide. Reactions of this kind have been often repeated and varied since then.$^{1-2,5-8}$ Metal-mediated degradation of white phosphorus has also been widely studied after the discovery by Ginsberg and Lindsell of the first $P_4$ transition metal complex.$^9$ The degradation of white phosphorus has been summarized in pertinent reviews.$^{10-15}$ Some relevant species and pathways are shown in Scheme 1. Particular attention has been drawn recently to the reaction of white phosphorus with nucleophilic carbenes (I),$^{16-18}$ one silylene (II) ($\text{R} = 2,6-i\text{Pr}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_3$),$^{19}$ and also with Group 13 analogues of a carbene.$^{20,21}$

Two different routes for the degradation process were identified. In the case of the nucleophilic carbenes (reaction path $\mathbf{A}$),$^{16,17}$ a carbene, either $\text{NHC}^{22}$ [N-heterocyclic carbene], $\mathbf{Ia}$, or $\text{CAAC}^{23}$ [cyclic (alkyl)(amino) carbene], $\mathbf{Ib}$, add first to $P_4$, forming a triphosphirene species, $\mathbf{1}$. Further reactions with $P_4$ cause the formation of a $P_{12}$ cluster, $\mathbf{2}$,$^{17}$ which resembles a cut through the Hittorf phosphorus.$^{24}$ Alternatively, the silylene $\mathbf{II}$ reacts with $P_4$ in an entirely different way (reaction path $\mathbf{B}$).$^{19}$ In the first step, a bicyclobutane structure, $\mathbf{3}$, is formed which can subsequently react further to $\mathbf{4}$. On the basis of experimental investigations, the silylene $\mathbf{II}$ ($\text{R} = 2,6-i\text{Pr}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_3$) has been assigned$^{19}$ an electrophilic species while it is assumed that carbenes of type $\mathbf{I}$ are nucleophilic species. Thus, the experiments reveal a different reaction pathway for the silylene $\mathbf{II}^{19}$ as compared to the nucleophilic carbenes.$^{16,17}$ Interestingly, the strongly sterically encumbered silylene $(\text{CH}_2)_2[\text{C(SiMe}_3\text{)}_2]_2\text{Si}:^{25}$ as well as the silicon analogues of nucleophilic Arduengo-type carbenes$^{26}$ seemed to be unsuitable for $P_4$ activation, though the silylene $\mathbf{II}$ ($\text{R} = 2,6-i\text{Pr}_2\text{C}_6\text{H}_3$) reacts gently with $P_4$ at ambient temperature.$^{19}$

In essence, the mechanism of the degradation of white phosphorus by electrophilic and nucleophilic silylenes and carbenes is poorly understood. This fact prompted me to investigate the reaction path of the silylene $\mathbf{II}$ with the substituents $\text{R} = \text{H}, \text{CH}_3$ towards $P_4$ by quantum chemical calculations, to uncover the essential difference to its nucleophilic congener $\mathbf{III}$ ($\text{R} = \text{H}, \text{CH}_3$), the carbon analogue of silylene $\mathbf{II}$. It will be shown that silylene $\mathbf{II}$ prefers an insertion pathway, $\mathbf{B}$, in which the degradation of one $P_4$ is catalyzed by a second $P_4$. On the contrary, reaction of $\mathbf{III}$ is initiated by a nucleophilic approach, following pathway $\mathbf{A}$.

It is a non-synchronous process³⁰ and thus occurs in two consecutive steps. In the first stage of the reaction for the electrophilic carbene **5a**, electron density is transferred from the π-orbital of the olefine into the unoccupied p-orbital at the carbene ($\pi \to \text{p}$). For the nucleophilic carbene **5b** electron donation occurs first from the σ-orbital of the carbene to the π* orbital of the olefine ($\sigma \to \pi^*$). Both approaches are especially facile; since the π (π*)-orbitals occur at the same face of the olefine, they are directed towards the frontier orbitals of the carbene. A frontier orbital model for the carbene reactivity has been addressed to the olefinic 1.2-addition.³¹⁻³³ Accordingly, carbene reactivity has a dual property; a carbene is simultaneously nucleophilic and electrophilic.³³

For P₄ addition, the situation for the addition reactions is more complicated. Each corner of the tetrahedron can be represented by a pseudo-PH₃ molecular orbital system.³⁴ The relevant frontier orbitals of PH₃ are shown in Fig. 1.³⁵

The HOMO is derived from the lone pair (in z-direction) at the phosphorus while the LUMO refers to a degenerate set of antibonding orbitals. These stem from antibonding overlap of the pₓ and pᵧ orbitals with the s-orbitals at the hydrogens. The orbitals below (above) the HOMO (LUMO) are not shown here, the full orbital set is described in the book of Gimarc.³⁵ The local orbitals of a PH₃ unit can be used to construct the full set of molecular orbitals for the P₄ tetrahedron; the sets of local orbitals span the full set of molecular orbitals in T_d symmetry. The set $4 \times \text{a}_1$ (local) transforms as $\text{a}_1 + \text{t}_2$, whereas the set $4 \times \text{e}$ (local) transforms as $\text{e} + \text{t}_1 + \text{t}_2$. According to the calculations (RI-BP86/TZVP) the corresponding Kohn-Sham orbitals correspond to HOMO $-$ 1 6t₂ ($-7.5$ eV), HOMO 2e ($-6.7$ eV), LUMO 2t₁ ($-1.8$ eV), LUMO $+$ 1 7t₂ ($-1.5$ eV). Thus one can assign the HOMO $-$ 1 (t₂) to a combination of lone pair orbitals at P and the HOMO, e, to a combination of PP bonding orbitals. Alternatively, the LUMO provides (antibonding) p-orbitals which are perpendicular to the directions of the lone pairs.

Based on these symmetry considerations, an electrophilic (El) or nucleophilic (Nu) carbene could attack the P₄ tetrahedron at various positions, either at the corners (i, k) or at the edge (j) of the tetrahedron, as shown in Scheme 3.

An electrophilic (El) carbene could attack at the lone pairs at the phosphorus atoms (i) or the P–P bond (j). In approach i, one lone pair at the phosphorus tetrahedron will be coordinated to the electrophile, *i.e.* forming a π-complex of a silylene (carbene) with P₄. In approach j, the electron density of the P–P bond will flow to the electrophile and the P–P bond breaks with insertion of the silylene. In reaction path k, the nucleophilic carbene approaches the tangential, empty p-orbitals at the corner positions. This leads (*vide infra*) to the formation of a distorted trigonal bipyramidal shape at one phosphorus atom. Consequently, different approaches are possible (paths i and j for an electrophilic and k for a nucleophilic carbene or a silylene), leading to different products.

White phosphorus possesses almost no strain,³⁶ an early prediction that was confirmed later by *ab initio* calculations.³⁷⁻³⁹ Thus the P–P bonds possess little, if any, π-character since the resulting bonds are only slightly bent. Advocating the same approach as known for the carbene addition to an olefinic bond, the following approach paths are to be expected (Scheme 4).

The initial phases refer to an electrophilic (j, **6a**), or nucleophilic (k, **6b**), silylene (carbene) approach P₄. The σ-orbital refers to the HOMO and σ* to the LUMO of P₄. Note that the nucleophilic approach should be more facile since better overlapping empty, tangential p-orbitals are available (see also Fig. 1). A lower activation energy for the nucleophilic path will be established later in the discussion (*vide infra*).

The P-P bond is partially broken and the $\sigma$-orbital of the silylene binds to one phosphorus while the other phosphorus interacts with the p-orbital of the silicon atom. At the same time, a second $P_4$ unit adds to the silicon from the back as a nucleophile and a trigonal bipyramidal bonding geometry (TPB) is formed. For the silyl cation, this gives considerable stabilization energy. $^{40,41}$ Overall, the TPB formation would lower the activation energy for the reaction path towards $P_4$.

Furthermore, silicon is a higher element homologue of carbon in which the valence s-orbital is stronger contracted than its valence p-orbital. $^{42}$ Therefore, the valence s-orbital at silicon is reluctant to hybridize (orbital non-hybridization $^{43,44}$) and appears inert. This dictates an electrophilic behaviour for II.

### b The electrophilic approach

An accurate numerical description of the various bonding features at phosphorus and its corresponding clusters is rather difficult. It requires the usage of large, flexible basis sets as well as an adequate electron correlation treatment. $^{45,46}$ For the case studied here, the triple-zeta basis set of Ahlrichs et al., $^{47}$ and, for the electron correlation treatments, the BP86 density functional $^{48}$ as well as the MP2 approximation $^{49}$ were employed. In addition, single point calculations at the RI-MP2/TZVPP level were carried out. Details of the various computational procedures are given in the Theoretical section.

As a silylene compound, II with $R=H, CH_3$ was chosen, since a derivative of II (R = 2,6-$iPr_2C_6H_3$) was studied experimentally in the Driess group. $^{19}$ In the present quantum chemical study, II is compared with the hitherto unknown carbon analogue III. Other silylenes are known but, in general, they are substituted with bulky groups for kinetic protection $^{50,51}$ and become unreactive towards $P_4$.

According to the considerations outlined in the previous section, the following reaction paths (1) to (3) were first considered (Scheme 6).

In (1), the silylene (II) adds to one $P_4$ and forms a transition state (A) which further decomposes to 3. Alternatively, in (2), the silylene reacts simultaneously with two $P_4$ and forms a TPB 6c. It subsequently splits into 3 with expulsion of one $P_4$. Reaction (1) follows a bimolecular type, and reaction (2) a termolecular type of kinetic behaviour.

A particular type of reaction is presented in (3). First, a $\pi$-complex is formed (via type i interaction, see Scheme 3). Second, the complex C reacts further with one $P_4$ to form B, the TPB transition state geometry. Its decomposition leads to 3 plus $P_4$. Overall, reaction (3) can be considered as a pseudo-bimolecular reaction as it is known for Michaelis- Menten kinetics. Compared to reaction (2), it has the advantage of a lower entropy requirement to drive the reaction.

Pictures of the various energy-optimized transition state geometries, as well as those of the product are shown in Fig. 2. Two different stationary points for the transition states were obtained. In A, the P-P bond of the tetrahedron is already partially broken and the silylene adopts an electrophilic transition state, according to 6a (see Scheme 4). Adding a second $P_4$ leads to a transition state (B) in which the P(1)-Si(3) bond of the tetrahedron is essentially lengthened and the silicon atom adopts a TPB; a second $P_4$ unit binds loosely to the silicon center, 6c (Scheme 5). The bonding parameters chosen for Fig. 2 emerge from the BP86/TZVP level of sophistication. Similar geometries were obtained at the MP2 level of approximation but the additional $P_4$ is somewhat shorter $(\approx 0.05$ Å) bound to the axial silicon center.

Another aspect must be considered here. In the usual cyclo-propanation reaction, the electrocyclization leads from a double bond (educt) to cyclopropane (product). The shortening of the C-C bond results in approximately 15 percent difference between a double and a single bond (1.34 vs. 1.52 Å). In the case studied here, the results are different. At the given computational level (BP86/TZVP) the P-P bond length(s) emerge as 2.2 Å (closed $P_4$) but change to $\approx 2.7$ Å in the transition state geometry. This is an increase of $\approx 40\%$ in the transition state formation.

The full breakage of one single bond in the $P_4$ tetrahedron forms a biradical 7a, which can also be represented as alter-native zwitterionic structures 7b and 7c (Scheme 7).

In other words, the P-P bond breaking is a high energy process per se. $^{52}$

Electron density is withdrawn from silicon while negative charge is accumulated at P(1) and depleted at P(2) (for the numeration see Fig. 1). The energy barriers for the first step in the electrophilic reaction are summarized in Table 1.

The values in Table 1 relate to the reaction models (1) to (3) (see Scheme 6). The participation of a second $\text{P}_4$ lowers the overall energy barrier by $\approx -4$ kcal mol$^{-1}$. Thus, the pentagonal transition state geometry is favored over the tetragonal geometry by this amount. The calculated values emerge from a comparison of the electronic energies of the transition state geometries with reference to their individual components, $\text{P}_4$ and silylene (see Table 1). The MP2 treatment of the hypersurface yields somewhat lower barriers than the BP86 treatment. It must be noted that density functionals of various kinds are open to criticism with respect to certain outcomes in the calculation of structures and energies. $^{53-56}$ Overall, the free energy of reaction to $\mathbf{3}$ is slightly exothermic.

One may question whether the lowering of the reaction energy barrier by a second $\text{P}_4$ in the reaction is the result of a basis set superposition error (BSSE)$^{57}$ in the transition state geometry. Their explicit considerations did not show a substantial effect on the calculated energy barriers. $^{58}$ A further improvement to the TZVPP basis set (level C in Table 1) does not essentially alter the barrier. A vibrational analysis at this computational level could not be attempted due to the large size of the extended basis set.

The participation of a second $\text{P}_4$ considerably increases the free energies of activation. This is mainly due to the loss of the translational energy contributions, which are required to freeze at the same time in two molecules (in reaction (1)) or three molecules (in reaction (2)) within the transition state geometry. However, the cooperation of a second $\text{P}_4$ (under formation of the TPB species) lowers the (electronic) energy barrier $E_{\text{a}}$ for the reaction.

Reaction model (3) differs from model (2) in that, before the attack of $\text{P}_4$, a $\pi$-complex of the silylene with $\text{P}_4$ is formed. This can be done by weak interaction of the LUMO of the silylene with one lone pair of the $\text{P}_4$ tetrahedron (approach path i, see Scheme 3). The calculations indicate that such an interaction exists, but the coordination is rather weak ($\approx -$ 1.5 kcal mol$^{-1}$). The energy profit for TPB formation in the transition state is small in comparison with the bond energies of a TPB-shaped silyl cation. $^{40,41}$ Since reaction (3) is second order in overall rate, the entropy contributions for the transition state formation are less compared to the termolecular reaction (2). This was studied in detail for parent $\text{II}$ ($\text{R} = \text{H}$) at the BP86/TZVP level for evaluation of free energy contributions (see Table 1). Since the statistical analysis for the entropy contributions is evaluated for the gas phase but the experiments are performed in solution, an adequate evaluation of the overall free energy contributions is questionable within the given approach. A further inspection of the reaction coordinate by IRC calculations indicates that the product formation to either $\mathbf{3}$ is just completed by stretching of the axial donor bond from $\text{P}_4$ to the silicon center. It causes a shortening of the other axial Si–P bond and, finally, the geometry collapses into the product with departure of the second $\text{P}_4$ unit.

### c The nucleophilic approach
The silylene II mainly owes its electrophilicity to the inertness of the $\sigma$-orbital on silicon. It adds to the $P_{4}$ tetrahedron in an electrophilic pathway as it is usually sketched for electrophilic carbenes. If this assertion is correct, then III, the carbon analogue of II, could act as a nucleophilic carbene in a different approach. A nucleophilic pathway (A) has recently been observed for the addition of nucleophilic carbenes like CAAC and NHC to $P_{4}^{16,17}$ For completeness, the reaction of III with $P_{4}$ was also calculated. As has been known from our previous studies, $^{16,17}$ in the first stage of the reaction (nucleophilic attack), a trigonal bipyramidal conformation at one corner of the $P_{4}$ tetrahedron is formed. This refers to approach path k in Fig. 1. Various stationary points for the addition reaction are shown in Fig. 4.

III adds to the transition state (first phase of the carbene addition reaction) as a nucleophilic species, a (distorted) trigonal bipyramid (III-TS) is formed. In the subsequent electrophilic phase, the carbene twists with concomitant formation of a cyclotriphosphene unit 1. The latter can further react to a $(P_{12})$ system $2.^{17}$ A full report on quantum chemical studies of the whole hypersurface from 1 to 2 with NHC adding to $P_{4}$ will be reported elsewhere. $^{59}$ The energy parameters of the various stationary points for the reaction of III with $P_{4}$, i.e. the formation of the cyclotriphosphene intermediate (analogous to 1), are collected in Table 2.

Methyl cations are reluctant to adopt pentacoordination. $^{60}$ Consequently, a second $P_{4}$ is not expected to lower the energy barrier for the overall reaction. The investigations on II and III indicate that electrophilic versus nucleophilic attacks of a $P_{4}$ with silylenes or carbenes lead to different reaction channels. ### Conclusions
The addition reactions of a silylene (Driess type), and its analogue carbenes to P₄ were studied, the results can be summarized as follows:

(1) the energy barrier for electrophilic approach to the P–P bond in P₄ is sizeable. This is due to the fact that P₄ is less strained, and the peripheral bonds possess only weak π-character to facilitate the approach of an electrophilic silylene.

(2) The approach is enhanced by addition of a further P₄ to the transition state, with formation of a trigonal bipyramid at the silicon center. The bonding situation is reminiscent of the silyl cation but, overall, the formation of a trigonal bipyramid is much less pronounced. The electrophilic approach of the silylene is hindered by the inertness of the σ-orbital at the silicon.

(3) In contrast to the silylene, the corresponding carbon analogue prefers an entirely different pathway. The different approach path is dictated by the particular electronic situation of the P₄ tetrahedron. Preferentially, the first phase of the reaction is a nucleophilic attack at P₄ and leads to a cyclotriphosphene species.¹⁶,¹⁷

Our investigations have far reaching consequences. If the electrophilic approach of a silylene to the P–P bond is assisted by a second P₄, then P₄ can be considered as a nucleophile assisting the reaction. In other words, P₄ adds in an autocatalytic cycle to the reaction. Cooperative effects,i.e.the second P₄, assisting the degradation of the first one were observed before in skillful experiments on the transition metal-promoted activation of P₄.⁶⁴,⁶⁵ In the case presented here, the question remains whether such cooperation effects can be performed equally well with other nucleophiles, such as amines, phosphinesetc. Further experimental and theoretical investigations have to be dedicated to this aspect.

### Theoretical section
All calculations were performed with the GAUSSIAN suite of programs (G03)⁶⁶ and the TURBOMOLE 5.7 set of program systems.⁶⁷ Stationary points are characterized by corresponding vibrational analysis, the transition state geometries were determined by search procedures. Furthermore, the connection of the transition states towards educt and product were confirmed by IRC procedures.⁶⁸ The TZVP basis set⁴⁷ was used in conjunction with the BP86 density functional⁴⁸ or the MP2(fc) approximation.⁴⁹ The resolution of the identity (RI) approximation⁶⁹ was employed in combination with the enlarged TZVPP basis set.⁴⁷ This makes the calculation of large structures feasible in the RI-MP2 approach. The population analysis followed the natural population scheme.⁷⁰ The MOLDEN program packet was utilized for drawing the equilibrium structures determined in the computations.⁷¹
