# Solvent-Induced Bond-Bending Isomerism in Hexaphenyl Carbodiphosphorane: Decisive Dispersion Interactions in the Solid State

Silas Böttger,⁺ Marco Gruber,⁺ Jörn Eike Münzer,⁺ Guy M. Bernard,⁺ Nis-Julian H. Kneusels,⁺
Christina Poggel,⁺ Marius Klein, Frank Hampel, Bernhard Neumüller, Jörg Sundermeyer,*
Vladimir K. Michaelis,* Ralf Tonner,* Rik R. Tykwinski,* and Istemi Kuzu*

ABSTRACT: Previous reports in the literature describe that the crystallization of hexaphenyl carbodiphosphorane (CDPᴾʰ) from a variety of solvents gives a "bent" geometry for the P−C−P moiety as the solid-state molecular structure. However, a linear structure is observed when CDPᴾʰ is crystallized from benzene. Here, we report detailed spectroscopic and theoretical studies on the linear and bent structures. X-ray powder diffraction examinations show a phase transition of linear CDPᴾʰ upon the loss of cocrystallized benzene molecules, which is accompanied by the bending of the P−C−P unit. Studies on the linear and bent structures (i.e., X-ray powder diffraction, solid-state NMR, UV−vis spectroscopy, and IR spectroscopy) show significant differences in their properties. Investigations of the solid-state structures with density functional theory-based methods (PBE-D3) point toward subtle dispersion effects being responsible for this solvent-induced bond-bending isomerism in CDPᴾʰ.

## INTRODUCTION
Carbodiphosphoranes (CDPs), $R_3P-C-PR_3$, are formally carbon(0) compounds where the central carbon atom is stabilized by two neutral phosphine ligands.¹⁻² The bonding of the central carbon atom to the phosphine ligands is best described by donor−acceptor interactions ($L \rightarrow C \leftarrow L$). The two non-bonding electron pairs of the central carbon(0) atom with a coordination number of two show $\sigma$- and $\pi$-type symmetry.¹⁻³ This bonding situation, and the following theoretical and experimental studies (vide infra), lent crucial support for the Lewis structure description involving a carbon(0) atom that was first postulated by Kaska et al. and commented by Schmidbaur.⁴⁻⁵ However, CDPs and their notation were previously part of a controversial discussion.⁶

Hexaphenyl carbodiphosphorane (CDPᴾʰ) was the first CDP and was synthesized in 1961 by Ramírez and coworkers.⁷ In the following years, the thriving chemistry of CDPs was developed by Ramírez, Matthews,⁸ Schmidbaur,⁹ Appel,¹⁰ and Bestmann.¹¹ They synthesized various types of CDPs and examined the reactivity of CDPs toward electrophiles.¹² Since the 1990s, the understanding of CDPs possessing peculiar double-donor properties has revitalized the chemistry of carbodiphosphoranes.¹³ They were frequently used as strong donor ligands in coordination and organometallic chemistry.¹⁴⁻¹⁶ For example, a series of CDP adducts with main group Lewis acids¹⁷ and complexes with late transition metals¹⁸ were synthesized where they acted as two- or four-electron donor ligands.¹⁹⁻²² Furthermore, CDPs were applied in homogeneous catalysis²³ and in frustrated Lewis pair chemistry.²⁴

The first solid-state molecular structure of CDPᴾʰ based on X-ray crystallography was reported in 1971 by Vincent and Wheatly, revealing a significantly bent geometry with P−C−P angles of $130.1(6)^\circ$ and $143.8(6)^\circ$ for the two independent CDPᴾʰ molecules in the unit cell (structure B).²⁵⁻²⁶ A few years later, a second solid-state structure of CDPᴾʰ was described, which also featured a bent molecule with a P−C−P angle of $131.7(3)^\circ$ (structure A).²⁷⁻²⁸ Based on theoretical investigations at the density functional theory (DFT) level, the optimized geometry of CDPᴾʰ shows a P−C−P angle of $136.9^\circ$, which is in good agreement with the known experimental values.²⁹ Thus, experimental results concur with theoretical findings that a bent motif dominates the molecular structures of CDPs.²⁹ It should be noted, however, that previous calculations also indicated a shallow bending potential

This shallow potential is a general phenomenon found for carbones and related compounds and culminates in the quasilinear structures of carbon suboxide ($\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$ or $\text{C(CO)}_2$).$^{29-31}$ In 2017, Parkin and Quinlivan reported a new structure of $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$ with a linear $\text{P}-\text{C}-\text{P}$ unit (structure C), which they obtained when using benzene as a solvent for recrystallization. They emphasize that the "coordination geometry at zerovalent carbon may be very flexible", which they supported by DFT calculations.$^{32}$

Until the work of Parkin and Quinlivan, only one experimentally characterized example of a CDP derivative with a linear $\text{P}-\text{C}-\text{P}$ unit ($180.0^\circ$) was known, namely hexakis(dimethylamino)-carbodiphosphorane ($\text{CDP}^{\text{NMe2}}$), which was reported by Appel and co-workers.$^{33}$ DFT calculations (BP86/def-SVP) suggested a value of $175.2^\circ$ for this molecule.$^{34}$ During our experimental work with CDPs, we also obtained a linear structure of $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$ (structure C) when using benzene as a solvent. Therefore, we decided to systematically study the influence of various solvents on the crystallized structures of $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$. Our work confirms that the linear isomer is only obtained upon crystallization from benzene. The structural and spectroscopic properties of the various $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$ structures were examined by solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), infrared (IR), and ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopies. In combination with the extensive spectroscopic and computational analysis, we can now provide a rationale for the solvent-induced bond-bending isomerism in $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$.$^{35}$

## EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

**General Considerations.** $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$ was prepared according to literature procedures.$^{36,37}$ All recrystallizations of $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$ were performed under an atmosphere of dry argon using standard Schlenk techniques. In the case where reagent grade quality solvents were not used, the solvent was dried by literature methods,$^{38}$ distilled prior to use, and stored over molecular sieves (3 or 4 Å).

**X-ray Determination.** Although structures A and C were reported previously, we provided our crystallographic data for A and C to show that we obtained them from our genuine work. Single crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction were obtained by cooling hot saturated solutions to room temperature. Relevant data concerning crystallographic data, data collection, and refinement details are summarized in the Supporting Information (Table S4). Data were collected at 100(2) K with a STOE IPDS II diffractometer (CCDC 1982549–1982553 and 1982555) or D8 Quest Bruker diffractometer (CCDC 1982554) using Mo K$\alpha$ radiation ($\lambda = 71.073$ pm) and $\omega$-scan rotation. The structures were solved by direct methods, and the refinement of all non-hydrogen atoms was performed with SHELX-2014 and SHELXL-2018 software.$^{39}$ All non-hydrogen atoms were refined with anisotropic thermal parameters. Hydrogen atom positions were calculated based on idealized positions using a riding model.

Data for CCDC 1980395 and 1980396 were collected with a SuperNova (Dual, Cu at zero, Atlas) diffractometer. During data collection, the crystal was kept at 173 K (CCDC 1980396) and 153 K (CCDC 1980395). Using Olex2,$^{40}$ the structures were solved with the ShelXS$^{41}$ structure solution program using Direct Methods and refined with the SHELXL$^{39}$ refinement package using least-squares minimization. Data for CCDC 1973814 were collected with a Bruker D8/APEX II CCD diffractometer at 173 K (programs used for diffractometer operation, data collection, data reduction, and absorption correction were those supplied by Bruker). The structure was solved with the ShelXT-2014$^{42}$ structure solution program using intrinsic phasing and refined with the ShelXT$^{39}$ refinement package using least-squares minimization. The structure figures were generated with ORTEP$^{43}$ and Mercury software.$^{44}$

**X-ray Powder Diffraction.** Powder patterns, as shown, were recorded on a STADI MP (STOE Darmstadt) powder diffractometer with Cu K$\alpha_1$ radiation and $\lambda = 1.54056$ Å at room temperature.

**IR Spectroscopy.** IR spectra were recorded with a Bruker Tensor 37 spectroscope with a Platinum ATR (attenuated total reflectance) accessory.

**UV–Vis Spectroscopy.** Optical absorption spectra of the solid samples were recorded on a Varian Cary 5000 UV–vis/NIR spectrometer employing a Praying Mantis accessory (Harrick) in the range of 200–800 nm in the diffuse reflectance mode. The samples were prepared under argon and then placed into the UV–vis beam. Solution optical absorption spectra were recorded on a Varian Cary 5000 UV–vis/NIR spectrometer employing a double beam technique in the range of 200–800 nm. The samples were measured as solutions in cuvettes (1 cm), which were prepared using Schlenk techniques under argon and then placed into the UV–vis beam.

**Solid-State NMR Spectroscopy.** Solid-state $^{31}$P NMR spectra of the samples were acquired on a Bruker Avance 500 NMR spectrometer operating at 202.5 MHz for $^{31}$P. Solid-state $^{31}$P NMR spectra were acquired with cross-polarization and magic-angle spinning at 5.0 or 8.0 kHz using a $^1$H $45^\circ$ pulse of $2.0\ \mu\text{s}$ and a contact time of 2.0 ms; the recycle delays were 100 (linear sample, structure C) or 120 s (bent sample, structure A). Between 24 and 396 transients were collected for these $^{31}$P NMR spectra, which were referenced to 85% $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ ($\delta = 0$ ppm) by setting the isotropic peak of ammonium dihydrogen phosphate to 0.81 ppm. All solid-state $^{31}$P NMR data were acquired using a two-pulse phase-modulated (TPPM) $^1$H decoupling field of 62.5 kHz.$^{45}$

**Computational Details. Solid-State NMR.** DFT-calculated phosphorus chemical shifts were obtained using the Gaussian 16 suite of programs.$^{46}$ Becke's three-parameter hybrid functional$^{47}$ and the correlation functional of Lee, Yang, and Parr$^{48}$ (i.e., B3LYP) were used with the triple $\zeta$ basis set cc-pVTZ that was included with the Gaussian 16 package. Structures used for the calculations were those obtained from X-ray diffraction apart from the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bond lengths, which were fixed at $1.08$ Å. Calculated $^{31}$P shieldings were converted to chemical shifts according to $\delta_{\text{calc}} = 328.35 - \sigma_{\text{calc}}$, where 328.35 is the absolute shielding value for phosphoric acid as reported by Jameson et al.$^{49}$

**Structural Optimization and Bonding Analysis.** Structural optimization of the molecular crystal of $\text{CDP}^{\text{Ph}}$ was performed with DFT starting from the experimental structures and using periodic boundary conditions as implemented in the VASP package (ver. 5.4.1).$^{50}$ The plane-wave expansion of the Bloch functions was truncated at an energy cutoff of 500 eV using the projector-augmented wave (PAW)$^{51}$ approach with standard PAWs and an "accurate" precision.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Single-Crystal X-ray Studies. We used CDP<sup>Ph</sup> as a synthon³⁷˙⁵⁸ as well as a donor ligand in organic and organometallic syntheses.⁵⁹ While conducting these studies, samples of CDP<sup>Ph</sup> are commonly crystallized, giving X-ray-quality crystals. For example, we often obtained single crystals from these mixtures within the NMR tube when monitoring reactions by in situ NMR spectroscopy. X-ray diffraction studies regularly yielded the known molecular structure of CDP<sup>Ph</sup> with a bent P−C−P moiety (131.7(3)°) in the orthorhombic crystal system (P2₁2₁2₁, structure A).²⁷ When crystallized from benzene, however, the linear structure of CDP<sup>Ph</sup> was obtained, which crystallized in the trigonal crystal system R$\overline{3}$ (structure C) as described by Parkin and Quinlivan in 2017.³² Interestingly, structure C appeared only to arise when either C₆D₆ or C₆H₆ were present as a solvent. Structure C was also obtained through the crystallization of CDP<sup>Ph</sup> from a mixture of 1,3,5-trifluorobenzene/C₆D₆ (1:1). The interesting selectivity found for benzene prompted us to examine the crucial parameters that lead to a linear conformation of the P−C−P unit in CDP<sup>Ph</sup> as well as possible solvent effects. Switching to an alternative aromatic solvent, toluene, gave three different bent conformers structures: A and D (triclinic, P-1) and structure E (monoclinic, C2/c) (Table S1). Interestingly, the P−C(0) and P−C<sup>Ph</sup> bond lengths of structures A, D, and E are essentially the same, but the structures differ slightly in the P−C−P bond angles (Table 2). Crystallization from 1,2-dichlorobenzene and 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene (mesitylene) gave structure A, while chlorobenzene afforded both structures A and D (Tables S2 and S3). Attempts to obtain crystals from hexafluorobenzene led only to decomposition products. In non-aromatic solvents, structure B crystallized from diglyme either at room temperature (two independent CDP<sup>Ph</sup> molecules in the monoclinic crystal system C2, Table 2) or upon slow cooling from a boiling solution.²⁵ Structure A was obtained either at a low temperature (−160 °C) or upon slow cooling from diglyme from a boiling solution.²⁷ Crystallization from acetonitrile yielded either bent structure A or E, while structure A also resulted from cyclohexane (Tables S2 and S3).

The experimentally determined P−C(0) and P−C<sup>Ph</sup> bond lengths for bent CDP<sup>Ph</sup> correspond well with the calculated values (BP86/SVP).¹˙²⁹ The calculated P−C−P bond angle in

the equilibrium bent structure is $136.9^\circ$. The calculated energy that is necessary to expand the angle of CDP$^\text{Ph}$ in the gas phase from the equilibrium bent structure to the linear form (P$-$C$-$P $180^\circ$) is quite small and amounts only to $3.1$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ (Table 1).$^{29}$ The main difference between the bent and linear molecular structures of CDP$^\text{Ph}$ is the P$-$C(0) bond length. In linear structure C, the P$-$C(0) bond is shorter by ca. 3 pm (Figure S1). Weak H$\cdots$C contacts (273.7 pm) between the $o$-phenyl protons and the C(0) center are also present (vide infra).

A close examination of the crystal structure of linear structure C shows that the co-crystallized benzene molecules seem to play a crucial role in forming this conformer. Intermolecular C$\cdots$H contacts between the CDP$^\text{Ph}$ phenyl rings and the benzene molecule were observed (Figure S1); however, from a distance criterion they were deemed to be too long (>300 pm) for consideration as a stabilizing CH/$\pi$ effect, $^{60}$ although previous investigations have shown that many of these interactions, albeit small in contribution, can add up to a significant energy contribution. $^{61}$ One benzene molecule aligns three CDP$^\text{Ph}$ molecules in a virtual $C_3$ symmetry (Figure 1). As described above, attempts to obtain the linear conformer from solvents with a virtual $C_3$ symmetry, e.g., 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene (mesitylene) and cyclohexane, gave only CDP$^\text{Ph}$ structure A, while a combination of 1,3,5-trifluorobenzene and $d_6$-benzene (1:1) resulted in the linear form structure C (as observed for pure benzene).$^{62}$

X-ray Powder Diffraction. During analyses of the crystalline CDP$^\text{Ph}$ fractions by powder diffraction measurements, some curious observations were made. Three crystal phases of CDP$^\text{Ph}$ were examined: orthorhombic structure A, trigonal structure C, and triclinic structure D. Structure A gave a powder diffractogram that is in very good agreement with the corresponding simulated powder diffraction pattern from single crystal X-ray data (1980395, Figure S4). During the first measurement with crystals of structure C, we were able to detect the partial desolvation of the crystals, resulting in a second crystal phase. This is a phase transition from the trigonal phase of structure C to the monoclinic phase of structure B (C2 space group, Table 2) that occurs due to the loss of benzene molecules. Kaska and co-workers also observed a phase transition for crystals of CDP$^\text{Ph}$. However, they stated that upon "standing for a long period of time at room temperature", monoclinic CDP$^\text{Ph}$ crystals slowly converted into crystals in the orthorhombic phase (i.e., structure B$\rightarrow$A).$^{27}$ In our experiment, the crystalline sample was carefully dried in a stream of argon to vaporize the remaining liquid benzene prior to the powder diffraction measurement. This drying process probably induced the release of some co-crystallized benzene molecules. Hence, two crystal phases are present in the corresponding powder diffractogram (structure C$\rightarrow$B, Figure 2). This phase transition can be prevented by using single crystals directly from the mother liquor without further drying. In this case, the measured and simulated X-ray powder diffraction data are in good agreement (1982550, Figure S5). Only a small amorphous fraction was present, which was probably due to the recrystallization and preparation processes of the sample. Crystals of structure D, on the other hand, gave powder diffractograms of low quality and with only a few very intense reflections.$^{63}$ Recrystallization from toluene did not improve crystal quality and yielded structure A.$^{64}$

The simulated data in red correspond to the linear CDP$^\text{Ph}$ structure C (trigonal), and the simulated data in dark green correspond to the bent structure B (monoclinic, C2).

In summary, these results show that the different crystal phases can readily interconvert either in solution during recrystallization (structure D$\rightarrow$A) or in the solid state by desolvation (structure C$\rightarrow$B). It is therefore important to identify the correct crystal phase of the CDP$^\text{Ph}$ single crystals at hand before further characterization.

Based on the solid-state characteristics of the different isomers of CDP$^\text{Ph}$ (Table 3), structures A, C, and D/E should be spectroscopically distinguishable. In the following sections, spectroscopic properties of the three most common crystal phases of CDP$^\text{Ph}$ are described: orthorhombic (structure A), trigonal (structure C), and triclinic (structure D). Prior to analysis, the crystal phases were unambiguously identified by powder or single crystal X-ray diffraction analysis (i.e., the determination of the unit cell parameters).

UV-Vis Spectroscopy. The electronic absorption properties of the different crystal phases were investigated by UV$-$vis spectroscopy. In solution, UV$-$vis measurements of CDP$^\text{Ph}$ were performed in either toluene or benzene. As expected, the $\lambda_{\text{max}}$ value for the maximum absorption was the same in both solvents ($\lambda_{\text{max}}$ = 322 nm, Figure S6). In the solid state, however, subtle differences in the excitation energies were observed (Figure S7 and Table S6). The trend in the electronic excitation energies ($E_{\text{onset}}$) of the CDP$^\text{Ph}$ conformers are as follows: structure A, 2.57 eV (unsolvated); structure C, 2.60 eV (co-crystallized with two molecules of benzene); and structure D, 2.63 eV (co-crystallized with 0.5 molecule of toluene). Thus, a trend linking the excitation energies to the conformation of the P$-$C$-$P unit in CDP$^\text{Ph}$ cannot be confirmed, presumably due to the presence of co-crystallized solvent molecules.

IR Spectroscopy. The IR spectrum of CDP$^{\text{Ph}}$ is dominated by ring and deformation vibrations of the PPh$_3$ groups and the phenyl substituents.$^{67}$ The relevant vibrations of the central P−C−P unit are strongly coupled, and the frequencies of these coupled vibrations highly depend on the P−C−P bonding angle $\alpha$ (Figure S11). The course of the frequencies as a function of the bonding angle $\alpha$ is depicted in Figure 4. With a bonding angle of 90°, the coupling shows a minimum, and the frequencies of the symmetric ($\nu_{\text{s}}$) and asymmetric ($\nu_{\text{as}}$) stretching vibrations are similar and close to the characteristic frequency of the isolated C−P group ($\nu(\text{C=P})$ 1000 cm$^{-1}$).$^{68}$

For the linear arrangement ($\alpha = 180^\circ$), the coupling is maximal, and the frequencies of $\nu_{\text{s}}$ and $\nu_{\text{as}}$ are different and also differ from $\nu(\text{C=P})$, i.e., $\nu_{\text{s}}$ shifts to lower frequencies and $\nu_{\text{as}}$ (and $\delta_{\text{s}}$) shifts toward higher frequencies.$^{68}$

When comparing the IR spectra of structures A, C, and D (Figures S12−S15), the areas at 1300, 700, and 500 cm$^{-1}$ attract attention (Figure 5). All CDP$^{\text{Ph}}$ conformers feature a band at 1300 cm$^{-1}$, but this is clearly less intense for linear structure C (Figure 5). For this isomer, the less intense bands between 1300 and 1250 cm$^{-1}$ correspond to vibrations of the PPh$_3$ groups (the co-crystallized solvent molecules do not interfere in this region). The bands between 1300 and 1250 cm$^{-1}$, also present in the other conformers, are shifted toward lower frequencies. Therefore, the intense band at 1300 cm$^{-1}$ is attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration ($\nu_{\text{as}}$) of bent CDP$^{\text{Ph}}$ (structures A and D). DFT Studies of Gas-Phase and Solid-State Structures.
The experimental results suggest that subtle effects are responsible for the linear and bent structures of CDPᵖʰ. We thus investigated the solid-state structures with DFT methods (PBE-D3) using periodic boundary conditions that considered the full periodicity of the crystal and compared these results to the linear and bent arrangements of the CDPᵖʰ molecule. As a first step, we optimized the structure of the bent and linear structures A and C, respectively, and found results in very good agreement with the experimental data (Table 4). Specifically, we could reproduce the bent and linear arrangements of the P−C−P unit in the two structures. Next, hypotheses regarding the driving force for this arrangement were tested. First, the benzene solvent molecule was removed from structure C, and the atomic positions as well as the structural parameters of the unit cell were reoptimized, resulting in structure C′. The volume of the cell increased slightly (+1.3%), but no structural

changes were observed. In particular, the linear arrangement of the P−C−P unit remained after the removal of the benzene molecule. Even if the P−C−P angle was distorted to 140°, the structural optimization led back to the linear arrangement (without consideration of symmetry). This suggests that the linear arrangement is stable in this molecular packing even without the benzene molecule being present. Next, specific intramolecular interactions that could distinguish the two arrangements were examined. Weak hydrogen bonding of the hydrogen atoms in the *ortho* positions of the phenyl rings to the non-bonding electron pairs at C(0) should lead to a significant lengthening of the $C_{ortho}−H$ bonds. As shown in Table 4, however, this is not the case for any of the molecular structures. Notably, the linear structures show a perfectly staggered arrangement of phenyl rings $(<(C_{ipso}−P−P'−C'_{ipso}) = 60°)$, while the bent isomers are more distorted $(<(C_{ipso}−P−P'−C'_{ipso}) = 25°$ to $49°)$, which has been noted before.³²

Additional insight was gained through the analysis of the relative energies of the two crystal structures. The linear arrangement of the $C'$ structure is more stable by 4.3 kcal mol⁻¹ than structure A when the PBE functional is used for the energy evaluation. Nevertheless, if a dispersion attraction via the DFT-D3 scheme is considered the bent structure becomes more stable by 20.9 kcal mol⁻¹. The additional dispersion-type interactions in structure C, including the benzene co-crystallite, lead to the further stabilization of the linear structure (the numbers cannot be directly compared due to the different number of atoms in the unit cell). This indicates that the additional dispersion interaction with the benzene co-crystallite leads to a molecular packing with linear CDPᴾʰ moieties being preferable, possibly via a template effect. After this packing has been reached, the interaction with benzene is no longer required for the stabilization.

The electronic structures of the CDPᴾʰ molecules were analyzed with the help of the energy decomposition analysis (EDA, BP86-D3), which was previously applied to study CDPᴾʰ molecules.¹,²,²⁹ Table 5 shows the results for the established decomposition of the C(0)−P bond in terms of a donor−acceptor picture. The molecules extracted from the solid-state structure (right columns) show very similar results in comparison to the optimized bent and linear gas-phase structures (left columns). In both cases, the bent structure is intrinsically more strongly bound $(\Delta E_{int})$, which is due to the higher electronic interaction $(\Delta E_{int}(elec))$, while the dispersive attraction is comparable $(\Delta E_{int}(disp))$. The linear structures show higher values for the three EDA terms, which is consistent with shorter P−C bond lengths (Table 4) and greater orbital overlap. The major contribution to bonding in both arrangements is derived from orbital interactions $(\Delta E_{orb})$ that provide 70% of the total attractive interaction terms. The analysis of deformation densities in the framework of the natural orbitals for chemical valence approach reveals notable differences in the covalent bonding contributions. For the linear arrangement, donation from the phosphine groups to C(0) is significantly stronger when compared to that of the bent structure $(\Delta E_{orb}((L)_2→C))$, while all other terms show similar values. From the previous analysis,² it is known that the orbital interaction quantified in the term $\Delta E_{orb}((L)_2→C)$ originates from donation of the in-phase and out-of-phase combination of non-bonding electron pairs at P to the empty orbital at C(0). The other contributions are understood as the $\pi$-backdonation from the non-bonding electron pair at C(0) to $\sigma^*(P−C)$ orbitals (negative hyperconjugation, $\Delta E_{orb}(C→(L)_2)$) and the polarization at C(0) (mixing of s and p orbitals, $\Delta E_{orb}(pol)$). Thus, the main feature of the linear arrangement is the stronger donor/acceptor bond between C(0) and the phosphine ligands. Nevertheless, this increased stabilization is overcompensated by a larger Pauli repulsion term $(\Delta E_{Pauli})$, ultimately leading to a preference for the bent arrangement by a few kilocalories per mol.

The computational analyses thus suggest that differences between the linear and bent arrangement result from a delicate balance of molecular (orbital interaction vs Pauli repulsion) and packing effects (electronic vs dispersive interactions). The influence of intramolecular dispersive interactions is especially highlighted in the gas-phase structures (Table 4). The optimization including the DFT-D3 scheme gives a much more acute P−C−P angle (121.9 vs 136.9°) and a nearly ideally eclipsed arrangement compared to the previous investigation without D3 correction.²⁹ Thus, intramolecular dispersive interactions lead to more acute P−C−P angles, while intermolecular dispersion forces might result in a linear arrangement.

### CONCLUSIONS
As predicted by theoretical and experimental investigations, the central PCP moiety of hexaphenyl carbodiphosphorane (CDPᴾʰ) is “flexible”, and little energy is required for the interconversion between a bent and linear structure.³² We offer additional experimental confirmation of these predictions by the characterization of two new bent conformers (structures D and E) in addition to characterizing the known CDPᴾʰ structures A, B, and C via crystallization experiments from a range of organic solvents. Crystallization from benzene always yields structure C, whereas it is not yet possible to predict the

conformer that should result from crystallization using other solvents. Therefore, the crystal phase should always be determined by solid-state diffraction or NMR structural methods. On the other hand, linear and bent isomers show distinct spectroscopic properties (via NMR, UV−vis, and IR spectroscopy) in the solid state that are characteristic of their geometry, allowing one to identify the relative conformation. Computational investigations at the DFT level showed a subtle balance of intramolecular (donor/acceptor interactions and Pauli repulsion) and intermolecular (electronic stabilization and dispersion) interactions leading to either the bent or the linear structures. The benzene molecule seems to act as a template for a molecular packing with linear CDPⁱᵖʰ moieties but is not needed to stabilize the linear form in the final crystal. Currently, we are examining the solid-state properties of other CDPs to build a general understanding of the structural "flexibility" of these intriguing molecules.
