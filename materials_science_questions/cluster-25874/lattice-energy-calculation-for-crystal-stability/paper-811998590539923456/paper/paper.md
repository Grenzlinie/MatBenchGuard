# Solid-State Analysis of Low-Melting 1,3-Dialkylimidazolium Hexafluorophosphate Salts (Ionic Liquids) by Combined X-ray Crystallographic and Computational Analyses

W. Matthew Reichert,† John D. Holbrey,‡ Richard P. Swatloski,† Keith E. Gutowski,†
Ann E. Visser,§ Mark Nieuwenhuyzen,# Kenneth R. Seddon,*,‡ and Robin D. Rogers*,†

Department of Chemistry and Center for Green Manufacturing, The University of Alabama, Tuscaloosa, Alabama 35487, QUILL and School of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering, The Queen's University of Belfast, Belfast, Northern Ireland, United Kingdom BT9 5AG, and Savannah River National Laboratory, Aiken, South Carolina 29808

Received October 24, 2006; Revised Manuscript Received February 19, 2007

ABSTRACT: The interactions of ions in the solid state for a series of representative 1,3-dialkylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts (either ionic liquids or closely related) have been examined by crystallographic analysis, combined with the theoretical estimation of crystal-packing densities and lattice-interaction energies. Efficient close-packing of the ions in the crystalline states is observed, but there was no compelling evidence for specific directional hydrogen-bonding to the hexafluorophosphate anions or the formation of interstitial voids. The close-packing efficiency is supported by the theoretical calculation of ion volumes, crystal lattice energies, and packing densities, which correlated well with experimental data. The crystal density of the salts can be predicted accurately from the summation of free ion volumes and lattice energies calculated. Of even more importance for future work, on these and related salts, the solid-state density of 1,3-dialkylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts can be predicted with reasonable accuracy purely on the basis of on ab initio free ion volumes, and this allows prediction of lattice energies without necessarily requiring the crystal structures.

## Introduction

The term "ionic liquid" (IL) describes salts that have low melting points and are liquid at, or below, 100 °C.¹ Typically, salts that form ionic liquids are comprised of an organic cation and an anion (which may be inorganic or organic) that are charge diffuse in nature. The wide liquid range and undetectable vapor pressure under normal conditions of many ionic liquids has led to the current interest in using them to replace traditional organic solvents for separations,² synthesis and catalysis,³ and electrochemistry.⁴,⁵

Ionic solids (traditional salts) are typically considered to be high-melting-point solids—a result of the strong coulombic attractions between the ions of opposite charges. A shift from this conventional view of ionic materials is needed to develop and study ionic liquids because in order to be liquid at low temperature, the Coulombic attractive forces in the liquid state must, necessarily, be diffuse to inhibit or suppress crystallization.

A number of design approaches have been used to prepare ionic liquids: overcoming the enthalpy of fusion by reducing the lattice energy of the solid state, introducing conformationally flexible hydrocarbon groups into the ions that act as non-charge-carrying buffers between the charge centers, incorporating low-symmetry cations, and use of weakly coordinating charge disperse anions. The exceptional properties of 1,3-dialkylimidazolium bis{(trifluoromethyl)sulfonyl}amide ionic liquids⁶ combine all these features; notably, the bis{(trifluoromethyl)sulfonyl}amide anion combines charge dispersion with conformational flexibility.⁷

For typical ionic liquid systems, such as the archetypal 1-alkyl-3-methylimidazolium salts,⁵ varying the length and/or degree of branching of the alkyl chain of the imidazolium ring can dramatically change the melting point of the ILs.⁸ This has been attributed to variation in the rotational freedom of the alkyl chain of the cation and the influence this has on the ability of the cations to pack efficiently (or not) within the crystalline cell of the solid salts. Inhibition of crystallization can lead to glass formation, plastic crystals, and polymorphism in the solid state.⁹ Branching in alkyl substituents similarly can lead to increases in melting point through restriction of rotational freedom, but can also conversely reduce melting points when branching results in enantiomeric mixtures.¹⁰

It is difficult to directly study the structure and interactions in the liquid state of ionic liquids. Neutron scattering approaches have been used on a small number of ionic liquid systems¹¹ and can be combined with computational simulation,¹² but it still remains a difficult and specialized approach, with practical limits on the size of the systems that can be effectively studied.

To gain some insight into the nature of ionic liquids, and particularly into features controlling the solid−liquid phase change, a systematic investigation of the solid-state structure of ionic liquids in their crystalline form by single-crystal X-ray diffraction has been made with the aim of determining the range of coulombic and intermediate (hydrogen-bonding) interactions present within the solid state. Here, salts containing the hexafluorophosphate anion ([PF₆]⁻) have been studied. The [PF₆]⁻ anion is a large, highly symmetrical ($O_h$), pseudo-spherical anion that is only weakly coordinating. Although the long term instability of this anion¹³ to hydrolysis make it unsuitable for many practical applications as an ionic liquid component, it provides an excellent model system for the investigation of cation−anion interactions.

## Experimental Section

All the salts were prepared by metathesis from their corresponding alkyl sulfate, bromide, or chloride precursors in water. Crystals suitable for single-crystal X-ray diffraction were then obtained by recrystalli- zation of the salts from ethanol−water (for salts of 2, 3, 6, 7, and 9) or by slow controlled cooling of the neat ionic liquid $([12][PF_6])$.

Single-crystal X-ray data for the $[secC_4mim]^+$ (6) and $[tertC_4mim]^+$ (7) salts were collected on a Siemens P4 diffractometer. Data for the $[C_2mim]^+$ (2), $[C_2dmim]^+$ (3), $[(isoC_3)_2im]^+$ (9), and $[C_{14}mim]^+$ (12) salts were collected on a Siemens SMART CCD diffractometer. Details regarding the collection and treatment of the data, along with unit-cell dimensions, are included in the Supporting Information. Any unusual features of the structure refinements are discussed below.

## Results and Discussion

### X-ray Diffraction.
X-ray crystal structures were determined here for six 1,3-dialkylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts $([2][PF_6]$, $[3][PF_6]$, $[6][PF_6]$, $[7][PF_6]$, $[9][PF_6]$, and $[12][PF_6])$.

Existing published structures for $[1][PF_6],^{14}$ $[2][PF_6],^{15}$ $[4]-$ $[PF_6],^{16}$ $[5][PF_6],^{17,18}$ $[8][PF_6],^{19}$ $[10][PF_6],^{20}$ $[11][PF_6],^{21}$ and $[12][PF_6]^{22}$ were used for comparison. The original refinement of $[2][PF_6]$ did not include positions for the hydrogen atoms, therefore, for the purposes of the comparisons in this paper, we will refer to the structural results we obtained. Our original refinements of $[4][PF_6]$ and $[10][PF_6]$, discussed here, were previously used to generate the figures and brief discussion of the close contacts reported in refs 16 and 20, respectively.

The original refinement of $[12][PF_6]^{22}$ was carried out on data collected at two different temperatures. The low-temperature data set is similar to our refinement, although it was refined with disorder of the hydrogen atoms bonded to the methyl group next to the ring (which we did not find). Nonetheless, both structures are essentially identical. For the purposes of the comparisons discussed in this paper, we will refer to the structure $[12][PF_6]$ determined in our labs.

Diagrams showing the ionic unit, atom numbering scheme, and atomic displacement parameters for $[2][PF_6]$, $[3][PF_6]$, $[4]-$ $[PF_6]$, $[6][PF_6]$, $[7][PF_6]$, $[9][PF_6]$, and $[12][PF_6]$ are shown in Figure 2. Structure solutions were carried out using direct methods in SHELXTL version 6.14. All the atoms were readily located. Two of the structures, $[3][PF_6]$ and $[6][PF_6]$, are disordered. In $[3][PF_6]$, only the anion is disordered, and the fluorine atoms were modeled in two different positions with occupancy set to 60%/40%. The hydrogen atoms for $[3][PF_6]$ were located but constrained to ride on bonded atoms during refinement.

The structure of $[6][PF_6]$ was initially solved in the space group $P2_1$, in which the sec-butyl group showed complex disorder indicating the presence of both enantiomers. However, not all the components were distinguishable from one another and it was modeled as having two main configurations, which were related by a mirror plane through the methylimidazolium ring and the C5 position, suggesting the actual space group to be $P2_1/m$. The structure was then refined using the higher- symmetry space group. The disordered sec-butyl group was ultimately modeled with 50%/50% occupancy about the mirror plane and refined restrained using ISOR, DFIX, and SIMU. $^{23}$ The anion in $[6][PF_6]$ also resides on a mirror plane and is disordered. The atom F1 was modeled in two positions at 50% occupancy, and F2 and F3 were modeled with three positions at 33% occupancy each.

No disorder was observed for the remaining structures [2]- $[PF_6]$, $[4][PF_6]$, $[7][PF_6]$, $[9][PF_6]$, and $[12][PF_6]$. Hydrogen atoms were added and allowed to refine unconstrained (except as noted) in order to obtain proper close contact interactions between the cations and hexafluorophosphate anions. In some cases, this led to inappropriate $H-C-H$ bond angles, and to remedy this, those hydrogen atoms were refined constrained $([2][PF_6]$ and $[12][PF_6])$. Note, X-rays are electron density sensitive, and thus the hydrogen distances are shortened with respect to the nucleus.

### Calculations.
The structures of the $[PF_6]^-$ anion and the cations 1−9 (Figure 1) were optimized at the density functional theory (DFT) level with the B3LYP exchange correlation functional$^{24}$ and the DZVP2 DFT-optimized basis sets$^{25}$ as isolated ions. Vibrational frequencies were also calculated at this level to ensure that the structures corresponded to local minima on the potential energy surfaces. The ion volumes were taken as that inside the 0.001 au contour of the electron density calculated at the B3LYP/DZVP2 level. All calculations were performed using the Gaussian03$^{26}$ suite of programs on the SGI Altix 350 at the Alabama Supercomputer Center.

$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{L}}=U_{\mathrm{L}}+\left[p\left(\frac{n_{m}}{2}-2\right)+q\left(\frac{n_{x}}{2}-2\right)\right] R T \quad(2)
$$

where $p$ and $q$ are equal to 1 for 1:1 salts, and $n_{m}$ and $n_{x}$ are equal to 3 for monatomic ions, 5 for linear polyatomic ions, and 6 for nonlinear polyatomic ions. Lattice entropies were also estimated using a volume-based formalism²⁹ parameterized for anhydrous ionic solids shown in eq 3

$$
\Delta S=1360 V+15 \quad(3)
$$

where $V$ is the volume as described above and units are J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Lattice free energies were then calculated as $\Delta G=\Delta H$ $-T \Delta S$.

The longer chain imidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts, including [C₁₀mim][PF₆] ([10][PF₆]), [C₁₂mim][PF₆] ([11][PF₆]), and [C₁₄mim][PF₆] ([12][PF₆]), were not used in the lattice-energy analysis. Here, nonelectrostatic van der Waals interactions are likely very large in the lattice and would not be treated effectively by eq 1, which reduces uncertainties in nonelectrostatic energy contributions due to averaging. It is also noted that the $V^{-1/3}$ dependence of eq 1 makes no assumptions of spherical ions. Lattice-energy determination in this manner has been shown to yield agreement to within less than 4−5% for most salts on the basis of comparison to experimental values and full-scale computations.²⁷

Correlation of the experimentally determined cation volumes ($V_{\text{exp}}$) from the crystal structures of the [PF₆]⁻ salts of 1−9 with those calculated ($V_{\text{calcd}}$) by theory is shown in graphical form in Figure 3. The fit between theory and experiment is excellent and shows clearly that the densities of the crystalline 1,3-dialkylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts can be described accurately on the basis of the theoretical gas-phase ionic volumes. This in turn demonstrates that the ion packing in the solid state is entirely driven by coulombic attractions, with the ions forming the most accommodating close-packing array in each case. (This view of the packing is further supported by investigation of potential C−H···F interactions noted below and provided in Table 2 and Figures 4 and 5).

The lattice energies, $U_{\text{L}}$, in Table 1 are listed from highest to lowest, indicating a progression of cation size from smallest to largest due to the inverse dependence on the volumes. The largest lattice energy is predicted for [1][PF₆] (484.9 kJ mol⁻¹) and the smallest lattice energy is predicted for [9][PF₆] (445.2 kJ mol⁻¹). Over the range of salts, the total net increase in total volume is 0.09 nm³, resulting in an overall decrease in the lattice energies of 39.7 kJ mol⁻¹.

The lattice enthalpies at 298 K ($\Delta H_{\text{L}}$) follow the same trend as the lattice energies, as each simply contains an $nRT$ correction (eq 2) of 5.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The $-T\Delta S$ term to determine $\Delta G_{\text{L}}$ was calculated using eq 3. Because the entropy is directly proportional to the total ion volume, the $-T\Delta S$ term increases with increasing volume, reversing the trend in the lattice energies. The $-T\Delta S$ term amounts to between −98.7 and −136.4 kJ mol⁻¹ over the range of salts and makes the free energies more favorable because of a net change in the number of free particles for the reaction. The lattice free energies at 298 K ($\Delta G_{\text{L}}$) range in magnitude from 391.2 kJ mol⁻¹ for [1][PF₆] to 313.8 kJ mol⁻¹ for [9][PF₆], an overall difference of 77.4 kJ mol⁻¹, nearly twice as large as the net lattice energy difference. We have shown previously that these quantities can be used to provide reliable assessments of salt formation and stability energetics using isolated ion calculations.³⁰

We note that on the basis of this simple volume-based approach, the accuracy of the calculated lattice energies and enthalpies is such that a direct correlation between $U_{\text{L}}$ and melting point is likely precluded, as relatively small changes in the free energy are indicative of much larger changes in melting point. In addition, it is currently unclear how solvation effects on the ions may be described to account for free-energy changes in the liquid state. However, these issues are currently being investigated in our laboratories to obtain a better understanding of salt energetics in condensed states.

Analysis of Close Contacts. Potential C−H···F interactions are provided in Table 2 and Figures 4 and 5. For the analysis of the close contacts, we chose an upper limit bounded by the sum of the standard van der Waals radii (H···F = 2.67 Å). In contrast to modeling calculations performed by Dong et al.,³¹ the distances and angles of the close contacts show that the interactions are extremely weak and, therefore, less like true hydrogen bonds and more Coulombic in nature. The C−H···F contacts for all of the salts cluster around the sum of the van der Waals radii (as shown in Figure 5) with a wide angular distribution. Both indicate less than ideal bond directionality and angles.³²

Generally, weak hydrogen bonds are described as those with an $H\cdots A$ (A = acceptor) distance of $>2.2$ Å.³³ In this case, the $H\cdots A$ interaction is generally regarded as originating from electrostatic and dispersion interactions. Thus, the weak interactions that are present in the crystal structures of the short chained imidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts suggest that the forces responsible for packing in a crystalline lattice are predominantly electrostatic or Coulombic in origin. This also suggests that lattice energies should be well-described by eq 1 and will not be severely underestimated because of hydrogen-bonding.³⁴

The side views of the cations presented in Figure 4 show several locations of the $[PF_6]^-$ anions above and below the imidazolium rings and in close contact with the C and N atoms of the rings. The direct interactions between the delocalized positive charge of the imidazolium ring and the anion is further indication of the coulombic nature of ILs based on these ions. Similar positioning of anion around the cation has been observed in neutron diffraction data and other structural studies.¹¹,¹⁴

Calculation of the structure of isolated gas-phase ion pairs has been made for a number of ionic liquids by Hunt et al., Dong et al., Turner et al., and Talaty et al., including 1-alkyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate.³¹,³⁵,³⁶,³⁷ Under these conditions, ion-pairing is observed with an associated close contact of the anion with the more acidic hydrogen atoms of the cation. For $[C_4mim][PF_6]$,³¹,³⁷ the closest contact between the C(2)−H⋯F was calculated to be around 2 Å and was interpreted as a hydrogen bond. These contacts calculated theoretically are significantly shorter than those observed here in the solid state, or in the liquid state for $[C_1mim][PF_6]^{11}$ by neutron scattering, where the structure and ion−ion separations in the liquid state correlated strongly with the solid-state structure.¹⁴ Of course, it must also be noted that calculations on isolated ion pairs (i.e., the gas phase) overestimate charge−charge interactions compared to those in the bulk (either solid or liquid state), in which the effective charges are reduced because of polarization.¹²

### Conclusions

The crystal structures of 1,3-dialkylimidazolium hexafluo- rophosphate salts are close-packed. The solid-state structures are dominated by the efficient packing of the ions in a crystalline lattice, with no indication of specific hydrogen-bonding from cation to anion. This is supported by the absence of directional C−H⋯F hydrogen bonds or regions of low-density void space in the lattice, and from theory in which the crystal density was correlated with predicted free-ion volumes.

The crystal density can be predicted accurately from the summation of free-ion volumes and lattice energies calculated.

Perhaps of even more importance for future work, the solid- state density of 1,3-dialkylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate salts can be predicted with reasonable accuracy purely on the basis of ab initio free-ion volumes, which allows estimations of lattice energies without necessarily requiring the crystal structures.
