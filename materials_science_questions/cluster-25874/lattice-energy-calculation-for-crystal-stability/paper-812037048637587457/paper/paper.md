# Chlorine-Influenced Changes in the Molecular Inclusion and Packing Properties of a Diquinoline Host

Jason Ashmore, Roger Bishop,* Donald C. Craig, and Marcia L. Scudder

School of Chemistry, The University of New South Wales, UNSW Sydney NSW 2052, Australia

Received June 7, 2006; Revised Manuscript Received September 20, 2006

**ABSTRACT:** Chloro-substituted diquinoline dibromide **6** is a versatile host molecule that includes guest molecules of different functionality in several different ways. The crystal structures of **6** and its lattice inclusion compounds with carbon disulfide, dichloromethane, acetone, chloroform, benzene, and toluene are reported and analyzed in crystal engineering terms. Also described is the quinolinium inclusion compound **(11)**·(acetophenone). The inclusion properties of **6**, and the ways that it achieves these, are very different from those of its non-chlorinated parent, **4**. Different types of aromatic interfacial packing are discussed. There is far greater dependence on these types of interactions and a complete absence of the aromatic edge−edge $C-H\cdots N$ dimer motif in the crystal structures of **6**.

## Introduction

Lattice inclusion compounds¹,² are produced when the sum of host−host, host−guest, and guest−guest attractions in a crystal is more favorable than that available for packing of the solid apohost. The design of new lattice inclusion hosts therefore remains an imprecise science. One successful approach has been to synthesize molecules that pack together inefficiently in the solid state, either because of their awkward shape or because of incompatible intermolecular forces.³⁻⁵ There is then greater probability of guest inclusion and concomitant decrease in the lattice energy value.⁶

Recently, we have been studying the systematic inclusion chemistry of molecules such as the V-shaped aromatics **1−5** (Scheme 1) in the solid state.⁷⁻¹¹ These molecules are built from three modular units. The aromatic wings allow potential aromatic offset face−face (OFF) and edge−face (EF) associations;¹²,¹³ the central alicyclic linking group provides limited conformational folding or twisting and additionally generates pseudo-$C_2$ symmetry, whereas the bromine atoms attenuate the aromatic interactions and also provide hot spots for guest association.

A variety of weak noncovalent interactions are observed in the inclusion structures formed by hosts **1−5**. In addition to OFF and EF motifs, halogen−halogen,¹⁴,¹⁵ $C-H\cdots N$ dimer,⁸,¹⁰ $\pi$-halogen,¹⁶,¹⁷ $N\cdots$halogen,¹⁸,¹⁹ and PHD interactions²⁰⁻²² can also be present. Examples of several of these are illustrated in Figure 1. Along with factors such as size, shape, and symmetry, it is the best combination of such motifs that determines the inclusion packing arrangement that is adopted.

Despite the close similarity of the host molecular structures, their supramolecular host−guest chemistry varies considerably. Compound **3**, for example, shows widespread inclusion behavior in which two molecules of the host wrap around each guest, enclosing it within a molecular pen.⁹,²³ However, investigation of its chlorinated analogue **5** revealed an almost complete loss of inclusion properties.¹¹,²⁴ This paper investigates the effect of similar substitution on the properties of the new dichloro derivative **6**, compared to those of its parent compound, **4**.

Crystal Structure of Apohost 6. We have recently observed that crystallization of diquinoline hosts from trifluoromethyl- benzene frequently results in good quality crystals of the apohost. $^{31}$ This was found to be the case for 6, but the crystalline apohost could also be obtained from chlorobenzene, allyl cyanide, or monoglyme solutions. A single-crystal grown from chlorobenzene was used for the X-ray structure determination described here.

Apohost 6 crystallizes in the orthorhombic space group Pbca, with its molecules packing together in the pronounced zigzag layers shown in Figure 2. Within each layer, the molecules of 6 are translated along $b$ and are hence present as homochiral columns. The handedness of these columns alternates along the $c$ direction between successive zigzag layers.

The enantiomers of 6 alternate along each zigzag and are associated by means of two types of aromatic interfacial interaction. In the center of each linear section of the zigzag, two of these molecules interact by means of an OFF interaction around an inversion center. Hence, the apex of one V-shaped molecule is oriented up and the other is oriented down.

At each zigzag turning point, two columns of opposite handedness become interdigitated. This creates the second, and different, type of aromatic interfacial overlap and results in the directional change of the zigzag. The opposite enantiomers of 6 molecules have their apexes all pointing in the same orientation, giving a stack of aromatic wings (+)-up, (-)-up, (+)-up, etc., along the $b$ direction; or (-)-down, (+)-down, (-)-down in the adjacent zigzag layers. Where the aromatic wings abut at these turning points, the molecules are mutually tilted to a small extent, thereby creating a series of moderately effective interfacial interactions in which the interatomic $C\cdots C$ distances lie within the range 3.3-3.9 Å (Figure 3).

There is one $Cl\cdots Br$ interaction (3.80 Å) operating within the layers. Between the layers, there is a second $Cl\cdots Br$ (3.94 Å), plus $N\cdots Br$ (3.65 Å) and $Cl\cdots Cl$ (3.96 Å) interactions. There are no $N\cdots Cl$ or $Br\cdots Br$ associations under 4.00 Å in this structure.

Crystal Structures of the Carbon Disulfide, Dichlo- romethane, and Acetone Compounds. Crystallization of 6 from carbon disulfide yields the lattice inclusion compound (6)₂·(carbon disulfide) in monoclinic space group $C2/c$. The hosts pack as nearly flat layers, in which the molecules of 6 are present as chains of alternating enantiomers linked by aromatic OFF interactions (Figure 4). Adjacent chains in each layer are separated from each other by the $CS_2$ guest molecules, which lie in bowl-shaped cavities, indicated by the circles in Figure 5.

Each molecular bowl in (6)₂·(carbon disulfide) is constructed from two homochiral host molecules (one from each of two adjacent layers) that surround a twofold axis on which the carbon of the $CS_2$ guest is positioned. This dimeric host unit is held together by two identical $N\cdots Br$ (3.85 Å) interactions near its center, and two identical $Cl\cdots Cl$ (4.03 Å) interactions near its extremities (Figure 6). The latter can also be regarded as being part of an obliquely oriented aryl-aryl EF interaction. The structure is further stabilized by two pairs of host-guest pyridine ring $\pi\cdots S$ associations (with $C\cdots S$ distances in the ranges 3.7-4.5 and 4.1-4.5 Å, respectively). Additional interactions present in this structure comprise interlayer $Cl\cdots Br$ (3.81 Å) and $N\cdots Br$ (3.56 Å) and intralayer $Cl\cdots Br$ (3.66 Å) associations. There are no $Br\cdots Br$ contacts under 4.00 Å.

This type of arrangement is also adopted by (6)₂·(dichlo- romethane) and (6)₂·(acetone), which are isostructural with the $CS_2$ compound. Crystal Structure of the Chloroform Compound. Compound 6 crystallizes from chloroform as $(6)_2\cdot(chloroform)_3$ in the monoclinic space group $P2_1/c$. There are two independent chloroform guests, both of which are disordered and occupy sites between the host layers in the bc plane. One of the guests (black C) surrounds a center of symmetry, and only the major disorder component of the other (ca. 0.67, purple C) is shown in Figure 7.The host layers are rather wider and more diffuse than in related diquinoline layer structures.³² This situation results from the unusual molecular packing illustrated in Figure 8. Each layer is built from centrosymmetric host $\pi$-halogen dimer (PHD) units, which are highlighted here by enclosure within large red ellipses. The four $\pi\cdots$halogen interactions of each PHD unit are symbolized by heavy black lines. These PHD units abut in a herringbone-like manner. All four external aromatic faces of a PHD unit interact with different neighbors of the same chirality by means of exo, exo-facial interactions in which the V-shaped apexes of the hosts point in opposite directions. The apparent crowding in this arrangement is alleviated by a directional change in the aromatic packing. A swivel point occurs at each of these interfacial locations. It is the formation of these swivel points that causes the widening of each layer. The intermolecular packing within each layer is further enhanced by the presence of centrosymmetric $Cl\cdots Br\cdots Br\cdots Cl$ motifs (interatomic distances 3.73, 4.00, 3.73 Å) extending across the width of the layers. These halogen arrangements are highlighted by the small black ellipses shown in Figure 8.

Crystal Structure of (11)·(Acetophenone). An attempt was also made to obtain a lattice inclusion compound by recrystallization of 6 from acetophenone, but no solid material was obtained until several months later. This material then proved to be the unexpected compound (11)·(acetophenone) in monoclinic space group $P2_1/c$.

Our benzylic dibromide hosts are known to be labile materials. For example, heating 4 with aqueous acetone results in a double $S_N1$ reaction, giving an excellent yield of the corresponding diol with unusual retention of configuration.²⁹ The reaction product may be isolated as the free base, or the hydrobromide salt, depending on the pH value used in the reaction workup. We presume that 6 underwent a similar process with adventitious moisture, leading to the monohydrobromide 11 (Scheme 3), during its prolonged crystallization.

The host cations in (11)·(acetophenone) form homochiral columns along b, and columns of alternating chirality are linked by $O-H\cdots Br^-$ and $N^+-H\cdots Br^-$ hydrogen bonding to produce parallel layers of 11 in the bc plane. As illustrated in Figure 10, the acetophenone guests lie between these layers.

The packing relationship of two adjacent columns is shown in Figure 11. Along each column, cations of the same handedness associate by means of aromatic inter-facial interactions that involve the protonated (quinolinium) wing of one cation and the non-protonated (quinoline) wing of its neighbor. These exo,exo-facial interactions between homochiral molecules result in a swivel interaction very similar to those described earlier, and this results in the undulating nature of the cation column.

Numerical Comparison of the Crystal Structures of 6. The molecular structure of 6 is flexible because of the presence of its alicyclic linker group. One means of comparing structures is the fold angle, defined by the black circles shown on structure 6 in Scheme 2. These values (89.2−96.9⁰) are consistent and not atypical. The packing coefficients (68.1−70.1%) are even more uniform, and it is noteworthy that the value for the apohost lies in the middle of this range. Ultimately, however, it is the packing energy values that are critical.

Crystal lattice energy calculations were performed on the crystal structures of 6 and its compounds using the Cerius² package.³⁴ This gave the lattice packing energy per mole of unit cells (Table 2). Correction to a common standard volume is necessary for a meaningful comparison. Here, the total energy was divided by the unit cell volume/1000, which is equivalent to normalizing all seven structures at a common volume of 1000 Å³.

Pure 6 has the highest relative energy (−136 kcal mol⁻¹), and once again, this finding is in accord with the concept of an apohost molecule with packing difficulties. The benzene, carbon disulfide, acetone, and toluene inclusion compounds have lower, but comparable, values (−143, −145, −149, and −152 kcal mol⁻¹); next is the dichloromethane compound (−170 kcal mol⁻¹); and finally, the chloroform compound has the lowest relative energy (−198 kcal mol⁻¹) of the series.

Conclusions from Comparison of the Crystal Structures. Comparison of diquinoline 3 with its dichloro derivative 5 showed a severe reduction in inclusion properties. This is not the case for the dichloro compound 6, which has inclusion properties as rich as its parent compound, 4.¹⁰,³⁵,³⁶ However, in common with all our diquinoline hosts, minor modification in molecular structure results in major alterations to the supramolecular properties of this new compound. One way of looking at this situation is that we have optimized the molecular design of new lattice inclusion hosts while at the same time severely reducing prediction of exactly how they will behave. This is to be expected, because much weaker attractions than hydrogen bonds are involved.

The chlorine-substitution is found to drive the supramolecular behavior in new directions for both compounds 5 and 6. The most striking difference for 6 (Table 3) is the complete absence of the aryl edge−edge C−H⋯N dimer interaction (Figure 1), compared to the behavior of 4 and its precursor 9, where this motif is commonplace.¹⁰ The sole observation of this arrangement in this work was as part of the structure of (11)·(acetophenone). The chloro-substitution present in 6 is still compatible with potential formation of this packing motif, but

there is the additional option of $N\cdots Cl$, halogen$\cdots$Cl, PHD, pen, and swivel OFF interactions, and these are now favored.

For some of our diquinoline hosts (such as $3^9$), the type of host packing remains remarkably constant and is only influenced in minor detail by guest variation. The opposite is true for ${\bf 6}$. Isostructural compounds are produced using carbon disulfide, dichloromethane and acetone, but otherwise the crystal structures vary immensely. The use of closely related benzene, toluene, chlorobenzene, and trifluoromethylbenzene solvents for recrystallization of ${\bf 6}$ is particularly striking. Entirely different inclusion structures were produced in the first two cases, whereas the last two experiments yielded crystals of the apohost.

In part, these outcomes result from the chlorinated host ${\bf 6}$ placing increased emphasis on aromatic face-face interactions for its molecular packing. This work has revealed new variations of interfacial aromatic packing, such as the swivel-type interactions (interfacial stacking incorporating directional change) that are preferred for *exo,exo*-facial interactions between homochiral partners. We now intend to explore the wider implications of such interactions in crystal engineering.
