# An Analysis of the Molecular Packing of Maleic Hydrazide

David Hall and Christine E. L. Headford

Chemistry Department, University of Auckland, New Zealand
Received November 2, 1979; accepted March 4, 1980

A molecular packing analysis of the hydrogen-bonded layer structure of maleic hydrazide shows that even in such a structure, the van der Waals energy is the dominant component of the lattice energy. The hydrogen-bond energy of the observed structure is greater than that for alternative structures based on a different mode of hydrogen bonding, and this makes the observed more stable.

## INTRODUCTION

The stable tautomer of the compound commonly known as maleic hydrazide, or more systematically 1,2-dihydropyridazine-3,6-dione, has been shown both spectroscopically¹ and by crystal structure determination² to be the monohydroxy-monoketo form, I.

Maleic hydrazide is interesting because it acts as a plant growth inhibitor,³ and in view of the similarity between its molecular structure and that of the pyrimidine bases, in particular uracil, it has been suggested that the basis of its action may be erroneous incorporation in the nucleic acid molecule.⁴ The base-pairing properties of such a nucleoside analog have been discussed.⁵ An NMR spectral study indicated hydrogen-bonding association of molecules in dimethyl formamide solution,⁶ and at low-temperatures chains of molecules II, in which all potential hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor atoms are so involved, were proposed to exist. At higher temperatures, or in the presence of water, hydrogen-bonded association with solvent molecules was thought to become more important. ## EXPERIMENTAL AND RESULTS

Lattice energy was calculated as the sum, over all atom-atom contacts between a reference molecule and those that surround it in the crystal, of the function

$$
U = \Sigma\left[-Ar^{-6} + Br^{-12} + (q_1q_2/D)r^{-1} - Gr^{-10}\right]
$$

where $r$ is the interatomic distance. A slightly modified version of the program PCK6⁷ was used, together with the potential parameters of Momany et al.⁸ The program contains routines⁹ to accelerate the convergence of the sums in $r^{-1}$ and $r^{-6}$; the actual summation was over all contacts less than $6$ Å. $A$ and $B$ are conventional Lennard-Jones parameters, and $G$ is the attractive parameter used for the hydrogen-bond function. It is nonzero only for a contact between an electronegative atom and a hydrogen which is itself attached to an electronegative atom, in which case $A$ is zero. Parameters $q$ are partial changes assumed to exist on all atoms to represent bond polarity. The dielectric constant $D$ was assumed to be 2.0.⁸ The Coulombic term is calculated for all atom pairs, including those involved in hydrogen bonding. The charge distribution for maleic hydrazide was calculated using the CNDO/2 procedure¹⁰ and is shown in Figure 3. This treatment of Coulombic energy follows that of Momany et al.⁸ in the derivation of the potential parameters; it has been recognized¹¹ that CNDO/2 underestimates atomic charges and that the effective dielectric constant value assumed is probably closer to 4. For the purpose of the packing analysis the molecule was assumed to be rigid and planar, with nonhydrogen bond lengths and angles as in the observed structure, and with hydrogen atoms in positions calculated according to the bond lengths of Momany et al.⁸

In the structure there is a short intermolecular contact of $2.41$ Å between an ethylenic hydrogen atom and the hydroxylic oxygen; such contacts have been observed previously in structures of molecules of this type, and a weak hydrogen bond-like attraction between these atoms has been proposed. Only the normal van der Waals interaction was included in the force field assumed for this calculation, and the equilibrium value for the contact was $2.47$ Å, somewhat shorter than the optimal value of $2.88$ Å. The calculation was repeated including a weak hydrogen-bond term (actually that proposed⁸ for the N—H---N interaction) for the atom pair, but this had negligible effect on the agreement between the minimum energy and observed structures. It appears that the short contact is no more than a geometric consequence of the structure, and that no unusual weak bond need be invoked.

The unit cell was then transformed to one in which the axes $a$ and $b$ lie in the mean plane of molecules, as in Figure 1, and for which the parameters are $a = 6.890$, $b = 10.511$ Å, $\gamma = 88.7^\circ$. The two-dimensional layer in which the molecules are connected as in II was then obtained by translating alternate rows of molecules graphically to give Figure 2; the lattice parameters so derived for this layer are $a = 9.415$, $b = 7.935$ Å, $\gamma = 86.3^\circ$.* It then remains only to specify the stacking of these layers to define an alternative structure. If it is assumed that this will be triclinic, as was the observed structure, the remaining three cell parameters need to be specified; only two of these are independent if the volume occupied per molecule is required to be as for the observed structure. On this basis and by assuming all combinations of $50^\circ$, $70^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $110^\circ$, and $130^\circ$ for each of the cell angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$, 25 structural models were generated. For each, lattice energy was minimized, initially with respect to $c$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$ only, and subsequently with respect to all 12 lattice and molecular parameters. The hydrogen-bond parameters for the O—H---N bond were assumed to be as for N—H---O.⁸ Three low-energy structures resulted, details of which are listed in Table II. An alternative structure which is based on the layers as in Figure 2 need not necessarily be triclinic, i.e., successive layers may be related other than by a simple translation. The obvious alternative relating operations would be a screw axis or a glide plane; if as the layers are centrosymmetric it is assumed the structure will be also, the likely monoclinic space groups are $P2_1/a$, $P2_1/b$, and $P2_1/n$, the axes being defined with regard to Figure 2 and thus in accord with the first axial setting.¹² The $c$ axial length was again defined by the requirement that the molecular volume equal that observed, and a further alternative model in each space group was thus derived. Lattice energy was minimized for all three as done previously, but none led to energy of less than $-34$ kJ mole⁻¹, and these modes of stacking obviously offer no advantage. However, as the hydrogen-bonded entity in the alternative structure is a chain rather than a plane (see Fig. 2), the possibility that these chains may not be coplanar but alternately inclined so as to pack in a herring-bone fashion, similar to that observed for aromatic hydrocarbons, needs to be considered. The chains lie parallel to $a$, and the likely monoclinic space groups are now $P2_1/b$ or $P2_1/n$, with the cell parameters $b$, $c$, and $\gamma$ variable (again only two being independent), as also is the angle of the hydrogen-bonded chains to the $a,b$ plane. The assumptions that this angle could be $30^\circ$ or $60^\circ$, that $b$ could be 5.9 or 9.9 Å (cf. 7.9 Å for the planar layer), and that $\gamma$ could be $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$, or $120^\circ$ gave 12 models in each space group. These were refined as done previously, and the lower-energy solutions thus obtained are listed in Table II. None of these is more stable than the triclinic structures based on planar layers.

## DISCUSSION
It is apparent from Table I that both the cell and the molecular parameters of the energy minimum are very close to those of the observed structure, confirming that the energy parameters of Momany et al.,⁸ which although intended primarily for use with polypeptides were based on a considerable body of experimental data, are appropriate for this molecule. In considering the breakdown of lattice energy into components, it is necessary to bear in mind that the hydrogen-bond function of these authors does not include the Coulombic contribution to that bond and that a substantial part of what might normally be regarded as hydrogen-bond energy is thus calculated as and included in Table I under Coulombic energy. Nonetheless, it remains true that in what might readily be described as a hydrogen-bond-dominated crystal structure, the van der Waals energy still represents more than 60% of the total lattice energy.

The most successful alternative structures are those in which layers as in Figure 2 are simply stacked and which like the observed structure belong to the triclinic system. In each of the three listed in Table I, the van der Waals energy is slightly greater than that for the observed, i.e., the general packing of the molecules, intra- and interlayer, is actually more efficient. The superiority of the observed structure lies in the greater hydrogen-bond component (which reflects also in the Coulombic energy), which is the direct consequence of the considerably greater strength of the O---H---O bond *à-vis* the O---H---N. [The $\epsilon$ (minimum energy) values for the 10-12 functions describing these interactions are 24.8 and 4.6 kJ mole⁻¹, respectively.⁸] Although the hydrogen-bond pattern of Figure 2 does not require the chains of molecules to be coplanar, it is apparent that they do pack most effectively in this way. All of the monoclinic structures listed in Table II have lower van der Waals energy than the triclinic alternatives based on planar layers, thus indicating that herring-bone style packing is not advantageous for these zigzag chains.

It can then be asserted that the crystal structure of maleic hydrazide is determined by the mode of hydrogen bonding which is optimal, but this is not as may commonly be supposed because the hydrogen bonding is the dominant contributor to the lattice energy. The van der Waals energy is the principal component of this energy, but the hydrogen bonding is an effective discriminator between alternative structures that otherwise are very comparable in stability.
