# Lattice potential energy and standard molar enthalpy in the formation of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide
$(1-\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br})(\mathrm{s})^{*}$

Liu Yu-Pu(刘玉普), Di You-Ying(邸友莹)${}^{\dagger}$, Dan Wen-Yan(淡文彦),
He Dong-Hua(何东华), Kong Yu-Xia(孔玉霞), and Yang Wei-Wei(杨伟伟)

College of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering, Liaocheng University, Liaocheng 252059, China

(Received 4 August 2010; revised manuscript received 8 September 2010)

This paper reports that 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide (1-$\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br}$)(s) has been synthesized using the liquid phase reaction method. The lattice potential energy of the compound 1-$\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br}$ and the ionic volume and radius of the 1-$\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}^{+}$ cation are obtained from the crystallographic data and other auxiliary thermodynamic data. The constant-volume energy of combustion of 1-$\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br}$(s) is measured to be $\Delta_{c}U_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(1-\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br},\ \mathrm{s})=-(7369.03\pm3.28)\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$ by means of an RBC-II precision rotating-bomb combustion calorimeter at $T=(298.15\pm0.001)$ K. The standard molar enthalpy of combustion of the compound is derived to be $\Delta_{c}H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(1-\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br},\ \mathrm{s})=-(7384.52\pm3.28)\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$ from the constant-volume energy of combustion. The standard molar enthalpy of formation of the compound is calculated to be $\Delta_{f}H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(1-\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Br},\ \mathrm{s})=-(1317.86\pm3.67)\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$ from the standard molar enthalpy of combustion of the title compound and other auxiliary thermodynamic quantities through a thermochemical cycle.

**Keywords**: 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide, lattice potential energy, ionic radius, standard molar enthalpy of formation

## 1. Introduction

Recently, as the energy crisis becomes increasingly serious, phase change materials (PCMs) are receiving more and more attention. Most traditional PCMs store or deliver energy by means of solid–liquid phase transitions.$^{[1]}$ Being types of solid–solid phase change materials (SSPCMs),$^{[2,3]}$ $(\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3})_{2}MX_{n}$ $\{(n=4$ or $6)$, where $M$ is a divalent or trivalent metal ion ($M$=$\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$, $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$, $\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}$, $\mathrm{Cd}^{2+}$, $\mathrm{Co}^{2+}$, etc.) and $X$ is a negative haloid ion$\}$, they have been extensively investigated in the past few decades as they show a variety of structural and magnetic phase transitions. They are characterized by high enthalpy and reversible solid–solid phase transitions between two polymorphic forms in the temperature range of 273–393 K. Furthermore, $(\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3})_{2}MX_{n}$ can be directly processed into arbitrary shapes. Therefore, these new SSPCMs show great potential for their future use and currently new fields for their application are expanding rapidly. They may be applied effectively in fields such as solar energy utilization, waste heat recovery, energy-storage and so on. As one of the necessary intermediates or raw materials, 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide (1-$\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}\mathrm{Br}$) plays an important role in the formation of $(\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3})_{2}M\mathrm{Br}_{n}$ in which the $\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{3}^{+}$ cation bonds with the $M\mathrm{Br}_{n}^{2-}$ anion through ionic bonds.$^{[4]}$

In recent years, great attention has been paid to research involving 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide. *Gordon et al.* investigated the crystal structure and some properties of n-dodecylammonium bromide by x-ray diffractometer, automatic variable-temperature moving-film powder camera and single-crystal techniques in 1953.$^{[5]}$ In 1974, Lunden provided a three-dimensional single-crystal study of n-dodecylammonium bromide.$^{[6]}$ These studies focused on the crystal structure. However, some important thermodynamic data such as lattice potential energy, standard molar enthalpies of combustion and formation of the title compound have not been reported until now. The standard molar enthalpy of combustion of a substance is one of the most fundamental thermody- namic properties used when calculating a substance's standard molar enthalpy of formation and other ther- modynamic data. Standard molar enthalpy of for- mation is one of the most important thermodynamic properties used to calculate enthalpy changes, equi- librium constants and theoretical yields of reactions in which the substance is involved. In addition, the standard molar enthalpy of formation of a substance is closely related to other physical, biological, physio-logical and chemical properties. $^{[10]}$

Combustion calorimetry is often used to deter- mine the standard molar enthalpies of formation of many important organic substances in industry and scientific research based on the data of enthalpies of combustion and some auxiliary thermodynamic quantities. The precision rotating-bomb combustion calorimeter is one of the basic devices necessary in some thermochemical research, and is especially use- ful for the determination of the combustion energies of some elements, organic compounds, metal organics, etc. $^{[11]}$ In this work, the lattice potential energy is cal culated and the constant-volume energy of combustion ($\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}U_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}$) of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide is deter- mined using an RBC-II precision rotating-bomb com- bustion calorimeter. ## 2. Experimental section

### 2.1. Synthesis of 1-dodecylamine hydro- bromide

The 1-dodecylamine and hydrobromic acid (≥ 40 mass %) chosen as the reactants and anhydrous ethyl alcohol used as the solvent were all of analytical grade. The reactants were weighed accurately at the molar ratio of $n(\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{25}\mathrm{NH}_{2}):n(\mathrm{HBr})$=1:1, and slowly dis- solved into the correct amount of anhydrous ethyl al- cohol with sufficient stirring. The mixture was heated and stirred under reflux for 6 h. Thereafter the mix- ture was condensed by boiling off some of the liquid until a crystal membrane emerged on the surface of the solution. The final solution was naturally cooled to room temperature and filtered, and a colourless crystal was obtained. The product was re-crystallized three times with anhydrous ethyl alcohol and a colour- less transparent crystal was produced. Finally, the sample was placed in a vacuum desiccator to dry in a vacuum at a temperature of 303.15 K for 6 h. The final product was put into a weighing bottle and preserved in a desiccator. The mass fraction purity of the com- pound was determined to be > 0.994 by high perfor- mance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The contents of the bromide in the compound were determined bychemical analysis. Elemental analysis (C, H, and N) was carried out on a Vario EL III CHNOS instrument made in Germany. These results showed that the mass fraction purity of the compound was > 0.994.

### 2.2. Rotating-bomb combustion calori- metry

The constant-volume energy of combustion of1-dodecylamine hydrobromide was measured using a precision rotating-bomb combustion calorimeter(model: RBC-II type, made in Northwest University, China). The structure and principle of the calorimeter was described in detail in Ref. [12]. The initial tem- perature of the combustion reaction was automatically maintained at $T=(298.15\pm 0.001)$ K. The uncertainty of the constant-volume energy of combustion results obtained from the calorimeter could reach $\pm 0.05\%$.

The sample was burned under an oxygen pressure of 2.5 MPa in the presence of $0.001\ \mathrm{dm}^{3}$ of double- distilled water in the bomb to ensure equilibrium in the final state after combustion. The mole fraction purity of the oxygen used in the experiment was of research grade (> 0.99998). The mass of the sam- ple was calibrated from air buoyancy on the basis of the determination of the density for a pellet of the sample. At $T=(298.15\pm 0.001)$ K, the differential quotient of the constant-volume energy of combustion with the oxygen pressure, $(\partial U/\partial P)_{T}$, for the solid was assumed to be $-0.2\ \mathrm{J\cdot g^{-1}\cdot MPa^{-1}}$, a typical value fororganic solids. $^{[13]}$

The corrected value of the heat exchange between the calorimetric tube and its surroundings was calcu-lated by means of the following equation according tothe Linio-Pyfengdelel-Wsava formula: $^{[12,13]}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta(\Delta T)=&nV_{0}+[(V_{n}-V_{0})/(\overline{T_{n}}-\overline{T_{0}})]\\
&\cdot\left[(T_{0}+T_{n})/2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}T_{i}-n\overline{T_{0}}\right],\ (1)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\Delta(\Delta T)/\mathrm{K}$ denoted the corrected value of the heat exchange; $n$, the number of readings in the main

(or reaction) stage; $V_0$ and $V_n$/K·min⁻¹, the temperature drift rates in the initial and final stages, respectively ($V$ was positive when the temperature decreased); $\overline{T_0}$ and $\overline{T_n}$/K, the average temperatures of the calorimeter during the initial and final stages, respectively; $T_0$ and $T_n$/K, the first and last readings of the main stage; $\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} T_i$, the sum of all the temperature readings of the main stage except for the last one; $(V_n - V_0)/(\overline{T_n} - \overline{T_0})$, a constant related to the performance of the calorimeter.

After the experiment ended, the analytical methods used for the final products (gas, liquid, and solid) of the combustion reaction were the same as those in Ref. [12]. The combustion products were analysed mainly for carbon dioxide by the Rossini method.[13] No soot was observed in the sample crucible after each of combustion experiments. During the determination of the energy equivalent of the combustion calorimeter and measurements of the constant-volume energy of combustion of the sample, the mole ratio of real amounts of carbon dioxide produced in the combustion to the theoretical amounts were determined to be > 0.9992. This demonstrated that the combustion of the sample in the oxygen bomb was complete. Qualitative tests for CO with indicator tubes were negative within the limits of their sensitivity {mole fraction $x$ (CO) < $1\cdot10^{-6}$}. As a result, either carbon deposits or carbon monoxide formed during the combustion and the amount of $\text{NO}_x$ in the final gas phase was negligible.

The energy equivalent of the calorimeter was determined from seven combustion experiments using benzoic acid with mole fraction purity (0.99998), which was purified three times by sublimation before use and its certified constant-volume energy of combustion $\Delta_{\text{c}}U = -26434$ J·g⁻¹ at $T = 298.15$ K was recommended by National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). The energy equivalent of the combustion calorimeter was determined to be $\varepsilon = (18604.99 \pm 8.14)$ J·K⁻¹ by the following formula under the same experimental conditions:[14]

$$
\varepsilon = (Q \cdot W + q_{\text{c}} + q_{\text{N}})/\Delta T, \tag{2}
$$

where $\varepsilon$/J·K⁻¹) was the energy equivalent of the calorimeter; $Q$/J·g⁻¹), the energy of combustion of benzoic acid; $W$/g, the mass of benzoic acid; $q_{\text{c}}$/J, calibrated heat of combustion of wire $\{q_{\text{c}} = G \cdot b$, in which $G$/J·cm⁻¹) was the combustion enthalpy of Ni–Cr wire for ignition and $G = 0.9$ J·cm⁻¹; $b$/cm, the length of the actual Ni–Cr wire consumed in ignition$\}$; $q_{\text{N}}$/J, correction value of total acid $\{q_{\text{N}} = 5.97c$, in which $5.97$/J·mol⁻¹) was the enthalpy of solution and formation of nitric acid corresponding to $1$ cm³ of $0.1000$ mol·dm⁻³ of sodium hydroxide solution; $c$/cm³, the volume of the consumed $0.1000$ mol·dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide solution$\}$; $\Delta T$/K, the corrected temperature rise of the combustion reaction. ## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Lattice potential energy, ionic volume and ionic radius

Equation (3) used to estimate lattice potential energy of general type of salts $M_pX_q$ is obtained from Ref. [9]

$$
U_{\mathrm{POT}} = \sum n_i z_i^2 (\alpha'/V_{\mathrm{m}}^{1/3} + \beta'), \tag{3}
$$

where $\alpha'$ and $\beta'$ are appropriate fitted coefficients chosen according to the stoichiometry of the salt, $n_i$ is the number of ions with a charge $z_i$ in the formula unit and $V_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the molecular volume.

For the salts $MX(1:1)$, $MX_2(1:2)$ and $M_2X$ (2:1), equation (3) is changed to:

$$
U_{\mathrm{POT}} = |z_+||z_-|v(\alpha'/V_{\mathrm{m}}^{1/3} + \beta'), \tag{4}
$$

where $z_+$ and $z$ are the respective charges on the cation and anion of the compound, $v$ is the number of ions per molecule and equals $(p+q)$. In the case of the salts with a formula $MX$ and a charge ratio (1:1) like 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide, $z_+ = 1$, $z_- = 1$, $p = 1$, $q = 1$, $v = 2$, $\alpha' = 117.3\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}{\cdot}nm}$, $\beta' = 51.9\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$, and $V_{\mathrm{m}}$ is in unit of $\mathrm{nm^3}$,[⁹]

$$
V_{\mathrm{m}}/(\mathrm{nm^3}) = M_{\mathrm{m}}/\rho N_{\mathrm{A}} = 1.66045 \times 10^{-3} M_{\mathrm{m}}/\rho, \tag{5}
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{A}}$ is Avogadro's constant, $6.02245{\times}10^{23}$ $\mathrm{molecule{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$. Thus, formula (4) is changed to[⁹]

$$
U_{\mathrm{POT}} = \gamma(\rho/M_{\mathrm{m}})^{1/3} + \delta, \tag{6}
$$

where the values of the constants for $MX$ (1:1) are: $\gamma = 1981.2\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}{\cdot}cm}$ and $\delta = 103.8\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$. The density $\rho/(\mathrm{g{\cdot}cm^{-3}})$ and molar mass $M_{\mathrm{m}}/(\mathrm{g{\cdot}mol^{-1}})$ are obtained from crystal structure data of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide in Table 2, $\rho = 1.177\ \mathrm{g{\cdot}cm^{-3}}$, and $M_{\mathrm{m}} = 266.26\ \mathrm{g{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$.[¹⁵] The $V_{\mathrm{m}}$ is calculated to be $0.3756\ \mathrm{nm^3}$ according to Eq. (5), consequently, the lattice potential energy of the compound is determined to be $U_{\mathrm{POT}} = 428.95\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}}$, which reveals that the structure of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide is stable.

In addition, for a salt of molecular formula $M_pX_q$,

$$
V_{\mathrm{m}} = pV_+ + qV_-, \tag{7}
$$

where $V_-$ and $V_+$ are the volumes of the anion and cation, $p = 1$ and $q = 1$ for $MX$ (1:1), $V_-$ for $\mathrm{Br^-}$ is reported to be $0.0363\ \mathrm{nm^3}$.[¹⁶] The $V_+$ (the volume of the cation $1\mathrm{-C_{12}H_{25}NH_3^+}$) is estimated to be $0.3394\ \mathrm{nm^3}$ based on $V_{\mathrm{m}} = 0.3757\ \mathrm{nm^3}$. The ionic radius of the cation $(r_+)$ of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide can also be derived to be $0.4327\ \mathrm{nm}$ in terms of $V_+ = 4\pi r_+^3/3$ if the cation may be approximately seen as a sphere.

In comparison with the past work[¹⁷] for 1-dodecylamine hydrochloride (listed in Table 2), it can be seen that the ionic radius of the $1\mathrm{-C_{12}H_{25}NH_3^+}$ cation $(r_+ = 0.4327\ \mathrm{nm})$ of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide almost agrees with that in 1-dodecylamine hydrochloride $(r_+ = 0.4289\ \mathrm{nm})$. However, the lattice potential energy of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide $(U_{\mathrm{POT}} = 428.95\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}})$ is slightly smaller than that of 1-dodecylamine hydrochloride $(U_{\mathrm{POT}} = 433.47\ \mathrm{kJ{\cdot}mol^{-1}})$. This is mainly due to the different volumes of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ and $\mathrm{Br^-}$. As we know, the volume of $\mathrm{Br^-}$ $(V_{\mathrm{Br^-}} = 0.0363\ \mathrm{nm^3})$ is larger than that of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ $(V_{\mathrm{Cl^-}} = 0.0298\ \mathrm{nm^3})$. Therefore, the electrostatic force between the cation and anion for 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide is smaller than that for 1-dodecylamine hydrochloride; and the lattice potential energy of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide is smaller than that of 1-dodecylamine hydrochloride.

### 3.2. Constant-volume energy of combustion of the title compound

The method for determining the constant-volume energy of combustion of the sample is the same as

that used in the calibration of the calorimeter with benzoic acid. The constant-volume energy of combus- tion of the sample according to equation (2) can be calculated to be:

$$
-\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U=\left(\varepsilon \cdot \Delta T-q_{\mathrm{c}}-q_{\mathrm{N}}\right) / W,\qquad(8)
$$

where $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U /\left(\mathrm{J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}\right)$ is the constant-volume energy of combustion of the sample; $W / \mathrm{g}$ is the mass of the sample; the physical meanings of other symbols are the same as those in Eq. (2).

The measured results of the constant-volume en- ergies of combustion of 1-dodecylamine hydrobro- mide, $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U /\left(\mathrm{J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}\right)$, is indicated in Table 3. At $T=$ 298.15 K, the differential quotient of the constant- volume energy of combustion with the oxygen pres- sure, $(\partial U / \partial P)_{T}=-0.2 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1} \cdot \mathrm{MPa}^{-1} \cdot{ }^{[13]}$ Consider ing that the pressure in the oxygen-bomb in the com- bustion of the sample is corrected from 2.50 MPa to 0.1 MPa (standard pressure), $\Delta P \approx-2.49 \mathrm{MPa}$, the change in the constant-volume energy of combus- tion $\Delta\left(\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U\right)$ is calculated to be $0.50 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}$. There- fore, the real constant-volume energy of combustion of the title compound is corrected to be $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}=$ $-(27676.56 \pm 12.31) \mathrm{J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}+0.50 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}=-(27676.06 \pm$ $12.31) \mathrm{J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}=-(7369.03 \pm 3.28) \mathrm{kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ based on $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U\left(\mathrm{~kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}\right)=\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U\left(\mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{g}^{-1}\right) \cdot M\left(\mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}\right) \cdot 10^{-3}$.

### 3.3. Standard molar enthalpy of combus- tion of the title compound

The standard molar enthalpy of combustion of 1- dodecylamine hydrobromide, $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}$ refers to the en- thalpy change of the following combustion reaction at $T=298.15\ \mathrm{K}$ and $P=100\ \mathrm{kPa}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&1-\mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NH_{3}\cdot Br(s)}+75/4\mathrm{O_{2}(g)} \\
=&12\mathrm{CO_{2}(g)}+27/2\mathrm{H_{2}O(l)}+1/2\mathrm{N_{2}(g)}+\mathrm{HBr(l)}.(9)
\end{aligned}
$$

The standard molar enthalpy of combustion of the sample can be derived from the constant-volume en- ergy of combustion at $T=298.15\ \mathrm{K}$ by means of the following formula: $^{[18,19]}$

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}=\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} U_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}+\Delta n \cdot R T,\qquad(10)
$$

$$
\Delta n=\Sigma n_{i}(\text{products, g})-\Sigma n_{i}(\text{reactants, g}),\ (11)
$$

where $\Sigma n_{i}$ is the total amount (in mole) of gas presented as products or reactants, $\Delta n(1-\mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NH_{3}\cdot Br,\ s})=-25/4\ \mathrm{mol},\ R=$ $8.314\ \mathrm{J\cdot K^{-1}\cdot mol^{-1}}$ and $T=298.15\ \mathrm{K}$. The calcu- lated standard molar enthalpy of combustion of 1- dodecylamine hydrobromide is derived to be $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(1-$ $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NH_{3}\cdot Br,\ s})=-(7384.52\pm3.28)\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}}$.

### 3.4. Standard molar enthalpy of forma- tion of the title compound

The standard molar enthalpy of forma- tion of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide, $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(1-$ $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NH_{3}\cdot Br,\ s})$, is calculated by designing a ther- mochemical cycle $^{[18-20]}$ according to Eq. (9) as fol- lows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{28}N\cdot Br,\ s}) \\
=&12\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(CO_{2},g)+27/2\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{H_{2}O,l}) \\
&+\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{HBr,l})-\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{28}N\cdot Br,\ s}),\ (12)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{CO_{2},\ g})=-393.51\pm0.13\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}},^{[21]}$ $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{H_{2}O,\ l})=-285.83\pm0.04\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}},^{[21]}$ $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(\mathrm{HBr,l})=-121.55\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}}.^{[22]}$

The standard molar enthalpy of formation of the title compound can be derived to be $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{o}}(1-$ $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{25}NH_{3}\cdot Br,\ s})=-(1317.86\pm3.67)\ \mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}}$.

## 4. Conclusions

The crystal structure of 1-dodecylamine hydrobromide (1–C₁₂H₂₅NH₃·Br)(s) was measured using x-ray crystallography. The lattice potential energy of the compound 1–C₁₂H₂₅NH₃·Br, the ionic volume and ionic radius of $1$–$\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{NH}_{3}^{+}$ cation were obtained.
