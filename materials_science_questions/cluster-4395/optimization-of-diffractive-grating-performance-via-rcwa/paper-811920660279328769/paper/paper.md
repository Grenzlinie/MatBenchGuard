# Contrast in the evanescent near field of $\lambda/20$ period gratings for photolithography

Sharee J. McNab and Richard J. Blaikie

Light propagation through gratings with periods as small as $\lambda/20$ is investigated computationally by use of the multiple multipole method in two dimensions. High image contrast is evident close to the grating. Strong evanescent decay of the high spatial frequency components is observed with the region of high contrast shrinking linearly as the period of the grating is decreased. Simulations were performed for TE and TM polarizations with the TM polarization providing the dominant contrast compared with TE, which is strongly attenuated owing to the polarizing effect of the gratings. These results show good promise for optical contact lithography in the evanescent near field of a shadow mask to attain feature sizes smaller than $\lambda/20$. © 2000 Optical Society of America

## 1. Introduction

Low-cost nanolithography techniques are generating a lot of interest because of their potential to provide process solutions for applications such as biomedical nanostructures, optical artificial dielectrics, high-density storage, and security encoding. Some of these techniques include nanoimprint lithography,¹ attenuating phase shifting masks,²ˣ³ scanning-probe lithography,⁴ˣ⁵ and near-field optical techniques.⁶⁻⁹

We have demonstrated feature sizes of 100 nm on a 400-nm period with evanescent near-field optical lithography⁶ (ENFOL) with exposure wavelengths between 365 and 436 nm. This resolution is below the diffraction limit for an equivalent lens-based optical system. ENFOL is a contact exposure method that utilizes conformable $\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$ masks that are held in intimate contact with the substrate under vacuum. This is an extension of conformable mask contact photolithography¹⁰ into the evanescent near field. Ultrathin resists are necessary, with thicknesses on the order of 60 nm found experimentally to give good results.

Understanding the range and characteristics of near-field phenomena is an important step to improving the performance of ENFOL. Diffraction problems for anything but the simplest geometry have not been solved analytically, and simulations for conformal mask photolithography have concentrated on the Fresnel near field.¹¹ Recently, new interest in understanding the evanescent near field has been generated by the discovery of near-field scanning optical microscopy, and this combined with improved computing power has resulted in several numerical techniques for evaluating this regime. Multiple multipole (MMP) techniques,¹²ˣ¹³ rigorous coupled-wave analysis,¹⁴ Greens tensor techniques,⁷ and beam-propagation methods¹⁵ are just a few prominent examples.

Here computational results from two-dimensional MMP simulations,¹⁶ˣ¹⁷ of ENFOL masks are presented. The subwavelength grating mask models are described, and a brief outline of the MMP technique is given. Electromagnetic (EM) field solutions that show the potential for ENFOL to achieve photolithography with better than $\lambda/20$ resolution are presented.

MMP is a semianalytic technique in that the EM fields are approximated by a set of basis functions that are solutions to Maxwell's equations. The pro-gram numerically selects the coefficients to these ba-sis functions that minimize the errors for the boundary conditions at matching points distributed along the boundary. Once the coefficients have been determined, a full-vector solution can be found at any point. $^{17,18}$ There are three main components to be specified in constructing the models, the boundaries, the domains, and a set of expansions that act as fictitious sources for the EM fields.

### A. Boundaries
The gratings are rectangles in two dimensions with their length being in the third dimension of this two-dimensional simulator. The rectangles that define the opaque regions of the mask have had their sharp corners replaced by an arc with a radius of 5 nm. Sharp corners result in a singularity in the EM fields and hence increase the errors. Even with this im-provement the maximum error along the boundaries is still found at the points where an arc and a straight edge meet, as at this point the boundary is not infi-nitely differentiable.

To take advantage of the periodicity of the gratings only a simple unit cell is defined in terms of its do-mains, boundaries, and expansions. To emulate the effect of neighboring cells on the EM fields, special-ized periodic boundaries are placed on each side of a unit cell.

### B. Domains
Domains are regions with homogeneous material properties with their extent defined by the various boundaries. In this simple system four domains are defined, three of the domains (the interior of, the area above, and the area below the unit cell) are air with $\epsilon_{r}=\mu_{r}=1$. The fourth domain is the opaque grat-ing material, which has been assigned the properties of chrome at a wavelength of 436 nm, i.e., $\epsilon_{r}=$ $-13.24+i 14.616$ and $\mu_{r}=1.^{19}$ Chrome was chosen for its low skin depth (17.1 nm) at this wavelength.

### C. Expansions
One expansion provides the excitation, and the re-maining expansions act as fictitious sources for the scattered EM fields. Various basis expansions are employed, all of which fulfill Maxwell's equations. The majority of expansions used in the models are multipole expansions, which are based on Hankel functions. They have a singularity at their origin so they are located outside of the domain for which they are active. Simulations were performed with normally incident TE- and TM-polarized excitations (orientations defined in Fig. 1). Two Rayleigh ex-pansions were used to act as the reflected and the transmitted wave at the entry and the exit point of the unit cell, respectively. The Rayleigh waves are a special case of harmonic expansions for periodic structures that take into account the periodic sym-metry. $^{18}$ The expansions have been distributed across the various boundaries and assigned an order number for the expansions to obtain less than 1% maximum relative error and better than 0.05% aver-age relative error at the boundary matching points. The TE simulation results are generally 2 orders of magnitude better than this because they do not have the same field discontinuity as in the TM case.

The results presented here have been for the case of gratings in which light can propagate owing to one dimension (in our model the $z$) being sufficiently long compared with the wavelength. This is a limitation for photolithography with more general geometries such as circles and squares because no propagating polarization exists when these circles or squares are much smaller than $\lambda$; hence there is little energy available behind the mask. For grating structures of random orientation no polarization alignment to the source is required because with the use of an unpolarized light source there will always be a com- ponent of the light available to expose these gratings.

### 4. Conclusions
Results from this research suggest that there is no fundamental limit to the resolution that can be ob- tained when one operates in the evanescent near field of a grating illuminated with a TM-polarized source. Simulation results show that sufficient intensity and contrast exists for periods down to $\lambda/20$, indicating that the evanescent near field is ripe to be harnessed for low-cost nanolithography.

We made these observations by analyzing results from full-vector computations of the scattered EM fields from metallic gratings that incorporate realis- tic material parameters. A linear relation between

the resolvable period and the half-contrast depth that is independent of the exposing wavelength was observed, which implies increasingly strict requirements on the thickness of the imaging layer as the period is reduced.

Although no fundamental limit to resolution is evident, there are plenty of process challenges to overcome, such as pattern transfer with the thin imaging layers required, achieving large-scale intimate contact, and making high-resolution masks. These factors will set a practical limit, which is still being tested.
