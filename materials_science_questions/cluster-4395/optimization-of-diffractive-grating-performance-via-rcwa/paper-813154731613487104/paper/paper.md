## ABSTRACT

Euglenoids are unicellular aquatic organisms. These microalgae show a typical surface structure that distinguishes them from the other protists. Most cells are naked and bounded by a plasma membrane surrounded by a pellicle formed by overlapping bands. It is well known that all terrestrial and aquatic organisms are exposed to UV-A and UV-B radiation. This radiation is potentially harmful to life and since it can penetrate up to 12 meters in the water, it can reduce survival, growth and production of phytoplankton. However, the organisms have developed numerous protection mechanisms intended to reduce such damage, such as the production of pigments and other repair mechanisms. However, the possible protection that could provide the first barriers before entering into the cell has not been explored yet. In this paper we investigate, from an electromagnetic point of view, the role played by the pellicle of euglenoids in the protection of the cell against UV radiation. To do so, we investigate the electromagnetic response of different species that exhibit different behaviors against the UV radiation. We solve the diffraction problem by using the Chandezon Method and obtain the reflectance of the pellicle for the UV wavelengths. The results show that the corrugated pellicle could contribute to increase the reflectance, thus reducing the penetration of the UV radiation within the cell and therefore, minimizing the damage and increasing the survival of these organisms.

Keywords: UV protection, microalgae, natural photonic crystals

## 1. INTRODUCTION

It is well known that during ages of evolution Nature has developed a remarkable variety of intricate, amazingly detailed structures that exhibit singular electromagnetic properties. The interaction of light with these complex microstructures produce different effects, such as iridescent colors, metallic appearance, extremely pure colors, etc.,$^{1-5}$ depending on the optical mechanisms involved.$^{1-5}$ However, not only visual effects are generated. Depending on the characteristic size of the material inhomogeneities, interesting features can also be obtained in other regions of the spectrum, such as the ultraviolet and the infrared. Most of these effects are employed by the owner species for different biological functions such as thermal regulation and UV protection. For instance, the high altitude flower *Leontopodium alpinum* (Edelweiss) has a UV protection mechanism based on the use of guided modes along fibers containing UV absorbing material which have nano-metric photonic structures acting as wavelength selective couplers.$^{6}$ Based on structural mechanisms, biomimetic artificial structures are being designed and developed for diverse applications.$^{7}$ Besides, they can be combined with adequate pigments

Euglenoids are unicellular flagellated organisms which mostly live in freshwater bodies. They can also be found in marine and brackish waters and most of them are free living. They can be either autotrophic (e. g. *Euglena gracilis* and *Monomorphyna megalopsis*) or exclusively heterotrophic (e. g. *Peranema trichophorum*). These microalgae show a typical surface structure that distinguishes them from the other protists. Most cells are naked and bounded by a plasma membrane surrounded by a pellicle formed by overlapping bands (strips), the maximum number of which is a stable number for each species. They can be arranged either longitudinally or helically.

Among the main events of biological evolution, the atmosphere oxygenation was a gradual process occurred over a period of 1,5 billion years with subsequent formation of the ozone layer. This layer prevents UV-C radiation, highly damaging to any biological molecule, from reaching to the Earth. However, all terrestrial organisms, as well as aquatic organisms that live in the surface of water bodies, are exposed to UV-A and UV-B radiation. This radiation is potentially harmful to life and since it can penetrate up to 12 meters in the water, it can reduce survival, growth and production of phytoplankton.⁹ˡ¹⁰ These organisms have developed numerous protection mechanisms intended to avoid or reduce such damage, such as the production of pigments and repair mechanisms in hours of darkness as well as during daylight.¹¹⁻¹³ However, the possible protection that could provide the first barriers before entering into the cell have not been explored yet. Therefore, in this paper we investigate the role played by the pellicle of the euglenoids in the protection of the cell against UV radiation, from an electromagnetic point of view. To compute the optical response of the pellicle, we use the differential method developed by Chandezon *et al.*¹⁴ (generally called C-method). This method has proved to be very versatile and has demonstrated a successful performance to solve the problem of diffraction of light at corrugated gratings.¹⁴⁻¹⁸

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we explain the experimental procedures involved in the assays and in the obtention of the electron microscopy images. We also summarize the C-method used to compute the response of the corrugated structure. In Section 3 we show the results obtained, including the algae viability, ultrastructural observations by TEM, and the calculated reflectance of the analyzed structures. Finally, the conclusions and perspectives of the present work are discussed in Sec. 4.

## 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

### 2.1 Cell cultures

*Euglena gracilis*, *Peranema trichophorum* and *Monomorphyna megalopsis* were isolated from the Matanza River, Buenos Aires, Argentina. Samples were filtered with a plankton net (mesh size 20 $\mu$m). An unialgal culture was obtained using Pasteur capillary pipettes. Single cells were washed twice with physiological solution, and poured into glass tubes (100 ml) containing Soil Water Medium (SWM) which was stabilized with CaCO₃. After two months, a stable culture developed. Stock cultures were maintained in SWM at 24 $\pm$ 1°C on a 12h/12h dark/light cycle with 25-35 $\mu$E/m²/sec illumination provided by 40-watt, cool white, fluorescent tubes.

### 2.2 UV radiation assays

When algal growth was verified, eighteen Petri dishes containing 10 ml of soil-water-CaCO₃ medium were prepared. Six dishes were inoculated with 10⁴ cell/ml of *Euglena gracilis*, six with *Peranema trichophorum* and six with *Monomorphyna megalopsis*. Three replicates of each species were covered as control. All samples were irradiated with a fluorescent tube producing UV-B light (Iguana Light 5.0) during 8 hours. In treated cultures the volume was maintained by addition of culture medium.

### 2.3 Cell viability determination

The cell viability was analyzed by Trypan Blue exclusion assay.¹⁹ An aliquot (1 ml) of cell suspension was mixed with 0.1 ml of 0.4% of Trypan Blue stain in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Counts of stained cells were done with an Olympus BX50 microscope using Neubahuer chambers. The preparations were examined within 5 minutes in order to avoid possible photodynamic cytotoxicity.²⁰ Another aliquot (1 ml) of the cell suspension was fixed

An aliquot of each cellular suspension was observed with an Olympus BX50 microscope to verify the survival of each species. Cells for TEM were collected by centrifugation at 3500 x g for 20 min. ### 2.5 Statistical analysis
The data shown in the results section are averages from three independent experiments for each species and growth condition, each experiment consisting of the control and irradiation treatments. The statistical signifi- cance of the differences between controlled and treated cultures and between species was determined by two-way ANOVAs, in order to evaluate the differences between the treatments and the viability of species. Comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 5 and values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.

### 2.6 Electromagnetic model
Let us consider diffraction from a grating illuminated by a plane wave. The geometry of the diffraction problem is shown in Fig. 1. The grating surface $y = a(x)$ is periodic such that $a(x \pm d) = a(x)$, with $d$ as the period. The grooves of the grating are along the $z$ axis. The periodically corrugated boundary divides the space into two regions filled by materials with refraction indices $n_1$ (region $y > a(x)$) and $n_2$ ($y < a(x)$), respectively. Both media are isotropic, homogeneous, and non-magnetic. A linearly polarized electromagnetic plane wave of angular frequency $\omega$ is incident from the region $y > a(x)$, its wave vector $\vec{k}_i$ lies on the mean section of the grating ($xy$ plane) and forms an angle $\theta_0$ with the $y$ axis. To solve the electromagnetic problem we used a computer code based on the C-method, the details of which are available elsewhere. $^{14-16}$ The main idea of the C-method is to simplify the boundary conditions by using a very simple nonorthogonal curvilinear coordinate transformation that maps the grating interface onto a plane. ## 3. RESULTS

### 3.1 Algae viability and ultrastructural observations by TEM

The treatment with UV radiation showed significant differences to their respective controls in the viability of *Monomorphyna megalopsis* and *Peranema trichophorum* (Figure 2). *Euglena gracilis* showed no significant differences between irradiated and control. However, when comparing the viability of the irradiated cells, no significant differences were observed between *E. gracilis* and *P. trichophorum*. On the other hand, the viability of irradiated *M. megalopsis* is significantly below that of the other two species under the same conditions. This implies that the species *E. gracilis* and *P. trichophorum* mostly survive to the UV radiation, whereas *M. megalopsis* does not.

In Fig. 3 we show TEM micrographs of the three species under study, which highlight the pellicle profile. As observed in this figure, the pellicle profile of *E. gracilis* and *P. trichophorum* exhibits a rather regular pattern which resembles a diffraction grating. However, as expected for natural structures, the distance between adjacent grooves varies from one individual to the other, and even in different regions within the same pellicle. We obtained images of different individuals of each species and measured the minimum (dmin) and maximum (dmax) distance between adjacent grooves and their corresponding depths, hdmin and hdmax, respectively. In Tables 1 and 2 we show these values for four cells of *E. gracilis* (Table 1) and for six cells of *P. trichophorum* (Table 2). On the other hand, the pellicle of *M. megalopsis* does not exhibit such a regular pattern, and presents quasi-planar regions of lengths greater than 2.2 $\mu$m, approximately (see Fig. 3(c)). Therefore, it can be regarded as planar for the UV radiation wavelengths.

### 3.2 Electromagnetic response
As mentioned above, the species E. gracilis and P. trichophorum, which exhibit a regularly corrugated pellicle, mostly survive to the UV radiation, whereas M. megalopsis, which is almost planar, do not. This result prompted us to analyze the electromagnetic response of the corrugated structures in comparison to that of a planar interface, which represents the response of M. megalopsis. For this purpose, we approximate the actual profile of the pellicle of E. gracilis and P. trichophorum by a sinusoidal interface which separates the two media: inside and outside the microorganism. Since their habitat is water, the refraction index of the external (incident) medium is taken to be 1.33. According to Nakano et al.,$^{23}$ the major chemical components of the E. gracilis pellicle complex and the plasmatic membrane are proteins, polar lipids as phosphatidylethanolamine, and carbohydrates as xilose, fucose, and ramnose. Most of these components have refractive indices ranging between 1.46 and 1.6. Since the pellicle is a highly conserved character in euglenoids, it can be considered that the pellicles of M. megalopsis and P. trichophorum have a similar composition. Therefore, for the calculation we considered the interior of the cell as a homogeneous medium with an average refraction index of 1.5.

We show results of the total normalized reflectance $(R)$, which is defined as the total reflectance of the periodic grating normalized to the reflectance of a planar interface between the same two media, and is calculated as
$$
R = \frac{\sum_{n} (e_n^{\mathrm{TE}} + e_n^{\mathrm{TM}})}{|r^{\mathrm{TE}}|^2 + |r^{\mathrm{TM}}|^2}, \tag{2}
$$
where $e_n^{\mathrm{TE}}$ ($e_n^{\mathrm{TM}}$) is the efficiency of the $n$-th reflected diffraction order for TE (TM) polarization and $r^{\mathrm{TE}}$ ($r^{\mathrm{TM}}$) is the Fresnel reflection coefficient for TE (TM) polarization.$^{24}$

In Fig. 4 we consider a sinusoidal interface separating two media with refraction indices 1.33 and 1.5. The period is $d = 0.28$ $\mu$m and the depth-to-period ratio is $h/d = 0.4$, which are typical values found in the pellicle of the microalgae (see Tables 1 and 2). We show a contour plot of $R$ as a function of the angle of incidence and of the wavelength, for the UV and visible ranges. As observed in the figure, within the UV region, the reflectance of the corrugated interface is lower than that of a planar interface ($R < 1$) except for small angles of incidence ($\theta_0 < 10^\circ$, approximately). This result suggests that the reflectance of the corrugated pellicle is more significant for small angles of incidence. Therefore, in Fig. 5 we analyze the normal incidence case. For this figure we consider a sinusoidal interface separating two media with refraction indices 1.33 and 1.5, under normal incidence, and show contour plots of $R$ as a function of the incident wavelength and of the depth-to-period ratio, for different values of the period. These values were chosen taking into account the values of the distance between adjacent grooves presented in Tables 1 and 2: 0.256 $\mu$m, 0.276 $\mu$m, 0.315 $\mu$m and 0.495 $\mu$m. ## 4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

We have shown that the UV-reflectance of the species that exhibit a periodic pattern in their pellicle is higher than that of a planar interface for a wide region of parameters. This behavior could explain why most of the population of *Euglena gracilis* and of *Peranema trichophorum* survived to UV exposure and most of the *Monomorphyna megalopsis* individuals did not. These results suggest that the first barriers of the cell could play an important role in the protection against UV radiation. Moreover, the pellicle characteristics (depth, period) seem to be critical parameters to obtain the desired performance of the electromagnetic response. However, there are still several aspects that should be investigated. On one hand, to simplify the model and the electromagnetic treatment, we considered a sinusoidal profile of the grating. However, it has been observed in the transversal cut images that the profiles are not exactly sinusoidal, and also vary from one sample to the other. Since the electromagnetic response depends on the geometry of the grating, reflectance calculations that take into account a more accurate representation of the actual profile could provide more insight into this problem. Another aspect that could be improved is the determination of the refraction index. Although our estimations are based on the chemical components, the cells are not homogeneous and therefore the refraction index varies from point to point. The simplified model used in this work could not take into account these inhomogeneities. However, other essentially numerical methods such as FDTD$^{25}$ or a photonic simulation method$^{26}$ could account for these inhomogeneities. It would also be interesting to investigate if the pigments present in *E. gracilis* also contribute to its protection against UV-radiation. In this case, the absorptance of the cell could also be an indicator of this kind of protective mechanism.
