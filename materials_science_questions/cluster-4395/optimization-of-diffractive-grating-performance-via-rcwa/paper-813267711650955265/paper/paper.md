# High angular tolerance thin profile solar concentrators designed using a wedge prism and diffraction grating

Tanant Waritanant $^{a}$, Sakoolkan Boonruang $^{b}$, Te-Yuan Chung $^{a,*}$

$^{a}$ Department of Optics and Photonics, National Central University, No. 300 Chungda Rd., Chungli City 320, Taiwan
$^{b}$ Photonics Technology Laboratory, National Electronics and Computer Technology Center (NECTEC), National Science and Technology Development Agency (NSTDA), 112, Thailand Science Park, Pathumthani 12120, Thailand

Communicated by: Associate Editor Yogi Goswami

## Abstract

This paper proposes a novel design for a high angular tolerance thin profile solar concentrator comprised of a wedge prism and dif- fraction gratings. A concentrated broad solar spectrum of up to 500 nm can be achieved by utilizing two diffraction gratings located on the top and bottom surfaces of the wedge prism. The gratings diffract the incident light guiding it into the wedge prism. The guided light is then projected onto the solar module located at the end face of the prism. With this coupling mechanism, the concentrator's length-to- thickness ratio can exceed 7, the maximum concentration ratio exceeds 3.77, and angular tolerance at 90% of the maximum collection efficiency can reach ±35° and ±3° along the two incident axes and reach ±53° and ±9° at 50% of the maximum collection efficiency, respectively. In addition, the polarization dependence, as a property of the one-dimensional diffraction grating, is suppressed. As a result, the conversion efficiency is enhanced 1.67 times over cases where a single grating is used.

## 1. Introduction

For decades, solar concentrators have received consider- able attention in the field of photovoltaic (PV) applications due to their ability to enhance the total conversion effi- ciency of photovoltaic systems (Würfel, 2005). An element is used to concentrate the broad solar spectrum, normally from 400 to 900 nm, onto a small region of a photovoltaic (PV) cell (Castro et al., 2010). This can significantly reduce the effective cost of the PV module. Only a few new approaches have been proposed in recent years such as the flat-facet concentrator (Liu et al., 2012), quantum dot based luminescence concentrator (Schüler et al., 2007; Gallagher et al., 2007) and the spectral splitting concentra- tor (Stefancich et al., 2012). The new designs are currently under development and still facing various challenges. Therefore, the most common and widely adopted design concepts are still the Fresnel lens, parabolic mirror and compound parabolic concentrator (CPC) (Parida et al.,2011; Xie et al., 2011; Chemisana, 2011). The Fresnel lens, parabolic mirror and trough designs require large and mostly hollow space which extends the thickness of the entire PV module. A precise tracking mechanism is neces- sary to ensure the maximum efficiency, due to a very low angular tolerance of less than ±1° for most solar concen- trators with a high concentration ratio (Wang et al.,2010). For stationary designs, the CPC is the most studied configuration. However, a CPC is usually bulky and/or heavy. Several thin profile solar concentrator designs have been proposed, most of which apply the usual coupling devices such as wedge prisms (Maruyama and Osako, 1999), diffractive components (Kostuk and Rosenberg, 2008; Kostuk et al., 2009), micro-lens arrays (Karp et al., 2010), or dielectric waveguides (Park et al., 2010). The two main limitations for such systems are the angular tolerance and the concentration ratio. The angular tolerance prevents the design from being used without a tracking system. The concentration ratio contributes directly to the PV module costs per Watt. Based on this observation, it stands to reason that the efficiency of thin profile solar concentrators can be improved by combining wedge prisms and diffraction gratings, which would allow for a much thinner PV module profile and better angular tolerance than is possible with the commercially available solar concentrator PV modules.

Waritanant et al. (2010) studied the performance of a device with a metallic blazed grating on the bottom surface of the wedge prism. They found that for the optimum design, with a wedge prism refractive index of 1.5, the collection efficiency could be as high as 0.32 when the grating has an aspect ratio of 5.6. The limitation of the structure arises from the sensitivity of the blazed grating to the spectrum as well as to the polarization of the incident light. This restricts the spectral range and minimizes the collection efficiency.

We adopt the wedge structure and two diffraction gratings in the proposed design. The aim is to overcome the dispersion and polarization independence properties of the grating that may limit the collection efficiency, ratio of power guided into the PV device and the incident power. Compared to the structure with a single grating, the structure with two gratings can concentrate the solar spectrum up to 500 nm which is approximately 2 times broader than when a single grating is used. The concentration ratio is increased to more than 3.77. This design has a relatively high concentration ratio and can be thinner, lighter and more compact in comparison with other stationary concentrator designs.

## 2. Mechanisms of the proposed solar concentrators

The schematic arrangement of the new wedge concentrator is shown in Fig. 1. The structure is comprised of a wedge prism and two diffraction gratings located on the top and bottom of the prism, defined as the transmission grating and reflection grating, respectively. The elements are separated with small air gaps to ensure no de-coupling due to the multiple reflections at the interface between the prism and the gratings. The diffractive characteristics of the blazed gratings will allow the majority of the light to be in the first diffraction order and reach the condition of total internal reflection (TIR), thereby allowing the design to have a higher surface and concentration ratio.

As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the solar spectrum illuminates the top surface of the concentrator (defined as surface $I$ in the figure) with incident angles $\theta_{Inc.}$ and $\phi_{Inc.}$ on two separate planes of incidence. The light propagates into the wedge prism, where the guided power can be maximized with the assistance of two diffraction gratings. Since the wedge prism consists of an inclined bottom surface, light rays that satisfy TIR at either the top or bottom surface of the wedge prism are guaranteed to have a larger reflection angle after each reflection until they reach the end face, defined as surface $II$. The collected light finally exits the wedge prism through surface $II$ where the PV cell is located.

To make a comparison among different types of solar concentrators, three figures of merit, the collection efficiency, concentration ratio, and angular tolerance are used. The collection efficiency ($CE$) can be written as in Eq. (1) (Maruyama and Osako, 1999), where it is defined as the ratio of power guided to the PV device ($P_{out}$) and the initial value of power illuminating the concentrator ($P_{in}$) as in

$$
CE = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}. \tag{1}
$$

The concentration ratio ($CR$) is the product of the collection efficiency ($CE$) and the surface ratio, which is the ratio between the area of the illuminated surface ($A_I$) and the area of the exit surface ($A_{II}$), as written in the following equation (Maruyama and Osako, 1999):

$$
CR = CE \frac{A_I}{A_{II}}. \tag{2}
$$

Given the three figures of merit mentioned, the design challenge is how to maximize the collection efficiency of the solar concentrator, while keeping the surface ratio small enough to provide a maximum concentration ratio.

The coupling mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 2. Conceptually, the light entering from surface $I$ of the element is coupled into the prism by the transmission grating as indicated by the solid black arrows. The grating diffracts most of the light into the first order wave. In order to avoiding de-coupling of the light by the grating, there is an air gap between the grating and the prism. Thus, the first order waves in the air gap are refracted into the prism with an angle $\psi_{1r}$. To guide the first order waves into the prism, the grating is designed to diffract light in such a way that an angle $\psi_{2r} = \theta_w + \sin^{-1}(\sin \theta_{dt}/n_w)$ is greater than the critical angle between the prism and the air.

To guide a broad spectrum of light into the prism, one needs to optimize the grating and the prism parameters. The part of the solar spectrum that cannot be guided into the wedge prism by the first grating is diffracted back into the wedge prism by the bottom grating, as indicated by the dashed black arrows. The bottom grating behaves as a reflection grating. The grating located on the bottom surface of the prism also has an air gap to avoid the de-coupler problem.

### 3. Calculation results and discussion

Taking the dimensions of the wedge prism and the structures of the two gratings into account, there are a total of six important adjustable parameters that can be varied to ensure the high efficiency of the design: the refractive index of the wedge prism $(n_{w})$, apex angle of the wedge prism $(\theta_{w})$, transmission and reflection grating periods $(\Lambda_{t}, \Lambda_{r})$, and transmission and reflection grating blaze angles $(\alpha_{t}, \alpha_{r})$. The value selection for each parameter is explained in the next section. In the following discussion, the prism material absorption coefficient is not considered.

![](./images/813267711650955265_5.jpg)

Fig. 3. Changes in collection efficiency versus wedge apex angle ($\theta_w$) for different reflection grating blaze angles. The vertical dashed line indicates the value of $\theta_w$ chosen for further calculations.

In this paper, the Ray Tracing Calculation together with Rigorous Coupled Wave Analysis (RCWA) (Moharam et al., 1995a, 1995b) are used to calculate the solar power projected on the PV cell. The calculations are performed separately for different incident angles, polarization, and wavelength of operation.

For each operation, the geometrical optics was used in the ray tracing calculation to trace the diffraction and refraction of each ray in the device. Here, the input rays have uniform power incident on the transmission grating with the same angles but different locations. The change in direction of each ray after passing through the grating is calculated by the grating equation, while the change in power due to diffraction efficiency of each diffraction order is calculated using RCWA. The directions and power from different orders of the diffracted light are fed in the ray tracing calculation. The calculation processes continue to trace the location, direction, and power for every ray produced by diffraction and refraction in loops until each ray either travels out of the prism through surface II and reaches the PV cell, travels out of the structure through other surfaces, or has a power of less than 0.01% of its initial value.

Finally, the collection efficiency (CE) is calculated from the summation of the power from all the rays that reach the PV cell, normalized by the total power of the incident ray under calculations. As none of the surfaces of the wedge prism have an AR coating, the Fresnel reflection is considered for all prism surfaces. The absorption of light in the prism is minute across the spectral range of interest (400–900 nm) and is therefore not considered.

The initial values of the six adjustable parameters in the simulation are chosen so that, for normal incident rays across the interested spectral range, the first diffraction order satisfies the total internal reflection condition at the wedge surfaces and is guided to the surface II. The area of surface I in the simulation is set to be 10 cm² while the wedge prism apex angle can vary. The refractive index of the wedge prism is chosen to be 1.48 (Fused Silica) since lower refractive indices of the prism provide lower loss due to Fresnel reflection. Moreover, the bulk cost of fused silica is lower than other optical grade materials.

The effect of each parameter mentioned in section two is simulated for a normally incident solar spectrum. The calculation results plotted in Fig. 3 demonstrate the change in collection efficiency versus the wedge prism apex angle (for each structure for different values of blaze angle in the reflection grating ($\alpha_r$)) while keeping the period ($\Lambda_r$) at 1140 nm. In the calculations, the parameters of the transmission grating remain the same ($\alpha_t = 62^\circ, \Lambda_t = 610$ nm). The results for each design show that the collection efficiency is maximized with the structure with a wedge prism apex angle ($\theta_w$) of 8.1°.

## 4. Conclusion
We propose a new design for a high angular tolerance thin profile solar concentrator which utilizes wedge prisms $(n=1.48)$ with one blazed reflection grating and one blazed transmission grating. The goal is to achieve a thin profile solar concentrator for PV applications. The design achieves a maximum collection efficiency of 0.5375, and a concentration ratio of 3.77. The length-to-thickness ratio of the module exceeds 7, while the angular tolerances at half maximum collection efficiency can reach $\pm 53^{\circ}$ and $\pm 9^{\circ}$ in the two planes of incidence $(\pm 35^{\circ}$ and $\pm 3^{\circ}$ at $90 \%$ of the maximum collection efficiency). The output characteristics are studied and the optimal design parameters are also proposed.
