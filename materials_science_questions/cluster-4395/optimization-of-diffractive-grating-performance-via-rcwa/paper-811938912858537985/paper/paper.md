## ABSTRACT

High efficiency diffractive light incouplers for light guides are described. We show that it is possible to couple white LED light into light guide with good white balance and high incoupling efficiency by using binary radial grating structures. Furthermore, a double-sided perpendicularly oriented grating configuration at opposite sides of a thin light guide is shown to provide significantly higher overall efficiency than one sided grating. This is a configuration that can be used in many mobile backlighting applications. In addition, a double grooved binary grating is introduced which can produce theoretically up to 100% incoupling efficiencies by using monochromatic light sources. This high efficiency is comparable to the slanted gratings. Replication techniques and incoupling efficiency simulations for slanted gratings are introduced by using a high refractive index material ($n$=1.71@525 nm).

Keywords: Diffractive optics, grating, incoupler, light guide, replication

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Diffractive incoupling of light into light guides is very important in numerous mobile optical applications such as backlights, virtual displays, and integrated optical components$^{1,2,3}$. In all these cases there is a demand for thinner light guides and more efficient light incoupling methods. Usually the light guides have been equipped with incandescent light sources that are monochromatic or polychromatic pipe lamps. Recently white or RGB LED's are replacing the lamps being more light and cost-efficient and compact. If the light guide is thick, a butt coupling can be used, where the light source is attached on the edge of light guide. This coupling requires only matching the numerical aperture of the light guide to the angular distribution of the light source. If the light guide has is thin, say, less than 0.5 mm, and smaller that the dimension of the light source, the butt coupling becomes problematic. Therefore the coupling through surfaces must be considered and one method is to use surface relief gratings on the planar surface.

The binary gratings have typically a very low efficiency into one pre-defined direction or diffraction mode inside of the light guide and this is in most applications this is a show stopper. This problem can still be avoided by using a radial or two sided grating coupler geometry$^{4,5}$. Asymmetric gratings are more desirable in many cases, especially the blazed gratings, but they are difficult to manufacture when the periods is in the range of the wavelength of visible light$^{6}$. The symmetry of the grating line can be broken also by using a doubly grooved, reflective metallized or dielectric gratings$^{7,8}$. The theoretical coupling efficiencies are from 70% to 90% for metallized gratings, and can reach even 100% for double grooved dielectric gratings.

In many aspects a better choice is to produce a binary mask and then to etch binary slanted surface relief grating since that grating type is well known to couple monochromatic light into a light guide with very high efficiency, even better than 97% with the normal incidence to the light guide$^{9}$. The production cost of the slanted gratings has been high because the equipment, that are needed in the manufacturing process, are very expensive. This has so far restricted their use in the real consumer product applications. Therefore, developing effective replication technologies for slanted gratings was necessary in order to reduce the cost of manufacturing. We have recently optimized a process to reproduce high quality slanted gratings in large volume by using SiO₂ master and UV replication technology$^{10}$. The replication technology is now expanded to Nickel tools which are also used as moulds in the replication process.

## 2. RADIAL LIGHT INCOUPLER FOR MONOCHROMATIC AND WHITE LED'S

A schematic geometry of a radially-shaped grating input coupler is presented in Figure 1. The shape of the incoupling structure is a conically mounted binary diffractive structure in which the local grating period is chosen to deflect the incoming light beam inside of the light guide. This indicates that the propagation angles of the first diffraction order $\theta$ has to be greater than the angle of the total internal reflection $\theta_{\text{TIR}} = \arcsin(1/n)$, where $n$ is the refractive index of the light guide. This radial grating structure consists of various rings of binary gratings with different periods. If we consider the situation locally, the beam is coupled into the propagating diffraction orders inside the light guide and both of the main propagating first orders are symmetrically deflected in relation to the radial direction of the incoming beam. The grating period changes increasingly but it is assumed to be constant for each circular ring.

### 2.1 Monochromatic light incoupling

The efficiency simulations of the grating couplers have to be carried out rigorously and we applied the FMM-method¹². In the optimization we assumed an unpolarized illumination, which can be evaluated as the average of the TE- and TM-contributions i.e. $\eta = (\eta_{\text{TE}} + \eta_{\text{TM}})/2$. The incoupling efficiencies are solved separately for each ring (i.e. the period change $d_l$ is divided into 20 $\mu$m increments and the simulation is performed for the each grating period). Thus, the total incoupling efficiency of the LED radiation can be solved by summing up the ring contributions. The total incoupling efficiencies may be maximized by separately optimizing the local grating period $d_l$, fill-factor $f_l$, and the depth of the grating grooves $h_l$. However, taking into account the practical limitations, like time-consuming optimization and manufacturing of small features, we fixed $f_l = f = 0.5$ and require that $h_l = h$ has a constant yet unknown value over the whole structure. Our simulation results proved that grating is very tolerant against the depth variation which is a relief from a manufacturing point of view. ### 2.2 White LED light incoupling

Also, in the case of a white LED, we assumed that the light source is unpolarized and the incoupling efficiency of each ring had to be calculated separately for both states of polarization and different wavelengths⁴. For the parametric optimization, the maximum grating period for one incidence angle can be determined using the shortest wavelength $\lambda$ of the used light source spectrum. A typical white LED spectrum is illustrated in Fig. 3, in which the maximum emitted radiation is located at 470 nm and the range of wavelengths is defined from 380 nm to 760 nm.

During the optimization process, we had to sample the wavelength to 20 nm increments and the incidence angle to $10^\circ$ increments, otherwise the computational time would have been impractical. After the optimization, we calculated the incoupling efficiency more accurately by sampling the wavelength to 1 nm increments and simultaneously keeping the incidence angle increment at $10^\circ$. In Figure 3, the spectral coupling efficiencies are presented for four different refractive index values (grating is assumed to be on a refractive index of $n$=1.5). It can be easily noticed that the refractive index has a significant impact on the incoupled diffraction efficiency over the wide wavelength range. In addition, the uniformity of incoupling gradually becomes better over the selected wavelength range when the refractive index is increased from 1.46 to 2.05. At the same time, the total incoupling efficiency also increases remarkably, from 43% to 62%.

A flat coupling efficiency curve as a function of a wavelength is crucial if the white balance for the incoupled light is needed because the spectra of the incoupled light is a product of the power spectrum of the LED and the incoupling

efficiency. This indicates that the colour of the incoupled light does not completely correspond to the original colour of a white LED and this may change the white balance of the incoupled light. The CIE1931 colour coordinates $x$ and $y$ for the spectra of Figure 3 are calculated using colour matching functions based on CIE (Commission Internationale de l'Eclairage) 1931 standard observer. The coordinate values are presented in Figure 4. It can be seen that with the refractive index of $n=2.05$ the colour coordinate values of the incoupled light remain nearly the same as the values of the original white LED. However, when the refractive index decreases the coordinate values also shift towards the blue region but still the values stay inside the white domain of the CIE1931 chromaticity diagram. This indicates that when the light is coupled out from the light guide it appears as white to the observer and can therefore be used, for instance, in backlighting of keyboards.

## 3. DOUBLE SIDED LIGHT INCOUPLER FOR THIN LIGHT GUIDES

Light incoupling into thin light guides is practically a very difficult problem. In a normal situation, by using linear gratings the incoupled light hits back to the incoupling grating and is coupled efficiently out. This phenomenon, explained by the reciprocity theorem, cannot be avoided with asymmetric grating structures (e.g. slanted gratings) and thus other solutions have to be used. Here we introduce a double-sided grating configuration with perpendicularly oriented binary gratings on opposite sides of the light guide⁵. The geometry is shown in Figure 5. In the figure, $L$ is the thickness of the light guide and the origin of the coordinate system is fixed in the middle of the lower grating. As an example, part of the propagating rays, generated by the incident ray hitting the element at position $(x,y,z)=(0,0,L)$, are also drawn in the Figure 5. The incoupled ray "1" hits the lower grating in a conical mounting and therefore it is totally reflected and simultaneously split into two rays by the grating (diffraction angles can be calculated from the conical grating equation). When the ray "2" hits the upper grating, a part of it is coupled out and the remaining part is split

between the reflected diffraction orders. We point out that since the ray "2" reaches the upper grating in a direction other than the diffraction orders of the originally designed grating, the grating does not couple the beam out efficiently. This also holds for beams "3"-"6" which are also coupled out inefficiently. Finally, the rays leave the grating region and will propagate inside the light guide purely by total internal reflections outside of the grating aperture which is now our interest. This way the incoupling area in doubled, which affect directly to the overall coupling efficiency.

![](./images/811938912858537985_5.jpg)

Fig. 5. Geometry of double sided grating structure.

Since the optimization of the whole system would be a cumbersome and time-consuming problem, we optimized the top and bottom surface gratings separately (the subscripts t and b denote the gratings at top and bottom surfaces, respectively). The optimization of the top surface incoupling grating is a straightforward task, only the maximization of the first diffraction order efficiency for the TE-polarized light. We obtained the following parameters: period $d_t/\lambda = 0.95$, fill-factor $f_t = c_t/d_t = 0.3787$, and depth $h_t/\lambda = 0.7673$. The bottom grating parameters were then optimized to maximize the diffraction efficiency of the first reflected orders and the following parameters were obtained: $f_b = 0.3435$ and $h_b/\lambda = 0.2997$ with $d_b/\lambda = 1.8$.

We also solved the incoupling efficiencies by a simple tray tracing software for $L = 0.375$ mm and grating aperture size of 6 mm x 6 mm (i.e. for the grating area versus light guide thickness ratio of 16:1). The results are shown in Figure 6. The achieved incoupling efficiencies are from 25% to 55%. The corresponding incoupling efficiencies for one sided grating coupler are 2.0 % at $(x,y) = (0,0)$ mm and 36.7 % at $(x,y) = (3.0,0)$ mm. Thus, the incoupling efficiency is increased significantly for TE-polarization and a similar behaviour can also be obtained for TM or unpolarized light.

![](./images/811938912858537985_6.jpg)

Fig. 6. Incoupling efficiencies for 6 mm x 6 mm grating aperture with 0.375 mm PMMA light guide thickness. The results are presented for TE-polarization (left) and TM-polarization (right).

We processed the top and bottom side masters gratings with a size of $6$ mm $\times$ $6$ mm with electron beam lithography on a SiO₂-mask plate, assuming $\lambda = 632.8$ nm. We decided to make replicas on a SK9 UV-curable resin (Summer Laboratories) because the refractive index of this material is close to the corresponding value of a PMMA light guide. In the replication process, drops of SK9 UV-resin were dispensed first on the grating master with an injection metering device and then a PMMA foil was placed on top of the master grating. After this, the stack was cured with UV light. This process was also repeated with the other grating but this time the grating master was rotated so that the grating lines became perpendicularly oriented at the opposite sides of the light guide. The incoupling efficiency of the system was

measured with an integrating sphere (Labsphere SC-5500, 150 mm diameter) with a HeNe laser beam radius of 0.25 mm (λ = 632.8 nm) from the midpoint of the incoupling grating. Because direct measurements inside the light guide are not possible, the experimental efficiency was solved by making three indirect measurements (reflected, transmitted, and incident beams). By solving the experimental coupling the efficiency we got 25.0% and 14.5% for TE- and TM-polarized light, respectively. These measurements showed that the experimental and theoretical values are generally relatively close to each other and the most of the differences can be explained with the fabrication errors. The illustration of the incoupled light is shown in Figure 7.

## 4. DOUBLE GROOVED LIGHT INCOUPLER

In this section we present a dielectric grating structure that deflects the incident beam efficiently into the first diffraction order. The grating structure considered is illustrated in Figure 8. The grating structure consists of two grooves defined by the transition points $[x_1, x_2, x_3]/d$ and the depths of the structure are defined by $h_1$ and $h_2$. The grating is illuminated by a normally incident plane wave of wavelength λ. The refractive index of the upper space is $n_1$=1 (air) and the grating is in $n_2$ grating material, while the substrate is with $n_3$=1.46 refractive index. In the following we assume that the deepest groove goes through the grating material layer, i.e., the thickness of the grating layer is $h_1$+$h_2$. We require that only the diffraction orders for $m$=0 and $m$= ±1 can propagate inside the substrate, then the grating period $d$ is chosen by the grating equation taking into account the demand for the total internal reflection. The difficulties in the lithographic fabrication processes are related to the ratios of widths and depths of the structure. Our interest now was to maximize the first order diffraction efficiency $\eta_1$ and simultaneously keep the aspect ratio as small as possible by optimizing the transition points $x_{1,2,3}$ and depths $h_{1,2}$. After the optimization high first-order efficiencies were obtained, as illustrated in Table 2. The diffraction efficiency 100% can be achieved by a proper selection of the parameters. A fundamental constraint, especially in the case of thin light guides, is that the lateral dimension of the grating cannot be more than twice the light guide thickness when $(\theta_{41}$=$45^\circ$) propagation angle is assumed inside of a light guide. Otherwise, the beam reflected from the bottom surface hits back to the incoupling grating and becomes coupled out with the incoupling efficiency. The advantage of the proposed grating structure is that it has only vertical sidewalls and a high asymmetric structure.

## 5. SLANTED LIGHT INCOUPLERS

As introduced before, asymmetric grating structures are important for light incoupling. One of the simplest asymmetric gratings is a slanted grating and the replication technology for mass quantities is introduced here by using SiO₂ and Nickel masters as moulds. Furthermore, here we introduce a triangular slanted grating structure which provides a very flat incoupling efficiency as a function of incidence angle of the incoming light beam.

### 5.1 Replication by SiO₂ moulds

The replication technology was developed for binary slanted gratings. The grating masters were manufactured on a 5" x 5" x 0.09" SiO₂ planar substrate. First the substrate was coated with 80 nm chrome layer added with 20 nm thick low reflective chrome layer for the antireflection. Next the substrate was spin coated with Shipley S1805 photoresist followed by a baking process. Then the resist layer was patterned with a well known holographic recording using an Argon-ion laser with the wavelength of $\lambda$=363.8 nm with the target period of $d$=405 nm. The resist was then developed at 21°C in Shipley MF-351 developer (NaOH) and distilled water solution (1:5) for 60 seconds. After the development, the resist profile shown in Figure 11 was obtained having a good resist line roughness and a slight positive slope. The resist profile was transferred into the Cr layer by a chlorine dry etch process and the obtained Cr profile is shown in Figure 12. The grating has an excellent vertical profile shape for the following reactive ion beam etching (RIBE) with freon based chemistry. In the RIBE etching process ionized Argon beam is directed to the substrate with an oblique angle of incidence. The selectivity between SiO₂ and Cr is increased by adding reactive gas (freon) to the etching chamber (like in a normal reactive ion etching) and after the RIBE etching to the vertical grating depth of $h$=330 was obtained (Cr mask was removed by a standard wet etching process). Then the mould was anti adhesion treated and the grating structures were replicated on plastic substrate using a high refractive index resin ($n$=1.71@525 nm) from Mitsubishi Gas Chemical¹¹. After the separation of the mould and the plastic substrate the grating profile illustrated in Figure 13 was obtained. This grating profile was used to couple light into a light guide. The experimental diffraction efficiency of ~80% was obtained. Moreover, we found out, that the mould lifetime is long and can be used in hundreds of replication steps easily. We also studied how much the grating can be slanted so that the replication still can be performed with the chosen grating period. This depends of course on the vertical depth of the grating, which we now


limited to $h = 300$ nm for our test. We observed that the gratings can be replicated up to $50^\circ$ slanting angle before the grating line bottom starts to be ripped off in plastic. This is sufficiently far away from the practical slanting angles that are between $25$-$40^\circ$ for most of the applications

### 5.2 Slanted grating replication by Nickel tools

We also studied if it is possible to manufacture the slanted gratings using Nickel instead of $\text{SiO}_2$ masters. Nickel tools are superior since the first Nickel tool can be used as a master for the second and third generation Nickel tools, which leads to cheap master price for mass manufacturing of slanted gratings. The test was done by manufacturing a $\text{SiO}_2$ master as described in Section 5.1. After an anti-adhesion treatment the mould was ready for the electroforming. Firstly 50 nm thick Nickel layer was sputtered on the $\text{SiO}_2$ master and then we used an industrial electroforming line to grow $\sim$300 $\mu$m thick Nickel tool on the $\text{SiO}_2$ master. The Nickel tool was then easily separated from the master, which was a surprising result because of the large grating area of $\sim$50 mm x 110 mm (a lot of problems were expected in the separation). A SEM picture of the separated Nickel tool is shown in Figure 14 (left). The third generation Nickel shim was anti-adhesion treated and used as a mould to replicate the slanted grating with UV replication technology into NOA61 optical adhesive. The replication result is shown in Figure 14 (right). To our knowledge this is the first time that anyone has replicated slanted gratings successfully in large quantities in Nickel, and continued with the UV replication process.

![](./images/811938912858537985_12.jpg)

Fig. 14. Manufactured Nickel tool (left) and replicated grating from Nickel (right). The grating period is $d$=420 nm, vertical depth of $h$=290 nm and the slanting angle of $25^\circ$.

The results presented here offer great potential for the manufacturing of slanted diffraction gratings by a simple replication technology. The gratings have applications in the efficient incoupling and outcoupling of radiation with light guides, for example in virtual displays and backlights.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

In this paper several light incoupler grating structures were described. They show a lot of potential to produce relatively flat incoupling structures on light guides. The used light source spectra and the incoupling geometry define strongly the grating type which is the best for the desired application. We expect that the introduced grating types and the replication technology for slanted gratings can be used in many commercial consumer products due the high efficiency performance and the low end price. Furthermore, the usage of high refractive index plastic is feasible in mass replication purposes.
