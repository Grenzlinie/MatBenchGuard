# Fine structure of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations and the mechanical properties of NiAl in the hard orientation

M.J. Mills$^{\text{a}}$, J.E. Angelo$^{\text{a}}$, M.S. Daw$^{\text{a}}$, J.D. Weinburg$^{\text{b}}$, D.B. Miracle$^{\text{c}}$

$^{\text{a}}$Sandia National Laboratories, Livermore, CA 94551-0969, USA
$^{\text{b}}$Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA
$^{\text{c}}$Wright Laboratory, Wright-Patterson AFB, OH 45433, USA

## Abstract

The motion of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations is thought to play an important role in the deformation of NiAl single crystals when oriented along a $\langle 100\rangle$ crystal direction ("hard" orientation). In the present work, the structure of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations has been determined on several different length scales, using high resolution and weak-beam transmission electron microscopy. The observations indicate that $a\langle 011\rangle$ edge dislocations decompose into two separate $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations along the edge orientation, and that the overall shape of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation loops is non-planar. The embedded atom method has been used to study the energy and stability of $a\langle 011\rangle$ edge dislocations, and the effects of the stress state and temperature on the dislocation core structure and mobility. A possible mechanism for the motion of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations will be discussed, and related to the fracture and deformation behavior of NiAl in the hard orientation.

Keywords: Dislocation; Mechanical properties; Nickel; Aluminium

---

## 1. Introduction

The intermetallic compound NiAl(B2 structure) has a number of attractive properties which make it promising as a basis for alloys designed for high temperature applications. These properties include good high-temperature strength, high thermal conductivity, and both oxidation and corrosion resistance. For single crystals, the highest strength is achieved for deformation along $\langle 001\rangle$ directions—the "hard" orientation (see refs. [1] and [2] for comprehensive reviews of the physical and mechanical properties of NiAl).

For compression in the hard orientation, yield strengths exceeding 1 GPa are obtained at lower temperatures. However, an important problem is the lack of tensile ductility at lower temperatures. The onset of macroscopic plastic flow in tension occurs at about 600 K. Above this brittle-to-ductile transition temperature (BDTT), rapid softening is observed as a function of temperature, and a strong strain rate sensitivity is also reported. Several mechanisms have been offered to explain the onset of ductility and the intermediate temperature flow behavior. Recent transmission electron microscopy (TEM) investigations on deformed single crystals have clarified the active dislocations in the hard orientation [3,4]. At temperatures below the BDTT, $a\langle 111\rangle$ dislocations are prominent. However, above the BDTT, the density of $a\langle 111\rangle$ dislocations falls dramatically and, instead, $a\langle 011\rangle$ and $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations become dominant. Therefore, it has been suggested that either the glide of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations [3-5] or the climb of $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations [6] is responsible for deformation above the BDTT. The deformation rates achievable by the climb mechanism would seem to be too small to accommodate conventional deformation rates at temperatures near the BDTT (which corresponds to only $0.2T_{\text{m}}$, i.e. the melting point for stoichiometric NiAl) [7]. Therefore, the glide of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations would appear to be the most plausible deformation mode in hard-oriented crystals at intermediate temperatures.

If the motion of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations does control deformation in this regime, then this raises several important questions: (a) why is the motion of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations so difficult, leading to the extremely high yield strengths, and (b) what is the cause of the large

temperature and strain rate sensitivities? Based on previous conventional and weak-beam TEM studies [3-5], the answers to these questions are not obviously related to the core structure of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations, since no apparent dissociation (planar or otherwise) has been reported. Atomistic calculations using the embedded atom method (EAM) have indicated that there are large local displacements associated with the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation [8,9], suggesting that $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations should have a large Peierls stress for glide. While this could explain the high yield strengths, the strong temperature and strain rate sensitivities have not been explained on this basis.

In this paper, we will summarize the results of an extensive study of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations in NiAl. The overall dislocation morphology is described based on conventional TEM observations which suggest that edge dislocations are the slowest moving dislocation components.

High resolution TEM (HRTEM) observations of edge dislocations have revealed that $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations adopt a non-planar configuration, by decomposing into two separate $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations. The decomposition occurs on a scale smaller than that which is directly interpretable using weak-beam TEM. However, by coupling the weak-beam observations with multi-beam dynamical image simulations, it is shown that this decomposition is detectable and does occur for $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations of near-edge character. Both molecular static and dynamic calculations using the EAM also have been performed to explore the energetics and mobility of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations, and these results are related to the observed dislocation structures. Finally, the implications of these experimental and theoretical findings are discussed with respect to possible explanations for the macroscopic mechanical behavior.

## 2. Experimental and computational techniques

The microstructure produced by deformation of oriented bicrystals of stoichiometric NiAl was studied in this investigation. The bicrystals consisted of two single crystals (denoted A and B) diffusion bonded together. The compression axis and the grain boundary normal were parallel to the [001] direction and the [232] direction in crystals A and B respectively. Based on a previous investigation [5], a high density of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations is produced in crystal A near the bicrystal boundary. As described in detail elsewhere [5,7], this geometry was intended to promote the activation of non-$\langle 010\rangle$ slip in crystal A, through both elastic and plastic incompatibilities, while maintaining an overall "hard" orientation.

With respect to the present study, the important consequence of this geometry is that a high density of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations is produced in crystal A, within a millimeter or so from the bicrystal boundary. Therefore, this region of the sample was ideal for the detailed analysis of the structure of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations undertaken in this work. Slip on two orthogonal slip systems, i.e. $a[01\overline{1}]$ (on (011)) and $a[011]$ on (011), was found by slip trace analysis. Thin foils for HRTEM imaging were prepared by spark cutting slices of crystal A normal to the [100] direction, so that the $a[01\overline{1}]$ and $a[011]$ dislocations could be viewed "end on". Thin foils for the weak-beam investigations were prepared by slicing crystal A normal to the (011) slip plane for the $a[01\overline{1}]$ dislocations. It should be noted that the $a[011]$ dislocations can then be viewed with their slip planes end on. HRTEM images were obtained using a Jeol 4000EX model operating at 400 kV, while weak-beam observations were conducted using a Jeol 1200EX model operating at 120 kV.

The EAM calculations [10] were performed utilizing potentials developed specifically for NiAl [11]. The static calculations were conducted by first establishing the atomic positions around the cores of the dislocations, assuming anisotropic elasticity. The typical computational supercell was a cylinder with a radius of 10 nm. The atoms in an outer ring of the supercell (1 nm thick) were held fixed during the relaxations, to maintain the long-range elastic stress field around the dislocation. To compare the energies of core structures which might have different local compositions, the chemical potentials of Ni and Al were used, referenced to the stoichiometric NiAl (see ref. [12] for an example of this procedure applied to $\mathrm{Ni}_{3} \mathrm{Al}$ ). In practice, this was accomplished by transforming the functions such that the energies for each Ni and Al atom in a perfect crystal were equal to their respective chemical potentials, without altering the physical content of the functions. Molecular dynamics calculations also were conducted to explore the effects of stress and temperature on the core structures. Stresses were applied by imposing a state of pure shear on the (011) slip plane of the $a[01\overline{1}]$ dislocations, then holding the atoms in the outer ring of the supercell fixed.

Simulations of the weak-beam images were conducted using a multi-beam modification of the Howie-Whelan equations, including the "column approximation", after those proposed previously [13]. The extinction distances were obtained using the analytic fit parameters listed in the ref. [14]. To determine the elastic displacement fields for both the image simulations and the EAM calculations, the anisotropic elasticity theory [15] was used with the elastic constants $c_{11}=200 \mathrm{GPa}, c_{12}=120 \mathrm{GPa}$ and $c_{44}=120 \mathrm{GPa}$. The simulations shown below were performed for a foil 100

nm thick, with a foil normal and beam direction of [011], and a line orientation of [30 1 $\bar{1}$], which is close to the pure edge character. All simulated images have been scaled to the same maximum intensity value to allow a comparison of the relative image contrast.

## 3. TEM observations

An example of the overall dislocation microstructure generated in crystal A of the bicrystal specimens is shown in Fig. 1. Several truncated dislocation loops (labelled as A) can be seen, which have Burgers' vectors of $a[0\overline{1}\overline{1}]$. The glide plane for these dislocations is (011), which is also parallel to the TEM foil plane. The appearance of these dislocation loops suggests the expansion of dislocations away from sources. A prominent feature of the loops is that they preferentially elongate in the edge orientation. This preferred orientation cannot be explained based on line energy considerations, since the screw orientation actually has the lowest line energy [5,16]. These considerations suggest that the edge orientation has a relatively low mobility when compared with the other dislocation characters. It should be noted that the near-edge portions of the loops are composed of relatively straight segments separated by steps of mixed charcter (indicated as S in the figure), suggesting the possibility of a "kink-like" mechanism for the propagation of the near-edge dislocations.

It also should be noted that the marked extension of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations along the edge orientation noted in these bicrystal specimens has not been identified clearly in other investigations of hard-oriented single crystals [3,4]. In these previous studies, use of TEM foil planes other than the glide plane for the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations may have made determination of the overall dislocation morphology problematic. In any event, the microstructure present in the bicrystals enables detailed study of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation structures.

While these dislocation loops are lying on average parallel to their (011) glide planes, their overall shape is not planar. This has been determined by tilting experiments, and also can be seen clearly by inspection of several of the dislocations labelled B in the Fig. 1. These dislocations hae Burgers' vectors of $a[011]$, which are orthogonal to the A dislocations. Therefore, the nominal $(01\overline{1})$ glide plane of the B dislocations should be seen edge on in this view. However, several of the B dislocations are clearly not straight in this view, and deviate from the trace of the $(01\overline{1})$ plane. These deviations are indicative of extensive climb or cross-slip out of the glide plane.

To determine whether the low apparent mobility of the edges is due to an intrinsic feature related to the cores of these dislocations, HRTEM observations were conducted to observe the edge dislocations end on. Molecular dynamics calculations also have been performed to explore the effects of temperature and applied stress on the possible mechanisms for motion of the $a[0\overline{1}1]$ dislocation core. The applied stress state explored in the present study is one of pure shear relative to the nominal (022) glide plane. This shear stress would be present for deformation in the hard orientaiton. The result of such a calculation performed at 0 K and an imposed shear strain of 0.04 $\mu$m (approximatley 1250 MPa) is shown in Fig. 5(c). The core is no longer in the relatively planar configuration of Fig. 5(b), as can be seen by sighting along the (022) planes. In place of the open, distorted octahedral unit, two smaller triangular units are formed, as indicated in Fig. 5(c). These units are identical to the cores of individual $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations which also have been studied recently using EAM calculations [8,9,18]. The two $a\langle 010\rangle$ cores have different atom types at their cores—being comprised of Ni and Al triangles respectively. Thus, the perfect $a[0\overline{1}1]$ core has decomposed into narrowly separated $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations under the applied shear stress, which is in agreement with the TEM observations described above. Since the decom-position takes place by the coalescence and glide of the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ cores on their respective slip planes. The fact that glide has occurred can be seen by noting that the centers of the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations have shifted slightly to the left with respect to the original location of the perfect core in Fig. 5(b). Once this short-range glide associated with the decomposition takes place, additional motion of the decomposed core is not observed under this imposed shear stress, since the individual $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations do not have a glide force acting on them.

The driving force for this decomposition is not actually related to the applied stress state, since this same reaction occurs for molecular dynamics calcula-tions conducted at finite temperature and no applied stress. Instead, the decomposition appears to occur to lower the energy of the core itself. The total energy of the dislocation $(E_{T}(r))$ within a given radius $r$, based on the EAM has been computed using

$$
E_{\mathrm{T}}(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{T}}(r)} E_{i}-\mu_{\mathrm{Ni}} N_{\mathrm{Ni}}(r)-\mu_{\mathrm{Al}} N_{\mathrm{Al}}(r) \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{i}$ is the energy per atom, and $\mu_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{Al}}$ are the chemical potentials for Ni and Al respectively. $N_{\mathrm{Ni}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{Al}}$ are the number of Ni and Al atoms within the radius of interest, with the total number $N_{\mathrm{T}}=N_{\mathrm{Ni}}+N_{\mathrm{Al}}$. It should be noted that this calculation is valid since the present EAM potential has been partitioned such that the energies associated with the Ni and Al atoms in a perfect crystal are equivalent to their corresponding chemical potentials.

In general, the value of $E_{\mathrm{T}}(r)$ should include contributions from (a) the elastic strain (i.e. self-) energy, (b) the elastic interaction between the dislocations (for the decomposed case) and (c) the energy associated with the cores of the dislocations. In the present case, the elastic strain energy for a perfect $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation is the same as that for two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations based on anisotropic elasticity calculations [16]. For a radius of 9 nm, the energy of the relaxed, perfect core of Fig. 5(b) is $4.2\times 10^{-9}$ J m$^{-1}$, while the energy for the decomposed core is $3.6\times 10^{-9}$ J m$^{-1}$. The second value was determined following relaxation of the configuration of Fig. 5(c), after removing the imposed shear strain. Therefore, the energy of the decomposed core is less than that for the perfect core, despite the fact that the decomposed core will have an additional interaction energy term (contribution (b) above).

This result is consistent with the qualitative observation that the core of the perfect dislocation is more "open" (i.e. less highly coordinated) than that for the decomposed case. It is also noted that the interaction energy based on isotropic [19] and anisotropic elasticity calculations [20] between $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations, for the decomposed core in the edge orientation, is not dependent on their separation. This behaviour also has been verified using the EAM calculations [21], which implies that there is no restoring force tending to keep the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations together.

### 4. Discussion

Using HRTEM and weak-beam TEM, it has been observed that the $a\langle 011\rangle$ edge dislocation decomposes into a non-planar configuration consisting of two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations. The reason for the decomposition appears to be to reduce the core energy associated with

the more "open" structure found for the perfect dis- location based on the EAM calculations. Conse- quently, the perfect $a\langle 011\rangle$ core is a metastable structure which, in the molecular dynamics calcula- tions, readily decomposes upon the application of either stress and/or temperature. The decomposition transforms the perfect dislocation core, which is poten- tially mobile, to a core on which effective glide forces no longer act. After decomposition, the edge disloca- tion is effectively sessile with respect to motion via a conservative glide process.

However, it should be noted that, for deformation in the hard orientation, there is a climb force acting on one of the $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations. The climb of one of the $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations will produce a local vacancy con centration which is different from that at the other $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocation, which thus acts as a nearby sink for these point defects. As a result, an effective osmotic force [19,22] will operate on the second $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislo cation. Therefore, at sufficiently high temperatures in the hard orientation, the cooperative climb of the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations will tend to make them drift apart. Since there is no elastic interaction between the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations, they may climb apart limited only by the kinetics for transporting vacancies from one $a\langle 010\rangle$ core to the other core. Such a process may explain the somewhat larger separation between $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations observed in the HRTEM image of Fig. 2, and the much more widely decomposed con- figurations detected using weak-beam TEM. It is interesting to speculate that the transport of vacancies in such a cooperative climb process may be possible via pipe diffusion, which may occur at significantly lower temperatures than does diffusion in the bulk crystal.

This decomposition reaction is only likely along the edge orientation of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation for two principal reasons. First, only in this orientation do the $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations lie on low index planes (i.e. $\{020\}$ planes) on which decomposition can take place readily by glide. Secondly, for all other orientations of the overall $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations, there would be an attrac tive elastic interaction between the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ disloca tions, tending to resist the decomposition [19,23]. Consequently, the mixed and screw orientations for the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation are less likely to undergo the decomposition reaction. This reasoning explains the elongated shape of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation loops observed in Fig. 1. The decomposition of the edge dislocations also may contribute to the non-planar nature of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation loops, as evidenced by the B dislocations in Fig. 1. Let us consider that, after decomposition of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ edge dislocation, this component of the dislocation no longer has a single, identifiable glide plane. Meanwhile, the screw portions of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation can presumably undergo cross-slip. Consequently, there is little constraint on maintaining a singular glide plane, and the entire dislocation structure can become non-planar.

The fact that the edge orientation for the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation readily decomposes and becomes sessile implies that simple glide of these dislocations is not possible. Therefore, propagation of these dislocations must occur by the motion of non-edge components. The presence of steps of mixed character along near- edge portions of the dislocations (see the features labelled as S in Fig. 1) are suggestive of kink-like features. Thus, the lateral movement of these steps could accomplish the propagation of the near-edge portions of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations. This concept is similar to the "superlink" mechanism which has recently been proposed [24] and simulated [25,26] for the motion of superdislocations in $L1_{2}$ compounds.

Thermal activation is likely to play an important role in the lateral motion of these steps for at least two reasons. First, such motion would require the constric- tion of the decomposed edges, which will generally involve climb (if climb has contributed to the decom- position). It should be noted that the constriction of such decomposed segments could be mediated by pipe diffusion, which may explain how the climb-controlled motion of the steps could occur at relatively low homologous temperatures. Secondly, extensive climb or cross-slip of mobile steps will be necessary, because of the non-planar nature of the overall dislocation lines.

Such a thermally activated mechanism would explain several aspects of deformation in the hard orientation. The inactivity of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations at low temperatures can be rationalized, since the formation of the sessile, decomposed edge components will cause locking of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation sources. Only at higher temperatures will propagation of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ edges be possible, enabling these sources to operate. Secondly, at higher temperatures (above the BDTT), the yield strength should decrease dramatically with tempera- ture as motion of non-edge components becomes easier. Such a mechanism also implies a mobility- controlled deformation process, which is consistent with the observation of sigmoidal primary creep transi- ents at elevated temperatures (1273 K), in which the creep rate is initially low and increased with the strain [27]. Such a creep transient implies that dislocation motion and multiplication is sluggish. The observation of yield points in constant-strain-rate tests also are consistent with this interpretation [1,2,4]. Finally, if dislocation motion is mediated by climb, then a strong strain rate dependence is expected. While an exhaus- tive examination of the strain rate dependence of hard- oriented NiAl has not been performed, there are

indications that both the yield strength and the BDTT increase with increasing strain rate [1,2,4].

## 5. Conclusions
TEM studies have revealed that $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations in NiAl decompose into two separate $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations with orthogonal Burgers' vectors. The decomposition appears to occur preferentially along near-edge portions of the $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations. A variety of decomposition distances have been observed, from approximately 1 nm to over 100 nm. The decomposition tends to inhibit the motion of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations, as evidenced by the observed elongation of the loops along the edge orientation. These observations are consistent with EAM calculations which indicate that a perfect $a\langle 011\rangle$ edge dislocation is indeed a metastable configuration. Upon the application of shear stresses or moderate temperatures, the perfect dislocation undergoes a core transformation which yields a nascent decomposed structure. This decomposition is driven by a lowering of the core energy of the dislocation. A variety of distances separating the two $a\langle 010\rangle$ dislocations may arise, as a result of a coordinated climb process. The decomposition of $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocations effectively locks the edge portions of these dislocations, suggesting that motion of these dislocations is controlled by the lateral movement of non-edge components of the dislocation. It is speculated that this "kink-like" motion will be sluggish and strongly thermally activated, as a result of the constraint of the decomposition on the movement of the steps, as well as the non-planar nature of the overall $a\langle 011\rangle$ dislocation structures.
