## ABSTRACT
Pile-up interactions in the $\gamma$ phase of MC2 nickel-based superalloy have been studied by both atomic force microscopy and transmission electron microscopy. The examinations of surface slip-line patterns and the fine structure of disloca- tion pile-ups enable a minimum distance between pile-up slip planes to be derived. The behaviour of two pile-ups gliding in opposite directions has been simulated. The critical applied stress required for these pile-ups to cross over in the bulk crystal has been determined in good agreement with the microscopy observations.

## §1. INTRODUCTION
In highly concentrated solid solutions, plastic deformation is highly hetero- geneous on a micrometre scale with the propagation of dislocation pile-ups (Olfe and Neuhäuser 1988, Schwink 1988). This behaviour is often accounted for by the shearing of short-range ordered domains. As a result of this local order, a high friction stress acts on dislocations moving in the bulk and prohibits dislocations from slipping individually on crystallographic planes (Clement 1984, Reinhardt *et al.* 1987, Gerold and Karnthaler 1989, Schwander *et al.* 1992). Crystals of MC2 nickel-based superalloy $\gamma$ phase, referred to as MC2($\gamma$), are composed of no less than eight constituents with a high Cr concentration (26 at.%). Dislocation pile- ups in this material have already been studied by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and considerable data have been accumulated. Moreover the possibility of deforming the specimen inside a transmission electron microscope and observe *in situ* the pile-up propagation can provide valuable information on the hardening mechanisms and frictional forces involved in such a material (Jouiad 1996). In this case, the pile-up head acts as a probe scanning the solid solution and gives quanti- tative information about material toughness on a micrometre scale (Jouiad *et al.*1998).

In this paper, pile-up interactions have been studied *in situ* by both atomic force microscopy (AFM) and TEM techniques. The first method allows the obser- vation of the pile-up head at the surface and the analysis of the fine slip-line patterns on a nanometre scale; the second method provides interesting information about the fine structure of dislocations in pile-ups, giving rise to slip lines at the surface.

## §3. IN SITU ATOMIC FORCE MICROSCOPY OBSERVATIONS
MC2($\gamma$) samples have been studied by AFM using an experimental device with in-situ observation of the surface during deformation (Small et al. 1995, Coupeau et al. 1998a,b). Taking advantage of the atomic resolution offered by this class of scanning probe microscopy (Binnig et al. 1983, 1986, Giessibl and Binnig 1992), this apparatus is particularly suitable for analysing the fine-slip line structure on a nanometre scale, for following the process of dislocation emergence and for observing in situ the evolution of material surfaces during plastic deformation.

The MC2($\gamma$) samples were cut with the compression axis parallel to the cube axis [001] and observation face parallel to the (100) planes. The primary slip system in MC2($\gamma$) is the $\{111\}\langle 110\rangle$ with a Burgers vector $(a/2)[110]$, where $a$ is the lattice parameter. From the Schmid factor, slip lines are expected to lie along the $\langle 011\rangle$ direction at $\pm 45^{\circ}$ to the compression axis (figure 1). The unit step height corresponding to the emergence of one dislocation is equal to the Burgers vector component perpendicular to the surface:

$$
h_{\mathrm{e}}=b_{\perp}=b \cos \left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{a}{2}=0.176 \mathrm{~nm}.
$$

## §4. TRANSMISSION ELECTRON MICROSCOPY OBSERVATIONS
Both post-mortem and *in-situ* deformation observations have been performed in a side-entry JEOL 200 CX with applied voltage of 200 kV.

### 4.1. Post-mortem observations
MC2($\gamma$) samples used for the post-mortem TEM observations have been deformed previously up to $2\%$ by tensile tests at $500^{\circ}$C with a strain rate of approximately $10^{-4}\text{s}^{-1}$. Thin foils have then been cut with a $\{111\}$ crystallographic plane surface.

Figure 6 is a bright-field image with $\mathbf{g}=[1\overline{1}1]$, taken at $T=25^{\circ}$C, which corresponds to a characteristic dislocation microstructure of plastic deformation, for temperatures ranging from 20 to $500^{\circ}$C. In this case, deformation occurs principally by propagation of dislocations with $\mathbf{b}=(a/2)[1\overline{1}0]$ Burgers vector on (111) crystallographic planes. Few cross-slip events are observed; thus the pile-up A has moved consecutively in the (111) and $(11\overline{1})$ crystallographic planes before returning to its original plane. Many pile-ups exist in this area; they are composed of a few tens of dislocations and are sometimes locked in the bulk without any apparent obstacle, as can be seen at P, Q and R. In some cases multipoles are created owing to the interaction of two pile-ups with opposite Burgers vectors as at B and D. Plastic deformation is heterogeneous with work-hardened areas of high dislocation density and other areas where plastic mechanisms do not seem to occur, consistent with the AFM observations presented in the previous section. The presence of sources emitting dislocation loops can be noted at S. Finally, at the tail of the moving pile-ups, a few dislocations a, b and c are able to move individually and independently.

In such a micrograph, the smallest distance $d$ between pile-up slip planes is determined, for example in area X, to be

$$
d=25\pm2\ \text{nm}.
$$

### 4.2. In-situ deformation
*In-situ* deformation by tensile tests along the [100] crystallographic direction have been performed using a JEOL 200 CX at applied voltage of 200 kV using samples of a specific design.

In figure 7, two pile-ups moving under stress in the same direction on parallel (111) crystallographic planes, are observed. The evolution of dislocation equilibrium positions is shown being noted as a, b, c, d, e, f and i, j, k for pile-ups A and B respectively. The rate of either pile-up head is approximately $600\ \text{nm}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The slight shift of slip plane traces at the surface allows one to measure, after tilt correction (Vanderschaeve 1981), the distance $d$ between two neighbouring glide planes to be $39\pm3\ \text{nm}$. It is worth noting that this value is higher than the distance determined by post-mortem observations.

Each pile-up is composed of $n$ dislocations and glides on a plane separated from the other by distance $d$ (figure 8). The origin $\mathrm{O}$ is the symmetric configuration point. The dislocations are considered to be perpendicular to the $(x O y)$ plane and both screw and edge Burgers vector components are considered. The dislocations are numbered from 1 to $n$ (1 is the nearest dislocation from the origin in each pile-up) and $x(i)$ is the position of the $i$ th dislocation of one pile-up. Because of symmetry, the position of the $j$ th dislocation in the second pile-up is $-x(j)$ where $x(j)$ is the position of the $j$ th dislocation in the first pile-up. The force acting on dislocation $i$ is the sum of forces due to the applied stress $\tau_{\mathrm{a}}$ and the friction $\tau_{\mathrm{f}}$ stress, and interactions with the other dislocations. The interactions between two dislocations $i$ and $j$ are given as follows (Hull 1975): for two edge dislocations of the same pile-up,

$$
F_{x}^{\mathrm{se}}=\frac{\mu b_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}}{2 \pi(1-\nu)} \frac{1}{x(i)-x(j)} ;
$$

for two edge dislocations of different pile-ups,

$$
F_{x}^{\mathrm{de}}=-\frac{\mu b_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}}{2 \pi(1-\nu)} r \frac{r^{2}-d^{2}}{\left(r^{2}+d^{2}\right)^{2}} ;
$$

for two screw dislocations of the same pile-up,

$$
F_{x}^{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{\mu b_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{2 \pi} \frac{1}{x(i)+x(j)} ;
$$

for two screw dislocations of different pile-ups,

$$
F_{x}^{\mathrm{ds}}=-\frac{\mu b_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{2 \pi} \frac{r}{r^{2}-d^{2}}.
$$

Here $r=x(i)=x(j)$, and $b_{\mathrm{e}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{s}}$ are the edge and screw components respectively of the Burgers vector.

In these alloys, a high frictional stress acts on a few dislocation pairs at the pile-up head and often originates from the short- and/or long-range ordered domains. The number of leading pairs varies according to the material considered and the distribution of the dislocations in such pile-ups differs greatly from an ideal configuration (Hazzledine 1967). ## §6. CONCLUSIONS

From these AFM and TEM observations, the following conclusions concerning the plastic deformation of the MC2 nickel-based superalloy $\gamma$ phase can be deduced. Plasticity occurs by the propagation of planar dislocation pile-ups and is highly heterogeneous with dense step clusters separated by wide terraces at the surface. A minimum distance between pile-up slip planes of approximately 20-40 nm has been found.

The behaviour of two pile-ups gliding in opposite directions has been observed and simulated. The necessary critical applied stress for two pile-ups to cross over to cross over has been determined as a function of distance, for various numbers of dislocations. It shows unambiguously the difficulty that pile-ups in moving in the bulk crystal at a distance smaller than a few tens of nanometres. It is worth noting that increasing the dislocation numbers in each pile-up induces a decrease in the necessary applied stress. These experiments and simulations confirm that pile-up effects govern plastic deformation in these alloys.
