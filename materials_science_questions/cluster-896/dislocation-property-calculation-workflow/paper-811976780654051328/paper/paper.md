Résumé. — La méthode décrite par Rey et Saada (1976) pour calculer les propriétés élastiques des réseaux de dislocations plans et périodiques est appliquée entre autres cas au réseau nid d'abeilles. Un accent particulier est mis sur le calcul de l'énergie élastique. D'autres applications sont suggérées.

Abstract. — The method outlined by Rey and Saada (1976) to calculate the elastic properties of periodic planar dislocation networks is applied to cases such as honeycomb networks. Emphasis is put on the calculation of the elastic energy. Applications are suggested.

1. Introduction. — In a recent paper [1] the authors have given a discussion of the elastic properties of planar periodic dislocation networks. Assuming the network is in the plane $x_{1}ox_{2}$ (see Fig. 1a and b) one may define following Kröner [2] a dislocation density tensor in the form $\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{1},x_{2})\ \delta(x_{3})$. Since $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ is periodic in the plane $x_{1}ox_{2}$ it can be developed as a Fourier sum :

$$
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{1},x_{2}) = \sum_{\boldsymbol{g}} \boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\boldsymbol{g}} \exp 2 \pi i \boldsymbol{g} \boldsymbol{\rho} . \tag{1a}
$$

Here $\boldsymbol{g}$ refers to the reciprocal vectors of the periodic dislocation network.

The incompatibility $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is then easily calculated as :

$$
\boldsymbol{\eta}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}) = \mathbf{A}(x_{1},x_{2})\ \delta(x_{3}) + \mathbf{B}(x_{1},x_{2})\ \delta'(x_{3}) \tag{1b}
$$

$\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are periodic in the plane $x_{1}ox_{2}$ with the same periods. They can be developed in Fourier sums, the corresponding coefficients being $\mathbf{A}^{(\boldsymbol{g})}$ and $\mathbf{B}^{(\boldsymbol{g})}$. Complete general expressions for $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ have been given in [1].

The stress functions $\chi$ is then calculated in all cases of interest and from it the self energy can be obtained.

In this paper we wish to apply these results to the case of more complicated networks such as those described in figures 1a and 1b. Section 2 of this paper will be devoted to the derivation of such formulae and their application to a simple case. It will be shown that the method outlined in [1] leads to simple calculations which are more general and easier to perform

### 2. Self energy of planar dislocation networks formed from two independent sets of dislocations with no long-range stresses. — 2.1 GEOMETRY. —
We shall consider as in [1] (see Fig. 1a) two sets of dislocations such that each set $(p)$ is characterized by :
$\mathbf{t}^{(p)}$ Unit vector along the dislocation line.
$\lambda^{(p)}$ Vector perpendicular to $\lambda^{(p)}$ characterizing the period of $p$th set.
$\mathbf{b}^{(p)}$ The common Burgers vector of the dislocations.
$\mathbf{g}^{(p)}$ The reciprocal lattice vector.

All these symbols have been defined in [1]. We need to add to them :
$$
a^{(p)}=P_{p} Q_{p} \tag{2a}
$$

$P_{p}, Q_{p}$ and the unit cell are defined in figure $1 a$.

Having independent sets means [5] that the following equations (2a) and (2b) have to be verified simultaneously :
$$
\mathbf{t}^{(1)} × \mathbf{t}^{(2)} \neq 0 \tag{2b}
$$
$$
\mathbf{b}^{(1)} × \mathbf{b}^{(2)} \neq 0. \tag{2c}
$$

It is convenient to make use of the angle $\varphi$ between $\mathbf{t}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{t}^{(2)}$.

As shown by Frank [4] this network may transform into a honeycomb network (Fig. 1b) with a unit cell made of three segments $\mathbf{l}^{(p)}$ such that the well-known node condition is verified.

Then as in [1] we define $\mathbf{a}^{(1)}, \mathbf{a}^{(2)} ; \mathbf{l}^{(1)}, \mathbf{l}^{(2)}, \mathbf{l}^{(3)}$ the dislocation segments having their origin at the node $\mathrm{N}$
$$
\mathbf{l}=-\mathbf{l}^{(3)}. \tag{3a}
$$

The node relation is
$$
\sum_{P} \mathbf{b}^{(p)}=0. \tag{3b}
$$

The corresponding dislocation tensors, incompatibility tensors and the corresponding $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ tensors have been defined and calculated in [1].

### 2.2 CALCULATION OF THE SELF ENERGY IN THE CASE OF A HONEYCOMB NETWORK. —
Following the method outlined in [1] and using identical symbols, we consider two identical planar periodic honeycomb dislocation networks exhibiting no long range stresses (i.e. such that $\mathbf{B}^{(0)}$ is 0) translated with respect to each other perpendicular to the plane of the network. Let $x_{1} o x_{2}$ be the plane of the network and $r$ the distance between both networks; their interaction energy $E$ is defined by Kröner [2]

$$
\begin{aligned}
E=\int_{\text {space }} \eta_{i j}\left(x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}-r\right) & × \\
& × \chi_{i j}\left(x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}\right) \mathrm{d} x_{1} \mathrm{~d} x_{2} \mathrm{~d} x_{3} \quad(4)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ and $\boldsymbol{\chi}$ are respectively the incompatibility tensor and the stress function. They have been calculated in [1]. Since they are periodic in $x_{1} o x_{2}$ with the same periods one may define an interaction energy per unit surface $E_{1}$ :

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1}=-\frac{1}{\left|a^{(1)} × a^{(2)}\right|} \int_{\text {unit cell }} & \eta_{i j}\left(x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}-r\right) × \\
& × \chi_{i j}\left(x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}\right) \mathrm{d} x_{1} \mathrm{~d} x_{2} \mathrm{~d} x_{3}. \quad(5 a)
\end{aligned}
$$

The self energy per unit surface $E_{\mathrm{S}}$ is obtained by the usual procedure
$$
E_{\mathrm{S}}=\frac{1}{2} \lim _{r \rightarrow 0} E_{1}. \tag{5b}
$$

It must be emphasized that the self energy defined in (5b) is not unique, but depends on where the mathematical cuts are made. Therefore the dislocation core energy must be added to the results we obtain. We shall come back to this point in our conclusion.

Using the results of [1] one obtains after a lengthy but simple calculation
$$
E_{1}=\sum_{\mathbf{g} \neq 0} E_{\mathbf{g}} \tag{6a}
$$
$$
E_{\mathbf{g}}=2 \mu\left(\Phi_{\mathbf{g}}+\Psi_{\mathbf{g}}+r \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\Psi_{\mathbf{g}}-\Phi_{\mathbf{g}}\right)\right) \tag{6b}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{\mathbf{g}} & =\frac{1}{32 \pi^{3} g^{3}} × \\
& ×\left(A_{i j}^{-\mathbf{g}} A_{i j}^{\mathbf{g}}+\frac{v}{1-v}|\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{A}|^{2}\right) \exp (-2 \pi g r) \quad(6 c)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Psi_{\mathbf{g}}= & \frac{1}{8 \pi g} × \\
& ×\left(B_{i j}^{-\mathbf{g}} B_{i j}^{\mathbf{g}}+\frac{v}{1-v}|\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{B}|^{2}\right) \exp (-2 \pi g r). \\
& \quad(6 d)
\end{aligned}
$$

In these formulae $g$ is the length of the reciprocal vector $\mathbf{g}, \mu$ is the shear modulus and $v$ the Poisson ratio.

To study $E_{1}$ when $r$ varies we notice the following $\Phi_{\mathbf{g}}$ is a linear combination of terms such as
$$
\frac{g_{k} g_{l}}{g^{3}} \alpha_{m n}^{-\mathbf{g}} \alpha_{p q}^{\mathbf{g}} \exp (-2 \pi g r)
$$

while
$\Psi_{\mathbf{g}}$ is a linear combination of terms such as
$$\frac{\alpha_{m n}^{-\mathbf{g}} \alpha_{p q}^{\mathbf{g}}}{g} \exp (-2 \pi g r),$$
therefore
$\Psi_{\mathbf{g}}$ and $\Phi_{\mathbf{g}}$ exhibit the same variation in $\mathbf{g}$ as
$$\frac{\left|\alpha_{i j}^{\mathbf{g}}\right|^{2}}{g} \exp (-2 \pi g r).$$

As shown in [1]
$$\left|\alpha_{i j}^{\mathbf{g}}\right|^{2}=\frac{\sin ^{2}(\pi \mathbf{g l})}{\pi^{2}\left|\mathbf{a}^{(1)} \times \mathbf{a}^{(2)}\right|^{2}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{p=2} \frac{l_{i}^{(p)} b_{j}^{(p)}}{\mathbf{g}\left(\mathbf{a}^{(p)}-\mathbf{l}\right)}\right)^{2}. \quad(7)$$

Two cases must be considered : If $\mathbf{g}$ is on a straight line $D^{(p)}$ such that
$$\mathbf{g}\left(\mathbf{a}_{p}-\mathbf{l}\right)=0\qquad(8)$$
$\sin (\pi \mathbf{g l})$ and $\mathbf{g}\left(\mathbf{a}_{p}-\mathbf{l}\right)$ tend to zero in the same way and $\left|\alpha_{i j}^{\mathbf{g}}\right|$ is independent of $\mathbf{g}$.
If $\mathbf{g}$ is not on $D^{(p)}$ then $\left|\alpha_{i j}^{\mathbf{g}}\right|$ decreases with $\mathbf{g}$ as $|\mathbf{g}|^{-1}$.

From these considerations it is clear that the sum (6a) converges for all values of $r$ except $r$ equal to zero.

For large values of $r$, the exponential term allows the sum to be restricted to the next nearest neighbours.

For small values of $r$, i.e. $r$ much smaller than the size of the network, the sum has to be calculated numerically. However the behaviour for very small values of $r$ is easily obtained by restricting the summation to the three straight lines $D^{(p)}$. The calculation is then formally similar to that made for sets of infinite dislocation lines. Therefore, for small $r$ :
$$E_{\mathrm{s}}=-K_{1} \log r+K_{2}.\qquad(9)$$

It must be emphasized that restricting the calculation of the sum (6a) to the set of points belonging to the three straight lines $D_{p}$ indeed gives the correct behaviour of $E_{1}$ (or $E_{\mathrm{s}}$) for small $r$. However the procedure gives only a rough approximation of the exact numerical values. To obtain these values a numerical computation is needed. As will be shown in section 2.4 the numerical calculation is very simple.

### 2.3 TWO SETS OF PARALLEL INFINITE DISLOCATIONS.
— In the case described in figure $1 a$ equations (5a) and (5b) hold. The calculation of $E_{1}$ (or $E_{\mathrm{s}}$) is very simply expressed in terms of the tensors $\mathbf{C}^{(p)}$ and $\mathbf{D}^{(p)}$ defined as :
$$C_{i l}^{(p)}=\operatorname{Sym}\left[\left(\varepsilon_{i j 1} g_{1}+\varepsilon_{i j 2} g_{2}\right) t_{l}^{(p)} b_{j}^{(p)}\right]\qquad(10a)$$
$$D_{i l}^{(p)}=\operatorname{Sym}\left[\varepsilon_{i j 3} t_{l}^{(p)} b_{j}^{(p)}\right].\qquad(10b)$$

Making use of the same method one obtains :
$$\begin{aligned}
E_{1}= & \frac{\mu}{\left|a^{(1)} \times a^{(2)}\right|} \sum_{p=1}^{p=2} \times \\
& \times\left[\left(F_{p}+G_{p}\right) S_{p}+\left(G_{p}-F_{p}\right) X_{p} \frac{\mathrm{d} S_{p}}{\mathrm{~d} X_{p}}\right] \quad(11 a)
\end{aligned}$$
$$F_{p}=\frac{a^{(p)}}{\left(g^{(p)}\right)^{2}}\left[C_{i j}^{(p)} C_{i j}^{(p)}+\frac{v}{1-v}\left|\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{C}^{(p)}\right|^{2}\right]\qquad(11b)$$
$$G_{p}=a^{(p)}\left[D_{i j}^{(p)} D_{i j}^{(p)}+\frac{v}{1-v}\left|\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{D}^{(p)}\right|^{2}\right]\qquad(11c)$$
$$S_{p}=-\frac{1}{2 \pi} \log \left[1-\exp \left(-2 \pi X_{p}\right)\right]\qquad(11d)$$
$$X_{p}=g^{(p)} r.\qquad(11e)$$

When $r \to 0$
$$S_{p} \simeq-\frac{1}{2 \pi} \log \left(2 \pi X_{p}\right)\qquad(12a)$$
$$X_{p} \frac{\mathrm{d} S_{p}}{\mathrm{~d} X_{p}} \simeq-\frac{1}{2 \pi}.\qquad(12b)$$

From which we obtain :
$$\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{s}}= & -\frac{\mu}{4 \pi\left|\mathbf{a}^{(1)} \times \mathbf{a}^{(2)}\right|} \sum_{p=1}^{p=2} \times \\
& \times\left[\left(F_{p}+G_{p}\right) \log \left(2 \pi X_{p}\right)+\left(G_{p}-F_{p}\right)\right]. \quad(13)
\end{aligned}$$

The result is very similar to that obtained in the previous section.

### 2.4 APPLICATION TO THE CASE OF A PURE TWIST BOUNDARY IN THE (111) PLANE OF F.C.C. METALS.
#### 2.4.1 Honeycomb network.
— The geometry is well known and the minimum energy configuration corresponds to the symmetrical position with screw dislocations of equal length $l$. In that case, both $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{A}$ and $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{B}$ are 0 and use of (5) and of formulae (6) gives the result :
$$E_{1}=\frac{9}{2} \frac{\mu b^{2}}{\pi^{3} l} f(x)\qquad(14a)$$
$$x=r / l\qquad(14b)$$
$$\begin{aligned}
f(x)= & \sum_{\substack{n_{1} \neq 0 \\
n_{2} \neq 0}} \times \\
& \times \frac{N^{3 / 2} \sin ^{2}\left(\pi\left(n_{1}+n_{2}\right) / 3\right) \exp \left(-4 / 3 \pi N^{1 / 2} x\right)}{\left(2 n_{1}-n_{2}\right)^{2}\left(n_{1}-n_{2}\right)^{2}\left(n_{1}+n_{2}\right)^{2}} \\
&.
\end{aligned}\qquad(14c)$$
$$N=\left|n_{1}^{2}+n_{2}^{2}-n_{1} n_{2}\right|\qquad(14d)$$
$n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ are integer numbers.

$f(x)$ has to be calculated numerically. We give in figure 2 the plot of $f(x)$ which for small values of $x$

$(x \lesssim 0.1)$ is a logarithmic function of $x$. For small values of $x$ :
$$
E_{\mathrm{I}}=0.17 \frac{\mu b^{2}}{l} \log \left(\frac{l}{2.6 r}\right). \quad(15)
$$

Since the twist angle $\theta$ is :
$$
\theta=\frac{b}{l \sqrt{3}}. \quad(16a)
$$

We may write for small values of $x$ :
$$
E_{\mathrm{I}}=0.28 \mu b \theta\left[-\log \theta+\log \left(\frac{b}{4.5 r}\right)\right] \quad(16b)
$$

$$
E_{\mathrm{s}}=-0.14 \mu b \theta \log \left(\frac{4.5 r \theta}{b}\right). \quad(16c)
$$

2.4.2 Network made of two independent sets of parallel dislocations. - The characteristics of the two networks are summarized in table I. Using formulae (11), (12), (13) the self energy is calculated as :
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{s}}= & -\frac{4-3 v}{4 \sqrt{3} \pi(1-v)} \mu b \theta × \\
& ×\left[\log \theta+\log \left(\frac{4 \pi r}{\sqrt{3} b}\right)+\frac{1}{4-3 v}\right]. \quad(17)
\end{aligned}
$$

2.4.3 Remarks. - As indicated in the introduction the method for the calculation of the self energy of a honeycomb network is more general and easier to perform than that published elsewhere [3,4]. Here the numerical calculation is restricted to the calculation of a sum which can be performed with a small computer. The calculation for the case of two sets of infinite dislocations is simple too and more general than that published by Read and Shockley (1950) who obtained formula (17a). Formulae (16) and (17) allow for the calculation of energy $\Delta E$ gained by transforming the network of figure $1 a$ into the honeycomb network. It is easily verified that $\Delta E$ is positive (i.e. the honeycomb network is stable) provided $\theta$ is smaller than a critical value $\theta_{\mathrm{c}}$.

In the case calculated above, assuming $v \sim 1 / 3$ and $r \sim b / e$ one obtains :
$$
\theta_{\mathrm{c}} \sim 0.05. \quad(18)
$$

It must be made clear that the result (18) depends on the assumption made for the core size and that the core energy should be taken into account to obtain an accurate value of $\theta_{\mathrm{c}}$.

3. Effect of long range stresses. - One of the advantages of the method outlined in [1] is that it allows the calculation of energy even when there exist long range stresses. The condition for the existence of such stresses is that the tensor $\mathbf{B}^{(0)}$ defined in [1] be different from zero. In that case it has been shown that the long range stress is uniform in each half space limited by the boundary.

Therefore the only meaningful situations correspond to an assembly of parallel boundaries such that the uniform stress is zero outside the slab limited by the boundaries.

For such a slab of width $r$ the elastic energy can be written :
$$
E_{\mathrm{I}}=K g(r)+L r+\text { Const. } \quad(19a)
$$

$$
L=\mu\left(B_{i j}^{(0)} B_{i j}^{(0)}+\frac{v}{1-v}\left|\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf{B}^{(0)}\right|^{2}\right). \quad(19b)
$$

$K$ depends on the boundary and has been calculated in this paper.

$g(r)$ is a function decreasing with $r$.
$\mathbf{B}^{(0)}$ corresponds to one boundary.

From (19a) and (19b) it is seen that there must exist a critical value $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ for which the energy is a minimum :

$$
\left. \frac{\mathrm{d}g}{\mathrm{d}r} \right|_{r=r_{\mathrm{c}}} = -\frac{L}{K}. \tag{19c}
$$

Such a situation occurs for example when dislocations split outside the boundary as shown in [1] in a simple case. More complicated situations may occur which can be calculated by using the technique developed in this paper. It must be emphasized however that the term $Lr$ may give rise to three-dimensional instabilities such as those observed in stainless steel [8]. Lamellar compounds can be approximately treated within the frame of this method and an approximate width of the lamellae can be obtained through equations (19).

A detailed calculation would involve taking into account the long-range interaction between lamellae and differences between elastic constants.

However formulae (19) provide a limiting condition for the stability of such lamellar compounds. For small $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ the function $g(r)$ is well approximated as $- \log r$ therefore $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ is simply given as :

$$
r_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{K}{L}. \tag{20}
$$

Where $K$ and $L$ are easily calculated from formulae (16), (17) and (19). A condition for the stability of lamellar compounds is then that $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ be larger than $b$.

4. Conclusion. — The results obtained in [1] and in this paper allow simple calculations of the elastic quantities related to periodic planar distributions of dislocations.

As shown previously [5] a correct formulation of the elastic problem enables one to describe the geometry of grain boundaries and to show that Bollmann's theory is only partially correct [9]. Moreover it is well known [10] that the transformation relation between periodic lattices is not unique. To each transformation corresponds at least one dislocation distribution. To choose between those distributions one needs an energy calculation which can be made using the results of this paper.

The use of this kind of calculation is however severely limited for the following reasons :

1) Core effects, local relaxation and point defects are not included in the calculation. They are obviously important for large-angle boundaries [5].
2) Three-dimensional instabilities such as those described by Bollmann, Michaut and Sainfort [8] cannot be taken into account.
3) Most of the effects of elastic anisotropy are difficult to include in such calculations except for the case of small angle boundaries [11].
