# A Simple Approach to the Determination of the Distance between Dislocations in a Pile-up

By E. SMITH

Department of Metallurgy, University of Manchester

[Received 30 April 1969]

## ABSTRACT

The distance between the leading dislocations in a pile-up containing a large number of dislocations is estimated by assuming all except the leaders to be in the form of a super-dislocation. This very simple procedure gives results that are in excellent agreement with exact results previously obtained, while fairly accurate estimates are obtained even when there is only a moderate number of dislocations. Indication is also given of the various other situations to which the procedure might be applied.

THE mechanical behaviour of crystalline solids is intimately associated with the inhomogeneous nature of plastic deformation, and during the past 20 years considerable attention has been given to the theoretical analysis of models in which the plastic deformation is ideally represented by a pile-up of dislocations that are confined to a single plane. Many physical processes are dependent on the behaviour of those dislocations that are in the vicinity of the obstacle against which the dislocations are piled up, and it is therefore important to determine how the dislocations are dispersed in such a region.

Four different methods have been employed for such analyses:

(1) For a strictly limited range of situations the discrete nature of the dislocations is retained and analytical methods give (Eshelby, Frank and Nabarro 1951, Head and Thomson 1962, Chou 1967 a, b) their equilibrium positions in terms of the zeros of classic polynomial functions.

(2) For those situations where an analytical approach is impossible, computational methods have been used (see, for example, Head 1959, Chou and Whitmore 1961, Chou 1966, Hazzledine and Hirsch 1967, Hazzledine 1968, Li 1969) to obtain the equilibrium positions; the amount of computation increases with the number of dislocations in the system, and consequently this approach is attractive if a model involves only a limited number of dislocations.

(3) All the dislocations may be smeared into a continuous distribution (Eshelby 1949, Leibfried 1951), so that there is a surface dislocation density represented by a distribution function, which is obtained by solving an appropriate singular integral equation. This approach has been used extensively (see, for example, Head and Louat 1955) and is

particularly attractive where there is a large number of dislocations; it is characterized by its mathematical simplicity but difficulties arise if the specific physical phenomenon under consideration is directly dependent on the discrete nature of particular dislocations in the system.

(4) Certain vital dislocations are allowed to remain discrete, while the remainder are smeared into a continuous distribution. This approach was originally suggested by Head and Louat (1955) and Barnett (1967), and has been applied (Smith 1968 a, b) to a number of models, the accuracy of the method being demonstrated by comparing the results with the exact ones obtained by assuming all the dislocations to be discrete. This pro- cedure is mathematically relatively simple, and is a compromise between the continuous distribution approach, characterized by its ease of solution, and the discrete dislocation approach with its greater physical reality.

As an extension to the latter approach it is tempting to enquire whether accurate estimates for the spacings between particularly vital dislocations are obtained if a small number remain discrete while the others, instead of being smeared into a continuous distribution, are assumed to be in the form of a super-dislocation. Other workers (see, for example, Kronmuller and Seeger 1961) have used a super-dislocation to represent all the dis- locations in a pile-up and the approach described here may therefore also be regarded as an extension of such work.

Thus consider the model of $(n-1)$ edge dislocations, of Burgers vector $b$ in the $x$ direction and with their associated extra material in the positive $y$ direction, lying along the plane $y=0$ and forced by an applied shear stress $p_{x y}=-\sigma$ against an edge dislocation, which is fixed along the $z$ axis and has a Burgers vector $m b$ in the $x$ direction; in the simplified description of this situation it is assumed that all the free dislocations, except the leading one, are in the form of a super-dislocation of Burgers vector $(n-2) b$. The equilibrium positions $x=x_{1}$ and $x=x_{\mathrm{s}}$ of the leading free dislocation and the super-dislocation are given by the simultaneous equations

$$
\frac{m A}{x_{1}}-\frac{(n-2) A}{\left(x_{\mathrm{s}}-x_{1}\right)}-\sigma=0, \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(1)
$$

$$
\frac{m A}{x_{\mathrm{s}}}+\frac{A}{\left(x_{\mathrm{s}}-x_{1}\right)}-\sigma=0, \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad. \quad(2)
$$

with $A=\mu b / 2 \pi(1-v), \mu$ being the shear modulus and $v$ Poisson's ratio. When $n$ is large, $x_{1} \ll x_{\mathrm{s}}$ and relations (1) and (2) give the equilibrium distance of separation between the leading free dislocation and the fixed dislocation as $x_{1}=m(m+1) A / n \sigma$, and the situation is as if there is a single free dislocation and an applied shear stress of magnitude $n \sigma /(m+1)$. Extension of this argument readily shows that a better approximation to $x_{1}$ is obtained by assuming that there are two discrete dislocations the remainder being in the form of a super-dislocation with Burgers vector $(n-3) b$, when the situation is as if there are two free dislocations and an applied shear stress $n \sigma /(m+2)$. This procedure may be repeated with an increasing number of discrete dislocations, such that $x_{1}$ is obtained by

assuming that there are $p(\ll n)$ free dislocations subject to the applied shear stress $n\sigma/(m+p)$. Columns 2-4 of table 1 show the magnitudes (Chou 1967 b) of $x_{1}$ for various values of $m$ for a model in which there are $p$ discrete dislocations and an applied shear stress $\sigma$. The preceding approach has been used to give (columns 5-7) the values of $x_{1}$, again for various values of $m$, when there are $(n-1)\gg p$ discrete dislocations, the results being compared with the exact ones obtained (Chou 1967 b) for large values of $n$. The approach clearly gives very good results, the accuracy being governed by the number of discrete dislocations selected.

The preceding comparisons consequently demonstrate the attractiveness of this very simple approach, for the results obtained from a model with very few dislocations can quickly be used to give accurate results in a situation where there is a large (or even a limited) number of dislocations; the great advantage of the method is its mathematical simplicity coupled with the desirable feature that the physically important dislocations remain discrete. The procedure may be readily applied to situations that are extensions to that considered in this communication, for example:

(a) A pile up of superlattice dislocations, where a strip of antiphase boundary separates the two components of each superlattice dislocation; a somewhat similar problem arises when dislocations are separated into their associated partial dislocations.

(b) An array of dislocations not lying on the same plane as the fixed dislocation; this model may be generalized to consider the passage of an array of dislocations past any fixed obstacle.

(c) Two identical dislocation pile-ups on planes that are close to each other, thus representing a slip band in which some of the dislocations may have cross-slipped out of their original plane.

Such models and their application to physical situations will not be discussed here, the main purpose having been to outline the approach and demonstrate its accuracy.
