# EXPERIMENTAL AND THEORETICAL STUDY OF NICKEL IMPLANTED WITH 80 eV HELIUM-IONS

M. D'OLIESLAEGER, G. KNUYT, L. DE SCHEPPER and L.M. STALS

Materials Physics Department, Limburg Universitair Centrum, 3610 Diepenbeek, Belgium

Planar helium bubble arrays in Ni are observed by TEM after 80 eV $He^+$-implantation. Observations by other authors on Mo suggest that in the first stage of their growth these planar bubble arrays have in fact a platelet morphology. To explain this two-dimensional growth mode, we performed atomistic computer simulations. The results of these calculations show that a helium filled aggregate tends to expel neighbouring atoms at its outer edges. The atoms are displaced in the direction of a nearest octahedral position. The results point to the formation of an interstitial loop. The observed transformation of helium filled platelets into groups of smaller aggregates is studied by means of total energy considerations. It is established that, once a critical radius is attained, the platelet is unstable against transformation. An explanation is also given for the fact that two-dimensional aggregates are observed to be more stable in bcc than in fcc structures.

## 1. Introduction

An established property of helium in metals is its tendency to precipitate into bubbles. The latter, however, do not always have a three-dimensional morphology. The first experimental evidence that helium can precipitate in aggregates with a planar morphology was found for Mo [1-3]. The observed platelets, which all lay on {110}-planes, could be described as disk-shaped cavities containing helium. It was also observed that the platelets transformed into groups of smaller aggregates upon annealing to 1000 K. More recently, the observation of helium platelets in Ni was reported [4], and platelet-like aggregates of bubbles were observed [7].

In order to explain the two-dimensional growth mode of helium platelets in metals, several theoretical calculations have been made. Caspers et al. [5] performed atomistic computer simulations which suggested that the platelet-like growth of helium aggregates could be triggered by trap-mutation of a helium-vacancy defect. Self-interstitials, pushed out subsequently, would cluster two-dimensionally and thereby favour growth of helium aggregates in {110}-planes. Finnis et al. [6] used simplified models of platelets and bubbles to study their relative stability. They found that the bubble was always the preferred configuration. No quantitative theory could be given until now to explain the observation of helium platelets.

## 2. Experimental results

In order to study the nucleation behaviour of helium aggregates in nickel, foils were pre-implanted with 3 keV helium-ions and afterwards filled with a high dose of helium-ions at 80 eV. Planar helium bubble arrays were discovered, lying on {111}-planes. Detailed information is published elsewhere [7].

## 3. Atomistic computer simulations

We performed static computer simulations to explain the observed phenomenon for nickel. The technical details are described in [8]. In a first part of the simulation, it is supposed that the interstitials, which are pushed out at the boundary of the helium cavity, are not bound to the helium cluster. In this case, as it was reported earlier [8], the helium cavity grows in a two-dimensional way. In a second part of the simulation, we calculated the binding energy of the interstitial to the helium cluster. If was found that at ambient temperature the interstitial is bound to the cluster. Therefore, in the present study, we calculated again the growth of the aggregate for the case the interstitials are bound to the cluster. The details of these calculations will be published elsewhere [13]. We will present here a summary of the results. The atoms, in the plane of the platelet, bordering to the platelet, are pushed towards octahedral interstitial positions of the undisturbed lattice. These thus formed interstitials cluster two-dimensionally in the near vicinity of the cluster. The results suggest the formation of an interstitial loop. In fcc metals, such a dislocation loop is immobile and will remain in the near vicinity of the platelet.

energy considerations. When a helium-filled platelet is stable against transformation into a group of smaller aggregates, the total energy must increase at the begin- ning of this transformation. We distinguish the follow- ing three contributions to the internal energy of a gas-filled platelet.

(1) The elastic energy stored within the bulk of the metal. According to Sack [9], this energy for a platelet of radius $r_{\mathrm{p}}$ and at a pressure $p$, is given by:

$$
E_{\mathrm{el}}^{\mathrm{pl}}=\frac{8\left(1-v^{2}\right)}{3 E_{\mathrm{Y}}} r_{\mathrm{p}}^{3} p^{2}, \tag{1}
$$

with $v$ Poisson's ratio and $E_{\mathrm{Y}}$ Young's modulus.

(2) The surface term in the energy of the metal. In this term the interaction energy between gas and metal is taken into account.

(3) The interaction energy between the gas atoms. This is calculated by assuming that the gas atoms are located on a static close-packed lattice and interact with each other according to a Born-Mayer type potential [10]. Suppose that we have a two-dimensional helium aggre- gate with radius $r_{\mathrm{p}}$, which lies on a close-packed plane in an fcc structure; bordering to this platelet, there are $N_{\mathrm{s}}$, satellite-platelets with radius $r_{\mathrm{s}}\left(r_{\mathrm{s}}<r_{\mathrm{p}}\right)$. We call the platelet and its satellites the "system". For such a system it is complicated to calculate the total energy which is stored in the bulk metal. We therefore con- structed the following model to describe the system. The elastic energy is written as:

$$
E_{\mathrm{el}}^{\mathrm{pl}}=C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3} p^{2}
$$

with $r_{\text {eff }}$ an effective radius, which is determined as follows. In a good approximation the projection of a satellite on the circumference of the platelet is $2 r_{\mathrm{s}}$. The total length of this projection for $N_{\mathrm{s}}$ satellites is thus $2 N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}$. For these parts of the system, the distance from the center of the platelet to the outside of the total system is given by $r_{\mathrm{p}}+2 r_{\mathrm{s}}$. This suggests the following expression for $r_{\text {eff }}$:

$$
r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}=\left(r_{\mathrm{p}}+2 r_{\mathrm{s}}\right)^{3} \frac{N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}}{\pi r_{\mathrm{p}}}+r_{\mathrm{p}}^{3}\left(1-\frac{N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}}{\pi r_{\mathrm{p}}}\right).
$$

Since eq. (1) is only valid if the thickness of the platelet is small compared to its lateral dimensions, we will first discuss the model for large platelet radii and denote it as "the regime II-model". For small platelet radii, the thickness of the platelet becomes comparable to its radius and eq. (1) needs to be adapted. We will denote this adapted model as "the regime I-model".

### 4.1. Regime II

It can be shown [6,13] that if the platelet radius increases, the pressure decreases. This causes the dislo- cation loop, formed in the beginning of the growth to disappear. The total energy of a gas-filled platelet with a negligible thickness is then given by:

$$
E_{\mathrm{II}}=C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3} p^{2}+2 \pi \gamma_{1}\left(r_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}+N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)+E_{\mathrm{gas}}(V),
$$

with $p$ the internal pressure, $\gamma_{1}$ the surface-energy and $V=2 C r_{\text {eff }}^{3} p$ the elastic volume of the system. The varia- tion of the total energy $E_{\mathrm{II}}$, when $N_{\mathrm{s}}$-satellite platelets are formed, is given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta E_{\mathrm{II}}= & 3 C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2} p^{2} \delta r_{\mathrm{eff}}+2 C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3} p \delta p+\delta\left(2 \pi \gamma_{1}\left(r_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}+N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)\right) \\
& +\frac{\partial E_{\mathrm{gas}}}{\partial V}\left(6 C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2} p \delta r_{\mathrm{eff}}+2 C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3} \delta p\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Assuming that during the satellite growth, the total surface area of the system remains constant, and neg- lecting terms in $r_{\mathrm{s}}^{3}$ and $r_{\mathrm{s}}^{4}$, the variation of $E_{\mathrm{II}}$ can be written as follows:

$$
\delta E_{\mathrm{II}}=-C p^{2} N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2} r_{\mathrm{p}}\left[0.41-\frac{3 N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{\pi r_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}\right].
$$

This means that if a number of satellite platelets start to grow at the border of the platelet, the total energy of the system decreases and consequently the transforma- tion into a group of smaller aggregates can take place.

### 4.2. Regime I

In this model we consider a platelet with a thickness comparable to its radius. Suppose the elastic energy can be written as:

$$
E_{\mathrm{el}}=A r+B r^{2}+D r^{3}. \tag{2}
$$

In the case of regime II, the first two terms can be neglected. However, for small platelet radii, these terms play an important role and it will appear that they are responsible for the stability of the platelet. The linear term can be considered as the energy of the dislocation loop, formed by the pushed out interstitials, the exact position of which is not essential. The term in $r^{2}$ can be incorporated in the surface energy term of regime II. Apart from the correction on the elastic energy term, we also need to introduce an additional volume term, since it is clear that a helium platelet has a minimum volume. The thickness of the aggregate must clearly be greater than the interplanar distance between $\{111\}$ planes, since the helium between the planes will push the planes apart. We introduce a minimum thickness $d_{0}$, resulting in a minimum volume $V_{\min }$ and a lateral surface energy term. Since this term is also proportional to $r$, the lateral surface energy and the energy of the dislocation loop are taken together.

The total energy can thus be written as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{I}}= & C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3} p^{2}+2 \pi \gamma_{2}\left(r_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}+N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right) \\
& +2 \pi \gamma_{3}\left(r_{\mathrm{p}}+N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}\right) d_{0}+E_{\mathrm{gas}}(V),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $p$ the internal pressure, $\gamma_{2}$ and $\gamma_{3}$ the surface energies and
$$V=2 C r_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3} p+\pi d_{0}\left(r_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}+N_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right)$$
the volume of the system.

Upon investigating analytically the stability of the platelet, it can be shown that, under the assumption that the total surface remains constant, the variation of the total energy $E_{1}$ is given by:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta E_{1}= & 3 C p^{2} r_{\mathrm{s}} r_{\mathrm{p}} N_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\frac{\gamma_{3} d_{0}+\gamma_{2} r_{\mathrm{s}}}{2 \gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}\left(d_{0} / r_{\mathrm{p}}\right)}-\frac{2 r_{\mathrm{s}}}{\pi}\right. \\
& \left.+\pi d_{0}^{2} \gamma_{3}\left(\frac{2-\left(r_{\mathrm{s}} / r_{\mathrm{p}}\right)}{3 C p\left(2 \gamma_{2} r_{\mathrm{p}}+\gamma_{3} d_{0}\right)}\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
$$
which for $r_{\mathrm{s}} \ll r_{\mathrm{p}}$, yields $\delta E_{1}>0$. It should however be mentioned that this result does not mean a priori that the platelet is stable against transformation, since it is assumed that the total surface of the system remains constant during the transformation. Therefore, the complete energy surface, as shown in fig. 1., was calculated using the following values of the parameters: for $\gamma_{1}$ and $\gamma_{3}$, we have taken $0.1 \mathrm{eV} \AA^{-2}$, which is somewhat larger than the value determined by Daw et al. [12], in order to account for the interaction between the nickel and the helium-atoms. For the minimum thickness $d_{0}$ we have taken $2.5 \AA$. When we consider the platelet as an analogon of a small dislocation loop, we can determine the value of $\gamma_{2}$ by fitting the coefficient $B$ in eq. (2) to the expression for the energy of a dislocation loop. This yields $\gamma_{2}=0.23 \mathrm{eV} \AA^{-2}$. The used values of the parameters will be discussed in more detail in [13]. As can be seen on fig. 1a., there is a saddle-point in the energy surface. This is even more clearly visible when the viewing direction is parallel with the $r_{\mathrm{p}}$-axis, as shown in fig. 1b. The platelet has to surmount this energy barrier before transformation becomes possible.

The transition between the two regimes can thus be considered as the transition from a stable to an unstable platelet. The radius at which this transition takes place can be deduced from experiment. Assuming that during the transition the total volume remains in first approximation constant and using the experimental result that in nickel a group of smaller aggregates consists of approximately five aggregates with radius $\sim 5 \AA$ [7], the original platelet would have had a radius of $\sim 12 \AA$.

These calculations show that a helium-filled platelet is stable against transformation into a group of smaller aggregates until a certain critical radius is attained. Once this radius has been reached, the platelet becomes unstable as soon as a small satellite is formed. The occurrence of such satellites is inherent to the growth mode of platelets, since the platelet grows by pushing away atoms from their lattice sites at the borders of the platelet. A more detailed description of the stability calculations will be published elsewhere [13].

Combining the experimental and theoretical results with the known properties of the mobility of dislocation loops in fcc and bcc metals, we can give an explanation of the observed difference between fcc and bcc helium-platelets. It is known from experiments on molybdenum [12], that helium clusters grow by the loop punching mechanism. In bcc metals this is indeed possible since the loop, which is created in the vicinity of the platelet, is mobile. In the light of the proposed regime I and regime II models, the helium platelet in molybdenum should always be described by the regime I-model. Calculations show that the height of the saddle-point energy does not increase with increasing platelet radius. This means that at a sufficiently large radius, enough atoms contribute to the total energy to overcome the energy barrier. Obviously, a platelet will become unstable at much higher radii in bcc lattices than in fcc lattices. This might be the reason for the observation of true platelet structures having large radii and bcc metals,

## 5. Conclusion

It was found experimentally that helium aggregates in metals can have a platelet-like structure, which, under certain conditions, is unstable against transformation into a group of smaller aggregates. The results of atomistic computer simulations suggest that the platelet grows by pushing out atoms, bordering to the platelet, which then agglomerate into an interstitial loop. A theoretical model is developed to explain the fact that helium-filled platelets are unstable beyond a critical radius and that this radius is larger in bcc metals than in fcc metals.
