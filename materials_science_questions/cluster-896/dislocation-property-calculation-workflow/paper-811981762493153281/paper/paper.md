# The Occurrence of Glissile Shockley Loops in Field-ion Specimens of Iridium

By M. A. Fortes† and B. Ralph

Department of Metallurgy, University of Cambridge

[Received 28 March 1968]

## ABSTRACT

The paper describes observations of faulted loops with Burgers vector of the $a/6\langle 112\rangle$ type in iridium field-ion specimens. The densities observed in some specimens are as high as $10^{18}$ loops per ${\rm cm}^3$.

The occurrence of these loops is related to fracture of the specimens and specimen asymmetry. It is suggested that the high shear stresses operating on a field-ion specimen during imaging and evaporation homogeneously nucleate glissile Shockley loops. In some cases the intersection of these loops leads to a crack nucleus and then to fracture.

The presence of some image streaks in asymmetric specimens is shown to be related to the presence of faults, produced by shear, intersecting the surface.

## § 1. INTRODUCTION

THEORIES of the contrast to be expected from dislocated specimens in the field-ion microscope have been developed which allow either partial or complete characterization of dislocations and dislocation loops to be made (e.g. Pashley 1965, Ranganathan 1966, Sanwald, Ranganathan and Hren 1966, Brandon and Perry 1967, Fortes, Smith and Ralph 1968, Smith, Fortes, Kelly and Ralph 1968). While single perfect dislocations and perfect dislocation loops give rise to a pattern of contrast based on simple spirals, faulted loops and dissociated dislocations lead to a more complex form of contrast because in general either a step in the surface or a kink in the imaged rows of atoms is produced where the fault intersects the surface.

For each type of dislocation the contrast can be predicted in terms of parameters which depend on the Burgers vector $(\mathbf{b})$ of the dislocation and on the pole $(hkl)$ where the defect emerges. If $\mathbf{n}$ is the unit normal to $(hkl)$ we have:

$$\mathbf{b}.\mathbf{n}=pd_{hkl} \text{ for perfect dislocations} \quad \text{.... (1)}$$

and

$$\mathbf{b}.\mathbf{n}=qd_{hkl} \text{ for partial dislocations.} \quad \text{.... (2)}$$

$p$ is always an integer and its value gives the number of leaves in the spiral configuration which arises from the dislocation, after field evaporation (Smith *et al.* 1968). In general $q$ is non-integral. The fault associated with the partial dislocation gives rise to a step when it intersects the surface.

surface. The height of this step is $q'd_{hkl}$ (or $(1-q')d_{hkl}$, depending on the amount of field evaporation) where $q'$ is the non-integral residue of $q$. When $q$ is non-integral stepped spiral configurations will result. The number of leaves in the spiral is an integer $l$ such that $|q|<l<|q|+1$. When $q$ is an integer no step at the surface is associated with the fault, but in general a kink in the imaged row of atoms at the edge of the planes is produced where the fault intersects the edge. These configurations are called kinked spirals (or kinked rings if $q=0$). The magnitude of such kinks is always small but evidence has been given showing that they give rise to contrast. This means that even if $q$ is an integer it will be possible to distinguish between perfect and partial dislocations in the field-ion microscope (Fortes *et al.* 1968, Smith *et al.* 1968). The contrast from loops and dissociated dislocations can then be predicted in terms of their Burgers vectors. In the case of loops spirals will generally occur but the disturbance of the image will only involve those rings of atoms which are intersected by the plane of the loop. Dissociated dislocations give rise to a net spiral (unless $p=0$) so that in general they can be distinguished from loops.

Examples of both perfect and faulted loops found in field-ion images of iridium have already been presented (e.g. Fortes *et al.* 1968, Smith *et al.* 1968, Newman 1967). These examples prove the validity of the contrast theory outlined above. Some secondary effects do play a role and, in particular, the occurrence of a bright streak at the intersection of a fault with the surface will be discussed in this paper. However, these secondary effects are minor and it is generally possible to characterize the defects observed. Nevertheless, the unambiguous determination of the Burgers vector of dislocations and loops is not always possible. This is because the same values of $p$ (and $q$) can be obtained from different Burgers vectors of defects emerging in the same pole. For a dislocation loop the plane of the defect can be determined from its trace in the image. This is easier for the case of a faulted loop because the intersection of the fault with the surface is in general easy to establish in the image. The analysis is more complicated for asymmetric tips because the trace of a given plane will be imaged as a line with an orientation relative to the imaged poles which depends on the tip shape and on the projection involved in imaging (i.e. the ion trajectories), and neither is known. In the case of symmetric or nearly symmetric specimens the analysis of plane traces is simpler because in this case the projection is approximately stereographic (Fortes 1968).

## § 2. ObServations of LoOPS IN IRIDIUM

The loops which are characterized and discussed in this paper have been observed in iridium specimens in two different circumstances. Whilst it is believed that these loops have the same origin in both cases (see § 4) in this section the two cases will be treated separately and unified later.

(A) Field-ion specimens frequently fracture under the influence of the very high stresses which operate during imaging and evaporation; this process of fracture is often termed a flash due to its appearance in the microscope. In this process large volumes of the specimen are often removed but frequently with iridium specimens the amount of material removed is small (having a thickness of the order of one hundred angstroms) and the fracture surface approximates to a field evaporation end form; we term this process an 'identical flash'. If the specimen is then reimaged a very high density of small dislocation loops are found in the rest of the specimen volume which may be examined by field-ion micro- scopy (i.e. this density of defects is found for thicknesses of the order of 1000-2000 Å beneath the fracture surface).

(B) Loops similar to those discussed in (A) are frequently found in asymmetric specimens although often to a lower density. No direct correlation with an identical flash process can be made although many of the asymmetric specimens exhibiting these loops have been seen to fracture. It is also possible that fracture, in all the other specimens, has occurred during raising of the specimen voltage to the imaging field and gone unnoticed.

### 2.1. The Nature of Loops in Iridium Specimens which have been seen to Fracture

The results of this section are mostly derived from observations made in specimens where higher densities of defects were found. Four speci- mens of this type were observed and field evaporation sequences obtained for each after fracture. In all cases the densities were $10^{17}-10^{18}/cm^{3}$. This means that an average of ten defects intersects any imaged surface. No appreciable variation in the concentration of defects was found in the volumes examined by field-evaporation, which in one case corresponded to a thickness parallel to the tip axis of $\sim 1000$ Å. The high densities indicated have not been observed in all specimens which suffered identical flashes but similar defects have invariably been observed after fracture where generally none were seen before.

Figure 1 shows the image of a tip which fractured in the microscope. At least ten defects can be seen in the image. The specimen is reasonably symmetric although it can be seen that the [001] tip axis is not a four-fold axis of symmetry of the image. The asymmetry of the tips is conveniently described by assuming an elliptical cross section. The projection of this cross section is approximately elliptical and it can be shown that the major axis of the cross section of the specimen projects into the minor axis in the image (Fortes 1968). In the case of fig. 1 the major and minor axes of the cross section are parallel to $\langle 110\rangle$ directions. This is the most common type of asymmetry of [001] centred iridium specimens.

The Burgers vectors of the faulted loops were determined using the theory of contrast outlined in §1. Having determined the {111} plane of the defect there are, in the f.c.c. metals, four possible Burgers vectors for faulted loops in this plane (and their negatives). The values of $q$ for each Burgers vector were calculated from eqn. (2) after indexing the pole of emergence of the defect.

Often the contrast of each particular loop was consistent with it being either a Frank loop or a Shockley loop with a particular $a/6 \langle 112 \rangle$ Burgers vector (since these possibilities could lead to the same value of $q$). The examples given by Smith *et al.* (1968) were of this type. (These loops were in fact observed in specimens which suffered identical flashes.) However, cases have been found where a characterization of the nature of the loops is possible and in such cases these loops prove to be Shockley loops. Two examples will be given. In fig. 3 a faulted loop is seen emerging in the (011) plane of a [111] centred specimen. The plane of the defect is $(1\overline{1}1)$. For all Burgers vectors of faulted loops in this plane and intersecting (011), the values of $q$ are integers. For $\mathbf{b} = \pm a/3 [1\overline{1}1]$ and $\mathbf{b} = \pm a/6 [21\overline{1}]$ we get $q=0$; for $\mathbf{b} = \pm a/6 [121]$ and $\mathbf{b} = \pm a/6 [\overline{1}12]$ the value of $q=1$.

### 2.2. Observations of Loops in Specimens where Fracture in the Microscope was not Detected
This section deals with observations in asymmetric tips which were not seen to fracture while under observation in the microscope.

Although small and well-resolved faulted loops have been observed in such tips, often a major feature of the images is the pattern of bright streaks running approximately parallel to the directions of higher magnifica- tion. These streaks are frequently long and their main characteristics have been described previously, with particular emphasis on observations in tungsten (Ranganathan, Bowkett Hren, and Ralph 1965). The resolution of iridium images in the region of the streaks is generally poor so that the configuration of the imaged rings intersected by the streak is frequently impossible to determine. The streaks may persist during a considerable amount of field evaporation with little or no movement relative to the imaged poles, which suggests that they are associated with planar defects. The planes of the defects producing the streaks have previously been determined to be $\{110\}$ planes for the case of iridium (Ranganathan *et al*. 1965). It has been emphasised that streaks often arise from gross imperfections on the tip surface resulting from poor polishing (Ranganathan *et al*. 1965, Brandon 1966a, Loberg and Norden 1967). However, the present authors have obtained evidence which suggests that streaks in iridium are produced when a fault intersects the surface. This had already been suggested by Ralph who explained the streak in terms of an enhancement of the field at the step on the surface along the intersection of the fault with the surface (Ralph and Brandon 1963, Ralph 1964). If $\mathbf{R}$ is a vector describing the displacement of one side of the crystal relative to the other a step will be produced in the pole $(hkl)$ intersected by the fault only if $\mathbf{R}.\mathbf{n}$ is not equal to an integral number of interplanar spacings (Smith *et al*. 1968). The height of the step in a field evaporated surface is always smaller than $d_{hkl}$.

The considerations in iridium attributing image streaking to faults are as follows:

1.  A fault may give rise to a streak even if no step is produced by the fault (this has been found in some cases for faulted loops). Also not all faults, even those producing a step, will image as a streak.

The reason why a fault sometimes gives rise to a streak and sometimes does not is not clear. An adequate explanation may well come from a better understanding of the imaging process.

## § 3. NUCLEATION OF GLISSILE LOOPS BY SHEAR

The faulted defects observed in asymmetric tips and in particular those which give rise to long streaks have probably the same origin as those found after identical flashes. In fact all the loops observed in asymmetric 'streaky' tips had a contrast consistent with a $a/6\langle 112\rangle$ Burgers vector, and their planes were related to the asymmetry of the tips in the same way as that found for loops observed after fracture. This and the high densities observed indicate that the loops are formed by shear of the lattice, i.e. they are homogeneously nucleated in the specimens. This origin is not surprising taking into account the high stresses which arise in field-ion specimens, particularly during field evaporation.

Evaporation fields for the refractory metals are of the order of 4-5 v/Å and this gives for the force per unit area of surface (in a classical approach) $F^{2}/8\pi \sim 10^{11}$ dynes/cm². These stresses are of the order of $G/10$ ($G$ is the shear modulus), i.e. near the theoretical shear stress. Considering factors such as tip shape, field distribution and elastic anisotropy it can be seen that the pattern of stresses in the tip is complex, having hydrostatic and shear components. While calculations of the stress distribution cannot as yet be made it seems reasonable to assume that the shear stresses are of the order of $F^{2}/8\pi$ (e.g. if a hemispherical tip sitting on a cylinder is considered and a uniform field $F$ is applied on the hemisphere the maximum shear stress in the cylinder will be $F^{2}/16\pi$, for an isotropic material). In fact the observation of glissile faulted loops indicates that stresses close to the theoretical shear stress act in a tip. Such stresses are indeed

required for the homogeneous nucleation of glissile loops where the con- tribution due to thermal activation is small. This problem was originally considered by Frank who discussed the nucleation of stable perfect loops by a uniform shear (Frank 1950). The nucleation of Shockley loops by shear can also be discussed in similar terms (e.g. Kelly 1966). We shall use a similar treatment in order to determine the required activation energies for loop formation.

A uniform shear $\sigma$ is assumed to act in the direction of the Burgers vector of a possible loop. The energy of a loop of radius $R$ is approximately given by:

$$
E=\frac{G b^{2}}{4 \pi k} \ln \frac{2 R}{b_{0}}+\pi R^{2} \gamma.
$$

(Friedel 1964). The first term represents the line energy where $k \simeq 1$ and $b_{0}$ is a parameter related to the core energy which can take values between $\frac{1}{2} b$ and $2 b$ ; the second term represents the energy due to the fault, $\gamma$ being the stacking fault energy. This later term should be omitted for a perfect loop.

The work done by the stress in forming a loop of radius $R$ is approxi mately $\pi R^{2} \sigma b$ so that the energy required to form a loop of radius $R$ is:

$$
U=\pi R^{2}(\gamma-\sigma b)+\frac{1}{2} G b^{2} R \ln R / b_{0}.
$$

$U$ is maximum for a critical radius $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ given by:

$$
R_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{G b}{4 \pi(\sigma-\gamma / b)} \cdot\left[\ln R_{\mathrm{c}} / b_{0}+1\right].
$$

The corresponding and maximum value of $U$ is:

$$
U_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{1}{4} G b^{2} R_{\mathrm{c}} \cdot\left[\ln \frac{R_{\mathrm{c}}}{b_{0}}-1\right].
$$

$U_{\mathrm{c}}$ can be regarded as an activation energy for the formation of a loop by shear (Frank 1950). For all but very high stresses $U_{\mathrm{c}}$ will be very large and nucleation of loops with radius $R \geqslant R_{\mathrm{c}}$ will generally be impossible. In fig. 7 the ratio $U_{\mathrm{c}} / G b_{\mathrm{t}}{ }^{3}$ (where $b_{\mathrm{t}}$ is the Burgers vector of a total disloca tion) is plotted against $\sigma / G$ for perfect and faulted loops and for various values of the stacking fault energy $(f=\gamma / G b_{\mathrm{p}}$, where $b_{\mathrm{p}}$ is the Burgers vector of a partial dislocation). The relation $b_{\mathrm{p}}=b_{\mathrm{t}} / \sqrt{ } 3$ has been assumed and the parameter $b_{0}$ was taken equal to $\frac{1}{2} b$. This value leads to a theoretical shear stress of $G / 9$. Values of $b>\frac{1}{2} b_{0}$ give similar curves but for a given value of $f$ lead to larger values of $U$ at the intersection of the perfect and imperfect curves. The approximate nature of the previous treatment should be emphasized when trying to apply the results of fig. 7 to the particular case of iridium. Moreover in a field-ion specimen the stress state will not be uniform so that the work performed by the shear stresses in forming the loops will be a function of the location of loops in the specimen. Further, while under a uniform shear the loops

with radius $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ will expand until they intersect the surface, in actual specimens the final size of the loops and their shape will also depend on the stress distribution.

The stacking-fault energy in iridium and its variation with impurity content are not accurately known (a value $\gamma=3290 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ has been obtained for zone melted crystals (Haasen, Hieber and Mordike 1965)). The iridium wire used in the present work contained oxygen as the main impurity at a level of $\sim 500$ p.p.m. No evidence of dissociated dislocations

Thus $f \geqslant 0.01$ taking $G=21.3 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{dynes} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ (e.g. Friedel 1964). Since $G b_{\mathrm{t}}{ }^{3}=25 \mathrm{ev}$ the curves of fig. 7 show that nucleation of faulted loops in preference to perfect loops requires stresses near the theoretical shear stress and activation energies $U_{\mathrm{c}}>0.02 \times G b_{\mathrm{t}}{ }^{3}$, i.e. $U_{\mathrm{c}}>0.5 \mathrm{ev}$. The nucleation of perfect loops would occur at higher stresses requiring lower activation energies. At $78^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ the temperature at which the specimens were observed, $k T=7 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{ev}$ so that $U_{\mathrm{c}}>70 k T$ for stable faulted loops to be formed. It should be remembered here that the important stress for glissile loop formation is the resolved shear stress along the direction of the Burgers vector (more precisely the Peach-Kohler analysis should be used). It is then clear that depending on the stress distribution in the specimen either

one or both types of loops (perfect and faulted) can be nucleated. The fact that the majority of the loops observed are faulted must then be due to one or both of the following reasons:

1. The maximum stresses occur in {111} planes and along $\langle 11\overline{2}\rangle$ directions.

2. The activation energy available, in general, only allows the formation of stable Shockley loops.

In any case the required activation energies are of the order of $70kT$. It is possible that this calculated energy is too high owing to the approxi- mate method of the treatment; it is then plausible that the activation energies required are supplied by thermal activation. However, the particular circumstances of a field-ion specimen should be taken into account. The hydrostatic component of the field stress produces an expansion of the lattice in the region of tip and elastic energy is stored in this region. This expansion may reduce the required energies $(U_{c})$ below the value required in a simply sheared crystal. For the case of iridium the expansion is of the order of $3\%$ for a field of $5 \mathrm{v} / \mathring{A}$. The elastic energy stored in the tip is of the order of $0 \cdot 1 \mathrm{ev} /$ atom (Ralph 1964). How- ever, this energy is not all available even if fracture occurs because when the elastic energy is released the applied stress also decreases. The amount of energy released increases while the stresses decrease at the same time. However, $U_{c}$ increases as the stresses decrease and it increases more rapidly than does the amount of energy released. Conse- quently it is not thought that the release of elastic energy in the tip can be invoked to overcome the required activation energy.

## § 4. DiscUssion

The fact that field-ion specimens can withstand stresses of the order of the theoretical shear stress without deformation or fracture generally occurring is a result of the small dimensions of the region of high stresses and of the fact that this region is generally free from glissile imperfections. If a line defect or a dislocation source is present in the region acted upon by these high stresses (and, as the specimen is field evaporated this region penetrates more and more into the specimen) they will generally be affected by the stress state in the specimen. This is probably the reason why, for instance, dislocation lines when observed in the microscope are generally very short and disappear without trace.

The effect of the field stress on the specimens is probably of the same order for all metals in the sense that the ratio of the evaporation field to shear modulus is similar for the metals studied by field-ion microscopy (Brandon 1966 b).

Since the operating shear stresses are near the theoretical shear stress the possibility of formation of glissile loops in field-ion specimens may be general. This is in a way confirmed by the fact that observations of bright streaks in a number of other metals have been reported. The

effect of the stress and in particular the homogeneous nucleation of loops constitute an important limitation in the investigation of defect distri- butions with the field-ion microscope. On the other hand, useful informa- tion can be obtained of deformation processes which normally are not observed in specimens using other techniques. In particular a number of defects observed by field-ion microscopy in b.c.c. metals, especially in tungsten, are thought to be faulted loops produced in the same way as in iridium and their study in the light of the now available theories of contrast should give useful information about faulting in b.c.c. metals.

The possibility of faults giving rise to streaks has been proved although the reason why a bright streak arises is not yet clear. It is however tempting to say that streaks in other metals are often associated with faults produced by shear of the lattice under the high applied stress.

A more delicate problem is the relation between loop formation and fracture observed in the specimens which suffered identical flashes. As mentioned in $\S 2$ it is possible that very asymmetric tips which show faulted loops have fractured at one stage during observation although fracture of these specimens had not been detected. Alternatively the stress distribution and even the magnitude of the stresses are certainly differentin such tips (compared with symmetric or slightly asymmetric specimens) so that nucleation could have occurred under 'normal' observation. The difference in stress state would account for the growth of loops sufficient to give the observed long streaks. In those cases where fracture of the specimens has been observed (identical flashes) it is pertinent to discuss the relation between fracture and nucleation and growth of glissile loops. In particular it is worth attempting to establish whether fracture and loop formation are simultaneous events or whether one is the cause of the other. If fracture occurs first as a result of the nucleation and subsequent growth of a crack formed at a stress raiser already present in the tip, then it would be natural to admit that the higher stresses required for the nucleation of stable loops, are reached at the tip of the propagating crack. But in such a model it would be difficult to explain the observed relation between the planes and directions of shear with the asymmetry of the specimen. On the other hand, it would be then logical to conclude that all asymmetric tips which show loops had fractured at low voltages, which is not impossible but is unlikely.

Alternatively it is possible that, at a certain stage during observation(and field evaporation) of the specimens, the stress distribution in the tip will be such that the required stresses for the homogeneous nucleation of stable loops are reached. If this is so the observed relation between the planes and directions of glide with the asymmetry of the tips becomes evident. Fracture could occur as a result of the interaction of loops in different planes to produce a stress state in the vicinity of the intersection which would lead to the nucleation of a crack and this crack would then grow under the applied (field) stress. The fact that the shape of the fracture surface, after an identical flash, is very close to the field evaporated

end form of the tip is certainly an effect due to the hydrostatic stresses acting on the specimen, and indicates that the propagation of the crack is strongly affected by these stresses.

The nucleation and growth of loops seems then to have preceded and induced fracture. However, the special case of asymmetric tips which were not seen to suffer an identical flash raises some difficulties. Either all such tips have fractured at one stage before any observable image was obtained, which as mentioned above is not a very attractive assumption, or fracture does not necessarily occur in such tips as loops are formed and grow, because of a particular stress distribution in the tip.

It is seen that the problem of determining the stress distribution in a field-ion specimen and its relation to the shape of the specimen is of primary importance. The effect of elastic anisotropy may also play an important role (iridium is fairly anisotropic with $2C_{44}/(C_{11}-C_{12})=1\cdot59$ (Purwins, Hieber and Labush 1965). The activation energies required for the nucleation of stable loops are probably supplied by thermal activation only, although the expansion of the lattice due to the hydrostatic com- ponent of the stress may reduce the required energies compared with those which would be required in a lattice subject to pure shear.

As far as the authors are aware these are the first observations of homo- geneously nucleated glissile loops in metals. The fact that glissile faulted loops have never been identified in f.c.c. metals while they occur so fre- quently in field-ion specimens of iridium and other metals is definitely a result of the particular operating conditions of the field-ion microscope.
