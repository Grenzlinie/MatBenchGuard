# APPLICATION OF THE "0" LATTICE CALCULATION
## TO F.C.C./B.C.C. INTERFACES

M. G. HALL, $^{1}$ J. M. RIGSBEE $^{2}$ and H. I. AARONSON $^{3}$ 

$^{1}$ Centre for Materials Science, University of Birmingham, Birmingham B15 2TT, England, $^{2}$ Department of Metallurgy and Mining Engineering, University of Illinois, IL 61801, U.S.A. and $^{3}$ Department of Metallurgy and Materials Science, Carnegie-Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA 15213, U.S.A.

(Received 21 June 1984; in revised form 23 August 1985)

Abstract--The "0" lattice calculation of Bollman has been used to predict the structures of f.c.c./b.c.c. interfaces for a variety of orientation relationships ranging from Nishiyama-Wasserman to Kurdjumov-Sachs and for several commonly observed lattice parameter ratios. A number of basic vector formulations for the f.c.c. and b.c.c. structures have been investigated. They were found to have a marked effect on the shape of the "0" lattice unit cell and habit planes. Two methods were investigated to ascertain whether or not they would derive all useful habit planes directly, without changing the basic vectors. The first method, which used sectioning of the "0" lattice cell, was unable to derive all possible solutions, but by use of a different procedure, which considered combinations of the $\frac{1}{2}\langle 110\rangle$ dislocations in pairs, all possible interface descriptions were produced. Up to 7 distinct habit planes were derived, which were located in four separate regions of the stereogram. For each habit plane the theoretical dislocation structure (spacings, directions and Burgers vectors) and monatomic ledge spacings and directions were calculated. For each interface at least one array of dislocations is required, spaced 1.5-3.0 nm apart. The results are in good agreement with the computer modelling calculations of Rigsbee and Aaronson and also with those of surface dislocation theory.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The nature of the interfaces formed between the f.c.c. and b.c.c. structures represent an important class of problem for interface theory, but it is only compara- tively recently that much attention has been devoted to the subject. Two disparate theoretical approaches have however been employed with some success in predicting the interfacial structures and crys- tallography of these boundaries. These are (a) com-

puter modelling by Rigsbee and Aaronson [1] (R&A) which is based on a procedure first described by Hall et al. [2] and subsequently modified by Russell et al. [3] and (b) Bollman's "0" lattice theory [4-8]. The latter was first applied to f.c.c./b.c.c. interfaces in Ref. [6] and was then further expounded by Ecob and Ralph [7, 8] (E&R). Although fundamentally similar, the two methods differ in the way in which the calculations are performed: R&A use computer cal- culation of the crystal lattice co-ordinates and delin- eate directly the matching atomic positions, whereas in the "0" lattice calculation enhanced matching is only indirectly inferred from the size of the "0" lattice cell. Both methods show promise, and also produce results of a similar nature, but a detailed comparison of the two techniques has yet to be performed. Moreover, since the R&A method has been described by E&R as being "founded largely on concepts which can only be associated with incoherent boundaries" [8] and is therefore (presumably) likely to give the wrong results, it is important that a detailed com- parison of the two theories be made. This paper will perform such a comparison of the two methods and will point out some of the situations where each technique may be advantageous. Since certain as- sumptions used in previous treatments [4-8] of the f.c.c./b.c.c. problem require further consideration, the "0" lattice theory will first be briefly summarised and then the details of its application to f.c.c./b.c.c. will be re-examined.

## 2. THE "0" LATTICE PROCEDURE

In the "0" lattice method as described by Bollman [6] the $x$, $y$, $z$ co-ordinates of 3 non-coplanar lattice vectors in each of the two crystal structures are chosen and used to formulate the $3 \times 3$ matrices $\mathbf{S}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{S}^{(2)}$ of each structure. The lattices are taken to be interpenetrating, and it is essential to choose the nearest neighbouring atomic positions which are also closest to the origin. For an assumed orientation relationship the transformation matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is evaluated from [4]:

$$
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{S}^{(2)}\left(\mathbf{S}^{(1)}\right)^{-1} \tag{1}
$$

where $\mathbf{R}$ is a rotation matrix which conveniently specifies the orientation relationship.

The $x$, $y$, and $z$, co-ordinates of the basic vectors of the "0" lattice cell are then given by [4].

$$
x^{0}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)^{-1} \mathbf{b}^{(\mathrm{L})}=\mathbf{T}^{-1} \mathbf{b}^{(\mathrm{L})} \tag{2}
$$

where $\mathbf{b}^{(\mathrm{L})}$ are 3 (non-coplanar) Burgers vectors of interfacial dislocations and are usually assumed to be formed from the three column vectors of the matrix $\mathbf{S}^{(1)}$. Having obtained the basic "0" lattice vectors the cell may be completed by simple summation, inter- faces assigned and assessed in terms of dislocation spacings, geometry and habit plane. However, the size, shape and symmetry of the "0" lattice cell are markedly affected by the choice of $\mathbf{S}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{S}^{(2)}$ and it is easy to generate completely different interfaces, according to this choice (R&A made an equivalent finding in their computer modelling study). This problem will now be discussed in some detail.

## 4. ASSIGNMENT OF INTERFACES

### 4.1. Cell sectioning
In many crystal structures the $\{001\}$ planes are not the most densely packed and it should not be sur- prising if this were equally true for "0" lattices. However, as Bollman suggests [4] it is not just a matter of finding the most densely packed "0" lattice planes (as these will also contain the smallest dis- location spacings). Contact with the "0" lattice points should be maximised whilst minimising intersections with the cell walls. Clearly $\{001\}^{0}$ sections will satisfy this criterion (where the superscript 0 designates an " 0 " lattice plane). $\{110\}^{0}$ planes should also be considered as they pass through the intersection line of the cell walls and thus will be composed of only two sets of dislocations. Planes with higher indices will intersect all three cell walls and will therefore be composed of three sets of interfacial dislocations. They will also not usually pass through the centres of matching and therefore may possibly be neglected. At first sight, it may be thought that this consideration should also apply to $\{111\}^{0}$ , however see the more detailed analysis later.

Nine sections which are considered in detail are illustrated in Fig. 3. Three are the $\{001\}^{0}$ cell walls

$$
\mathbf{x}_{4}^{0}=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{0}+\mathbf{x}_{2}^{0} \tag{3}
$$
and according to Bollmann's "duality relationship" these will contain a dislocation array the Burgers vector of which is also the sum of the other two vectors
$$
\mathbf{b}_{4}=\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}_{2}. \tag{4}
$$

Therefore in the case of the f.c.c. crystal in which nearest neighbouring vectors for $S^{(1)}$ are employed the above reaction will cause Burgers vectors of the type $\frac{1}{2}\langle 112\rangle$ to be formed. Such boundaries are likely to be energetically unfavourable and the interfacial configurations shown in 7-9 may therefore be neglected.

Boundaries 4-6, on the other hand, are produced by the reaction, e.g.
$$
\mathbf{b}_{5}=\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}_{2} \tag{5}
$$
and as these will be formed of dislocations of the type $\frac{1}{2}\langle 110\rangle$ they must be considered on equal merit as the cell faces. For an assumed $S^{(1)}, S^{(2)}$ six interfaces containing $\frac{1}{2}\langle 110\rangle$ dislocations only are therefore derived for each unit cell by this procedure: three cell walls and three $\{110\}^{0}$ sections.

These results should be compared with a different approach derived next.

### 4.2. Dislocation pairs
One important aspect of "0" lattice theory is the close relationship that exists between the "0" lattice and $\mathbf{b}$ lattice. For every vector in the $\mathbf{b}$-lattice there is a corresponding "0" lattice vector. Recognising also that vectors uvw and $\overline{\mathbf{u}} \overline{\mathbf{v}} \overline{\mathbf{w}}$ in the $\mathbf{b}$ lattice also cause the "0" lattice vector to invert, there are six characteristic vectors of the $\mathbf{b}$-lattice (these are listed in Table 1) and there will therefore be six basic vectors for the "0" lattice. An interface may be defined by the cross-product of two "0" lattice vectors, and hence all possible interfaces containing only two sets of $\frac{1}{2}\langle 110\rangle$ dislocations will be constructed by considering the $\mathbf{b}$-vectors in pairs.† A total of 15 interface descriptions is produced in this way, equal to the number of ways that two (dislocation types) may be selected from six possibilities. The geometrical factors for each interface may then readily be calculated, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The specific equations for the habit plane, dislocation directions and spacings are presented in Table 2. Note that the line direction $(C_{2})$ and spacing of the $b_{1}$ dislocations is determined (mainly) by the $X_{2}^{0}$ vector and conversely the geometry of the $b_{2}$ dislocations is determined by the $\mathbf{X}_{1}^{0}$ vector.

The 15 interfaces derived by this method yield only 7 different habit planes. Of these, three habit planes are found to have a unique dislocation structure whereas each of the remaining four have three possible dislocation structures. This result is independent of both the orientation relationship and lattice parameters used as input data, since the former set of planes is constructed from dislocations on opposing edges of Thomson's tetrahedron, whereas the latter are from dislocations which together form the 4 faces in turn.

An alternative, particularly incisive, way of resolving the difference between 4.1 and 4.2 was contributed (anonymously) by a reviewer to this paper, and we reproduce these comments below.

### 5.2. Comparison with Ecob and Ralph
In their two papers on the structure of f.c.c./b.c.c. boundaries, E&R [7,8] introduce the concept of the arbitrary boundary (or "0" lattice section) and develop an essentially geometrical parameter $R$ which they consider most suited to describe such boundaries. They produced contour maps showing the variation of $R$ (and in some cases $P$) over a complete stereogram and observed minima at certain boundary orientations. It was asserted that these are the locations of the "best fit" boundaries. $R$ was concluded to be more useful than $P$ because: (a) $R$ exhibited sharper minima at the special boundary orientations, (b) $P$ did not always show the same locations of minima as $R$, and (c) the most favourable boundaries for $P$ were always parallel to the unit cell faces.

As shown in section 3, the assertion that the best interfaces will of necessity be parallel to the cell walls, is certainly not true for f.c.c./b.c.c. boundaries. The cell is fundamentally changed by a change in the vectors used to formulate $\mathbf{S}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{S}^{(2)}$ and here the indices and structures of the cell walls will also be altered. E&R's Fig. 4(a) and (b) showed this effect on the $R$ contour plot, but the consequences of this observation were not pursued in any detail; moreover, if other $\mathbf{S}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{S}^{(2)}$ combinations had been investigated, still more varied contour plots would have been produced.

Also, using the procedure developed by E&R, their first choice of vectors, the $(533)_{\text{f.c.c.}}$ interface will contain three sets of dislocations, whereas for the second choice only two sets of dislocations are produced. This is possibly why the authors observed only a diffuse minimum in $P$ for $(533)_{\text{f.c.c.}}$ in their initial treatment of the problem [7], whereas a much stronger minimum in $R$ was found in the later paper [8]. Since these changes in value are observed in both $P$ and $R$ it is unlikely that either parameter is any more useful than the other. For the 15 interfaces derived by the procedure described in Section 4.2 and for the wide range of orientation relationships studied here, $P$ follows $R$ in much the same way and there is very little to choose between these essentially geometrical parameters. It is probable too that consideration of the complete stereogram of $R$ (or $P$) versus boundary orientation is superfluous since, with the exception of the special orientations, three independent sets of dislocations are required in the "arbitrary" interface. As E&R implicitly recognised, the dislocation contribution to the energy of a partially coherent interface boundary should be assessed upon the basis of more rigorously derived equations, e.g. those of Frank and van der Merve [10] and Mathews (11, 12).

### 5.3. Structural ledges

Previous theoretical treatments [1-3] of f.c.c./b.c.c. interfaces have suggested that for many orientations, particularly those which are near to low index planes, interfaces will be composed of structural ledges. These may be considered to be formed by juxtaposed step faces of the close-packed planes alternating with risers of single (or more) atomic dimensions. In Fe-C-Si [15] there was strong evidence in support of this structure and all interfaces were found to consist of a mixture of structural ledges and dislocations. In a companion paper [17] in the $\mathrm{Cu}-0.3 \% \mathrm{Cr}$ system the evidence was less certain whereas in duplex stainless steels [9] structural ledges were thought to be entirely absent.

In this section it is shown how simple crys- tallography may be used to calculate structural ledge spacings and directions. All of the orientation re- lationships considered here have the close packed phases of both structures parallel and many of the interfaces derived lie close to these planes. In these circumstances the Terrace-Ledge-King (TLK) model which is normally applied to free surfaces shows that on atomic scale the interface is composed of alterna- ting juxtaposed $(111)_{\text {f.c.c. }} /(110)_{\text {b.c.c. }}$ ledge faces and the risers connecting them. Thus structural ledges are implicitly present in the "0" lattice interfaces.

†As R\&A¹ used the interledge spacings, projected onto $\{111\}_{\text {f.c.c. }}$ [tan replaces sin in equation (6)], for comparison purposes Fig. 10(a, b) also uses projected values. $$

$$

where $d_{111}$ is the spacing of the close-packed planes. The ledge direction is also given by the cross product of the directions $(u, v, w)$ and $(001)$, equal to $[v,-u, 0]$.

This method has been used to calculate the mon- atomic structural ledge spacings and directions for the range of orientation relationships and lattice parameters investigated by R&A [1]. Figure 10 com- pares the spacings calculated by the two methods for both "0-A" type interfaces [Fig. 10(a)] and "0-B" [Fig. 10(b)]. In general, the agreement can be seen to be very good: at worst the discrepancy between the two methods is only $20 \%$ and in most cases is much better than this.† When compared with Figs 6, 7 and 10 of R&A [1] the ledge directions also agree to within $\sim 5^{\circ}$. As noted by R&A [1] the presence of bi- or tri-atomic structural ledges would produce simple multiples of the values presented in Fig. 10; the ledge directions will not be affected.

It should, however, be recognised that some of the predicted interfaces are at a substantial angle to the close-packed planes. As the size of the step face decreases when this angle increases, in these circum- stances there will be little interfacial matching across the step faces and the TLK model will have doubtful value. To indicate the range of interfaces that may be described by this model, a circle showing the locus of a 2:1 step face/step height ratio has been included in

Fig. 7(a). It can be seen that most of the interfaces are included, with the exception of f and g with $\theta<2^{\circ}$ and d and e with $\theta>-2^{\circ}$. These interfaces also exhibit dislocation spacings considerably finer than those predicted for the more favourable interfaces and therefore seems unlikely to appear on both grounds.

The difference between ledge formation in Fe-C-Si compared with Cu-Cr and duplex stainless steel may possibly be explained if it is remembered that in Fe-C-Si the interfaces formed quickly whereas the overaged structure was observed in both Cu-Cr and duplex stainless steel. Thus a system of bi- or tri- atomic structural ledges may be of higher energy than a two dislocation or monatomic structural ledge and dislocation combination but may possibly be able to form more quickly.

### 5.4. Comparison with surface dislocation theory
In a recent paper Knowles and Smith [16] have shown how surface dislocation theory may be applied to f.c.c./b.c.c. interfaces, and though the authors do not calculate all of the interfacial parameters presented here, the habit plane of the authors' "best" interface is that of minimum strain energy (interface $P_{3}$ in Table 2, Ref. [16]), is in excellent agreement with the 3,4 interface detailed in Fig. 7(a, b). The values for $|B_{1}|$ and $|B_{2}| \dagger$ are also in good agreement with those calculated here. However, they do not show how the specific dislocation Burgers vectors may be assigned to this interface. It is noted, though, that the direction of maximum misfit in this interface $(=B_{2})$, using the present nomenclature, is close to $[0 \overline{1} 1]$ and will thus favour $\frac{a}{2}[0 \overline{1} 1]_{f.c.c. }$ dislocations, as predicted by the present theory.

### 5.5. Concluding remarks
In their comparison of some experimental obser- vations of interfacial dislocations in stainless steelswith an "0" lattice calculation, Howell et al. [9] obtained reasonable agreement with regard to their dislocation spacings, but poor experimental agree- ment from the contrast behaviour of the dislocations when observed in different $g$ -vectors. Referring to Section 4.2, if it is assumed that the dimensions given in their paper are actually "0" lattice vectors and not dislocation spacings (the authors do not explain in detail) then much better agreement is obtained. Thespacing of the $b_{2}$ array now becomes $|X_{1}^{0}| \sin 60$  $(=4.3 ~nm)$ and that of the $b_{1}$ array will be $|X_{2}^{0}| \sin 60$ (=1.7 nm), i.e. the identity of the dislocations will be inverted. For the limited number of g-vectors em- ployed by the authors reasonable agreement is now found between the theoretical $g \cdot b$ values and the experimental observations. It should be emphasised however that the analysis is far from complete.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS
1. When employed in a rigorous manner, the "0" lattice calculation will predict many of the important characteristics of f.c.c./b.c.c. boundaries. It is able to derive the overall habit plane, dislocation spacings, directions and Burgers vectors and may also calculate the structural ledge spacings and directions.
2. It is not sufficient to consider only three basic vectors when formulating the calculations, as the results are markedly affected by this initial assump- tion. Of the two methods suggested, by means of which every possible solution may be automatically derived, the "dislocation pairs" method was found to be the most satisfactory.
3. For the orientation relationship variants in the range Nishiyama-Wasserman to Kurdjumov-Sachs and for commonly observed lattice parameter ratios, the most favoured interfaces will comprise of two sets of dislocations typically with spacings $2.0-2.5 ~nm$ and $15-25 ~nm$ . These interfaces lie close to $(533)_{f.c.c. }$ for Nishiyama-Wasserman and rotate towards the cen- tre of the stereogram as $\theta$ is varied from 0 to $5^{\circ}$ . The finest set of dislocations will have a Burgers vector of $\frac{a}{2}[0 \overline{1} 1]$ whereas the widest set may be one of [101],[011] or [110] depending on the orientation re- lationship and interface that is formed.
4. The third best interface is $(111)_{f.c.c. }$ which for orientation relationships close to Nishiyama- Wasserman favours the formation of a dislocation network comprising of three sets of dislocations.
5. There is good agreement between the computer modelling results of Rigsbee and Aaronson [1] and the present "0" lattice calculations. Although the approaches may not be equivalent in principle, the methods are complementary as computer modelling may be used where it is not possible to perform the "0" lattice calculation with any certainty (e.g. when $det T \to 0$ ), whereas the " 0 " lattice method is simple to programme and in many aspects more elegant. Thus the claim of Ecob and Ralph [8] that the method used by Rigsbee and Aaronson is "only valid where the dislocation structures have no physical significance" is unjustified.
6. There is substantial agreement between results of surface dislocation theory [16] and those of the "0" lattice calculation, although the former does not appear to derive more than one interface for each orientation relationship.

In a companion paper [17], the results of the present paper will be compared with experimental observations in the $Cu-Cr$ system.

## APPENDIX I

The formation of a macroscopically planar interface with a network of three dislocations is investigated as follows. If the " 0 " lattice points $X_{1}^{0}$ and $X_{2}^{0}$ are such that

$$X_{3}^{0}=X_{1}^{0}+X_{2}^{0} \tag{A1}$$

then it must also be true that

$$b_{3}=b_{b}+b_{2}. \tag{A2}$$

The two-dislocation description of the interface of $b_{1}$ and $b_{2}$ may then be replaced with a 3-dislocation network, by extension of the dislocation node, as indicated in Fig. A1. If all 3 dislocations are of the type $\frac{a}{2}\langle 110\rangle$ there will be a saving in line energy if the line length $l_{1}+l_{2}$ is greater than the "reacted" line length $l_{3}+l_{4}+l_{5}$. For dislocations array

l_{2} & =\frac{d_{2}}{2} \sin \theta^{\prime} \\
