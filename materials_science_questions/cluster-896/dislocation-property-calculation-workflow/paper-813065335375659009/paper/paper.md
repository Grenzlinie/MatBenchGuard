# The energetics of dislocation cores in semiconductors and their role on dislocation mobility

João F. Justo$^{a,*}$, A. Antonelli$^{b}$, A. Fazzio$^{a}$

$^{a}$ Instituto de Física da Universidade de São Paulo, CP 66318, CEP 05315-970, São Paulo, SP, Brazil
$^{b}$ Instituto de Física Gleb Wataghin, Universidade Estadual de Campinas, CEP 13083-970, Campinas, São Paulo, Brazil

## Abstract

We investigated core properties of dislocations in zinc-blende semiconductors using ab initio total energy calculations. The core reconstruction energy of partial dislocations was found to scale almost linearly with the experimental dislocation activation energy. The electronic band structure related to dislocation cores was also determined. In an unreconstructed core, the gap states comprise a half-filled one-dimensional band, which splits up in bonding and antibonding states upon reconstruction. The energy states which lie in the electronic gap come from the cores of $\beta$ partials, while those related to $\alpha$ partials remain resonant in the valence band. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

The presence of dislocations in semiconductors can adversely affect the electronic properties of the material [1]. Current requirements for device miniaturization have challenged designers to increase the active region of the devices, which requires better control over the electronic properties of the dislocations which are always present in the material. Therefore, it is important to understand the physical properties of formation and motion of dislocations in semiconductors.

While dislocation mobility is low in type IV semiconductors, it is considerably higher in type III–V and II–VI compounds under equivalent conditions. Since these materials have the same type of active dislocations, it is important to identify what causes such wide range of dislocation mobilities. In semiconductors, dislocations glide in $\{111\}$ planes [2,3]. These (screw and $60^{\circ}$) dislocations move dissociated in partial dislocations separated by a stacking fault [4]. In a III–V compound, dissociation generates three types of dislocations: $\alpha$, consisting of a $30^{\circ}$ and a $90^{\circ}$ partial with both cores formed by type V atoms, $\beta$, consisting of a $30^{\circ}$ and a $90^{\circ}$ partial with the cores formed by type III atoms, and a screw consisting of two $30^{\circ}$ partials, one of each type. In a type IV material, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ dislocations are identical.

Dislocation motion is a thermally activated mechanism, controlled by the rates of kink formation and migration, with the velocity given by [2]

$$
v_{\mathrm{d}}=v_{0} \exp \left(-\frac{Q}{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}\right)
$$

where $v_{0}$ is a constant, $Q$ is the activation energy, and $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the Boltzmann constant. Recent experiments have measured kink formation and migration energies in silicon, and correlated these energies to the dislocation activation energy [5]. Since dislocation motion is controlled by the energetics of the atomic motions inside the dislocation core [6-8], it has been appreciated how motion should be controlled by the core properties [9]. For a dislocation to move through the lattice, reconstruction bonds must be broken. This provides an additional resistance to dislocation motion, above the usual lattice resistance to interatomic slip. The stronger the reconstruction bonds are, the higher should be the resistance to dislocation motion [8]. Here we established a direct connection between dislocation activation energy and core reconstruction energy of partial dislocations. This was achieved by computing the reconstruction energy of $30^{\circ}$ glide partial (both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ ) dislocations in several zinc-blende materials using ab initio calculations and comparing them to the respective experimental activation energies of $60^{\circ}$ glide dislocations. We focused on the $30^{\circ}$ glide partials motivated by the experimental data on $60^{\circ}$ dislocations, which show that the $30^{\circ}$ partial controls the dislocation mobility [10].

## 2. Theoretical model
We considered a 96-atom orthorhombic supercell with a dislocation dipole [2] consisting of an $\alpha$ and a $\beta 30^{\circ}$ partial in a type III-V material (or two identical $30^{\circ}$ partials in a type IV material). The cell geometry is such that dislocations are four lattice parameters apart in the glide plane, preventing core-core interactions. Fig. 1 shows a $\{111\}$ glide plane containing unreconstructed and reconstructed $30^{\circ}$ ( $\alpha$ and $\beta$ ) partial dislocations. Core reconstruction involves bond formation between atoms of the same species. Reconstruction energies of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ partials were computed by taking the difference in total energies of the configurations 1(b) and 1(a) and the configurations 1(b) and 1(c), respectively.

Full atomic relaxation of the configurations presented in Fig. 1 is a remarkable computational task using ab initio methods. Therefore, we initially relaxed the structures with a less computationally expensive model, using interatomic potentials. These atomic relaxed configurations were used as inputs to the ab initio calculations, when full relaxation could then be performed. However, for this procedure to really speed up the calculations, a reliable interatomic potential should be used, at least for dislocation core properties. One of such models, called (environment dependent interatomic potential) (EDIP), has been recently developed for silicon [11,12], and provides a superior description of dislocation properties [8,13]. This potential, combined with a conjugate gradient algorithm, was used to generate relaxed structures. The reliability of this procedure was tested by checking the atomic forces on the ab initio calculations for the input configurations. For all the compounds studied here, the Hellmann-Feynman forces on all atoms were already below 0.01 Ry/a.u.

The ab initio calculations [14] were performed within the density functional theory and the local density approximation framework. The Kohn-Sham equations were solved by the Car-Parrinello scheme using separable norm-conserving pseudopotentials [15,16]. The valence electron wavefunctions were expanded in a plane-wave basis set, with kinetic energy up to 16 Ry. The sampling in the Brillouin zone was performed using a set of two $k$-points [17]. Geometric optimization of the atomic structures was performed by allowing relaxation until the Hellmann-Feynman forces were smaller than 0.001 Ry/a.u. in all atoms.

This is the first direct evidence of the correlation between core reconstruction energy and dislocation activation energy.

We have additionally investigated the electronic properties of reconstructed and unreconstructed dislocation cores. The current picture of the electronic band structure associated to the dislocation core in semiconductors considers a band inside the materials gap. For an unreconstructed dislocation core, the electronic state at the core is a half-filled one-dimensional band in the band gap. For a reconstructed dislocation core, this one-dimensional band splits up into a filled bonding and an empty antibonding state. This model certainly requires refinement, specially for the case of III–V compounds. To investigate the electronic band structure of the dislocation core we focused our study for the case of aluminum phosphide (AlP). Our choice was motivated by the fact that for all the systems studied here, AlP was the material with the largest energy gap. This way, the identification of the one-dimensional band would become more evident than in the other cases. Still all the conclusions presented here may be extended to other zinc-blende materials.

Fig. 1 shows the electronic band structures of the dislocation cores. To compute the band structure, we considered a large set of $k$-points in the first Brillouin zone along the reciprocal lattice vector related to the dislocation line direction. A very small dispersion in the electronic energy levels is observed in any direction normal to the dislocation line (lower than 40 meV), the dispersion results from a one-dimensional band related to the dislocation line direction. Let us now consider the reconstruction of the $\beta$ dislocation (Figs. 1e and f). In the unreconstructed case (Fig. 1f), bonding and antibonding levels form a

half-filled energy band on the top of the energy gap. On the other hand, in the reconstructed case (Fig. 1e), bonding and antibonding levels split away, forming a gap of 0.15 eV. The last occupied level comes from the Al–Al core interaction. In the reconstructed case, the electronic charge distribu- tion of the last occupied level is highly localized at the interstitial region between Al core atoms. For the unreconstructed case, the charge distribution of the last occupied level is highly delocalized inside the dislocation core. For the case of $\alpha$ dislocation reconstruction, the situation is con- siderably different. The reconstructed bond be- tween the P–P core atoms generates an energy level resonant in the valence band, 4 eV below the top of that band. In the $\alpha$ unreconstructed dislocations, the highest occupied level (Fig. 1d), which forms a band in the bottom of the gap, comes from the $\beta$ reconstructed bond.

In summary, we investigated the core properties of partial dislocations in semiconductors. We identified a direct connection between the core energetics of partial dislocations, a microscopic property, and activation energy of dislocation velocity, a mesoscopic property. Considering our results, as well as the available activation energies for dislocation motion of II–VI zinc-blende semiconductors [25], which are below 1 eV, we estimate that the reconstruction energies for partial dislocations in II–VI materials are very weak, at 0.25 eV/core bond. Furthermore, we have unveiled the electronic band structure of the dislocation core. The $\beta$ dislocations control the mobility of the dislocations, as result of a larger core reconstruction energy. Moreover, they con- trol the electronic activity of the dislocations, being responsible for the dislocation related energy levels which appear in the materials gap.
