# The Force between Misfit Dislocations

By F. R. N. NABARRO†

Institut für Metallphysik der Universität Göttingen

[Received 23 June 1970]

## ABSTRACT
The force between two 'misfit' dislocations in the interface between two crystals having lattice parameters differing by a fraction $\epsilon$ equals the force between any two dislocations having the same Burgers vectors. The misfit dislocations are driven together by the shear stress in the interface. This stress is calculated for the case of two elastic half cylinders of radius $R$. When the half cylinders are embedded in a medium having the same elastic constants, the shear stress in the interface at a distance $x$ from their axis is $F \ln [(R+x)(R-x)]$, where $F=\epsilon \mu / 2 \pi(1-\nu)$. When the cylindrical boundary is freed, the stress near the axis is reduced to $16 F x / 15 R$, and the stress at the edge to $2 F$. The equilibrium spacing of the first few misfit dislocations to enter the interface is of order $(b R / \epsilon)^{1 / 2}$, but the spacing approaches the final value of $b / \epsilon$ when half of the interface contains misfit dislocations.

## § 1. INTRODUCTION
WHEN two lattices with parameters $b(1 \pm \frac{1}{2} \epsilon)$ are brought into contact, their equilibrium configuration contains in the interface a grid of 'misfit' edge dislocations of strength $b$, parallel dislocations being separated by $b / \epsilon$. Why do these parallel like dislocations not repel one another and glide along the interface out of the 'welded' crystal? We cannot properly say that the law of force between two dislocations in the presence of the misfit $\epsilon$ is different from the law in the absence of $\epsilon$. If two dislocations have Burgers vectors of magnitudes $b_{1}$ and $b_{2}$, the energy of the crystal contains self-energy terms proportional to $\epsilon^{2}$, to $b_{1}^{2}$ and to $b_{2}^{2}$, and interaction energy terms proportional to $\epsilon b_{1}, \epsilon b_{2}$ and $b_{1} b_{2}$. An interaction between the dislocations in the presence of the misfit would be proportional to $\epsilon b_{1} b_{2}$, and so outside the scope of linear elasticity theory.

Consider a rigid substrate lying in the region $y<0$, an epitaxial deposit stretching from $x=-R$ to $x=+R$ (fig. 1). Suppose the lattice parameter of the deposit is greater than that of the substrate, so that the deposit is compressed in the $x$ direction. By symmetry, there is no shearing stress $p_{x y}$ across the interface at the origin. If the thickness $t$ of the deposit is much less than $R$, the stress pattern within a distance $t$ of $x=\pm R$ is complicated. Over most of the interface there is a discontinuity of $p_{x x}$, but $p_{x y}=0$. In building up a theoretical model, we first represent the area of misfit by a continuous distribution of infinitesimal edge dislocations lying between $x=\pm R$ in an infinite medium (fig. 2). We then cut out a cylinder of radius $R$, and fully relax the stresses across the cylindrical surface. By a theorem of Eshelby (Nabarro 1967, Scattergood and Kocks 1970), it is not necessary to apply couples to the plane ends of the cylinder. The shear stress in the interface then determines the force on a single misfit dislocation. Since this stress vanishes at the origin, it becomes of order $\epsilon\mu$ (where $\mu$ is the shear modulus) only at distances from the origin comparable with $R$. If two misfit dislocations are introduced, and lie at $\pm X$, their mutual repulsion is proportional to $\mu b^{2}/X$, the stress in the interface drives them inwards with a force proportional to $\mu b\epsilon X/R$, and equilibrium is obtained

when $X$ is of order $(bR/\epsilon)^{1/2}$. The dislocations are now distant
$$R^{2}/X=(\epsilon R^{3}/b)^{1/2}$$
from their images in the cylindrical surface. Their force of interaction with their images is of order $\mu b^{2}(b/\epsilon R^{3})^{1/2}$, which is negligible in comparison with their direct interaction $\mu b^{2}(\epsilon/bR)^{1/2}$.

## § 2. The Stress Distribution for a Line of Misfit in an Infinite Medium

The stress function $\chi(x,y)$ produced by the line of infinitesimal dislocations in fig. 2 is
$$\chi=-(\epsilon Dy/2b)\int_{-R}^{R}\ln\left[(x-\xi)^{2}+y^{2}\right]d\xi,\quad....\quad(1)$$
where
$$D=b\mu/2\pi(1-\nu),\quad........\quad(2)$$
and $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio.

Integrating, we find
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)\chi= & -\frac{1}{2}Rr\sin\theta\ln\left[(R^{2}+r^{2})^{2}-4R^{2}r^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right]+2Rr\sin\theta \\
& +\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\ln\left\{\left[(R-r\cos\theta)^{2}\right.\right. \\
& \left.\left.+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right]/\left[(R+r\cos\theta)^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right]\right\} \\
& -r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\{\tan^{-1}[(R-r\cos\theta)/r\sin\theta] \\
& \quad+\tan^{-1}[(R+r\cos\theta)/r\sin\theta]\}.\quad(3)
\end{aligned}
$$

The second term gives no stresses, and may be neglected.

The stresses $p_{r\theta}$ and $p_{rr}$ are given by the equations
$$p_{r\theta}=-\partial(r^{-1}\partial\chi/\partial\theta)/\partial r$$
and
$$p_{rr}=r^{-1}\partial\chi/\partial r+r^{-2}\partial^{2}\chi/\partial\theta^{2}$$
in the forms
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)p_{r\theta}= & -\frac{1}{2}(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)\ln\left[(R^{2}+r^{2}-2Rr\cos\theta)/(R^{2}+r^{2}+2Rr\cos\theta)\right] \\
& +2\sin\theta\cos\theta\{\tan^{-1}[(R-r\cos\theta)/r\sin\theta] \\
& +\tan^{-1}[(R+r\cos\theta)/r\sin\theta]\} \\
& -4R^{3}r\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta/[(R^{2}+r^{2})^{2}-4R^{2}r^{2}\cos^{2}\theta]\quad.....\quad(4)
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)p_{rr}= & -\sin\theta\cos\theta\ln\left[(R^{2}+r^{2}-2Rr\cos\theta)/(R^{2}+r^{2}+2Rr\cos\theta)\right] \\
& -2\cos^{2}\theta\{\tan^{-1}[(R-r\cos\theta)/r\sin\theta] \\
& +\tan^{-1}[(R+r\cos\theta)/r\sin\theta]\} \\
& +2Rr\sin\theta[R^{2}(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)-r^{2}]/[(R^{2}+r^{2})^{2}-4R^{2}r^{2}\cos^{2}\theta].\quad(5)
\end{aligned}
$$

When $r \gg R$, $p_{r\theta} \to 2\epsilon DR \cos \theta/br$, which is the shear stress produced by a dislocation of strength $2\epsilon R$ at the origin.

Close to the glide plane, $\sin \theta \approx 0$, $\cos^2 \theta \approx 1$, and
$$\{\tan^{-1}[(R - r \cos \theta)/r \sin \theta] + \tan^{-1}[(R + r \cos \theta)/r \sin \theta]\}$$
is $\pi \operatorname{sgn} y$ when $|r/R| < 1$ and 0 when $|r/R| > 1$, where $\operatorname{sgn} y = +1$ when $y > 0$ and $\operatorname{sgn} y = -1$ when $y < 0$. We find that
$$(b/\epsilon D)p_{r\theta} = \ln [(R + x)/(R - x)] \ . \ . \ . \ . \ . \ (6)$$
for all $x$, and that $(b/\epsilon D)p_{rr} = -2\pi \operatorname{sgn} y$ when $|r/R| < 1$. The shearing stress drives the misfit dislocation at $X$ towards the origin, and the tangential stress is discontinuous across the interface. The shearing stress is absent in the case illustrated in fig. 1 and present in the case illustrated in fig. 2 because the ends of the deposit were considered to be constrained in the first case, while they can relax by deforming the matrix $r > R$ in the second case. When $|r/R| > 1$, $p_{rr} = 0$ on the line $y = 0$.

## § 3. The Stress Distribution in Two Misfitting Half Cylinders

We now free the boundary $r = R$ to provide a model of a finite epitaxial deposit on an elastic substrate.

Setting $r = R$ in (4) and (5), we find
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)p_{r\theta} = \frac{1}{2}(\cos^2 \theta - \sin^2 \theta)\ln [(1 + \cos \theta)/(1 - \cos \theta)] & \\
& + \pi \sin \theta \cos \theta \operatorname{sgn} y - \cos \theta, \quad (7)
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)p_{rr} = \sin \theta \cos \theta \ln [(1 + \cos \theta)/(1 - \cos \theta)] & \\
& - \pi \cos^2 \theta \operatorname{sgn} y - \sin \theta. \quad (8)
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the result
$$\ln [(1 + \cos \theta)/(1 - \cos \theta)] = 4 \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} [\cos (2s + 1)\theta]/(2s + 1),$$
we may rewrite these in the forms
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)p_{r\theta} = \frac{1}{3} \cos \theta + \frac{1}{2}\pi \operatorname{sgn} y \sin 2\theta & \\
& + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} [(2t - 1)^{-1} + (2t + 3)^{-1}] \cos (2t + 1)\theta \quad (9)
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b/\epsilon D)p_{rr} = -\frac{1}{2}\pi \operatorname{sgn} y - \frac{1}{3} \sin \theta - \frac{1}{2}\pi \operatorname{sgn} y \cos 2\theta & \\
& + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} [(2t - 1)^{-1} - (2t + 3)^{-1}] \sin (2t + 1)\theta. \quad (10)
\end{aligned}
$$

To relax the stresses on the boundary, we must add a stress field which cancels (9) and (10) when $r = R$, and is regular when $r < R$. An appropriate stress function is given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(b / \epsilon D) \chi^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2} \pi r^{2} \operatorname{sgn} y \sin ^{2} \theta & +\frac{1}{6} R^{-1} r^{3} \sin \theta \\
& +R^{2} \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[a_{t}(r / R)^{2 t+1}+b_{t}(r / R)^{2 t+3}\right] \sin (2 t+1) \theta. \quad(11)
\end{aligned}
$$

The corresponding stresses are given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b / \epsilon D) p_{r \theta}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{3} R^{-1} r \cos \theta & -\frac{1}{2} \pi \operatorname{sgn} y \sin 2 \theta \\
& -\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[2 t(2 t+1) a_{t}(r / R)^{2 t-1}\right. \\
& \left.+(2 t+1)(2 t+2) b_{t}(r / R)^{2 t+1}\right] \cos (2 t+1) \theta \quad(12)
\end{aligned}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b / \epsilon D) p_{r r}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2} \pi \operatorname{sgn} y+ & \frac{1}{3}(r / R) \sin \theta+\frac{1}{2} \pi \operatorname{sgn} y \cos 2 \theta \\
& -\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[2 t(2 t+1) a_{t}(r / R)^{2 t-1}\right. \\
& \left.+(2 t-2)(2 t+1) b_{t}(r / R)^{2 t+1}\right] \sin (2 t+1) \theta. \quad(13)
\end{aligned}
$$

The stresses given by (12) and (13) compensate those given by (9) and
(10) when $r=R$ if, for $t \geqslant 1$,
$$
2 t(2 t+1) a_{t}+(2 t+1)(2 t+2) b_{t}=(2 t-1)^{-1}+(2 t+3)^{-1} \quad. \quad. \quad(14)
$$
and
$$
2 t(2 t+1) a_{t}+(2 t-1)(2 t+2) b_{t}=(2 t-1)^{-1}-(2 t+3)^{-1}. \quad. \quad. \quad(15)
$$

This requires
$$
a_{t}=-(2 t-3) /(2 t-1) 2 t(2 t+3) \quad \text {...... (16) }
$$
and
$$
b_{t}=1 /(2 t+2)(2 t+3) \text {........... (17) }
$$

The complete stress function when the boundary is relaxed is then $\chi+\chi^{\prime}$, where $\chi$ is given by (3) and $\chi^{\prime}$ by (11), (16) and (17).

The shear stress in the interface when the boundary is relaxed is given from (6) and (12) by
$$
\begin{aligned}
(b / \epsilon D) p_{r \theta}= & \ln [(R+x) /(R-x)]-\frac{1}{3}(x / R) \\
& +\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[(2 t-3)(2 t+1) /(2 t-1)(2 t+3)\right](x / R)^{2 t-1} \\
& -\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[(2 t+1) /(2 t+3)\right](x / R)^{2 t+1} \\
= & \frac{16}{15}(x / R)+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left[(2 t+2) /(2 t+1)(2 t+3)(2 t+5)\right](x / R)^{2 t+1}. \quad(18)
\end{aligned}
$$

On putting $x=R$ in (18) we obtain $(b / \epsilon D) p_{r \theta}=2$. It might appear that $p_{r \theta}$ should vanish if we put $\theta=0$ and let $x \rightarrow R$, since the tractions on the boundary $r=R$ have been relaxed. However, the point $r=R, \theta=0$ is a singularity of the stress field. If we consider the last term in the expression

(4) for the unrelaxed stress, we may erect local cartesian axes $R\xi$ and $R\eta$ at this point, parallel to $-Ox$ and $+Oy$ respectively, by writing $r=R(1-\xi)$ and $\theta=\eta$. Then, to second order in $\xi$ and $\eta$, the last term in (4) makes a contribution to $(b/\epsilon D)p_{r\theta}$ of $-\eta^{2}/(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})$. The unrelaxed value of $(b/\epsilon D)p_{r\theta}$ on the interface $(\xi>0,\eta=0)$ is greater than that on the boundary $(\xi=0,\eta>0)$ by unity. Although the relaxation of the boundary removes the infinite stresses associated with the first term in (4), it does not remove the finite singularity associated with the last term$\dagger$.

### § 4. The Introduction of Misfit Dislocations
If we now introduce a single misfit dislocation of strength $-b$, it is attracted towards the origin with a force which decreases from $2\epsilon D$ at the edge of the interface to $16\epsilon Dx/15R$ at distances $x\ll R$ from the origin. This may be compared with the force produced by the unrelaxed stresses, which decreases from infinity at the edge to $2\epsilon Dx/R$ near the origin.

Two misfit dislocations at $\pm X$ repel one another with a force $bD/2X$. Equating this to the attractive force on each, we obtain

$$
X=\pm(15bR/32\epsilon)^{1/2}. \ . \ . \ . \ . \ . \ . \ (19)
$$

If there are three misfit dislocations, one settles at the origin and the others at $\pm(45bR/32\epsilon)$. Not only is the spacing much greater than the final equilibrium spacing $b/\epsilon$, but it increases as the number of misfit dislocations in the interface increases.

Other factors enter when the outermost misfit dislocations approach $X=\pm\frac{1}{2}R$. Suppose the number of misfit dislocations is then $N$. The spacing between an outermost dislocation and its neighbour is then of order $R/N$, and the total repulsive force on the outermost dislocation is of order $NbD/R$. The inward force produced by the stress in the interface is of order $3\epsilon D/8$. Equating these, we find $N\approx3\epsilon R/8b$, and a mean spacing $R/N\approx8b/3\epsilon$. From this stage on, the image forces reduce the repulsion between the misfit dislocations in the way indicated by Scattergood and Kocks.
