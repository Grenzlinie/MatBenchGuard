# Charge density and positron annihilation at lattice defects in aluminium

J Arponen†, P Hautojärvi†, R Nieminen† and E Pajanne‡

† Department of Technical Physics, Helsinki University of Technology,
SF-02150 Otaniemi, Finland
‡ A Ahlström Oy Karhulan Tehtaat, SF-48600 Karhula, Finland

Received 7 June 1973, in final form 17 July 1973

Abstract The Thomas-Fermi approximation is applied to calculate the electron charge distributions of vacancies and edge dislocations in aluminium within the jellium model. The various factors affecting positron trapping at crystal defects are discussed. A method is derived to evaluate the momentum dependent annihilation rate in a nonuniform electron gas.

The calculation gives a value of 24% of the bulk value for the electron density at the centre of a vacancy. The annihilation characteristics are calculated for the trapped positron, and a good agreement with experimental results is established.

An edge dislocation is described by a hollow core model. The hole radius of $0.85\,\text{Å}$ is found to reproduce the experimental lifetime of positrons in deformed aluminium. The electron density at the centre of the dislocation core reduces to 37% of its value in the bulk material. The momentum distributions of the annihilation quanta are calculated for various dislocation orientations, and a remarkable anisotropy in angular correlation curves is found.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years it has been discovered that the behaviour of thermalized positrons in solids is strongly affected by the presence of lattice defects. In metals this effect was first established for vacancies, and it has been unambiguously shown that increasing vacancy concentration leads to a reduction in the positron annihilation rate; observable changes are found in positron lifetimes even at vacancy concentrations of $10^{-6}$. One can also observe narrowing in the angular correlation curve of $2\gamma$ annihilation radiation as well as narrowing in the shape of the Doppler broadened annihilation line. These phenomena are usually interpreted in terms of a trapping model: positrons either annihilate free in the lattice or trapped in vacancies, where they feel a reduced electron density with the above mentioned consequences. For a thorough review of this area, see eg Seeger's paper (1973).

Similar effects have also been reported for crystals containing dislocations. Dekhtyar *et al* (1964) and Berko and Erskine (1967) studied the effect of plastic deformation in angular correlation curves and found remarkable narrowing. Hautojärvi *et al* (1970) measured positron lifetimes in heavily deformed metal samples and found saturation of the lifetime at about 20% deformation. The electronic structure of dislocations and positron trapping by them were discussed on the basis of angular correlation data by Hautojärvi (1972). Cotterill *et al* (1972) looked at positron annihilation in aluminium

in the presence of dislocation loops produced by the annealing of quenched specimens. Positron annihilation in deformed metals has been reviewed in some detail by Goland (1972).

Because positrons are very sensitive to defects and effectively probe the local electron density, annihilation experiments provide unique information about the internal structure of a defect, eg the charge displaced by a vacancy. Some attention has been paid to calculating the displaced charge and formation energies of point defects in metals; for a review, see eg March (1973). A vacancy is usually regarded as a centre for strong scattering of valence electrons, and the displaced charge is calculated through the distortion of the electron Bloch waves.

Hodges (1970) made a theoretical study of the trapping of positrons by metal vacan- cies and calculated an effective potential well binding the positron to a vacancy, to explain the observed trapping or nontrapping in a number of metals. However, he did not consider the redistribution of crystal electrons induced by a vacancy.

The positron-dislocation interaction has been treated by Martin and Paetsch (1972). They used the linear isotropic elasticity theory to derive an approximate two dimen- sional square well potential for the positron. In addition, they employed Brown's (1966) results for the electron distribution around an edge dislocation treated selfconsistently in the framework of linear elasticity theory. On the basis of their model they could not con- sistently obtain realistic results for the angular correlation curve and the positron life- time.

It is clear that in order to fully understand the details of positron annihilation in defective metals one must get insight into the electron distribution around imperfections. In $\S 2$ of this paper we present a model based on the Thomas-Fermi approximation to calculate the valence electron density in and around a vacancy and an edge dislocation. Furthermore, in $\S 3$ we discuss the factors affecting positron trapping at defects. The calculation of the momentum dependent annihilation rate of a positron in a nonuniform electron gas is considered in $\S 4$.

As a test case we use aluminium; there exists a lot of experimental data for it and, on the other hand, aluminium serves as an ideal metal, since annihilation with the ion core electrons plays a minor role. In the case of vacancies, discussed in $\S 5$, we refine our earlier calculations (Arponen et al 1973) and obtain a good agreement with both lifetime and angular correlation experiments. For dislocations it is shown in $\S 6$ that the structure of the core region of a dislocation is of decisive importance in determining the electron distribution and the parameters of the positron trapping process. We present a model for an edge dislocation consistent with experimental annihilation results. It is also argued that one should observe prominent anisotropy between various orientations in space in angular correlation curves for crystal with a definite dislocation orientation.

## 2. Electron density at defects

We consider the jellium model of metals, where the ions are thought to form a constant positive background of charge, and the valence electrons are treated as an electron gas. A defect is described by a certain deviation in the ionic charge from the constant back- ground. It produces changes in the electron density at the defect and disturbs the local charge equilibrium. We denote the ion and electron charge densities by $\rho_{i}$ and $\rho_{e}$, respectively. Their absolute values asymptotically approach the background density $\rho_{0}$.

The total charge density satisfies the Poisson equation†

$$
\nabla^{2} \phi(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right). \tag{2.1}
$$

The electrostatic potential $\phi$ is due to the local charge nonequilibrium and represents the field felt by a unit test charge. We make use of the statistical Thomas-Fermi theory in calculating the electron distribution $\rho_{\mathrm{e}}$. This choice will be discussed in $\S 7$. The principle is to minimize the total energy of the electron system, $E=T+V$, where the kinetic and potential energies are

$$
T=\frac{3}{5}\left(3 \pi^{2}\right)^{2 / 3} \frac{\hbar^{2}}{2 m} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r} n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})^{5 / 3} \tag{2.2}
$$

$$
V=\frac{e^{2}}{8 \pi \epsilon_{0}} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime} \frac{\left(n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})-Z n_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)\left(n_{\mathrm{e}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)-Z n_{\mathrm{i}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|}. \tag{2.3}
$$

Here $n_{\mathrm{e}}$ and $n_{\mathrm{i}}$ denote the ion and electron number densities and $Z$ is the valency of the metal. The condition for the minimum is

$$
\frac{\delta E}{\delta n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})}=\mu=\text { constant. } \tag{2.4}
$$

In the present case, this means that the external perturbation caused by the deformed ionic distribution does not change the chemical potential of the electron system. The local Fermi wavevector $k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{r})$ is then related to its equilibrium value $k_{\mathrm{F}}$ by the equation

$$
\mu=\frac{\hbar^{2} k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2 m}=\frac{\hbar^{2} k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{r})^{2}}{2 m}-e \phi(\boldsymbol{r}). \tag{2.5}
$$

The electron charge density is accordingly

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{e(2 m)^{3 / 2}}{3 \pi^{2} \hbar^{3}}(\mu+e \phi(\boldsymbol{r}))^{3 / 2}=-\rho_{0}\left(1+\frac{e}{\mu} \phi(\boldsymbol{r})\right)^{3 / 2} \tag{2.6}
$$

when $1+e \phi(\boldsymbol{r}) / \mu$ is non negative, but is zero otherwise. When this is inserted in equation (2.1), one arrives at

$$
\nabla^{2} \phi(\boldsymbol{r})=-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\left\{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\rho_{0}\left(1+\frac{e}{\mu} \phi(\boldsymbol{r})\right)^{3 / 2}\right\}. \tag{2.7}
$$

This is the basic equation for the following discussions of the electronic properties of a vacancy and an edge dislocation.

There must be a full screening of the displaced ionic charge to ensure the electrical neutrality of the system:

$$
\int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)=0. \tag{2.8}
$$

This is automatically fulfilled in the Thomas-Fermi theory.

† We use MKSA units throughout.

### 3. Positron states at defects

On considering the polarization of the medium caused by a thermalized positron one can ignore the tightly bound core electrons. The valence electron polarization is much stronger and the structure of the polarization cloud around the positron is not ade- quately described by a linear response theory. In spite of the lack of a detailed theory some progress has been made in understanding the quality of the dressed positron. It is evident that the electron density at the positron is strongly enhanced, as revealed by the electron-positron pair correlation function. The range of this enhancement at metallic densities is of the order of the Bohr radius $a_{0}$. The total screening charge exactly compensates the positron charge and therefore the dressed positron is a neutral particle with an intrinsic size corresponding to a radius $a_{0}$.

In the present case we must consider the motion of the dressed positron in a nonuni- form medium. Let us assume that the properties of the medium are spatially sufficiently slowly varying. Then the motion of the positron is necessarily slow, and the structure of the dressing cloud is always uniquely determined by the local electron density at the positron. In a homogeneous electron gas the positron has the chemical potential $\mu_{+}$, which is the same as the correlation energy $E_{corr}$ defined by Bergersen and Carbotte (1972) and Hodges and Stott (1973). Therefore the function $\mu_{+}(r)$ acts as a classical velocity independent potential, which exerts a force $-\nabla \mu_{+}(r)$ on the dressed positron. At the defect, the local decrease in the electron density leads to a smaller absolute value of the negative correlation energy (Hodges and Stott 1973). This means that the electron- positron correlation works against the localization of positron at defects. We define

$$
V_{\text{corr}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\mu_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})-\mu_{+}. \tag{3.1}
$$

The numerical values for $\mu_{+}(r)$ have been taken from the results of Hodges and Stott (1973).

Since the dressed positron is a neutral particle as a whole, one might be tempted to think that a classical electrostatic field has no effect on the particle. This conclusion is, however, not correct for the following reason. The definition of the Thomas-Fermi approximation (2.4) shows that the total electronic energy is stationary with respect to small variations in the electron density. A small local enhancement $\delta n_{e}(r)$ can freely move around in the system without experiencing any net forces. The reason is that if the potential $\phi(r)$ is the selfconsistent solution of the Thomas-Fermi equation, then the kinetic and the electric potential energies of the additional electrons everywhere add up to the constant electron chemical potential $\mu_{-}$. We apply the above argument also to the screening electrons around a positron, although the enhancement in this case is not very small. Hence, the screening charges do not feel any forces due to the electrostatic field $\phi(r)$, whereas the bare positron does, and thus the dressed positron sees the full potential $e\phi(r)$.

Within the jellium model the function $V_{\text{corr}}+e\phi$ is actually the full potential energy of the positron, since in the absence of external perturbations no other forces appear. Because the ionic charge density is in fact not a smooth function, the jellium model needs improvements. As discussed by Stott (1971), one can successfully simulate the effects of the nonuniformity of the ionic charge by a potential function. This function is zero in vacuo and is $E_{0}$, the bottom height of the positron energy band in a perfect solid, in the bulk material. As a natural generalization to systems of arbitrary density, we use the potential

$$
V_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})=E_{0}\left\{\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})}{n_{\mathrm{i} 0}}\right)^{2 / 3}-1\right\}
\tag{3.2}
$$

where the zero level has been shifted by $E_{0}$. In equation (3.2), $n_{\mathrm{i} 0}$ is the ion number density in a perfect solid. The exponent $\frac{2}{3}$ is obtained by assuming that the energy behaves qualitatively like the kinetic energy of a particle, which is localized within one unit cell. For the zero point energy $E_{0}$ in aluminium we have used the value 4.83 eV of Berko and Plaskett (1958).

To write down the Hamiltonian for the dressed positron we must make some assump- tion about the effective mass of the particle. Because no theory up till now predicts an effective mass substantially different from the bare mass at metallic electron densities, we take the mass to be equal to the bare mass $m$. Thus the Hamiltonian is

$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2 m} \nabla^{2}+V_{\mathrm{t}}
\tag{3.3}
$$

where $V_{\mathrm{t}}$ is the total trapping potential:

$$
V_{\mathrm{t}}(\boldsymbol{r})=e \phi(\boldsymbol{r})+V_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r})+V_{\text {corr }}(\boldsymbol{r}).
\tag{3.4}
$$

Of the various terms of the potential, the electrostatic part $e \phi$ appears to be the most important in practice, the second is $V_{\mathrm{i}}$ and $V_{\text {corr }}$ is the least effective of all.

The Schrödinger equation for the trapped positron is

$$
H \psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})+E_{\mathrm{B}} \psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})=0.
\tag{3.5}
$$

Here $E_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the binding energy and is equal to the energy of the dressed positron in a perfect solid minus the energy of the trapped positron. By construction, $\psi_{+}$is the proba- bility amplitude for the dressed positron, but in the limit of slow variations it should equally well give the probability amplitude of the bare positron.

## 4. Annihilation characteristics

In calculating the annihilation rate of the trapped positron we ignore the contribution due to annihilation with core electrons of the ions surrounding the defect. The total annihilation rate is calculated from the expression suggested by Brandt (1967)

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{t}}=\int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}\left|\psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2} \Gamma\left(r_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)
\tag{4.1}
$$

where $\Gamma(r_{\mathrm{s}})$ is the annihilation rate for a homogeneous electron gas characterized by the density parameter $r_{\mathrm{s}}=(1 / a_{0})(3 / 4 \pi n_{\mathrm{e}})^{1 / 3}$. In the present case, $r_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{r})$ is determined by the local electron density $n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})$. The Sommerfeld free electron formula for the annihilation rate is

$$
\Gamma_{0}=\pi r_{0}^{2} n_{\mathrm{e}} c
\tag{4.2}
$$

where $r_{0}$ is the classical electron radius. We define the enhancement factor $\gamma$ corresponding to a homogeneous electron gas by the equation

$$
\Gamma=\gamma \Gamma_{0}=\pi r_{0}^{2} c \gamma(r_{\mathrm{s}}) n_{\mathrm{e}}(r_{\mathrm{s}}).
\tag{4.3}
$$

$\Gamma(r_s)$ is approximated by the expression (Brandt and Reinheimer 1971)

$$
\Gamma\left(r_{\mathrm{s}}\right)=\frac{12 \cdot 0}{r_{\mathrm{s}}^{3}}\left(1+\frac{r_{\mathrm{s}}^{3}+10}{6}\right) \mathrm{ns}^{-1}
\tag{4.4}
$$

which fits the experimental data for metals quite well.

In contrast to the total annihilation rate, which is a local quantity, the angular correlation characteristics depend on a non local groundstate expectation value contained in the expression for the partial annihilation rate (Arponen and Jauho 1968)

$$
R(\boldsymbol{P})=2 \pi r_{0}^{2} c \iint \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r} \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime} \exp \left\{\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{P} \cdot\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right\}\left\langle 0\left|\phi_{+}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) \phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| 0\right\rangle.
\tag{4.5}
$$

Here $\phi_{+}$ and $\phi_{-}$ are the positron and electron field operators. We proceed to estimate (4.5) in the following way.

Let the electron-positron groundstate $|0\rangle$ be written as follows:

$$
|0\rangle=\int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r} \psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{+}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})|e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle
\tag{4.6}
$$

where $\psi_{+}$ is the previously defined positron wavefunction. It follows that

$$
\phi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})|0\rangle=\psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})|e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle
\tag{4.7}
$$

$$
n_{\mathrm{p}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\left\langle 0\left|\phi_{+}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})\right| 0\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2}\langle e, \boldsymbol{r} \mid e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle.
\tag{4.8}
$$

Since $|\psi_{+}(r)|^{2}$ is the positron density $n_{\mathrm{p}}(\boldsymbol{r})$, as discussed in the preceding section, the state $|e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle$ is normalized to unity. Equation (4.7) shows that the state $|e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle$ is obtained as a certain projection of the electron system, corresponding to the positron being at point $\boldsymbol{r}$. We emphasize that, due to the small mass of the positron, the state $|e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle$ is not like the ground state of the electron system in the case of a heavy point impurity at $\boldsymbol{r}$. The state $|e, \boldsymbol{r}\rangle$ represents an electron system, which has a strong enhancement in the density around $\boldsymbol{r}$ within the intrinsic radius of the dressed positron, and in which the electron density far away from $\boldsymbol{r}$ approaches the unperturbed value $n_{\mathrm{e}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)$. In fact, we know that

$$
\left\langle e, \boldsymbol{r}\left|\phi_{-}^{\dagger}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| e, \boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle \rightarrow \begin{cases}\frac{1}{2} n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r}) \gamma(\boldsymbol{r}) & \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime} \rightarrow \boldsymbol{r} \\ \frac{1}{2} n_{\mathrm{e}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) & \left|\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}\right| \rightarrow \infty.\end{cases}
\tag{4.9}
$$

The expectation value in equation (4.5) is now

$$
\left\langle 0\left|\phi_{+}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) \phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| 0\right\rangle=\psi_{+}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}) \psi_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\left\langle e, \boldsymbol{r}\left|\phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| e, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle.
\tag{4.10}
$$

Denote the mixed density for a nonuniform electron system in its ground state $|e\rangle$ by

$$
F\left(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)=\left\langle e\left|\phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| e\right\rangle.
\tag{4.11}
$$

For a homogeneous electron gas of density $n_{\mathrm{e}}$ this would be

$$
F_{0}\left(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2} n_{\mathrm{e}} g\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|\right)
\tag{4.12}
$$

where

$$
g(z)=\frac{3}{z^{3}}(\sin z-z \cos z).
\tag{4.13}
$$

The function $F_{0}$ thus has a width of the order of $|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}| \leqslant 2 \pi / k_{\mathrm{F}}$, outside of which region it is practically zero. Thus in the spirit of the Thomas-Fermi approximation, assuming the electron density in the nonuniform system to be spatially slowly varying, one can write

(Bethe 1968)

$$
F\left(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{2} n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{R}) g\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|\right)
\tag{4.14}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{R}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{r}+\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)$ and $k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})$ is the local Fermi momentum.

In the presence of a positron one must consider

$$
F_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)=\left\langle e, \boldsymbol{r}\left|\phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| e, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle.
\tag{4.15}
$$

According to equation (4.9) we have $F_{\mathrm{p}}(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{2} n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r}) \gamma(\boldsymbol{r})$. This result is consistent with the ansatz that the enhancement in the electron density can be described by

$$
F_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) \simeq\left(\gamma(\boldsymbol{r}) \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right)^{1 / 2}\left\langle e\left|\phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| e\right\rangle=\left(\gamma(\boldsymbol{r}) \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right)^{1 / 2} F\left(\boldsymbol{r}, \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right).
\tag{4.16}
$$

We believe that the expression (4.16) represents a reasonable approximation for the function $F_{\mathrm{p}}$. The form of the function $F$ is directly related to the momentum distribution of the electrons in the region surrounding the point $\boldsymbol{R}$. The forms of $F$ and $F_{\mathrm{p}}$ as functions of $\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|$ are the same, the only difference being in a multiplicative constant. Equation (4.16) is an extension of the argument used to justify the result that the angular correlation parabola corresponds to the unperturbed electron gas (Berko and Plaskett 1958). This argument assumes that the electron momentum distribution is essentially unaffected by the presence of a positron. In the limit of slow spatial variations in the electron density one can simplify equation (4.16) by replacing $\left(\gamma(\boldsymbol{r}) \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right)^{1 / 2} \simeq \gamma(\boldsymbol{R})$, and combining it with the equations (4.10) and (4.14) we get

$$
\left\langle 0\left|\phi_{+}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) \phi_{-}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}) \phi_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right| 0\right\rangle \simeq \frac{1}{2} n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{R}) \gamma(\boldsymbol{R}) \psi_{+}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}) \psi_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) g\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|\right).
\tag{4.17}
$$

The partial annihilation rate at total momentum $\boldsymbol{P}$ is then

$$
R(\boldsymbol{P})=\iint \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r} \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}^{\prime} \exp \left\{\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{P} \cdot\left(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right)\right\} \psi_{+}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}) \psi_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right) g\left\{k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|\right\} \Gamma\left(r_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{R})\right).
\tag{4.18}
$$

The quantity measured in slit geometry is

$$
I(p)=\frac{1}{(2 \pi)^{3}} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{P} R(\boldsymbol{P}) \delta(\boldsymbol{n} \cdot \boldsymbol{P}-p),
\tag{4.19}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{n}$ is the unit vector normal to the slit and the momenta of two $\gamma$ quanta. The total annihilation rate is

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{t}}=\frac{1}{(2 \pi)^{3}} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{P} R(\boldsymbol{P})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} p I(p).
\tag{4.20}
$$

We wish to make some remarks concerning the properties of the expression (4.18).

(i) In the case of a homogeneous system the positron wavefunction is constant and we obtain

$$
R(\boldsymbol{P})=\Gamma \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r} \exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{P} \cdot \boldsymbol{r}) g\left(k_{\mathrm{F}} r\right)=\Gamma \frac{6 \pi^{2}}{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}} \theta\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}-P\right).
\tag{4.21}
$$

Hence

$$
I(p)=\frac{3 \Gamma}{4 k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-p^{2}\right) \theta\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-p^{2}\right)
\tag{4.22}
$$

which is the well known inverted parabola.

(ii) If the variations in the positron wavefunction are slow compared to variations of

$g(k_{\mathrm{F}}|r - r'|)$, one obtains from equation (4.18) the formula first suggested by Brandt (1967)

$$
I(p)=\frac{3}{4} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{r}\left|\psi_{+}(\boldsymbol{r})\right|^{2} \frac{\Gamma\left(r_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)}{k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{r})^{3}}\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{r})^{2}-p^{2}\right) \theta\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{r})^{2}-p^{2}\right). \tag{4.23}
$$

This means that at each point $\boldsymbol{r}$ the positron annihilates as if it were in a uniform electron gas and produces the free electron parabola, the cut off momentum of which is determined by the local electron density $n_{\mathrm{e}}(\boldsymbol{r})$. In the present case, the conditions justifying the use of equation (4.23) are not fulfilled.

(iii) If the system is translationally invariant in the $z$ direction, as in the case of a dislocation, the positron wavefunction depends only on $x$ and $y$ and is normalized in the $xy$ plane. The $z$ integrations can be performed in equation (4.18) and we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
R(\boldsymbol{P})=\iiint & \int \mathrm{d} x_{1} \mathrm{~d} x_{2} \mathrm{~d} y_{1} \mathrm{~d} y_{2} \psi_{+}^{*}\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right) \psi_{+}\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right) \Gamma\left(r_{\mathrm{s}}(\boldsymbol{R})\right) \exp \left[\mathrm{i}\left\{P_{x}\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)\right.\right. \\
& \left.\left.+P_{y}\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\right\}\right] \frac{3 \pi\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})^{2}-P_{z}^{2}\right)^{1 / 2}}{k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})^{3}\left\{\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)^{2}\right\}^{1 / 2}} \\
& \times J_{1}\left[\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}(\boldsymbol{R})^{2}-P_{z}^{2}\right)\left\{\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)^{2}\right\}\right]^{1 / 2}
\end{aligned} \tag{4.24}
$$

where $J_{1}$ is the Bessel function. In calculating this result use can be made of the fact that

$$
g\left(k_{\mathrm{F}} r\right)=\frac{3}{4 \pi k_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}} \int \mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{q} \exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{q} \cdot \boldsymbol{r}) \theta\left(k_{\mathrm{F}}-q\right). \tag{4.25}
$$

Expression (4.24) is still complicated and difficult to use. However, it is obvious that the result for $R(\boldsymbol{P})$ predicts different forms for the angular correlation curve depending on the orientation of the dislocation with respect to the experimental setup.

## 5. Vacancies

A metal vacancy in a spherically symmetric model is described by the absence of ionic charge from a sphere centered at the vacancy:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(\boldsymbol{r}) & =0 & & r<R_{\mathrm{s}} \\
& =\rho_{0} & & r>R_{\mathrm{s}}.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.1}
$$

The Wigner-Seitz radius $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ is chosen to satisfy the condition

$$
\frac{4}{3} \pi R_{\mathrm{s}}^{3} \rho_{0}=Z e. \tag{5.2}
$$

The Thomas-Fermi equation (2.7) reduces in this case to a one dimensional form, and its solution is discussed in appendix 1. The resulting electron charge distribution $\rho_{\mathrm{e}}$ and the selfconsistent electric potential $\phi$ are shown in figure 1. According to the above model, the electron density at the centre of the vacancy is about $24 \%$ of its value in the bulk material. ## 6. Dislocations

In the following we consider the case of an infinitely long dislocation in aluminium. Aluminium is an fcc crystal and has its edge dislocations in the direction $[1 \overline{1} 2]$. The Burgers vector is $\frac{1}{2} a[110]$, where $a$ is the lattice parameter. There are six different atomic planes perpendicular to the dislocation line; thereafter the pattern is repeated. We take the $z$ axis along the dislocation line and the $y$ axis along the Burgers vector in the glide plane $(\overline{1} 11)$. According to the linear theory of elasticity, the displacements in the $x, y$ and $z$ directions are (Nabarro 1967)

$$
\begin{aligned}
& u=\frac{b}{2 \pi}\left\{\operatorname{arc} \tan \frac{y}{x}+\frac{1}{2(1-v)} \frac{x y}{r^{2}}\right\} \\
& v=\frac{b}{4 \pi(1-v)}\left\{(2 v-1) \ln r+\frac{x^{2}}{r^{2}}\right\} \\
& w=0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.1}
$$

Here $b$ is the Burgers vector $(2.85 \AA$ in $Al$ ), $v$ the Poisson ratio (about 0.3 in $Al$ ) and $r=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{1 / 2}$ . The equations (6.1) yield the dilatation in the $x y$ plane of the form

$$
\Delta(r, \theta)=\frac{b(1-2 v)}{2 \pi(1-v)} \frac{\cos \theta}{r}
\tag{6.2}
$$

where $\theta$ is the polar angle measured from the $x$ axis.

The expression (6.2) is singular at the origin and not valid in the core region of the dislocation. To solve the problem of the structure of the core region one needs informa- tion on the interactions between the dislocated ions. Cotterill and Doyama (1966) have made calculations of the atomic positions in dislocations of fcc copper, using an elasto- atomic model based on a Morse type potential between the ions. They found that the linear theory of elasticity is not strictly valid inside a cylinder with a radius of $9 \AA$ . If

the core region is replaced by a hollow cylinder, the equivalent hole radius determined by energy considerations is $0.8\ \text{\AA}$. However, Cotterill and Doyama pointed out that the core is neither hollow nor like a liquid. Unfortunately, no similar calculations on alu- minium are known to the authors.

It is known that plastic deformation decreases the density of metals by $0.02 \dots 0.2\%$. This is due to nonlinear effects, because equation (6.2) does not predict any net changes in the volume. According to Nabarro (1967) the increase in volume is of the order of $\frac{3}{2}b^3$ per lattice plane. If this is assigned to a hollow cylinder around the dislocation, we find a hole radius of $1.4\ \text{\AA}$ for aluminium.

To get at least a qualitative view of the situation, we describe an edge dislocation by the following model:
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(r, \theta)=
\begin{cases}
0 & r < R_{\mathrm{b}} \\
\rho_{0}(1+\Delta(r, \theta)) & r > R_{\mathrm{b}}.
\end{cases}
\tag{6.3}
$$

This means that the dislocation is considered as a hollow cylinder, outside of which the deformation is given by the linear isotropic theory. The radius of the cylinder, $R_{\mathrm{b}}$, will be chosen so that the lifetime of a positron trapped at a dislocation is consistent with the experimentally known quantity.

The Thomas-Fermi equation (2.7) is solved in cylinder geometry by an iterative method (see appendix 2). The potential $\phi$ behaves asymptotically as in the case of a line dipole
$$
\phi_{r \rightarrow \infty} C \frac{\cos \theta}{r}
\tag{6.4}
$$
where $C=\mu b(1-2 v) / 3 \pi e(1-v)$. This leads to the dipole moment condition
$$
\frac{1}{2 \pi \epsilon_{0}} \int_{0}^{\infty} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \mathrm{d} r \mathrm{~d} \theta r^{2} \cos \theta\left[\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(r, \theta)+\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(r, \theta)\right]=C
\tag{6.5}
$$
which must be fulfilled in addition to the monopole condition (2.8).

The total trapping potential for a positron, $V_{\mathrm{t}}$ is constructed following the ideas of $\S 3$. The positron Schrödinger equation is solved by a variational method with the trial function
$$
\psi_{+}(r, \theta, z)=A \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha r}(1+\alpha r)(1+\beta r \cos \theta) \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} k z}.
\tag{6.6}
$$

We assume complete thermalization, ie $k=0$; the positron has kinetic energy only in the $xy$ plane. The energy is minimized with respect to the parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

The calculating procedure is as follows: choose a plausible radius $R_{\mathrm{b}}$, evaluate the electron distribution $\rho_{\mathrm{e}}$ and the trapping potential $V_{\mathrm{t}}$, determine $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and calculate the positron lifetime from equation (4.1).

The experimental lifetime of 229 ps (Hautojärvi *et al* 1970) corresponds to the radius value $R_{\mathrm{b}}=0.85\ \text{\AA}$. For this radius, the minimum energy is $E_{\mathrm{B}}=2.79\ \text{eV}$ given by the parameters $\alpha=1.57\ \text{\AA}^{-1}$ and $\beta=-0.25\ \text{\AA}^{-1}$. In figure 4 we have plotted the ion and electron distributions and the electrostatic potential $\phi$ along the $x$ axis.

The angular correlation curves were evaluated from equations (4.19) and (4.24) for the three momentum components $p_{x}, p_{y}$ and $p_{z}$ of the annihilation quanta. In the $z$ direction the correlation function reduces to the form of equation (4.23). This is due to the fact that the system is translationally invariant and the positron has no momentum

## 7. Conclusions

The Thomas-Fermi approximation is not frequently used in problems of solid state physics. In atomic physics the comparison of this method with the more sophisticated Hartree and Hartree-Fock calculations shows that the Thomas-Fermi results eg for electron density are very good on the average (Gombás 1950). The application of the Hartree approximation in the case of lattice defects is difficult and therefore necessitates computational simplifications. This might seriously affect the significance of the results. The Thomas-Fermi theory, on the other hand, lends itself to straightforward numerical application.

The results of the vacancy calculations show good agreement with both the positron lifetime and the angular correlation experiments. As the calculation does not involve any adjustable parameters, this simultaneous fit gives further confidence in the Thomas- Fermi result for the electron density. The very small electron density inside a vacancy obtained by Stott *et al* (1970) is difficult to understand in the light of the annihilation characteristics of trapped positrons. Their result is evidently due to the strong repulsive scattering potential they have used for the electrons.

The various factors affecting positron trapping at defects have been considered. The difference in the positron chemical potential in a nonuniform electron gas is included as a potential term $V_{\text{corr}}$ for the positron. This lowers the binding energy of the trapped posi- tron, whereas the annihilation parameters are not appreciably altered.

The non local effects seem to be quite important for the angular correlation of posi- tron annihilation in a nonuniform electron gas. The formula (4.18) derived in this paper accounts well for these effects and is still not too complicated for numerical calculations.

The pure dipole type perturbation due to an edge dislocation given by the linear theory of elasticity does not localize the positron as strongly as predicted by measure- ments. By using a hollow-core dislocation model, in which the hole radius is determined to fit the positron lifetime results, we also find satisfactory agreement in the angular correlation curves. However, it would be physically more sensible to choose a nonzero ionic charge density inside the dislocation. This would correspondingly make the cut off radius larger. The model predicts a clear anisotropy in the angular correlation curves depending on the orientation of the dislocation with respect to the experimental apparatus.

## Appendix 1. Thomas-Fermi equation for a vacancy

We use the notation

$$
\phi(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{\mu}{e} U(\boldsymbol{r}) \tag{A1.1}
$$

where $U$ is now a dimensionless quantity. By the substitution $U(r)=f(r)/r$ we get the

equation (2.7), with the ionic charge distribution defined by equation (5.1), in the form

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2} f(r)}{\mathrm{d} r^{2}}=\varkappa^{2} r\left\{\left(1+\frac{f(r)}{r}\right)^{3 / 2}-\theta\left(r-R_{\mathrm{s}}\right)\right\} \tag{A1.2}
$$

where $\varkappa^{2}=8 k_{\mathrm{F}} / 3 \pi a_{0}$. Adding the term $-k^{2} f(r)$, where $k^{2}>0$, to the both sides of equation (A1.2), one arrives at the boundary value problem

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2} f(r)}{\mathrm{d} r^{2}}-k^{2} f(r)=S(f(r)) \\
& f(0)=0 \\
& f(r) \rightarrow 0 \text { when } r \rightarrow \infty.
\end{aligned} \tag{A1.3}
$$

Here

$$
S(f(r))=\varkappa^{2} r\left\{\left(1+\frac{f(r)}{r}\right)^{3 / 2}-\theta\left(r-R_{\mathrm{s}}\right)-\frac{k^{2}}{\varkappa^{2}} \frac{f(r)}{r}\right\}. \tag{A1.4}
$$

A natural choice is $k^{2}=\frac{3}{2} \varkappa^{2}$, the usual Thomas-Fermi screening constant, because then the right hand side of equation (A1.4) reduces in the linear limit to the ionic charge displacement. Equation (A1.3) can be iteratively solved by starting from a suitable initial guess $f_{0}$, eg $f_{0}(r)=0$. The recursion formula is

$$
f_{n}(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} r^{\prime} G\left(r, r^{\prime}\right) S\left(f_{n-1}\left(r^{\prime}\right)\right) \tag{A1.5}
$$

where

$$
G\left(r, r^{\prime}\right)=-\frac{1}{k}\left\{\sinh k r^{\prime} \mathrm{e}^{-k r} \theta\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)+\sinh k r \mathrm{e}^{-k r^{\prime}} \theta\left(r^{\prime}-r\right)\right\}. \tag{A1.6}
$$

The method gives a rapid convergence and the distribution (2.6) determined by $\phi(r)$ satisfies the neutrality condition (2.8) accurately.

### Appendix 2. Thomas-Fermi equation for a dislocation

We divide the dimensionless potential $U$ into two parts

$$
U(r, \theta)=U_{\mathrm{L}}(r, \theta)+u(r, \theta), \tag{A2.1}
$$

where $U_{\mathrm{L}}$ satisfies the linearized form of equation (2.7), with the ionic displacement (6.3)

$$
\nabla^{2} U_{\mathrm{L}}(r, \theta)-k^{2} U_{\mathrm{L}}(r, \theta)=S_{1}(r)+S_{2}(r) \cos \theta. \tag{A2.2}
$$

Here

$$
\begin{aligned}
& S_{1}(r)=\varkappa^{2} \theta\left(R_{\mathrm{b}}-r\right) \\
& S_{2}(r)=-\frac{\varkappa^{2} b(1-2 v)}{2 \pi(1-v) r} \theta\left(r-R_{\mathrm{b}}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{A2.3}
$$

The solution of equation (A2.2) with the asymptotic form (6.4) can be written

$$
U_{\mathrm{L}}(r, \theta)=U_{1}(r)+U_{2}(r) \cos \theta \tag{A2.4}
$$

where

$$
U_{l}(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} r^{\prime} G_{l}\left(r, r^{\prime}\right) S_{l}\left(r^{\prime}\right) \quad l=1,2. \tag{A2.5}
$$

The Green functions are in this case

$$
G_{l}(r, r')=-r' I_{l}(k r') K_{l}(k r) \theta\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)-r' I_{l}(k r) K_{l}\left(k r^{\prime}\right) \theta\left(r^{\prime}-r\right), \tag{A2.6}
$$

Here $I_{l}$ and $K_{l}$ are the modified Bessel functions.

The nonlinear part $u$ satisfies the residual equation

$$
\nabla^{2} u(r, \theta)-k^{2} u(r, \theta)=\varkappa^{2}\left\{(1+U(r, \theta))^{3 / 2}-1-\frac{3}{2} U(r, \theta)\right\}. \tag{A2.7}
$$

We denote the right hand side of equation (A2.7) by $S(u(r, \theta))$: the equation can be iteratively solved from the integral form

$$
u_{n}(r, \theta)=-\frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{0}^{2 \pi} \mathrm{d} \theta^{\prime} \int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{d} r^{\prime} r^{\prime} K_{0}\left(k\left|r-r^{\prime}\right|\right) S\left(u_{n-1}(r, \theta)\right). \tag{A2.8}
$$

In numerical calculations, the Bessel functions have been replaced by their polynomial approximations (Abramowitz and Stegun 1965). A convenient initial guess is eg $u_{0}=0$. The convergence is good. Both the screening condition and the dipole condition are numerically well satisfied.
