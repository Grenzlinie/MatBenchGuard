# Core structures of the a-edge dislocation in InN
H. P. Lei, $^{a)}$ P. Ruterana, and G. Nouet $^{b)}$
Structure des Interfaces et Fonctionnalité des Couches Minces (SIFCOM), UMR CNRS 6176, ENSICAEN, 6 Boulevard du Maréchal Juin, 14050 Caen Cedex, France

X. Y. Jiang
State Key Laboratory of Functional Materials for Informatics, Shanghai Institute of Microsystem and Information Technology, CAS, Changning Road 865, Shanghai 200050, China

J. Chen
Laboratoire de Recherche sur les Propriétés des Matériaux Nouveaux, Institut Universitaire de Technologie d'Alençon, 61250 Damigny, France

(Received 5 January 2007; accepted 5 February 2007; published online 12 March 2007)

Stillinger-Weber potential parameters were optimized for InN by fitting to the bulk material properties and point defect energy in order to calculate the core structure of the $a$-edge dislocation. The initial displacement field is imposed in the perfect crystal according to isotropic linear elasticity theory, and then the system is relaxed to the minimum energy. The different origins of the displacement field generate three cores with four, eight or five/seven atoms. The calculated energies indicate that the four-atom core structure is the most stable for InN. © 2007 American Institute of Physics. [DOI: 10.1063/1.2712799]

III-V nitride compound semiconductors and their alloys are promising materials for optoelectronic device applications. With a band-gap energy of about 0.7 eV for InN, $^{1,2}$ ${\mathrm{In}}_{x}{\mathrm{Ga}}_{1-x}\mathrm{N}$ alloys provide the possibility to cover a continuous spectral range from near infrared to ultraviolet. In addition, InN has the smallest effective mass and highest electronic drift velocity. $^{3,4}$ These interesting properties of InN make it suitable for high speed integrated circuits. Therefore, great efforts have been made to improve the growth of InN and corresponding alloys. $^{5-7}$ However, the large lattice mismatch and thermal expansion coefficient difference between the layers and substrates result in a high density of threading dislocations. Therefore, it is of importance to know the atomic structures of these dislocations for a better understanding of the physical properties of InN. So far, the $a$-edge and $c$-screw dislocations have been studied by high resolution transmission electron microscopy. $^{8-10}$ In this letter, we present the atomic structures of the $a$-edge dislocation in wurtzite InN as investigated by computational simulation using the Stillinger-Weber (SW) potential.

The Stillinger-Weber potential $^{11}$ was previously used to simulate the physical properties and atomic structures of GaN dislocations. $^{12-14}$ In the framework of the SW potential, only the radial two-body and angular three-body terms are considered in the total energy (Table I). For these dislocations in InN, wrong bonds, i.e., In-In and N-N, are taken into account in addition to In-N bonds. $^{15}$ By fitting to the bulk material properties and point defect energy, the potential parameters are obtained (Table I). In order to check the validity of the parameters, bond length, lattice constants, elastic constants, and Frenkel/Schottky defect energies are calculated and compared to experimental, first-principle, and Shell model $^{16-18}$ values (Table II). For elastic constants, the maximum difference is not more than $14\%$ ($C_{44}$) within acceptable limits. As for point defects, one nitrogen (indium) atom is moved from an equilibrium site to an octahedral site to create a Frenkel anion (cation) defect, and then the whole structure is relaxed under periodic boundary conditions. A Schottky defect is treated similarly. Around the nitrogen (indium) vacancy, wrong bonds In-In (N-N) are formed between neighboring atoms. The defect energy is calculated as the difference between the energies of the perfect and defective structures. The energies of three defects are in fair agreement with those calculated using the Shell model. $^{16}$ The difference may originate from the fact that the Shell model considers the long range interaction but no angular term, in contrast to the SW potential. The energies of two inversion domain boundary structures (with and without wrong bonds), IDB and IDB*, are also calculated (Table II). The energy of IDB* is five times smaller than that of IDB. Although no experimental or first-principle values have yet been reported, our energies may be considered as acceptable in reference to the published values from $ab$ initio investigation in GaN. $^{19}$

With these parameters, we have studied the atomic structures of the $a$-edge dislocation in InN. The initial system is a perfect crystal in the form of a rectangular parallelepiped with sides along the $[1\overline{2}10]$(x axis), $[10\overline{1}0]$(y axis), and [0001](z axis) directions. The size of the parallelepiped is $61a_{0}(1/3)[1\overline{2}10]×61\sqrt{3}/2a_{0}[10\overline{1}0]×2c_{0}[0001]$ with a total of 36 112 atoms, which is far too large for any $ab$ $initio$ investigation. The dislocation line is along the [0001] direction and the glide plane is $(10\overline{1}0)$. Periodic boundary conditions are applied along the dislocation line and fixed boundaries are imposed along the two perpendicular directions. The dislocation is introduced by imposing the displacements on all the atoms according to isotropic linear elasticity theory.$^{20}$ Three regions are defined: Inner and middle regions are two coaxial cylinders with the axis along the dislocation line and the radii are 83 and 99 Å, respectively. In the two regions, all the atoms are relaxed, but only the energy of the atoms in the inner region is accounted for. In the outer region, the atoms are fixed; its thickness makes sure that all inside region atoms have a full neighborhood within the range of the interatomic potentials. The calculation of the minimum energy is performed using the quench molecular dynamics method.$^{21}$ The thermodynamic temperature is calculated at each step and the relaxation process is ended when this temperature is less than $10^{-7}$ K.

Similar to GaN,$^{12}$ three core configurations are generated: four-, five/seven-, and eight-atom core structures. They are typically obtained by placing the dislocation line in P1, P2, and P3 [Fig. 1(a)], corresponding to the origin of the displacement field imposed to the initial perfect structure. P1 and P3 are located between the wide spaced $(10\overline{1}0)$ lattice planes, whereas P2 is located between the closely spaced planes. The relaxed core configurations are shown in Figs. 1(b)-1(d). The four-atom core is characterized by four bonds practically in the same plane. Indium and nitrogen atoms sit on top of each other along this row. The atom configuration of the five/seven-atom core is characterized by wrong bonds N-N and In-In in successive planes, and in the eight-atom core one dangling bond may be present.

Far away from the dislocation line, the strain energy of a dislocation in an isotropic crystal is given by $E_{\text{total}}$=$A_{\text{th}}\ln(R/r_{c})$ within the linear elasticity theory,$^{20}$ where $r_{c}$ is the core radius and $R$ is the radius of a cylinder containing the dislocation line. For edge dislocations, the prelogarithmic factor $A_{\text{th}}$ is $A_{\text{th}}=\mu b^{2}/4\pi(1-v)$, where $\mu=(1-v)(C_{11}^{2}-C_{12}^{2})/2C_{11}$, and $v$ and $b$ are the Poisson ratio [0.399 for InN (Ref. $^{22}$)] and Burgers vector, respectively. The total energy is expressed as $E_{\text{total}}$=$A_{\text{th}}\ln(R/r_{c})$+$E_{c}$, where $E_{c}$ is the core energy stored in the cylinder of radius $r_{c}$. Figure 2 shows the energy $E_{\text{total}}$ stored in a cylinder of radius $R$ as a function of $\ln(R)$ for the three core configurations. The values are compared with GaN (Table III). $A_{\text{th}}^{\text{SW}}$ is determined by using the elastic constants calculated by the SW potential in Table II.

whereas $A_{\text{th}}^{\text{fit}}$ is obtained by fitting the total energy $E_{\text{total}}$ to the radius $R$ of the cylinder according to $E_{\text{total}}$=$A_{\text{th}} \ln(R/r_c)$+$E_c$, i.e., the slope of the linear part of every curve in Fig. 2. For the four-, five/seven-, and eight-atom cores, the slope is similar, $0.43\ \text{eV}/\mathring{\text{A}}$. The core radius is determined by the point from which the curve becomes linear (Fig. 2). The core radius is about $8.7\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ for the three cores. However, unlike the case of GaN, $^{12}$ the four-atom core presents the minimum $E_c$, and its energy is the lowest, meaning that this is the most stable atomic configuration for the $a$-edge dislocation in InN. The discrepancy with GaN may be explained by the bond length ratio of In–In $(2.80\ \mathring{\text{A}})$ to N–N $(1.54\ \mathring{\text{A}})$ in InN which is higher than in Ga–Ga $(2.44\ \mathring{\text{A}})$. Therefore, deformation is probably much more severe in InN for the five/seven-atom core, leading to a larger energy in comparison to GaN. On the other hand, the intensity factor of three-body interaction $\lambda(18.5)$ and In–N bond energy $(1.99\ \text{eV})$ in InN are smaller than those of GaN (32.5 and $2.17\ \text{eV}$, respectively), which explains also why the increase in energy for the four-atom core in InN is smaller than in GaN. This result may be related to the recent report using multiscale theoretical investigation which showed that the four-atom core could be the most stable atomic configuration for the $a$-edge dislocation in GaN, $^{23}$ in the case of tensile strain.
