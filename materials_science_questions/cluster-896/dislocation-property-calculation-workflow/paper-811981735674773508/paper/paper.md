# The Energy of Dissociated Triangular Frank Dislocation Loops in F.C.C. Metals

By P. HUMBLE, R. L. SEGALL† and A. K. HEAD
with an Appendix by H. P. W. GOTTLIEB‡

Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organization, Division of Tribophysics, University of Melbourne

[Received 30 April 1966, and in revised form 5 August 1966]

## ABSTRACT

The energy of a dissociated triangular Frank dislocation loop has been calculated for two models of the way in which the loop may dissociate to form a stacking-fault tetrahedron. By considering the equilibrium between the dissociated Frank dislocation and a complete stacking-fault tetrahedron, a relation between the edge-length of the defect, $l$, and the stacking-fault energy of the metal, $\gamma$, has been obtained. The influence on the $l-\gamma$ relation of the choice of dislocation core energy and the stress present in the metal has been investigated. The results of the calculations have been applied to the experimental observations of Loretto *et al.* (1965) on stacking-fault tetrahedra and triangular Frank dislocation loops formed in plastically deformed copper, silver and gold. The values of stacking-fault energy of these metals so determined are $\gamma_{\mathrm{Cu}}=59\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^2}$, $\gamma_{\mathrm{Ag}}=15\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^2}$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{Au}}=45\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^2}$. The value for silver is now compatible within experimental error with the value deduced from observations on three-fold dislocation nodes.

## § 1. INTRODUCTION

Silcox and Hirsh (1959) showed how stacking-fault tetrahedra could be formed in face-centred cubic metals by the dissociation of triangular Frank dislocation loops. Further, they pointed out that an estimate of the stacking-fault energy of the metal could be made by considering the equilibrium between a Frank dislocation loop and a stacking-fault tetrahedron as a function of the size of the defect. Czjzek *et al.* (1962) considered this problem in detail and, by numerical computation, obtained a relation between $l$, the edge-length of the defect and $\gamma$, the stacking-fault energy for which the energy of the two defects is equal.

Loretto *et al.* (1965) found experimentally that stacking-fault tetrahedra and triangular Frank dislocation loops were formed during plastic deformation of face-centred cubic metals and alloys. By measuring the maximum size of the tetrahedron and the size of the smallest Frank triangle, they were able to establish the value of the edge-length at which

In particular, the value of stacking-fault energy of silver is found to be more than a factor of two lower than that deduced from measurements of three-fold dislocation nodes (Loretto *et al.* 1964) using the theory of Brown and Thölén (1964).

From the published graphs of Czjzek *et al.*, it is possible to show that the energies given for various complete stacking-fault tetrahedra are inconsistent with the expression for the energy of this defect derived by Yoffe (1960). However, since it was found impossible to correct their calculations, the whole problem has been reconsidered. This was facilitated by the derivation of explicit formulae for the mutual elastic interaction energies between straight line segments of dislocation. These formulae are presented in the Appendix.

In the present calculations, the relation between $l$ and $\gamma$ has been obtained for two models of the way in which a triangular Frank dislocation may dissociate to form a tetrahedron. The influence on the $l-\gamma$ relation of the choice of dislocation core energy and the stress present in the metal has been investigated. The results of these calculations have been applied to the experimental observations on stacking-fault tetrahedra and triangular Frank dislocation loops formed in plastically deformed metals.

## § 2. The Derivation of $l-\gamma$ Curves from Models of a Dissociated Frank Dislocation Loop

Since the precise nature of the errors in the calculations of Czjzek *et al.* could not be fully determined from their published results, no corrections to their calculations were attempted, and the problem was completely reformulated.

In order to calculate the energy of a dissociated triangular Frank dis- location loop, it is necessary firstly to assume a model of its geometry. As a first approximation to the configuration of the stable dissociated Frank loop, the defect was considered as a stacking-fault tetrahedron truncated parallel to the original fault plane. Figure 1 (a) shows a side face of this truncated tetrahedron. In the figure, AD, DC and CB are stair-rod dislocations whose Burgers vectors have a magnitude of $b/3$ and AB is a Shockley dislocation whose Burgers vector has a magnitude of $b/\sqrt{3}$. The separation, $h$, between the parallel segments of stair-rod and Shockley dislocation is taken as the variable parameter. This approximation is termed the one parameter model.

This model was chosen because its relatively simple geometry allows us to write down an explicit and reasonably concise expression for the energy, $E$, of the defect in terms of the separation, $h$, the edge-length, $l$, the stacking-fault energy, $\gamma$, and the cut-off radii $\delta_{\text{Sh}}$ and $\delta_{\text{SR}}$, for Shockley and stair-rod dislocations respectively. This expression has been evaluated with the

aid of a computer to give curves of the energy, $E$, of the defect as a function of $h$, varying $h$ in steps of $(\sqrt{3} l / 2) / 20$.

In order to obtain curves of the edge-length at which a dissociated Frank loop transforms to a stacking-fault tetrahedron versus the stacking-fault energy from the $E$-$h$ curves applicable to the work of Loretto *et al.* (1965), it is necessary to decide under what conditions the transformation will occur. This involves an assessment of the significance of any energy barrier which may exist during the transformation of Frank loops to tetrahedra. Loretto *et al.*, in applying the calculations of Czjzek *et al.*, assumed that if, for a given $l$ and $\gamma$, the minimum energy of a Frank loop was equal to or greater than the energy of a tetrahedron, then a tetrahedron would form. This criterion ignores any energy barrier which may have to be surmounted in transforming a Frank loop to a tetrahedron.

The $l$--$\gamma$ curves presented here are derived from the $E$-$h$ curves which satisfy the criterion that the minimum energy of a dissociated Frank loop is equal to the energy of the corresponding stacking-fault tetrahedron; this criterion is similar to that used by Czjzek *et al.* and Loretto *et al.*. Such $E$-$h$ curves are here termed critical curves and the significance of the energy barrier involved in these curves is discussed in $\S 4$.

To investigate the effect of varying the core radii both on the energy of the defects and on the form of the $l$--$\gamma$ curves, the cut-off radii were made proportional to the Burgers vectors of the appropriate dislocations. The proportionality factor, $n$, was allowed to take values of $\frac{1}{2}, 1$ or 2. Throughout this paper, unless otherwise stated, the value of Poisson's ratio, $\nu$,

In order to obtain a direct comparison between the results of Czjzek *et al.* and the present calculations, the second model for the configuration of the dissociated Frank loop was chosen to be similar to that used by Czjzek *et al.* In our configuration, shown in fig. 1 (b), a length $p$ at each end of the Shockley dislocation is allowed to rotate through an angle $\theta$, before meeting the stair-rod dislocations. Thus, our model and that of Czjzek *et al.* have the same number of degrees of freedom. Our model is here termed the three-parameter model.

Using this model, it is no longer easy to write down a simple explicit expression for the energy of the defect in terms of the three variable parameters, $h$, $p$ and $\theta$. Instead it is more useful to write a computer programme which considers each pair of straight segments of dislocation in turn, sets up the coordinate axes as defined in the Appendix for each particular case, calculates the interaction energy and then sums all the component interaction energies.

## § 4. Discussion

In §2 it was mentioned that a criterion has to be applied to the $E$-$h$ curves in order to obtain from them pairs of values of $l$ and $\gamma$ with which to plot the $l$--$\gamma$ curve. This criterion thus directly affects the value of $\gamma$ deduced for a defect of a particular edge-length. The criterion suggested by Czjzek *et al.*, adopted by Loretto *et al.*, and the one which has been used in the previous sections of this paper is that for which the minimum energy of the dissociated Frank loop is just equal to the energy of the complete stacking-fault tetrahedron. It was noted earlier that this criterion ignores any energy barrier which may have to be surmounted in transforming a Frank loop to a tetrahedron.

In the course of a recalculation of the $l$--$\gamma$ relation similar to that presented here, Jøssang and Hirth (1966) have adopted a criterion in which, for a given $l$ and $\gamma$, the $E$-$h$ curve has a point of inflection with a horizontal tangent. For this criterion, therefore, there is, by definition, no energy barrier to be surmounted in transforming a dissociated Frank loop into a stacking-fault tetrahedron. This criterion will clearly result in a lower value of stacking-fault energy for a particular edge-length, than the value obtained using the criterion of Czjzek *et al.*

In the absence of an applied stress, the value of $\gamma$ deduced using the criterion of Jøssang and Hirth would be correct. However, experiments such as those of Loretto *et al.* do involve an applied stress, and we have therefore attempted to assess the importance of this stress in overcoming any possible energy barrier involved in the formation of stacking-fault tetrahedra from triangular Frank dislocation loops.

Assuming a symmetrical model for the dissociated Frank dislocation loop, and assuming that the local stress present in the metal is aiding equally the further separation of all three Shockley dislocations from the three stair-rod dislocations, then by finding the maximum value of the

product of the slope of the $E$-$h$ curve, and the reciprocal of the total length of Shockley dislocation at the point where the slope is measured, it is possible to deduce the stress necessary to overcome the energy barrier, in the absence of thermal activation.

For the particular case of annealed polycrystalline silver in the experi- ments of Loretto *et al.*, the specimens which were deformed at room temperature were deformed by reverse bending around a 0·1 in. mandrel. The strain, and thus the corresponding value of the stress, is not easy to estimate, but the value of the strain is thought to be about $0.5\%$; $2\%$ strain is considered to be an upper limit. These values of strain correspond to stresses of about $415\mathrm{Kg/cm^2}$ and about $690\mathrm{Kg/cm^2}$ respectively (Carreker 1957). If we identify the local stress with the stress applied to the metal, then, with the assumptions outlined above, it is possible to plot the $E$-$h$ curves for the value of the edge-length of the largest tetrahedron reported by Loretto *et al.* for silver (260$b$) for several values of $\gamma$ until those curves are found which just require stresses of $415\mathrm{Kg/cm^2}$ and $690\mathrm{Kg/cm^2}$ to transform the Frank loop into a tetrahedron. If it is assumed that the largest tetrahedron observed by Loretto *et al.* was formed under the optimum stress conditions outlined above, this procedure leads to values of the stacking-fault energy of silver for $n=1$ on the three- parameter model of $1.85\times10^{-3}Gb$ and $2.07\times10^{-3}Gb$ respectively. These values are to be compared with values of $\gamma$ of $1.52\times10^{-3}Gb$ deduced using the criterion of Jøssang and Hirth and $1.90\times10^{-3}Gb$ using the criterion of Czjzek *et al.* Since the value of $\gamma$ deduced, using the criterion of Czjzek *et al.*, lies between the values deduced, making the above assumptions about the influence of stress, one may conveniently use values deduced on the criterion of Czjzek *et al.* when calculating values of stacking-fault energy from the experimental values of Loretto *et al.*

Since the strain, and hence the stress, in the specimens deformed by Loretto *et al.* was in fact non-uniform, they argued that if the stress was playing any significant role in the transformation of Frank dislocation loops to stacking-fault tetrahedra, then there ought to be an overlap in the sizes of the largest tetrahedron and the smallest Frank triangle observed in a given metal. They did not, in fact, observe such an overlap, and deduced therefore that stress played no significant role in the trans- formation. However, on the basis of the present arguments, this obser- vation may also be explained if the stress necessary to overcome the energy barrier involved in the transformation is a rapidly increasing function of the edge-length of the defect. For in this case, if such an overlap exists, it would be small, and well within the experimental error involved in measuring the defects. The present calculations show that this is probably the case, since for the case of silver when $l=260b$, increasing the edge-length by $10\%$ requires an increase in stress of about $40\%$ to overcome the energy barrier.

The value of stacking-fault energy for silver of $1.85\times10^{-3}Gb$ calculated from the experimental value of $l_0$, the edge-length of the largest tetrahedron,

is strictly an upper limit to the stacking-fault energy and a corresponding value of the lower limit could be obtained from the value of $l_{\mathrm{F}}$, the edge-length of the smallest Frank triangle. Hence, if we take the mean values of $l_{0}$ and $l_{\mathrm{F}}$ reported by Loretto *et al.* for copper, silver and gold, we obtain estimates of the true stacking-fault energies of these metals. These values are listed in table 3, together with the several parameters used in their derivation. They have been obtained on the criterion of Czjzek *et al.* using the three parameter model for $n=1$. As indicated earlier for the case of silver, values deduced on this criterion are probably adjusted for the influence of the applied stress used in the experiments of Loretto *et al.*

It should be pointed out that the values of $\gamma$ listed in table 3 differ from those given by Loretto *et al.* This arises for four reasons. Firstly, these workers used the ratios of the values of the edge-lengths of the largest tetrahedra observed in these metals to scale the stacking-fault energies obtained from the $l$-$\gamma$ relation given by Czjzek *et al.* to the value for silver of $21\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^{2}}$ obtained from the observation of three-fold nodes. Secondly, they used the value of $l_{0}$, and not the mean of $l_{0}$ and $l_{\mathrm{F}}$ when using this $l$-$\gamma$ relation. Thirdly, they used only the $l$-$\gamma$ relation of Czjzek *et al.* for which Poisson's ratio was 0·4. Lastly, slightly different values of the shear modulus have been used here.

The value of $15\cdot4\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^{2}}$ for the stacking-fault energy of silver given in table 3 is to be directly compared with a value of $22\cdot7\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^{2}}$ deduced by substituting a value of $450\ \mathring{\mathrm{A}}$ for the radii of three-fold dislocation nodes (Loretto *et al.* 1964) in the appropriate formula of Brown and Thölén (1964) together with the same values of the interatomic spacing, shear modulus, Poisson's ratio and the inner cut-off radius. The experimental error involved in measuring the edge-lengths of tetrahedra is about $\pm15\%$, whereas the reported scatter in node radii is about $\pm33\%$. Thus the value for the stacking-fault energy of silver deduced from observations on stacking-fault tetrahedra and triangular Frank dislocation loops formed in deformed silver is $15\pm2\ \mathrm{ergs/cm^{2}}$ and that deduced from observations on three-fold dislocation nodes in silver is $23_{-6}^{+9}\mathrm{ergs/cm^{2}}$. These values are now just compatible.

In the above analysis, the inner cut-off radius of the Shockley dislocation has been taken as the Burgers vector of that dislocation for both theories. It should be emphasized that, although this is a consistent procedure, it is an arbitrary one. Moreover, an additional assumption has been made in the case of tetrahedra and dissociated Frank dislocation loops by taking the inner cut-off radius of stair-rod dislocations as their Burgers vector. This choice is consistent with that made for Shockley dislocations, but again it is arbitrary and need not represent the true physical situation. It may not even represent a correct relative situation as far as the core energies of Shockley and stair-rod dislocations are concerned.

It is interesting that the values of $\gamma$ deduced from both theories are uncertain by similar amounts for the same range in $n$. Thus, when the values of $\gamma$ calculated for $n=\frac{1}{2}$ and $n=2$ are compared with the values calculated for $n=1$, the uncertainty in the values of $\gamma$ deduced from measurements on tetrahedra and measurements on nodes is about $\pm 11\%$ and $\pm 10\%$ respectively.

The method of obtaining $\gamma$ from observations on triangular Frank dislocations and stacking-fault tetrahedra formed in deformed metals is experimentally applicable to materials of a larger range of stacking-fault energies than that using extended three-fold dislocation nodes. The main disadvantage of the former method is that stress can aid the formation of tetrahedra which are metastable rather then stable, and some assumption must be made about the significance of this factor. The calculations presented in this paper provide the basis on which the stacking-fault energy may, in fact, be deduced from such observations.

## APPENDIX
### THE MUTUAL ELASTIC ENERGY BETWEEN STRAIGHT DISLOCATION SEGMENTS
#### General Formulae

In this Appendix are given a set of explicit formulae for the mutual elastic interaction energy between straight dislocation segments. From these it is possible to obtain explicit expressions for the self-energy of any closed dislocation configuration which is composed of straight line segments.

The mutual elastic energy of interaction, $E_{\mathrm{I}}$, between two straight dislocation segments in an isotropic medium may be written, following de Wit (1960) as:

$$E_{\mathrm{I}}=b_{i} b_{j}{ }^{\prime} M_{i j},$$

where $b_{i}$ are the components of the Burgers vector of one segment and $b_{j}{ }^{\prime}$ are the components of the Burgers vector of the other segment. The summation convention over $i$ and $j$ is implied. As de Wit remarks, there is no unique expression for the energy of a part of a dislocation configuration, only an energy of the whole. Thus there are various possible expressions for the $M_{i j}$. The ones given in the following sections of this Appendix are derived from expression 15.9 of de Wit.

Using the relations 6.7, 6.8 and 6.9 of de Wit for straight line segments of dislocation, the expression 15.9 of de Wit reduces to:

$$
M_{i j}=\frac{-G}{4 \pi}\left[J\left(\gamma_{j}{ }^{\prime} \gamma_{i}+\frac{2}{m-1} \gamma_{i}{ }^{\prime} \gamma_{j}-\frac{m}{m-1} \delta_{i j} \gamma_{k}{ }^{\prime} \gamma_{k}\right)-I_{i j} \frac{m}{m-1} \gamma_{k}{ }^{\prime} \gamma_{k}\right],
$$

where
$$
J=\iint \frac{d s d s^{\prime}}{R}
$$

and
$$
I_{i j}=\iint \frac{X_{i} X_{j} d s d s^{\prime}}{R^{3}}
$$

$X_{i}$ is defined in terms of the coordinates $x_{i}$ and $x_{i}{ }^{\prime}$ along respective dislocation segments by the relation:

$$
X_{i}=x_{i}-x_{i}{ }^{\prime},
$$

$$
R=\left(X_{i} X_{i}\right)^{1 / 2} \text {. }
$$

$\gamma_{i}$ and $\gamma_{i}{ }^{\prime}$ are the direction cosines of elements of dislocation line $d s$ and $d s^{\prime}$ respectively.

The integral $J$ has been evaluated by Campbell (1915) for the cases of mutual inductances in electromagnetic theory.

In the above expressions of de Wit the parameter, $m$, is related to Poisson's ratio, $\nu$, by

$$
\nu=1 / m \text {. }
$$

With a suitable choice of the location and orientation of the axes the integrals $J$ and $I_{i j}$ may be evaluated individually.

The first case considered is where the dislocation segments have a general skew orientation. The remaining cases are special limiting cases of this but must be given explicitly for computer use since the general case would involve the numerical cancellation of infinities.

### Case 1. Skew segments of dislocation

A right-handed set of orthogonal axes is set up relative to the two dislocation segments $\mathrm{AB}$, and $\mathrm{A}^{\prime} \mathrm{B}^{\prime}$, as shown in fig. $4(a)$. The $x$-axis is placed along the positive direction of the first segment, $\mathrm{AB}$, and the $z$-axis is oriented so that it is perpendicular to both segments of dislocation.

This means that the second segment, A'B', is parallel to the $x-y$ plane. The origin of coordinates is chosen as the point of intersection of the first segment with the projection of the second segment on the $x-y$ plane.

The lengths AB and A'B' are $d$ and $d'$ respectively, $d$ and $d'$ always being positive; the perpendicular distance between segments is $p$, and the

projections of the segments on the $x-y$ plane make an angle $\theta$, measured anti-clockwise from AB to the projection of A'B'.

The distances of the start of segment AB and projection of segment A'B' from the origin, namely OA and O'A', are $\delta$ and $\delta'$ respectively, measured in the positive direction of the respective segment, so that $\delta$ and $\delta'$ may be positive or negative. For example, in fig. 4 (a), $\delta$ is positive and $\delta'$ negative. The various distances between the starting and finishing points of the segments are defined as follows:

$$
R_1=\text{AB}',
$$

$$
R_2=\text{A'B},
$$

$$
R_3=\text{AA}',
$$

$$
R_4=\text{BB}',
$$

where $R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4$ are always positive quantities.

With this geometry the quantities $M_{ij}$ are given as follows:

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{11}}{G}=\frac{\cos\theta}{1-\nu}[R\cos\theta+S(\cos^2\theta-\nu)+T(1-\nu)]-p\Omega\cot\theta,
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{22}}{G}=\frac{\cos\theta}{1-\nu}[-R\cos\theta+S(1+\sin^2\theta)+T]-\frac{2p\Omega\cot\theta}{1-\nu},
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{33}}{G}=\frac{\cos\theta}{1-\nu}[S+T],
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{12}}{G}=\frac{\sin\theta}{1-\nu}[R\cos\theta-S(\sin^2\theta-\nu)-T(1-\nu)]+p\Omega,
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{21}}{G}=\frac{\sin\theta}{1-\nu}[R\cos\theta+S(\cos^2\theta-2\nu)-2\nu T]+\frac{2p\nu\Omega}{1-\nu},
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{13}}{G}=\frac{4\pi M_{31}}{G}=\frac{p\cos\theta}{1-\nu}[Q_1-Q_2],
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{23}}{G}=\frac{4\pi M_{32}}{G}=\frac{p\cot\theta}{1-\nu}[Q_4-Q_3+(Q_2-Q_1)\cos\theta],
$$

where

$G$ is the shear modulus,

$\nu$ is Poisson's ratio,

$$
R=R_3+R_4-R_1-R_2,
$$

$$
Q_1=\ln\left[\frac{d'+\delta'-(d+\delta)\cos\theta+R_4}{\delta'-(d+\delta)\cos\theta+R_2}\right],
$$

$$
Q_{2}=\ln \left[\frac{d^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}-\delta \cos \theta+R_{1}}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta \cos \theta+R_{3}}\right],
$$

$$
Q_{3}=\ln \left[\frac{d+\delta-\left(d^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}\right) \cos \theta+R_{4}}{\delta-\left(d^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}\right) \cos \theta+R_{1}}\right],
$$

$$
Q_{4}=\ln \left[\frac{d+\delta-\delta^{\prime} \cos \theta+R_{2}}{\delta-\delta^{\prime} \cos \theta+R_{3}}\right],
$$

$$
S=(d+\delta) Q_{1}-\delta Q_{\tilde{z}},
$$

$$
T=\left(d^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}\right) Q_{3}-\delta^{\prime} Q_{4},
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Omega= & \arctan \left(\frac{p \cot \theta}{R_{4}}+\frac{(d+\delta)\left(d^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}\right) \sin \theta}{p R_{4}}\right) \\
& -\arctan \left(\frac{p \cot \theta}{R_{2}}+\frac{(d+\delta) \delta^{\prime} \sin \theta}{p R_{2}}\right) \\
& -\arctan \left(\frac{p \cot \theta}{R_{1}}+\frac{\left(d^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}\right) \delta \sin \theta}{p R_{1}}\right) \\
& +\arctan \left(\frac{p \cot \theta}{R_{3}}+\frac{\delta \delta^{\prime} \sin \theta}{p R_{3}}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The values of each of the arctans contained in $\Omega$ are to be evaluated between $-\pi / 2$ and $+\pi / 2$.

Case 2. Non-parallel co-planar segments not starting at a common point

A right-handed set of orthogonal axes is set up with respect to the segments as shown in fig. $4(b)$. The $x$-axis is placed along the positive direction of the first segment $\mathrm{AB}$, and the $z$-axis is oriented so that it is normal to the plane containing the two segments. The $y$-axis is placed in the plane of the two segments so that the origin of coordinates is at the point of intersection of the extension of the first segment $\mathrm{AB}$ with the extension of the second segment $\mathrm{A}^{\prime} \mathrm{B}^{\prime}$. The quantities $d, d^{\prime}, \delta, \delta^{\prime}, \theta, R_{1}$, $R_{2}, R_{3}$ and $R_{4}$ are defined in a similar manner to the previous case, and the $M_{i j}$ are given by the same expressions putting $p=0$ and $\Omega=0$.

Case 3. Co-planar segments starting at a common point

In this case, the axes are set up in a similar way to that described for case 2 (fig. $4(b)$ ) except that the origin of coordinates is now placed at the common starting point of the two segments.

In this case $R_{3}=0, R_{1}=d^{\prime}, R_{2}=d$, and $\delta=\delta^{\prime}=0$.

The $M_{i j}$ are given by the expressions of Case 1 if both $Q_{2}$ and $Q_{4}$ are formally replaced by zero in addition to putting $p=0$ and $\Omega=0$ as in case 2.

### Case 4. Parallel segments

The axes are oriented in a similar manner to the previous two cases, but since in this case the two segments do not intersect when produced, the origin of coordinates is chosen as the starting point, A, of the first segment, AB. This is shown in fig. 4 (c). In this case the perpendicular distance between the lines is $p$, $\theta=0$ and $\delta=0$, but the quantities $d$, $d'$, $\delta'$, $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$ and $R_4$ are defined in the same way as for case 1.

The quantities $M_{ij}$ are given by the expressions:

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{11}}{G} = \frac{2-\nu}{1-\nu} R + dQ_1 + (d'+\delta')Q_3 - \delta'Q_4,
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{22}}{G} = \frac{1}{1-\nu} \left[ dQ_1 + (d'+\delta')Q_3 - \delta'Q_4 \right],
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{33}}{G} = \frac{1}{1-\nu} \left[ R + dQ_1 + (d'+\delta')Q_3 - \delta'Q_4 \right],
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{12}}{G} = \frac{4\pi M_{21}}{G} = \frac{p}{1-\nu} (Q_4-Q_3),
$$

$$
M_{13}=M_{31}=M_{23}=M_{32}=0,
$$

where

$$
R=R_3+R_4-R_1-R_2,
$$

$$
Q_1=\ln\left[ \frac{d'+\delta'-d+R_4}{\delta'-d+R_2} \right],
$$

$$
Q_3=\ln\left[ \frac{d-d'-\delta'+R_4}{-d'-\delta'+R_1} \right],
$$

$$
Q_4=\ln\left[ \frac{d-\delta'+R_2}{-\delta'+R_3} \right].
$$

### Case 5. Collinear, non-overlapping segments

In this case, both dislocation segments are placed along the $x$-axis so that their positive directions lie along the positive direction of the $x$-axis. The starting point, A, of the first segment AB is placed at the origin of coordinates. In this case, therefore, $p=0$, $\theta=0$ and $\delta=0$ but otherwise the quantities $d$, $d'$, $\delta'$, $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$ and $R_4$ are defined in the same way as for case 1.

The quantities $M_{ij}$ are given by the expressions:

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{11}}{G} = Q_5,
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{22}}{G} = \frac{Q_5}{1-\nu},
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi M_{33}}{G} = \frac{Q_5}{1-\nu},
$$

$$
M_{12}=M_{21}=M_{32}=M_{23}=M_{31}=M_{13}=0,
$$

where

$$
Q_5=R_1\ln R_1 + R_2\ln R_2 - R_3\ln R_3 - R_4\ln R_4.
$$

### Case 6. The self-energy of a dislocation segment

The final case which must be considered is the self-energy of a dislocation segment. As usual in such calculations, the preceding expressions give an infinite self-energy for a segment, and some more or less arbitrary inner cut-off must be assumed. The convention of Bullough and Foreman (1964) is a convenient inner cut-off to use, since in this case the resulting self-energy can be identified as the total elastic strain energy in the medium outside a cylindrical volume surrounding the dislocation. Using this convention, the self-energy, $E_{\mathrm{S}}$, of a straight length, $d$, of dislocation is given by:

$$
E_{\mathrm{S}}=\frac{G d}{4 \pi(1-\nu)}\left\{b_{\mathrm{S}}{ }^{2}\left[(1-\nu) \ln \left(\frac{2 d}{\epsilon}\right)-(2-\nu)\right]+b_{\mathrm{E}}{ }^{2}\left[\ln \left(\frac{2 d}{\epsilon}\right)-\frac{1-2 \nu}{4(1-\nu)}\right]\right\},
$$

where $b_{\mathrm{S}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{E}}$ are the screw and edge components of the Burgers vector of the dislocation segment and $\epsilon$ is the radius of the excluded cylinder.
