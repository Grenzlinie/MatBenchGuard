# Screw-dislocation motion in b.c.c. transition metals
## Model calculation using a tight-binding-type electronic theory

By AKIKAZU SATO and KIN-ICHI MASUDA

Department of Materials Science and Engineering,
Tokyo Institute of Technology, Ookayama, Meguro, Tokyo 152, Japan

[Received 19 June 1979 and accepted 8 November 1979]

## ABSTRACT

Screw-dislocation motion under various types of shear stress has been investi- gated using a tight-binding-type electronic theory. The repulsive core-core interaction energies are simulated by a Born-Mayer potential, as well as a simple power law potential. Calculated Peierls stresses $\tau_{p} / \mu$ for $\alpha$ -Fe (W) are 0.054(0.036),0.058(0.032) and $0.080(0.062)$ for shear on $\{110\}$ planes, on $\{112\}$ planes in the twinning direction, and on $\{112\}$ planes in the anti-twinning direction respectively, where $\mu$ is the shear modulus. It is found that the motion of screw dislocations is strongly dependent upon the filling of the $d$ -band and the orientation of the applied stress. These results qualitatively explain the anisotropic plastic behaviour of b.c.c. transition metals and the differences in the orientation dependence of the observed yield (Peierls) stress between the transition metals.

## § 1. INTRODUCTION

It has been observed that body-centred cubic (b.c.c.) metals exhibit characteristic plastic behaviour, such as a rapid increase in the flow stress with decreasing temperature, breakdown of the Schmid law, and complicated orientation dependences and asymmetry of the yield stress and of the slip geometry (Taoka, Takeuchi and Furubayashi 1964, Christian 1970). In the case of the transition metals these effects are very pronounced and it is of great interest to investigate them theoretically. For example, for transition metals there is a striking difference in the orientation dependence of the Peierls(yield) stress $\tau_{p}$ between metals such as tungsten, molybdenum and tantalum $(N_{d} \simeq 4,5, N_{d}$ being the number of $d$ -electrons ; hereafter referred to as type-B transition metals) and metals like $\alpha$ -Fe $(N_{d} \simeq 7$ ; referred to as type-A transition metals). The Peierls stress $\tau_{p}$ of the former (type-B) transition metals behaves $\tau_{p}(\chi=30^{\circ})>\tau_{p}(\chi=0^{\circ})>\tau_{p}(\chi=-30^{\circ})$ , while the $\tau_{p}$ of the latter (type-A) transition metals exhibits the behaviour $\tau_{p}(\chi=30^{\circ})>\tau_{p}(\chi=-30^{\circ})>\tau_{p}(\chi=0^{\circ})$ ,where $\chi$ is the angle between the maximum shear stress plane and the (110) plane.

In order to understand the mechanisms behind this characteristic plastic behaviour of transition metals a number of model calculations have been performed, in the main using pair potentials between the metal atoms. Chang (1967), using an anharmonic pairwise potential, has estimated the magnitudes of the Peierls stress and the core energy of an $(a / 2)\langle 111\rangle$ screw dislocation in $\alpha$ -iron. A detailed study of the screw dislocation core behaviour

under the effect of a shear stress applied on a {110} plane has been carried out by Duesbery, Vitek and Bowen (1973) for a number of pair potentials repre- senting materials with different binding strengths. Minami, Kuramoto and Takeuchi (1972, 1974) used the inter-atom row potentials originally introduced by Suzuki (1968) to calculate the Peierls stress of the screw dislocations in b.c.c. metals for the range, $-30^{\circ} \leqslant \chi \leqslant 30^{\circ}$. A number of similar model calcu lations using pair-interaction potentials have also been performed (Heinrich and Schellenberger 1971, Diener, Heinrich and Schellenberger 1971, Basinski, Duesbery and Taylor 1970, Doyama and Cotterill 1966, de Hosson, Sleeswyk, Caspers, Van Heugten and van Veen 1976).

However, these model calculations based on the pair-interaction potentials have not succeeded in explaining the systematic differences in the orientation dependence of $\tau_{p}$ between type-A and type-B transition metals. This seems to result from the fact that these pair-potential methods are incapable of explicitly taking into account the effect of the filling of the $d$-bands, which plays a leading role in calculations of the surface tension (Cyrot-Lackmann 1968, 1969), vacancy formation energy (Allan and Lannoo 1976), stacking fault energy (Ducastelle and Cyrot-Lackmann 1971) and the formation energies of other lattice defects. Furthermore, one has to bear in mind that the pair potentials derived from bulk properties do not take into account the changes in the force constants between pairs of atoms near the lattice defects, resulting from the redistribution of valence electronst.

In this paper we present a tight-binding-type electronic theory for $d$-electrons around a dislocation, and study the motion of a screw dislocation in b.c.c. transition metals in detail. We demonstrate that the above-mentioned differences in the orientation dependence of $\tau_{p}$ between type-A and type-B transition metals can be explained in terms of this electronic theory.

The format of the present paper is as follows. In $\S 2$ we present the principles of the calculations of screw dislocation motion in b.c.c. transition metals. Results of numerical calculations and related discussions are given in $\S 3$. Section 4 is devoted to conclusions.

## § 2. PRINCIPLE OF CALCULATIONS

It is well known that the $d$-electron contribution to the cohesive energy of transition metals explains the general trend and magnitude of the corresponding experimental results (Friedel 1969). Furthermore, the evolution of the atomic volume and the elastic modulus along the series of transition metals has been explained by the filling of the $d$-bands (Ducastelle 1970). It seems therefore reasonable to investigate the various (electronic) properties of lattice defects in transition metals by considering the $d$-band deformation due to the defects.

We use a moments technique for the $d$-band which has already been used with some success to describe various properties of transition metals (Cyrot- Lackmann 1966, 1968). We neglect the influence of $s$-electrons and $s-d$

mixing. However, the effects of $s(p)$-electrons could be included partially in the calculations if the short-range repulsive energies were taken into account, as mentioned later.

The local (d-electrons) density of states $\rho_{i}(E)$ on atom $i$ is approximated by a Gaussian curve fitted to the second moment $\mu_{2 i}$ :
$$
\rho_{i}(E)=\frac{10}{\sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2 i}\right)} \exp \left(-\frac{E^{2}}{2 \mu_{2 i}}\right),\quad (1)
$$
taking the free-atom energy level as the origin of the energies. The factor 10 in eqn. (1) is due to the degeneracy of the $d$-band, and the second moment $\mu_{2 i}$ of atom $i$ is given by
$$
\mu_{2 i}=\sum_{j} \beta_{i j}{ }^{2},\quad (2)
$$
where $\beta_{i j}$ is an effective resonance integral which is related to the usual two centre integrals $d d \sigma, d d \delta$ and $d d \pi$ (Ducastelle and Cyrot-Lackmann 1971) by
$$
\beta_{i j}{ }^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\left[d d \sigma^{2}(i j)+2 d d \pi^{2}(i j)+2 d d \delta^{2}(i j)\right].\quad (3)
$$

Following Ducastelle (1970) and Allan and Lannoo (1973, 1976), we assume that the effective resonance integral $\beta_{i j}$ varies exponentially with the inter-atomic distance $r_{i j}$
$$
\beta_{i j}=\beta_{0} \exp \left(-q r_{i j}\right).\quad (4)
$$

It has been shown that if $r_{0}$ is the equilibrium inter-atomic distance in the middle of the transition series, a reasonable value of $q$ can be given as $q r_{0} \approx 3$ (Ducastelle 1970).

One can now determine the band structure energy $\delta E_{\mathrm{e}}$ as a function of the atomic displacements, from the expression
$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta E_{\mathrm{e}}=10 \sum_{i} \int_{-\infty}^{E_{\mathrm{f}}}\left(E-E_{\mathrm{f}}\right) & \left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2 i}\right)} \exp \left(-E^{2} / 2 \mu_{2 i}\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\frac{1}{\sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2}\right)} \exp \left(-E^{2} / 2 \mu_{2}\right)\right\} d E \quad(5 a) \\
=-10 \sum_{i}\left\{\sqrt{ }\left(\mu_{2 i} / 2 \pi\right)\right. & \exp \left(-E_{\mathrm{f}}{ }^{2} / 2 \mu_{2 i}\right)-\sqrt{ }\left(\mu_{2} / 2 \pi\right) \exp \left(-E_{\mathrm{f}}{ }^{2} / 2 \mu_{2}\right) \\
& \left.+\left(E_{\mathrm{f}} / 2\right)\left[\operatorname{erf}\left(E_{\mathrm{f}} / \sqrt{ }\left(2 \mu_{2 i}\right)\right)-\operatorname{erf}\left(E_{\mathrm{f}} / \sqrt{ }\left(2 \mu_{2}\right)\right)\right]\right\}, \quad(5 b)
\end{aligned}
$$
where
$$
\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{ } \pi} \int_{0}^{x} \exp \left(-t^{2}\right) d t
$$
and $E_{\mathrm{f}}$ and $\mu_{2}$ are the Fermi energy and the second moment for the unperturbed transition metal $d$-band respectively. The appearance of $E_{\mathrm{f}}$ (the integrand in eqn. $5(a)$ ) is related to the shift of the crystal Fermi energy which enables us to keep the number of electrons constant. It is noted that, in eqns. $(5 a)$ and $(5 b)$, the variations in the diagonal potential created by the lattice defects are neglected and the Friedel sum rule is not satisfied (non-selfconsistent calculation). The validity of using a non-selfconsistent method (eqns. $(5 a)$ and $(5 b)$ ) for a screw dislocation will be discussed later ( $\S 3.5)$.

To account for the repulsive core-core interaction energies (arising from the interatomic $d-d$ repulsion and the increase in $s p$ kinetic energy upon compression) at small distances, we consider a Born-Mayer type potential, as well as

a simple power law potential given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{r}}\left(r_{i j}\right) & =C_{0} \exp \left(-p r_{i j}\right), & & (6 a) \\
& =\tilde{C}_{0} / r_{i j}{ }^{n}. & & (6 b)
\end{aligned}
$$

The Born-Mayer potential in eqn. (6 a) has been used by many authors (Ducastelle 1970, Allan and Lannoo 1973, 1976) for the ion-core interactions of transition metals, while a simple power law potential in eqn. (6 b) has been used by Born and others for the repulsive forces between ions in ionic crystals (Seitz 1940). In general both repulsive potentials give qualitatively the same results for the elastic properties of transition metals. However, in order to reproduce the experimental values of the bulk modulus and the elastic constants of a given transition metal simultaneously, it is better to use one of these two repulsive potentials, depending on the elastic properties of the (given) transition metals as will be shown later in the table. The repulsive energy $E_{\mathrm{r} i}$ per atom is then given, for the potentials given by eqns. (6 a) and (6 b), as

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{r} i} & =\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j} C_{0} \exp \left(-p r_{i j}\right), & & (7 a) \\
& =\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j} \tilde{C}_{0} / r_{i j}{ }^{n}, & & (7 b)
\end{aligned}
$$

where sum over $j$ includes the neighbouring atomic sites around the site $i$.

From eqns. (5) and (7), one can calculate the total energy as a function of the atomic displacements. The parameters appearing in the present calculation are chosen so as to satisfy the equilibrium condition and, so as to reproduce the experimental values of the cohesive energy $E_{\mathrm{c}}$, the bulk modulus $B$, and the elastic constant $C_{44}$, with $q r_{0}=3$. For the Born-Mayer potential of eqn. (6 a),

$$
F q r_{0}\left(4 E_{1}+3 \alpha E_{2}\right)\left(4 E_{1}+3 E_{2}\right)^{-1 / 2}-C_{0}\left(4 p r_{0} R_{1}+3 \alpha p r_{0} R_{2}\right)=0,
$$

$$
-F\left(4 E_{1}+3 E_{2}\right)^{1 / 2}+C_{0}\left(4 R_{1}+3 R_{2}\right)=E_{\mathrm{c}},
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{9}\left[-F\left(q r_{0}\right)^{2}\left(4 E_{1}+3 E_{2}\right)^{-2 / 3}\right. & \left\{\left(4 E_{1}+3 \alpha E_{2}\right)^{2}+24(1-\alpha)^{2} E_{1} E_{2}\right\} \\
& \left.+C_{0}\left\{4\left(p r_{0}\right)^{2} R_{1}+3 \alpha^{2}\left(p r_{0}\right)^{2} R_{2}\right\}\right]=B\left(\frac{\alpha r_{0}}{2}\right)^{3}, \quad(10)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\left(F q r_{0} / 18\right)\{4 & \left.\left(4 q r_{0}-1\right) E_{1}-9 \alpha E_{2}\right\}\left(4 E_{1}+3 E_{2}\right)^{-1 / 2} \\
& +\left(C_{0} / 18\right)\left[4\left\{2\left(p r_{0}\right)^{2}-p r_{0}\right\} R_{1}-9 \alpha p r_{0} R_{2}\right]=C_{44}\left(\frac{\alpha r_{0}}{2}\right)^{3}, \quad(11)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $F=10\left|\beta_{0}\right| \pi^{-1 / 2} \exp \left(-E_{\mathrm{f}}^{2} / 2 \mu_{2}\right), \quad E_{1}=\exp \left(-2 q r_{0}\right), \quad E_{2}=\exp \left(-2 \alpha q r_{0}\right)$, $R_{1}=\exp \left(-p r_{0}\right), R_{2}=\exp \left(-\alpha p r_{0}\right)$ and $\alpha=2 / \sqrt{ } 3$. In the above formulation, we have taken into account the contribution of the second nearest neighbours. The repulsive part of the above formula for $C_{44}$ differs from that obtained by Fuchs (1936). This discrepancy seems to come from the fact that in our derivation the two strain components $\epsilon_{x y}$ and $\epsilon_{y x}$ are considered simultaneously so that the unphysical rotation of the crystal may be avoided, while Fuchs considered single shear in one plane parallel to one surface of the cube. For the repulsive potential in eqn. (6 b) one can obtain an explicit expression denoting the equilibrium condition, and expressions for $E_{\mathrm{c}}, B$ and $C_{44}$ as well.

For the b.c.c. structure, one can determine the three unknown parameters $\beta_0$, $C_0$ and $p$ in eqns. (5) and (7 $a$) by use of eqns. (8), (9) and (10). $C_{44}$ can then be obtained from eqn. (11) with the Born-Mayer potential. $C_{44}$ can also be calculated from the similar expressions as in eqns. (8), (9) and (10) with the simple power-law potential. As shown in the table, it was found to depend on the choice of the potential. When a lattice defect such as a dislocation, in which shear movement of atoms play a dominant role in determining the structure, is concerned, the value of $C_{44}$ may not be overlooked. One can now use the electronic-energy expression of eqn. (5 $b$), and the relations in eqns. (7 $a$) and (7 $b$) for the repulsive energies, to calculate the core structure and the Peierls stress of an $(a/2)\langle 111\rangle$ screw dislocation in b.c.c. transition metals (Fe, Nb and W). The computation process is as follows.

(1) First, we take a cylindrical crystal region with radius $10\sqrt{2}b/3$ ($b$ being the magnitude of the Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}$), consisting of 102 atomic rows (surrounded by the broken line) as shown in fig. 2. The validity of using a crystallite (cylindrical crystal region) size of the order $\sim 100$ atomic rows is discussed later.

(2) At the centre of the cylindrical crystal region, a screw dislocation is introduced with the strain field calculated from isotropic linear elasticity theory.

(3) The crystal is sheared homogeneously in the $z$-direction so that a known shear stress is applied to the crystal.

In the present results, the core configuration of an $(a / 2)\langle 111\rangle$ screw dislocation with no applied stress show relatively small deviation from that determined from linear elasticity theory. This is in contrast to the results of the model calculations based on the pair interaction potentials (Vitek 1974, 1976, Minami *et al.* 1972, 1974, Kuramoto *et al.* 1974). For example, Vitek (1974, 1976), and Vitek *et al.* (1970) obtained a core structure with threefold symmetry, which deviates substantially from that determined from linear elasticity theory ; most of the displacement is concentrated on the three $\{110\}$ planes radiating from the dislocation centre. On the other hand, Takeuchi (1979) found that the core structure depends strongly on the choice of the atomic row potential ; a screw dislocation can take also a non-dissociated structure as found in the present study, depending on the shape of the potential. These results seem to indicate that the calculated core structure depends strongly on the detail of the model.

To investigate the effects of the crystallite size on the calculated dislocation core structure, we also calculated the core structure of an $(a / 2)$. $\langle 111\rangle$ screw dislocation in $\alpha$-Fe using cylindrical crystal regions with 226 and 547 atomic rows. The differences between results calculated with crystallite with 102 atomic rows and one with 226 (or 547) atomic rows is too small to be distinguished in these kind of figures. A further increase in the crystallite size would only lead to trivial changes in the calculated core structure, but would require a great increase in computation time. We consider that the configuration obtained with a crystallite with 102 atomic rows is sufficiently close to that which would be obtained with an infinite crystallite.

The changes in the core configuration with increasing shear stress for $\chi=0^{\circ}$ are shown in figs. $4(a)$ and $(b)$. As shown in fig. $4(a)$, when a shear stress of $0.054 \mu$ ($\mu$ is the shear modulus) is applied in the (011) plane so that a force $\tau b$ acts in the $[21 \overline{1}]$ direction on the dislocation, the core configuration becomes asymmetric but the force is not enough to move the dislocation. When the stress is increased to $0.062 \mu$, the dislocation moves over the neighbouring low-energy position as shown in fig. $4(b)$ (and shown schematically in fig. $5(a)(A)$ ), and then stops, because of the imposed boundary conditions, with a similar core configuration as in fig. $4(a)$. Furthermore, when the stress is increased to $0.068 \mu$, the dislocation moves over the second low-energy position, as is schematically shown in fig. $5(a)(B)$. This is in contrast to the results obtained using the inter-atomic (Vitek 1976) or inter-atomic row (Minami et al. 1974) potentials, where the dislocation is shown to move along a zigzag path for $\{011\}\langle 111\rangle$ shear.

Figure $5(b)$ shows curves of the total energy $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ (for 102 atomic rows) versus $N_{\mathrm{I}}$ for a unit length of an $(a / 2)\langle 111\rangle$ screw dislocation in $\alpha$-Fe, where $N_{\mathrm{I}}$ is the number of iterative calculations. The rapid decrease in $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ corresponds to a significant displacement of atoms around the dislocation. It means that two stages of this rapid decrease in $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ are necessary to move the screw dislocation to the next low-energy position in the case of $\chi=0^{\circ}$ for $\alpha$-iron.

When the shear stress is applied in the (112) plane in the anti-twinning direction $(\chi=30^{\circ})$, so that the force acts in the $[10\overline{1}]$ direction, the dislocation core motion follows a zigzag path, as schematically shown in fig. 6 (a) (the corresponding curves of $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ versus $N_{\mathrm{I}}$ are shown in fig. $6(b)$ ). This is consistent with the intuitive expectation that the screw dislocation will move towards the direction of the applied force. However, this behaviour of a screw dislocation motion for $\chi=30^{\circ}$ differs completely from that obtained using the interatomic row potentials (Minami et al. 1972, 1974). These authors predicted the surprising behaviour that the screw dislocation moves towards the twinning direction even when the applied force acts in the anti-twinning direction.

The most interesting case is that of a stress applied in the (112) plane in the twinning direction $(\chi=-30^{\circ})$. The displacement of the core and the corresponding curves of $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ versus $N_{\mathrm{I}}$ are shown in figs. $7(a)$ and $(b)$, respectively. As shown in fig. $7(a)$, the dislocation begins to move towards the direction of the applied force [110] at a stress of $0.062 \mu$, forming a split configuration (A in fig. $7(a)$ ). It continues to move over the next metastable position, keeping the split configuration (B in fig. $7(a)$ ), and subsequently moves to the next low-energy position (C) with a non-split core configuration. Here it is

important to note that, when the dislocation moves from the original position to the next low-energy position, three stages of rapid energy decrease appear in the curvers of $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ versus $N_{\mathrm{I}}$. This is in contrast to the case of $\chi=0$ or $30^{\circ}$, where only two stages of rapid energy decrease appear in the curves of $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ versus $N_{\mathrm{I}}$.

Summarizing the behaviour of screw dislocation motion discussed above, one notices that the results are very sensitive to the model used to evaluate the defect energetics in b.c.c. transition metals. Therefore the motion of screw dislocations should be investigated using the fundamental model (microscopic electronic theory) rather than using the classical central force model.

We also checked that a small shift in $N_{d}$ $(|\Delta N_{d}|\approx0.4$ for $N_{d}=7)$ does not change appreciably the above results for the behaviour of screw dislocations. However, a large shift in $N_{d}$ leads to fundamental changes in the screw-dislocation motion, as will be discussed in $\S 3.2$. The behaviour of screw-dislocation motion in b.c.c. transition metals therefore depends on the filling of the $d$-band $(N_{d})$.

### 3.2. Screw-dislocation motion for type-B transition metals

The behaviour of screw-dislocation motion in type-B transition metals ($N_d \approx 4, 5$) differs significantly from that in type-A transition metals ($N_d \approx 7$), especially for the $\chi = -30^\circ$ orientation. The screw-dislocation motion for the $\chi = 0$ and $30^\circ$ orientations is essentially the same as that in type-A transition metals. Figures 8 (a) and (b) show the motion of a screw dislocation and the corresponding curves of $E_{\text{T}}$ versus $N_{\text{I}}$ in a type-B transition metal (Nb, $N_d \approx 4$) for the $\chi = -30^\circ$ orientation. Although the split configuration appearing in the dislocation motion is similar in these two types of the transition metals, there is a distinct difference in the curves of $E_{\text{T}}$ versus $N_{\text{I}}$. That is, in contrast to the type-A transition metals, the screw dislocation in type-B transition metals moves towards the next low-energy position, maintaining a split configuration, as seen in the $0.026\mu$ curve in fig. 8 (b). Instead, the metastable split configuration (B) in fig. 7 (a) becomes unstable, as shown in the $0.030\mu$ curve in fig. 8 (b).

Thus the observed difference of

the Peierls stress between type-A and type-B transition metals is closely related to the stability of the split configuration of a screw dislocation under stress, particularly for the $\chi=-30^{\circ}$ orientation.

### 3.3. Orientation dependence of the Peierls stress $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$
Under zero applied stress, the dislocation core energy per 102 atomic rows of length $b$ was calculated to be 0.61 eV for $\alpha$-Fe, 0.27 eV for Nb and 1.43 eV for W, respectively. These values are in good accord with the values of $C_{44}$ listed in the table, showing that the evaluation of the dislocation energy presented here is in good agreement with the result of simple elasticity calcu- lation in which the energy is proportional to the shear modulus. This fact, therefore, justifies the following discussion of $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ given in units of the shear modulus.

The calculated Peierls stress $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ is plotted as a function of $\chi$ in fig. 9 for $\alpha$-Fe, Nb and W. Although the values of $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ are generally $\sim 10$ times larger than those obtained by experiment, the present results in fig. 9 clearly show that there are essential differences in the orientation dependence of $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ between type-A ($\alpha$-Fe) and type-B (Nb, W) transition metals, in agreement with experiment (Takeuchi, Kuramoto and Suzuki 1972, Lau and Dorn 1970, Argon and Maloof 1966, Spitzig and Keh 1970). ### 3.4. Effects of crystallite size
In order to investigate the effects of the crystallite size on screw-dislocation motion we have calculated the Peierls stresses for transition metals while systematically changing the crystallite size. Figure 10 shows the Peierls stress of $\alpha$-Fe for the $\chi=0^{\circ}$ orientation as a function of $N^{-1}$, where $N$ is the number of atomic rows inside a given cylindrical crystal region (cf. fig. 2). One can see that the calculated Peierls stress $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ decreases almost linearly as the value of $N^{-1}$ decreases. In addition, one notices that the order of magnitude of $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ for an $N \approx 100$ crystallite is the same as the extrapolated value of $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ for $N=+\infty$. We have checked that this is true for other orientations, and even for other transition metals. The results indicate that the calculated Peierls stress $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ for a crystallite with $\sim 100$ atomic rows is sufficiently close to that which would be obtained for an infinite crystallite.

### 3.5. Influence of ' self-consistency '

Here we investigate the influence of ' self-consistency ' on screw dislocation motion in transition metals. In this paper ' self-consistency ' means that electrical neutrality is maintained in the cylindrical crystal region (the Friedel sum rule ; the number of states below $E_{\mathfrak{t}}$ is constant).

Since the self-consistent diagonal potential $\alpha_{i}$ is related to the shift in the orbital energy of atom $i$ (Allan, Lannoo and Dobrzynski 1974), we may write

$$
\rho_{i}(E)=10 / \sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2 i}\right) \exp \left\{-\left(E-\alpha_{i}\right)^{2} / 2 \mu_{2 i}\right\}. \tag{12}
$$

Assuming that there is no charge transfer, one obtains

$$
\alpha_{i}=\left(1-\sqrt{ }\left(\mu_{2 i} / \mu_{2}\right)\right) E_{\mathfrak{t}}. \tag{13}
$$

In view of the detailed calculations on the localized electronic states of dislocations in metals (Brown 1978, de Hosson 1978), this assumption is not unreasonable. Thus, in the Hartree approximation, the self-consistent change in the electronic energy $\Delta E_{\mathrm{SC}}$ is calculated as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E_{\mathrm{SC}}=\sum_{i}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{E_{\mathfrak{t}}} E \delta \rho_{i}(E) d E-\right. & \alpha_{i} \int_{-\infty}^{E_{\mathfrak{t}}} \frac{10}{\sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2}\right)} \\
& \left.\times \exp \left(-\frac{E^{2}}{2 \mu_{2}}\right) d E-\frac{1}{2} \alpha_{i} \int_{-\infty}^{E_{\mathfrak{t}}} \delta \rho_{i}(E) d E\right] \quad(14 a) \\
=\sum_{i} 10(1-\sqrt{ }\left(\mu_{2 i} / \mu_{2}\right)) & \sqrt{ }\left(\mu_{2} / 2 \pi\right) \exp \left(-E_{\mathfrak{t}}^{2} / 2 \mu_{2}\right), \quad(14 b)
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\delta \rho_{i}(E)=\frac{10}{\sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2 i}\right)} \exp \left\{-\frac{\left(E-\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}}{2 \mu_{2 i}}\right\}-\frac{10}{\sqrt{ }\left(2 \pi \mu_{2}\right)} \exp \left(-\frac{E^{2}}{2 \mu_{2}}\right). \tag{15}
$$

Using eqn. (14 b) with the repulsive energies of eqn. (7), we have calculated the core configuration under zero applied stress, and the Peierls stress $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$, of $\alpha$-Fe for the $\chi=0^{\circ}$ orientation. In general the self-consistent results are quite similar to the corresponding results obtained from the non-selfconsistent model. This conclusion is not surprising and is consistent with the fact that the difference between the self-consistent and non-selfconsistent calculations is negligible when the exchange of charges is expected to be a minor effect (Allan *et al.* 1974, Allan and Lannoo 1976).

### § 4. Conclusions

We have investigated the motion of screw dislocations in b.c.c. transition metals in detail using a tight-binding-type electronic theory. The short-range repulsive energy, arising from the inter-atomic $d$-$d$ repulsion, and the increase in $sp$ kinetic energy upon compression, is taken into account and simulated by the usual Born-Mayer potential, as well as by a simple power-law potential. In general we found that the present results for the motion of screw dislocations are significantly different from those obtained by the pair-potential method. This indicates that the influence of the electronic structure on the screw-dislocation motion is far from negligible.

The present analysis can also include the effects of the filling of the $d$-band explicitly, and offers the possibility of determining the influence of electronic rearrangements on screw-dislocation motion, which is probably not the case for calculations based upon the pair-potential method. It is gratifying that the present calculation gives the essential differences in the orientation dependence of the Peierls stress between type-A and type-B transition metals, in agreement with experiment. However, the magnitude of the calculated Peierls stresses is considerably higher than that obtained by experiment.

For a more quantitative understanding of the screw-dislocation motion in b.c.c. transition metals there is a need for a detailed study of the electronic structure around the dislocation and the repulsive forces in transition metals. Such studies are now being made (Masuda and Sato 1980, Pettifor 1977, Cyrot 1977).
