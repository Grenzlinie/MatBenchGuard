# Determination of the Type of Ferroelectric Domain Nucleated around the Dislocation by the Mechanism of Domain Wall Nucleation

S. G. INGLE and J. G. DUPARE

Laxminarayan Institute of Technology, Nagpur University, Nagpur 440 010, India

(Received July 17, 1990; accepted for publication December 22, 1990)

According to the elastic theory of dislocations, the stresses in the region surrounding the core of the dislocation can be relieved if we give a small hypothetical displacement, called the relieving displacement, to the atoms in this region. This concept has been developed to show that nucleation of a suitable domain region is favoured around a dislocation, while the actual nucleation takes place under the influence of the suitably applied external dc electric field. There are several equivalent directions possible for the polar axis, and the relieving displacement can decide which one of these is to be preferred for the domain region to be nucleated around the dislocation. The approach is illustrated with reference to the case of $KNbO_{3}$ where the mechanism of domain formation is that of domain wall nucleation. It is shown that the effect of having a suitable domain around the dislocation is to reduce the strain energy of the dislocation. The domain width has been calculated, and it is found to be agreeing with the earlier observed experimental values.

KEYWORDS: dislocation, domain, spontaneous strain, continuum theory

## §1. Introduction

Bradt and Ansell $^{1)}$ observed the nucleation of a domain around a dislocation in $BaTiO_{3}$ under the influence of the externally applied dc electric field. Similar observations have recently been made in $KNbO_{3}$ by Li Qi, et al. $^{2)}$ Though the domain formation as above is generally believed, not much information is available about the mechanism of domain formation or the type of the domain formed. The present paper presents an approach based on the continuum theory of solids, that shows why a domain is nucleated around a dislocation, and what type of domain is preferred.

## §2. Basic Ideas

In a nonferroelectric crystal, the atomic arrangement in the region surrounding the given type of dislocation is always same. So, we can talk of a fixed value of the strain energy of the crystal on account of the dislocation. In ferroelectrics, the actual atomic arrangement in the surrounding region of the dislocation can be different depending upon the direction of polar axis in that region, while the polar axis can assume any one of the several equivalent directions available for it. Hence, we have a different value of strain energy for each possible direction of the polar axis in the domain surrounding the dislocation. It is obvious that the dislocation would prefer a direction for polar axis that would give minimum strain energy to the crystal. If the situation exists such that this energy is not minimum, the dislocation would try to induce the nucleation of a suitable domain around it so that its strain energy is reduced. Indeed, such a process must be associated with an activation energy, and may not occur spontaneously. However, under the influence of the suitably applied external electric dc field, the nucleation can be expected to take place as mentioned earlier. Indeed, in the case of $KNbO_{3}$ , Ingle and Joshi $^{3)}$ showed that the domain formation around the dislocation proceeds through the mechanism of domain wall nucleation, and the activation energy of nucleation can be calculated. They found that this is much higher than the strain energy of the dislocation, showing the absence of the spontaneous occurrence of nucleation of the walls around the dislocation in agreement with what is mentioned above. Such domain formation is shown schematically in Fig. 1, that has been reproduced from our earlier work. $^{3)}$ The figure shows the $KNbO_{3}$ single crystal of the orthorhombic phase with the pseudocubic choice of axes as shown in the figure. The dislocation is contained in the slip plane LMS, which is pseudocubic (101) plane. When the domain walls Y'X'S' and $Y^{\prime \prime} X^{\prime \prime} S^{\prime \prime}$ , are not nucleated, the direction of $P_{s}$ in the region surrounding the dislocation is as shown outside the region sandwiched between Y'X'S' and $Y^{\prime \prime} X^{\prime \prime} S^{\prime \prime}$ . Let this direction be called direction I. If the crystal can now reduce its strain energy with a different direction of polar axis in the surrounding region of the dislocation, nucleation of domain walls Y'X'S' and $Y^{\prime \prime} X^{\prime \prime} S^{\prime \prime}$ is induced under the influence of the electric field E applied in positive Z direction. Now, the region surrounding the dislocation has a new direction of polar axis, called direction II. It is important to note here that a suitable domain formation around the dislocation has taken place

It remains to be shown how the dislocation has lower strain energy when the direction of the polar axis in the surrounding region of the dislocation corresponds to direction II of the polar axis, obtained after domain wall nucleation. This has been done in the following using the concept of relieving displacement, employed in the elastic theory of dislocations. Since this concept is not much in vogue, a brief account of the same as is relevant to the present problem is given next.

## §3. Concept of Relieving Displacement
A good account of the concept of relieving displacement as applied to the elastic theory of dislocations has been given by Friedel.⁴) Figure 2 shows a cylindrical part of the crystal well inside the bulk, and is a part of the rest of the material with continuity. The straight screw dislocation and the Burgers vector lie along the axis of the cylinder. The radius $r_0$ represents the radius of the core of the dislocation, and $r_1$ is the radius of the cylinder considered. It represents the radius of the cylinder in which most of the strain energy of the dislocation is usually contained.

![](./images/812300332422922241_2.jpg)

Fig. 2. A cylindrical part of the undeformed cylinder with cubic symmetry.

Employing the cylindrical coordinates as shown in the Fig. 2, we have the stresses at any general point $N(r, \theta, x_3)$ due to dislocation as
$$
\sigma_{3 \theta}=\sigma_{\theta 3}=\frac{\mu b}{2 \pi r} \tag{1}
$$
and the other components are zero. $\mu$ is the shear modulus, and $b$ the Burgers vector. In the above, cubic symmetry has been assumed.

The stresses $\sigma_{3 \theta}$ exert a torsion on the ends of the cylinder, and the moment of the couple about the axis is
$$
T=\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}} r \sigma_{3 \theta} 2 \pi r \mathrm{~d} r=\frac{1}{2} \mu b\left(r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2}\right). \tag{2}
$$

Now, according to the theory of relieving displacement the stresses contained in the cylinder, excluding, of course, the small region around the dislocation line where atoms are displaced, can be relieved, if we give a small hypothetical displacement, called the relieving displacement, to the atoms in this region. Of course, such a displacement cannot be given in actual practice. The necessary relieving displacement $u'$ at any general point $N(r, \theta, x_3)$ is given by
$$
u_{\theta}^{\prime}=-A r x_{3}, \quad u_{r}^{\prime}=u_{3}^{\prime}=0 \tag{3}
$$
where $A$ is a constant. Physically it means that every point is rotated about the dislocation line, the magnitude of the linear displacement depending upon the radial distance of the point from the dislocation line, and its $X_3$ value. The points further away from the dislocation line suffer greater displacements, and for constant $r$, the displacement increases as we move the $-v_{\mathrm{e}} \mathrm{X}_{3}$ direction. As a result, the cylinder in Fig. 2 is deformed as shown in Fig. 3. We find that the generatrices of a cylinder of revolution about the axis are rotated in the plane normal to their radius with an angle appropriate to the radius. In effect, the cylinder becomes an hyperboloid of revolution.

![](./images/812300332422922241_3.jpg)

Fig. 3. The deformation produced in the cylinder when relieving displacement is incorporated.

The stress field of this displacement is in opposite sense to the stress field of the dislocation, and hence we have the "relieving displacement". The stresses generated by this relieving displacement are given as
$$
\sigma_{3 \theta}^{\prime}=\sigma_{\theta 3}^{\prime}=-\mu A r \tag{4}
$$
and the other components are zero. The similarity of the stress field of the relieving displacement as given by eq. (4) with the stress field of the dislocation as given by eq. (1) with opposite sense can be noted. The value of the constant $A$ in eq. (4) can be calculated from the fact that the torsional couple due these stresses given by eq. (4) compensate $T$, the torsional couple acting on the ends of the cylinder on account of the stresses caused by the screw dislocation acting along the axis of the cylinder, and given by eq. (2). We get
$$
\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{1}} r(-\mu A r) 2 \pi r \mathrm{~d} r=-\frac{1}{2} \mu b\left(r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2}\right)
$$
giving
$$
A=\frac{b}{\pi\left(r_{1}^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\right)}. \tag{5}
$$

## §4. Similarity of Deformation
In a ferroelectric crystal of perovskite type, the domain structure causes the deformation of the crystal as compared to its cubic phase on account of the spontaneous strain. Since different type of domains are possible in the crystal, the deformations produced in the region surrounding the dislocation shall be different in each case.

In the light of the discussion of the previous section, one can see that the dislocation should favour that domain which is associated with a deformation similar to that shown in Fig. 3 in which case the strain energy of the dislocation is reduced. Let this domain be called a 'suitable domain'. If the dislocation finds itself surround- ed by an 'unsuitable domain', it can induce the nuclea- tion of this 'suitable domain' under the influence of the externally applied dc electric field, as shown by Ingle & Joshi. $^{3)}$ Thus it is possible to work out the type of ferroelectric domain nucleated around the dislocation. This is discussed in the following with reference to $KNbO_{3}$ in its orthorhombic phase.

Figure 4 shows a $KNbO_{3}$ single crystal containing a dislocation in the slip plane LMS. The situation is similar to one observed in Fig. 1, but now the surface deforma- tion produced by the domain formation is shown. Under the influence of the externally applied dc field E, domain walls $Y^{\prime} X^{\prime} S^{\prime}$ and $Y^{\prime \prime} X^{\prime \prime} S^{\prime \prime}$ have been nucleated creating a $60^{\circ}$ domain around the dislocation with the directions of the polar axes as shown in the figure. As mentioned earlier, the nucleation takes place as the direction of $P_{s}$ around the dislocation before nucleation, marked I, is not favourable, while the new direction obtained after nucleation, marked II, is favourable. We find that the crystal region around the dislocation has now deforma- tion as seen in Fig. 4. If we compare the top surface BTRF which is a (001) plane, we find that it is perfectly plane in the cubic phase but bent across $X^{\prime} Y^{\prime}$ and $X^{\prime} Y^{\prime}$ in the orthorhombic phase on account of the domain structure. In fact, the Fig. 4 has been drawn making use of our earlier studies of the pseudocubic (001) surface deformations produced by the domain structures. $^{5)}$

If the surface deformation of the pseudocubic (001) plane shown in Fig. 4 is similar to the surface deforma- tion produced by the relieving displacement on the same plane as deduced from Fig. 3, we can conclude that the new domain structure reduces the strain energy of the dislocation. Figure 5 is, therefore, drawn to see how the deformation in the cylinder in the case of the relieving displacement is seen on the (001) plane.

Let us now consider a cylinder with radius $r^{\prime}$. The meaning of this particular radius will become clear later. In Fig. 5(a) both the undeformed and the deformed cylinders are shown for comparison. The dotted line with the hyperbolic shape shows the deformed section of the cylinder. The screw dislocation and the Burgers vector lie along the axis of the cylinder, and along the axis $OX_{3}$ of the rectangular set of axes $OX_{1}, OX_{2} \& OX_{3}$ as shown in the figure. Note that the direction $OX_{3}$ corresponds to the direction $OX_{3}$ in Fig. 2.

This view is then superimposed on the block in such a way that the original $\langle 111 \rangle$ direction as reflected in the view coincides with the original $\langle 001 \rangle$ direction. The orientations of the axes $OX_1$, $OX_2$ & $OX_3$ now obtained on superposition give the required directions of the axes $OX_1'$, $OX_2'$ and $OX_3'$ respectively.

With reference to these new set of axes, the plane BTRF in Fig. 5(a) is (001) plane, and the slip plane LMS, (101). In the undeformed state, the planes $Y'X'S'$ and $Y''X''S''$ are parallel to the slip plane LMS, where the points $X'$ and $X''$ lie on the cylinder. It is apparent that the planes like $Y'X'S'$ serve as domain walls in the ferroelectric case of $KNbO_3$, when pseudocubic symmetry is considered. When the cylinder is deformed on account of the relieving displacement, the point $X'$ on the undeformed cylinder moves to the new positions $X_d'$, and the point $X''$, to the new positions $X_d''$. It is clear that $X'X_d'$ should be parallel to the axis $OX_2$ according to the relieving displacement at $X'$ given by eq. (3). The displacement at $X''$ is opposite to that at $X'$; correspondingly, the original line $X'X''$ assumes a new position $X_d'X_d''$.

We find that there is a slight increase in the length of $X'X''$, which lies in $\langle 100 \rangle$ direction with respect to the axes $OX_1'$, $OX_2'$ and $OX_3'$. The new position $X_d'X_d''$ is also slightly inclined to the undeformed (001) plane with respect to the same set of axes.

Thus, if the relieving displacement is given only to the cylinder, and not to the region outside it, the (001) plane inside the cylinder will find itself shifted to the new position seen in Fig. 5(a) as compared to its undeformed position outside the cylinder. Thus we get sudden inclination of the (001) plane as it meets the points $X_d'$ and $X_d''$. This situation is very much similar to that obtained in $KNbO_3$ when domain structure as shown in Fig. 4 is produced as a result of domain wall nucleation under the influence of the electric field E. For comparison, the walls in Fig. 4 are also shown to be nucleated at $X'$ and $X''$ so that they correspond to the undeformed positions $X'$ and $X''$ in Fig. 5(a). It is seen that the extension in $X'X''$ on account of the deformation by the relieving displacement as seen in Fig. 5(a) is in complete agreement with the requirement for domain formation as shown in Fig. 4. With the monoclinic choice of axes that is highly suitable for pseudocubic geometry, we have that the orthorhombic unit cell has $a_m > c_m$, where $a_m$ is slightly greater than the cube edge, and $c_m$ smaller as shown by Triebwasser.⁶) The pseudomonoclinic unit cell of the orthorhombic phase as derived from the cubic cell is shown in Fig. 6 along with the choice of the axes $a_m$ and $c_m$, and the direction of the polar axis that is contained in the shear plane. The extension in the direction of the polar axis can be clearly noted. Therefore, if $X_d'X_d''$ has to correspond to the direction of $a_m$, as is the case with domain formation in Fig. 4, an extension must occur in it, as is indeed produced by the relieving displacement. Thus in terms of what has been said earlier, the dislocation favours the nucleation of the domain walls as shown in Fig. 4.

Since the extensions required by the relieving displacement and the spontaneous strain are in the same sense, the electrostatic considerations are completely taken care of. Also, the domain walls are nucleated on proper crystallographic planes (Fig. 4), and there is no charge accumulation at the wall. It should be noted that the desirability or otherwise of the domain to be nucleated can be best judged from the similarity of the deformation it produces, and the compatibility of the spontaneous strain with the extensions produced by the relieving displacement rather than from the direction of the relieving displacement that changes its direction continuously as we go round the dislocation. The deformation involving the extension as in $X'X''$ is the net result of the relieving displacements at all the positions taken together. In the present case, the directions of the relieving displacement are in the required sense both at $X'$ and $X''$ as seen in Fig. 5(a) but in general, the deformation of the solid and the extensions involved should serve as a proper guide. Since the relieving displacement is given only to the atoms inside the cylinder, and not to those outside it, sudden deformations are produced at the positions like $X'$ and $X''$ on the cylinder. Such deformations are accomodated in the real crystal structure by twinning with which the domain walls are associated. Thus, it is seen that the concept of relieving displacement provides a simple way of understanding why a dislocation favours the nucleation of a domain around it. The analysis of the above type also shows what type of a domain should be nucleated.

In the above considerations, the portion of the dislocation at M [Fig. 5(a)] is taken to be responsible for inducing the domain wall nucleation at points like $X'$ and $X''$. The walls are of micro dimensions, and as shown by Ingle & Joshi³) should have the length along the edge $Y'X'$ of the order of $0.4\ \mu$ for the applied field $10^4\ \text{Vm}^{-1}$. The length along the edge $X'S'$ is also small, being of the order of the length of the portion of the dislocation line at M. It may be noted that the other portions of the dislocation act similarly. It is shown in the next Section that $r'$ for any portion of the dislocation, like the one at M [Fig. 5(a)], depends only on $r_1$ that is constant here. Hence, the nucleating points corresponding to all the portions of the dislocation line lie on the same cylinder with radius $r'$, and can cause domain wall nucleation. The domain wall nucleation at each point like $X'$ takes place in-

dependent of each other, and does not depend on the magnitude of the 'relieving displacement' that varies as we move along the $X_{3}$ direction. The microwalls nucleated at X' and its neighbouring points in the direc- tion of the $X_{3}$ axis (parallel to the axis of the cylinder) are coplanar, and join with each other end to end producing a continuous macro domain wall. Thus, we get that a pair of macro domain walls, one each on either side of the slip plane, should be nucleated, and the macro do- main walls and the dislocation line should exist in the bulk over the same distance. This situation can be verified experimentally. The experimental observations of Mishra \& Ingle $^{7)}$ in $KNbO_{3}$ showed that the disloca tions are in the form of small elongated loops with screw character predominating. Eshelby $^{8)}$ showed for very small loops that the effect of the loop is equivalent to that of a straight dislocation at the point nearest to the region under consideration, which in the present case is the point on the cylindrical surface. The point pertaining to the loop that lies nearest to this region lies on the axis of the cylinder, and hence the equivalent straight disloca- tion lies along the axis of the cylinder. Now the observed larger loop elongated in the direction of the dislocation, can be considered as made up of small loops piled up one above the other, and hence the effect of the loop is equivalent to that of a straight dislocation of the length equal to the length of the loop in the direction of the dislocation which coincides in this case with the axis of the cylinder. Hence, we expect that the pair of the macro domain walls associated with the dislocation loop exists in the bulk only to the extent the dislocation loop exists. Indeed, it was observed that the pair of domain walls and the dislocation loop begin and terminate simultaneously in the bulk, whatever the length of the loop, which agrees completely with results given by the continuum theory.

§5. Calculation of Domain Width
It is of interest to know at what distance from the slip plane LMS [Fig. 5(a)] the dislocation favours the nuclea- tion of domain walls $Y' X' S'$ and $Y^{\prime \prime} X^{\prime \prime} S^{\prime \prime}$ . This gives the domain width of the domain nucleated.
Since the stress fields of the dislocation and the reliev- ing displacement are in opposite sense, and vary with the distance from the dislocation following different laws, it is reasonable to expect that the domain walls should be nucleated at a distance where neutralization of the stresses due to the dislocation is maximum, i.e. $\sigma_{\theta 3}=\sigma_{\theta 3}'$ . With respect to the cylindrical coordinates with $OX_{3}$ direction along the axis of the cylinder (Fig. 1), we have
$$\frac{\mu b}{2 \pi r^{\prime}}=\frac{\mu b r^{\prime}}{\pi\left(r_{1}^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\right)}\qquad(6)$$
where r' is the appropriate radius of the cylinder for do- main wall nucleation such that points like X' lie on the domain wall to be nucleated [Fig. 5(a)]. Referring to Fig.5(a), it is seen that half the domain width R is related to r' as
$$r^{\prime}=R \cos 45^{\circ} \quad(7)$$
since r' is perpendicular to the axis of the cylinder that lies in <111> direction while $X' X^{\prime \prime}$ is in < 100> direction with respect to the new set of axes $OX_{1}', OX_{2}'$ and $OX_{3}'$ . Substituting this value of r' in eq. (6), and neglecting $r_{0}$ in comparison with $r_{1}$ , we have
$$R=r_{1}. \quad(8)$$

Correspondingly, we get the domain width equal to $2 r_{1}$ .From the theory of the dislocation, it is known that $r_{1}$  depends upon the Burgers vector and the elastic con- stants of the material, and is usually of the order of a few microns, though never precisely defined. Taking $r_{1}=5 \mu$ , we get a value of the domain width $10^{-3} cm$ , agreeing with experiment. $^{7)}$ 
The result $R=r_{1}$ is significant in the sense that newly formed domain region covers and encloses most of the strain energy region of the dislocation. The nucleation is, therefore, quite effective in reducing the strain energy of the dislocation.

§6. General Remarks
If we consider the nucleation of a $90^{\circ}$ domain instead of $60^{\circ}$ domain shown in Fig. 4, we find that the allowed directions of polar axis for this $90^{\circ}$ domain when the direction of the polar axis outside this domain is as shown in Fig. 4, require that $X_{4}' X_{4}^{\prime \prime}$ must undergo contraction rather than extension, since it must correspond to $c_{m}$  axis rather than the $a_{m}$ axis. Obviously the formation of such a domain cannot be favoured, but in fact, has to be resisted. Also, the formation of $180^{\circ}$ domain is not favoured as it involves no changes in lengths as required by the relieving displacements. These considerations con- firm that only a $60^{\circ}$ domain with polar axis as shown in Fig. 4 can be nucleated. It must be remembered, however, that the present method of analysis is ap- plicable to only those ferroelectric materials that have cubic phase as the nonferroelectric phase, since the reliev- ing displacements are considered in a cubic material.
The relieving displacement is given to points within the cylinder, and not outside it. This results in sudden change in atomic arrangement at the cylinder. When the domain walls are nucleated at the points on the cylinder, there is twinning at the wall and the two processes are found to be complimentary [Fig. 5(a)]. Thus, the process of domain formation through domain wall nucleation is seen to be completely compatible with the concept of relieving displacement used in the continuum theory of solids.
The choice of a cylinder for consideration is just for the convenience of the use of the cylindrical coordinates. The relieving displacement can be considered in the region of any geometry surrounding the dislocation as given by eq. (3). Hence the relieving displacement for all the material contained between two nucleated planes around the dislocation (Fig. 4) can be considered whereby the strain energy in that region is reduced. It is known that most of the strain energy is contained in the region with radius $r_{1}$ . So, one need not normally go beyond this distance from the dislocation. The calcula- tion of domain width $2 r_{1}$ merely seems to emphasize this point.

### §7. Conclusion

Thus, the use of the concept of relieving displacement presents a simple way of solving the problem of understanding why a nucleation of a domain is favoured around a dislocation in a ferroelectric crystal. Also, we can work out what type of domain is preferred. The case of $KNbO_3$ is particularly appropriate for the application of the present approach, since the slip plane and the twin plane are crystallographically the same, giving simple geometry. Also, there exist deformations on account of the spontaneous strain, which can be compared. Further, the mechanism of domain wall nucleation greatly facilitates the domain formation around the dislocation in this case. One can calculate the domain width in agreement with experiment, and this value confirms the well known fact of the theory of dislocations that the strain energy of the dislocation is contained in a region of a radius of about a few microns.
