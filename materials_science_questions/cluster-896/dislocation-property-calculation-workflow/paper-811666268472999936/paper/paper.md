# DISLOCATION PILEUPS AND THE HALL-PETCH RELATION¹

Y. T. CHOU

Edgar C. Bain Laboratory for Fundamental Research, United States Steel Corporation
Research Center, Monroeville, Pennsylvania

The strength of a linear dislocation pileup depends on the nature of its barrier. Analysis is given for the case in which the barrier is a second-phase particle and for that in which the barrier is a locked dislocation with a different Burgers vector.

In the first case, where pileups lie in front of hard inclusions, it is shown that the Hall-Petch relation is valid and that the Petch slope increases as the ratio of the inclusion/matrix shear moduli increases. For a screw dislocation pileup, the Petch slope increases by a factor of about 2 for a modulus ratio of 3 and by about a factor of 3 for a ratio of 7. The increase for edge pileups is somewhat higher.

In the second case, where the barrier is a locked dislocation with a different Burgers vector, the Petch slope may be increased (or decreased) by a factor of $\sqrt{m}$, where $m$ is the ratio of the Burgers vector of the locked dislocation resolved in the slip plane to that of the free dislocations in the pileup.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Grain refinement provides a useful method for strengthening of metals (Darken 1961; Grange 1966). Hall (1951) and subsequently Petch (1953) and others have shown experimentally the existence of a simple relation between the yield or flow stress $\sigma$ and the average grain diameter $d$ in the form

$$
(1) \qquad \sigma = \sigma^* + kd^{-\frac{1}{2}},
$$

where $\sigma^*$ is the lattice friction stress and $k$ is known as the Hall-Petch slope. In interpreting eq. (1), Hall and Petch proposed a microscopic model, utilizing the stress concentration produced by a pileup of dislocations at the grain boundary. As the stress concentration reaches a critical value, it will trigger the motion of dislocations in the next grain and yielding takes place. Strikingly enough, eq. (1) can be derived readily from the theoretical results of Eshelby *et al.* (1951) on linear pileups. On the other hand, Li (1963), motivated by the thin-foil observations, formulated a rival model for eq. (1), in which the piled-up groups are replaced by dislocations generated from grain-boundary ledges.

Because of the simplicity of the Hall-Petch relation, an extensive research program has been contributed to this subject in recent years—a great many systems have been tested, wider grain-size ranges covered, and detailed mechanisms concerning temperature, impurities, or plastic strain proposed (see, for example, Jaswon and Richman 1964; Li 1963; Armstrong *et al.* 1962; Cottrell 1963). In his recent experiments on "Ferrovac E" iron and several low-carbon steels, Morrison (1966) has shown that the Hall-Petch yield relation is valid over the full grain size range of 1.6-400 $\mu$. Likewise, the validity of the Hall-Petch relation has been shown for sintered metal carbides (Gurland 1963), laminated steels (Embury and Fisher 1966) and binary eutectic alloys (Shaw 1966).

## 2. LINEAR PILEUPS IN A HOMOGENEOUS MEDIUM
For analyzing linear dislocation arrays, two representative methods are commonly used: an exact method in which the dislocations are treated as discrete entities (Eshelby *et al.* 1951) and an approximate method in which the dislocations are assumed to be smeared out and distributed continuously over the slip plane (Leibfried 1951; Head and Louat 1955). Both methods, however, have certain disadvantages. For instance, no analytical solutions are available from the discrete method for arrays containing positive as well as negative dislocations, such as double-ended pileups (Armstrong *et al.* 1966). In the method of continuously distributed dislocations, it is not possible to determine the stress at the tip of the array.

In all previous investigations on linear arrays, the leading dislocation was assigned the same Burgers vector, $\boldsymbol{b}$, as the free dislocations. Such a circum- stance may not be present if the piled-up group is in front of an immobile grown-in dislocation segment or near a grain boundary containing dislocation ledges (Keh 1961; Li 1963). What would one expect if the Burgers vector of the leading dislocation were different (in both direction and magnitude) from that of the free dislocations in the pileup?

Let us consider a simple case in which $n$ straight dislocations lie parallel to the $z$ axis, having the plane $y=0$ as slip plane and passing through the points $x_j$ on the $x$ axis (Fig. 1). The leading dislocation of Burgers vector $\lambda$ with a component $m\boldsymbol{b}$ in the slip plane, is locked at the origin.* The $n-1$ free dislocations of Burgers vector $\boldsymbol{b}$ are pushed towards the locked dislocation under an applied stress $\sigma$. \sigma=\left(2 A \sigma_{\mathrm{cr}} m\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Replacing $L$ by $d$ and $\sigma-\sigma^{*}$ leads to the Hall-Petch relation with slope
$$
k=\left(2 A \sigma_{\mathrm{cr}} m\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.
$$

For large $n$'s, Chou (1965), using the concept of continuous distribution of dislocations, formulated the problem by a singular integral equation,

$$
(14)\qquad \int_{0}^{L} \frac{f(x) \mathrm{d} x}{x-x_{0}}-K \int_{0}^{L} \frac{f(x) \mathrm{d} x}{x+x_{0}}=\frac{-\sigma}{A}.
$$

The distribution function $f(x)$ for the limit case $K=1$ was found to be

$$
(15)\qquad f(x)=\frac{2 \sigma}{\pi^{2} A} \cosh ^{-1}\left|\frac{L}{x}\right|,
$$

and the number of dislocations

$$n = L\sigma/\pi A. \tag{16}$$

From these relations and the results of Head, Chou then suggested that a linear relation
$$\left(\frac{\sigma}{A}\right) \frac{L}{n} \cong \alpha+\beta K \tag{17}$$
would hold for the range of $0 \leqslant K \leqslant 1$, where for large $n$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are nearly constants $(\alpha=2, \beta=\pi-2)$. The linear approximation of (17) was recently verified by the more general work of Barnett (1966).

Chou (1966c) showed that a Hall-Petch relation may be deduced from eq. (17) for large $n$ and $K<1$. One may assume that yielding takes place when the force exerted along the interface reaches a critical value $F_{\mathrm{cr}}$. By the principle of virtual work,
$$F_{\mathrm{cr}}=n \sigma b=\sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{A K b}{x_{j}+x_{i}} \cdot \tag{18}$$

Combining (17) and (18), and adding the friction stress $\sigma^{*}$, one obtains
$$\sigma=\sigma^{*}+\left(\frac{F_{\mathrm{cr}} A(\alpha+\beta K)}{b}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{-\frac{1}{2}}. \tag{19}$$

### 4. CONCLUDING REMARKS

In response to recent experimental efforts on the grain-size dependence of flow stress, we have set down some of our thinking concerning the subject. As shown in the preceding examples, the Hall-Petch relation is found to be generally valid. In addition to the classical cases of the single- and double-ended pileups considered by Hall, Petch, and others, the relationship is valid in the case where the barrier dislocation has a different Burgers vector, or where the barrier is a second-phase particle. It has been shown that the range of applicability extends from very large to very small grain sizes.

A point of caution, however, is noted. In the derivation of the relation, one has to choose a proper criterion for the yield stress or flow stress. At least three different criteria have been proposed, namely (a) the stress distribution ahead of the pileup (Hall 1951), (b) the critical stress concentration at the tip (Petch 1953), and (c) the critical force on the obstacle (Chou 1966c). Criterion (b) is, in fact, a special case of criterion (c) where the critical force is exerted on a dislocation. One may note that for the classical case (Hall 1951; Petch 1953) criterions (a) and (b) lead to the same Hall-Petch relation.

However, for the cases discussed above, different choices of the criterion may result in different conclusions. If one uses the criterion (a) for the case of a two-phase material, the stress-grain size relation is no longer of the Hall-Petch type (Armstrong and Head 1965; Barnett 1966). Applying criterion (a) to the case where the locked dislocation has a Burgers vector $m\boldsymbol{b}$ instead of $\boldsymbol{b}$, there would be no effect of $m$ on the Hall-Petch slope.* Furthermore, if criterion (b) is chosen in the case of a two-phase material, the stress deviates from the linearity of the Hall-Petch equation in the range of small $n$'s.
