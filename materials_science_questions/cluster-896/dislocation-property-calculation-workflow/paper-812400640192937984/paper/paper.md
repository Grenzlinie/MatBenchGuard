# THE YIELD AND FLOW STRESS OF Cu-1% Cd ALLOY
N. BEHNOOD,† R. M. DOUTHWAITE and J. T. EVANS

Department of Metallurgy and Engineering Materials, University of Newcastle upon Tyne,
Newcastle upon Tyne NE1 7RU, England

(Received 17 December 1979)

Abstract—The tensile properties of a commercial copper-1% cadmium alloy have been investigated at 77, 300 and 420 K. The presence of cadmium produces a sharp yield point similar to that observed in mild steel and in aluminium-magnesium alloys. The yield and flow stresses of the material were found to obey the Hall-Petch relation and it is concluded that cadmium strengthens copper mainly by increasing the grain-size-dependent component of the strength. The alloy exhibits strain ageing. The interaction between a cadmium atom and dislocations in the alloy is considered and it is shown that the atomic size misfit interaction is dominant. It is concluded that cadmium atoms can strongly pin both dissociated edge and screw dislocations in copper. The value of the grain-size-dependent component of the yield strength at low temperatures and its temperature dependence suggest that the critical event at yield is the unpinning of dislocation sources within the grain boundaries. It also appears that this process is still important in the region of homogeneous deformation at higher strains, in contrast to what occurs in copper, where the grain-size-dependent component of the flow stress diminishes markedly with increasing plastic strain. A possible mechanism for the persistence of a strong grain-size effect in the alloy is suggested.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Copper-1% cadmium alloy is widely used as an electrical conductor in circumstances when creep and fatigue resistance are required: the addition of cadmium greatly improves these mechanical properties but has only a small effect on the conductivity. This is an unusual combination of effects and it is thus surprising to find that there is apparently little published work on the mechanical properties of the alloy [1].

Bennett and Evans [2] recently reported that the copper-1% cadmium alloy is relatively resistant to creep and to the effects of cyclic loading. It was suspected that the beneficial effects of this alloy addition might arise from dislocation pinning by cadmium—an idea given support by the large atomic misfit $e_{a}$ for cadmium in copper shown in Table 1 [3].

A number of the properties of dilute copper alloys are demonstrated by Table 1 [3]. For instance, Cu-Sn alloys have a large $e_{a}$-value and one would expect strong dislocation pinning, as is observed [4]. Following the same reasoning, one might also expect strong pinning to occur in Cu-Cd and the other alloys in the second half of the table. On the other hand, the addition of Sn to copper increases the electron/atom ratio quite markedly whereas addition of Cd does not have the same large effect. There are two results of this. The resistivity of Cu-Sn increases more rapidly than that of Cu-Cd with increasing amounts of alloying element [5,6]. Also, the dislocation arrangements on straining are expected to be different in the two alloys: because of the effect of the electron/atom ratio on stacking fault energy, the dislocations in Cu-Sn tend to be arranged in planar groups [7] while the dislocation arrangement in Cu-Cd is expected to be more like the cell-structure developed in pure copper [8].

In alloys in which dislocations are pinned there is almost always a strong grain-size dependence of yield and flow stresses and there may also be a sharp yield point. This is investigated in the present work. The results for the Cu-Cd alloy are compared with those for commercial grade high-conductivity copper, where the yield and flow stresses are separated into grain-size-dependent and grain-size-independent components by means of the Hall-Petch equation. In the discussion the interaction between cadmium atoms and dislocations is evaluated in order to estimate the strength of pinning and the effect of Cd on the friction stress.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

The copper-1% cadmium alloy [analysis (wt.%): Cd, 0.98; Sn, 0.005; Zn, 0.002; balance Cu] was hot-rolled from 25 mm dia round bar to 12.5 mm dia. and then cold drawn to 6 mm dia. Cylindrical tensile specimens (20 mm gauge length, 3.2 mm gauge length dia.) were machined from the cold drawn bar and annealed in argon at temperatures between 400 and $1000^{\circ}C$ followed by furnace cooling to establish the different grain sizes. It was considered important that the material should be fully recrystallised as anomalous results can occur when specimens containing mixtures of more or less recrystallised grains are tested. Careful metallographic examination of electro-polished (60% orthophosphoric acid) sections of each specimen tested showed that recrystallisation was complete even for the finest grain sizes after the specified heat treatment. The average grain size of each specimen was measured by the linear intercept method, counting twin boundaries as grain boundaries [9,10] and traversing at least five hundred boundaries for each measurement. The grain size ranged from 6 to $100\ \mu\text{m}$.

The tensile specimens were electropolished before testing in a screw-driven Instron. An air furnace was used for tests at 420 K, a liquid nitrogen bath for tests at 77 K. In all the tests, the crosshead was driven at a constant speed giving a nominal strain rate of $8\times10^{-4}\text{s}^{-1}$.

Following the conventional interpretation of the Hall-Petch relation [9], the results in Fig. 6 may be taken as implying that the grown-in dislocations in annealed Cu-Cd alloy specimens are indeed pinned by cadmium atoms. Accordingly, some further tests were made in an attempt to observe the strain ageing effects which are usually associated with dislocation pinning. Specimens were strained $5 \%$ at $300 ~K$ and then aged at $150^{\circ} C$ . However, it was found that age ing produced only a slight inflexion in the force- displacement curve on restraining, rather than a return of the sharp yield point as found in mild steel [15]. It was thought that this might be because insufficient cadmium remained in solid solution in the specimens, which had been slowly cooled from the annealing temperature, so in further tests the speci- mens were first quenched from the annealing tem- perature and then strained $5 \%$ and aged as before. The force displacement curve, Fig. 7, for the quenched specimens showed a well defined yield point after strain ageing. Fig. 7 also illustrates another difference between steel and the Cu-Cd alloy: in the latter, the sharp yield point is not eliminated by quenching as it is in steels [15]. It may be that a key factor here is that thermal stresses are much smaller in Cu-Cd because of its good conductivity than they are in steels [16,17]. Further, the yield and flow stresses for the quenched specimens (Fig. 3) are sensibly the same as for the slowly cooled specimens although the Lüders elongation is reduced by quenching (Fig. 2). Finally, the copper-cadmium alloy shows no dynamic strain-ageing effects: neither repeated yielding nor ser- rated force-displacement curves have been observed

in the present work under any of the conditions of testing temperature and specimen heat treatment investigated.

## 4. DISCUSSION
The results show that the mechanical behaviour of Cu- $1\%$ Cd is more like that of mild steel than copper. The common features are the sharp yield point and the pronounced grain-size dependence of the yield and flow stresses. The results also show that the strengthening due to the cadmium originates in the $k$ and $k(\epsilon)$ terms in equations (1) and (2). In view of this, the principal results that require further discussion are:

(i) the sharp yield point and strain ageing, and the interaction between the dislocations and the cadmium atoms responsible for the effect;

(ii) the small effect of Cd on $\sigma_{0}$ and $\sigma_{0}(\epsilon)$ and the absence of a Portevin-le Chantelier effect;

(iii) the strong grain-size dependence of the yield stress and the persistence of the grain-size dependence of the flow stress to large $(\sim 30\%)$ plastic strains.

### 4.1 The strength of Cottrell pinning
Following Nabarro (Ref. [13], page 457), we consider that strong pinning of grown-in dislocations is essential to the development of a sharp yield point even though the process of yielding may not necessarily involve unpinning of the dislocations. As is conventional, the strength of pinning is measured by the maximum binding energy between the solute atom and a dislocation at a separation $\sim \mathbf{b}$, the Burgers vector. For an edge dislocation the long-range size interaction (Friedel [18]) is dominant, but as Fleischer [19] pointed out, a medium-range [18] modulus interaction may also contribute to the pinning of both edge and screw dislocations. More important perhaps, in an f.c.c. alloy unit dislocations may split, the partials being separated by a stacking fault width $w_{s}$, which for a dissociated screw dislocation in copper has a value $w_{s}/b \simeq 5$ [20] but is doubtless larger for grown-in dislocations in the alloy (Ref. [13], page 424). Such values of $w_{s}$ are sufficiently large that it is justifiable to consider the pinning of each partial dislocation rather than that of the pair. Because of the crystallography of the f.c.c. structure, one or both partial dislocations bounding a given stacking fault will then have a first-order misfit interaction with a substitutional solute atom [18]. For example, a unit screw dislocation with Burgers vector $\mathbf{b}$ will form partials having opposite edge components length $\sqrt{3}b/6$. Thus, through the misfit interaction, a cadmium atom may interact with both dissociated edge and dissociated screw dislocations.

The interaction energies are now evaluated. Consider a dislocation with its line lying along the vector $\mathbf{l}$. The edge component of $\mathbf{b}$ is $\mathbf{b}_{e}$ normal to $\mathbf{l}$ and the screw components is $\mathbf{b}_{s}$ parallel to $\mathbf{l}$. Taking the vectors $\mathbf{b}_{e}$ and $\mathbf{l}$ to define an orthogonal coordinate system, a solute atom located at $\mathbf{r},\theta$ interacts with the dislocation with interaction energies $\Delta E_{a}$ due to the misfit and $\Delta E_{m}$ due to the modulus interaction. Expressions for the $\Delta E$'s are given by Eshelby (e.g., Ref. [21]) and by Saxl [22] and in order to evaluate them it is necessary to consider the terms $e_{a} = \text{d}\ln a/\text{d}f$, $e_{G} = \text{d}\ln G/\text{d}f$ and $e_{\varkappa} = \text{d}\ln \varkappa/\text{d}f$ as $f \to 0$ [23], which define the change in the lattice parameter $a$, the shear modulus $G$ and the bulk modulus $\varkappa$ produced by an atomic fraction $f$ of the solute. Collecting the various contributions, we obtain (with Poisson's ratio $v$ and the atomic volume of copper $\sqrt{2}b^{3}/2$):

$$\Delta E_{a}=(\sqrt{2}/2)(1+v)/[\pi(1-v)]Gb^{3}(b_{e}/r)e_{a}\sin\theta\ (3a)$$

for the misfit interaction with an edge component $\mathbf{b}_{e}$; $\Delta E_{a}=0$ for the screw component;

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta E_{G}&=\sqrt{2}/(16\pi^{2})Gb^{3}(b_{s}/r)^{2}e_{G}, \\
\Delta E_{\varkappa}&=0
\end{align}
\tag{3b}
$$

for the modulus interaction with the screw component $\mathbf{b}_{s}$;

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta E_{G}&=\sqrt{2}/(16\pi^{2})[1/(1-v)]^{2}Gb^{3}(b_{e}/r)^{2} \\
&\quad\times e_{G}\{1-(2/3)\sin^{2}\theta(1+2v-2v^{2})\}, \\
\Delta E_{\varkappa}&=\sqrt{2}/(16\pi^{2})[(1-2v)/(1-v)]^{2} \\
&\quad\times \varkappa b^{3}(b_{e}/r)^{2}e_{\varkappa}\sin^{2}\theta
\end{align}
\tag{3c}
$$

for the modulus interaction with the edge component.

The total modulus interaction with either component of $\mathbf{b}$ is therefore
$$\Delta E_{m}=\Delta E_{G}+\Delta E_{\varkappa}$$

For Cu-Cd the data of Owen and Pickup [24] give $e_{a}=0.23$ and in the absence of experimental measurements of $e_{G}$ and $e_{\varkappa}$ we estimate $e_{G}=-0.83$ and $e_{\varkappa}/e_{G}=1.59$ from the elastic constants of Cu and Cd [25] using the expressions for an elastic sphere [21]. For the partial dislocation with edge component $\sqrt{3}b/6$ and screw component $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{b}$, the minimum values of the interaction energies with a cadmium atom at $r=b$ are $\Delta E_{a}\simeq -0.0305Gb^{3}$ and $\Delta E_{m}\simeq -0.0033Gb^{3}$. It is apparent that $\Delta E_{m}$ is negligible compared with $\Delta E_{a}$ and it follows that the corresponding maximum force between a dislocation and a solute atom due to $\Delta E_{m}$ is also negligible, as first pointed out by Saxl [22] and recently confirmed by Kuo and Arsenault [26]. Further, we note that this maximum force occurs at a different $(r,\theta)$ from that due to $\Delta E_{a}$ so the two possible contributions to the pinning are not simply additive as implicit in Fleischer's treatment [27]. Hence, $\Delta E_{m}$ is ignored in the rest of this discussion.

As always when expressions such as (3a) have to be evaluated there is some ambiguity about the appropriate values of $G$ to use [28] and a number of different values have been used in the past, ranging from $30.8\ \text{GN}/\text{m}^{2}$ [29] through $40.8\ \text{GN}/\text{m}^{2}$ [30] to the polycrystal value $48.3\ \text{GN}/\text{m}^{2}$. For the edge component of a dissociated screw dislocation, the appro-

priate energy factor $K$[31] incorporates $G/(1-v)$ and is equivalent to a value of $G=48.2\ \text{GN/m}^2$ in equations (3). Thus although Cu is moderately aniso- tropic we shall not be far wrong in using the poly- crystalline average; with this, $Gb^3\simeq0.8\ aJ\ (\sim5\ \text{eV})$ so that the maximum binding energy of Cd atoms with a pair of partial dislocations is about half that of a nitrogen atom with a screw dislocation in $\alpha$-iron [28]. Thus, at absolute zero there should be strong Cottrell pinning of the dissociated screw dislocations in the alloy.

### 4.2 The effect of Cd on the friction stress
At first sight, the effect of Cd on the friction stress term $\sigma_0$ in equation (1) seems to be rather small, particularly in comparison with published data for polycrystalline Cu-Sn alloys [4,7,32]. For the latter $e_a=0.265$ compared to 0.23 for Cd in Cu, and at $80\ \text{K}\ \sigma_0$ is $220\ \text{MN/m}^2$ for the 3.2 at.% Sn alloy, whereas the present measurements on 0.5 at.% Cd in Cu give $25\ \text{MN/m}^2$ at 77 K. At room temperature $\sigma_0$ is $\sim100\ \text{MN/m}^2$ for the same Sn alloy but only $5\ \text{MN/m}^2$ for the present material.

Before attempting to quantify the expected effect, we first note that according to Owen and Pickup [24] the equilibrium solid solubility of Cd in Cu at $300^\circ\text{C}$ is 0.3 at.% and we assume that the excess Cd content of the present alloy (probably as $\text{Cu}_2\text{Cd}$ at the grain boundaries) does not contribute to the hardening.

Nabarro *et al.* [33] have shown that at moderate temperatures (which they define) the friction shear stress is
$$
\tau_0=2\sqrt{2F_{\text{max}}wd_sf/b^4}, \tag{4}
$$
where the atomic volume of Cu is as before, $F_{\text{max}}=0.0305(9/4)Gb_e^2/h^{-2}$ is the maximum force between the solute atom and the partial dislocation, $h=3d_s/2=\sqrt{6}b/2$ is the perpendicular separation of the nearest solute atom site from the slip plane of the dislocation, $d_s$ is the interplanar spacing of the slip planes and $w=\sqrt{3}h/3$ is the so-called range of the force. Note that the factor 2 in equation 4 arises because each partial dissipates an energy $F_{\text{max}}w$ as it moves past each solute atom. With $f=3\times10^{-3}$, $\tau_0=5.4\ \text{MN/}\text{m}^2$ for the dissociated edge dislocation $(b_e=\frac{1}{2}b)$ and $3.1\ \text{MN/m}^2$ for the dissociated screw dislocation $(b_e=\sqrt{3}b/6)$. A similar calculation for the 3.2 at.% Sn alloy gives $38.7<\tau_0<67\ \text{MN/m}^2$.

The corresponding tensile stress $\sigma_0$ in the polycrys- talline aggregate is $\bar{m}\tau_0$ with $2.2<\bar{m}<3.1$. Thus the observed room temperature $\sigma_0$ values for both the Cu-Cd and the Cu-Sn alloys are within a factor two of the calculated values.

According to Labusch [34], at low temperatures the mode of dislocation interaction with solute atoms is determined by the value of $\beta=\sqrt{2F_{\text{max}}/(8T_Dw^2fd_s)}$ for any given combination of concentration and inter- action force. In the expression for $\beta$, $T_D$ is the line tension of the dislocation which may be estimated from the readily-calculated line energy for a dis- sociated unit screw dislocation [35] or taken from the computed value for Cu given by Barnett *et al.*[36]. Then $T_D\simeq Gb^2/4$ compared with the constant line tension approximation $D'_D=\frac{1}{2}Gb^2$ and $\beta=7$ for the present alloy. The line tension of an edge dislocation in Cu [36] is about a fifth of $T_D$ and $F_{\text{max}}$ is a factor $\sqrt{3}$ greater so $\beta$ is again significantly larger than unity. Hence for dissociated screws and edges, the Fridel limit applies [37] and
$$
\tau_0=0.0305(9/4)Gb^2b_e/(h^2l_e), \tag{5}
$$
with the solute atom spacing along the dislocation line taken to be $l_e=[2T_D\bar{l}^2/(\tau_0b)]^{1/3}$, where $\bar{l}$ is the planar spacing. Thus for a dissociated screw with $f=3\times10^{-3}$, $\tau_0=9.5\ \text{MN/m}^2$. With $\sigma_0=\bar{m}\tau_0$ as before, this is in rather good agreement with the 77 K result of the present work. Note that in this calcula- tion it is not appropriate to include the factor 2 as was done in equation (4). The $\tau_0$ value for a dis- sociated edge dislocation will be about three times greater than the calculated $\tau_0$ for the screw disloca- tion because both $b_e$ and $T_D$ are different.

Finally, it is worth remarking on the difference in solid solution hardening in f.c.c. alloys and in b.c.c. alloys. In particular, the measured value of $(1/G)(\text{d}\sigma_0/\text{d}f)$ due to nitrogen or carbon in $\alpha$-iron [38] is $\sim17$ times greater than that due to cadmium in copper yet the interaction energies in the two alloys differ by only a factor of about two. This discrepancy probably arises because of the different modes of dis- location mobility in the two crystal structures. In Suzuki's [39] model for instance, solid solution hard- ening in the b.c.c. metals is due to the effect that impurity atoms have on screw dislocation segments as they overcome the intrinsic lattice resistance to their motion (the Peierls-Nabarro barrier). Thus there is likely to be an interaction between the Peierls- Nabarro force and impurity hardening in b.c.c. metals which does not arise in f.c.c. alloys.

### 4.3 Strain ageing and the Portevin-le Chatelier effect
Polakowski [4] showed that the sharp yield point returned after ageing (for $1\frac{1}{2}\text{h}$ at $250^\circ\text{C}$) Cu-3.2 at.% Sn alloy specimens both as quenched and as annealed which had been strained about $6\%$. From the results of Russell and Vela [40], the maximum increase in flow stress (amounting to $11\ \text{MN/m}^2$) was observed after water quenching a medium grain size material containing 2.2 at.% Sn and after ageing under load at $100^\circ\text{C}$. For the present alloy, aged under zero load, the maximum increase (using the same convention as Russell and Vela [40]) was $5-6\ \text{MN/m}^2$ after $1\frac{1}{2}\text{h}$ at $150^\circ\text{C}$ following $4\frac{1}{2}\%$ strain. Part of the difference can no doubt be ascribed to the effect of ageing under finite and zero load respectively [41] and part to the difference in concentration of the two alloys. But the diffusion rates for Cd and Sn in Cu are so similar [42,43] that it is unlikely that the dynamics of the process play a significant part.

The only serious conflict between the results in the present study of Cu-Cd and published work on Cu-Sn is the absence of the Portevin-le Chatelier effect in the present materials. Neither repeated yield- ing nor serrations [44] have been observed in the present work. Both phenomena have been observed in quenched and in annealed Cu-Sn alloys having com- positions in the range 0.8-3.2 at.% Sn [44], at strain rates and testing temperatures comparable with those used here.

Clearly, Cu-Sn and Cu-Cd alloys ought to be com- parable, and on the basis of Cottrell's model [45] and using the semi-empirical relationships established by Russell [46] for dilute Cu-Sn alloys, one would expect dynamic strain ageing effects at 420 K at the strain rates used in the present work. However, look- ing further into the matter, because screw dislocation segments have to be split in order to give a strong interaction with impurity atoms, dynamic ageing should be favoured when the stacking fault width, $w_{s}$, is larger than the capture radius [47,48] for the pin- ning atom around the dislocation line. In the present alloy $w_{s}$ is likely to be comparatively small so that the capture radius for a given partial dislocation is larger than $w_{s}$. Thus, within the capture radius there is some cancellation of the stress field due to the opposite edge components of the partial dislocations. Internal stress-induced drift of the cadmium atoms then occurs within a semi-cylindrical volume with its axis along the partial dislocation line rather than within a cylin- drical volume as would occur in the more concen- trated Cu-Sn alloys. In the present alloy in fact there is less than one cadmium atom within the semi- cylindrical volume per atomic length of dislocation line, hence dynamic ageing is unlikely. No such stress- field cancellation occurs for the edge components of a dissociated unit edge dislocation of course, but it is possible that dynamic ageing does not occur in this present dilute alloy because the dissociated screw dis- locations are not effectively pinned.

Very recently, Mulford and Kocks [49] have drawn attention to the possible role in dynamic ageing of pipe diffusion along dislocations. In the model they propose, which is not at present quantitative, the solute atoms responsible for pinning diffuse from the forest dislocations, particularly at their points of intersections with the temporarily-held-up mobile dis- locations. The stacking fault may have an influence in this model too, as a reservoir of solute atoms: the larger $w_{s}$, the greater the supply of solute atoms avail- able for pinning.

### 4.4 The grain-size dependence of the yield strength

The above discussion (Section 4.1) indicates clearly that grown-in dislocations in the annealed Cu-Cd alloy should be strongly pinned and thus readily accounts for the observed sharp yield point: disloca- tions are held immobile by the pinning at applied stresses under which the velocity of a free dislocation would be large. The second notable feature of the results is the marked grain-size dependence of the yield and flow stresses, again similar to that observed in mild steel and in Al-Mg alloys but contrasting with copper and many other f.c.c. alloys. As the yield strength is perhaps easier to understand than the flow stress, since the latter involves strain hardening, the two regions of the stress strain curve are discussed separately in the following.

The original model of yielding in polycrystals pro- posed by Hall [50] and Petch [51] is still widely accepted despite intensive critical examination by later workers. In the model, the critical process for yield in materials having strongly pinned grown-in dislocations is the unpinning [52] or generation [53] of sufficient dislocation sources to produce macro- scopic deformation. Yielding then propagates from the few slip bands formed in isolated regions at the upper yield stress. The critical stress required for gen- eration or unpinning, $\tau_{c}$, is much larger than the applied stress and so is most likely to be achieved by stress concentration at or near the tip of existing slip bands in the isolated yielded grains. The length of the slip bands is taken to be equal to the grain size $d$ and so the latter controls the stress concentration factor: the grain-size dependence of the yield strength, equa- tion (1) follows.

If the critical process occurs at a distance $r$ ahead [54] of the tip of the slip band in the isolated yielded grain, then $k$ in equation 1 is given by

$$
k=\bar{m} \bar{m}^{\prime} \tau_{c} r^{\frac{1}{2}} \tag{6}
$$

where $\bar{m}$ and $\bar{m}^{\prime}$ are average orientation factors for the polycrystalline aggregate ($\bar{m}$ relates the applied tensile stress, $\sigma$ of equation (1), to the shear stress on the slip band in the yielded grain and $\bar{m}^{\prime} \simeq \frac{1}{2} \bar{m}$ relates the maximum concentrated shear stress ahead of the band to that on the slip plane in the unyielded grain).

Alternatively, if the critical process in the model is the production of a free dislocation from the grain boundary [55] at the tip [54] of the slip band in the yielded grain, $k$ is given by

$$
k=\bar{m}\left\{\bar{m}^{\prime} G \tau_{c} b /[\pi(1-v)]\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}. \tag{7}
$$

Assuming that the dissociated screw dislocation is pinned by cadmium atoms spaced $\lambda$ apart along the partial dislocation lines at a perpendicular distance $h$ from the slip plane, the maximum shear stress in the direction of $\mathbf{b}$ that is required to unpin the dislocation is

$$
\tau_{c}=0.0305(9 / 2) G b^{2} b_{e} /\left(h^{2} \lambda\right)
$$

with $\lambda=b$ and $h=3 d_{s} / 2$ as before, $\tau_{c} \simeq 0.03 G$ com pared with 0.066G for N-pinning of dislocations in $\alpha$-iron [28]. On the basis of this estimate and equa- tion 7, if unpinning is the critical process in these alloys, $k$ at low temperatures for Cu-Cd should be about a third the value for mild steel $(1.00 \mathrm{MN} / \mathrm{m}^{3 / 2})$. In fact, $k$ at $77 \mathrm{~K}$ for $\mathrm{Cu}-\mathrm{Cd}$ is $0.33 \mathrm{MN} / \mathrm{m}^{3 / 2}$. Such agreement between calculation and measurement

should not necessarily be taken as proof that unpin- ning is the critical process however, as both equations 6 and 7 and all the various mechanisms of yield are capable of giving values of $k$ that are in reasonably good agreement with observation [54]. The major dif- ference between the various possible mechanisms of yielding is to be found in the way in which $\tau_{c}$ is influenced by temperature: the creation of a disloca- tion loop should be insensitive to thermal activation while unpinning of a pinned dislocation should be strongly temperature dependent [55]. The present results in fact lie between these two extremes: $k$ in creases by about a third when the test temperature is reduced from 300 to $77 ~K$ and this is considerably less than could be expected from unpinning but signifi- cantly more than would be given by the temperature variation of the shear modulus. The temperature dependence of $k$ for $Cu-Cd$ is not too different from that of $k$ in mild steel [55] and $\alpha$ -brass [56] and it may be that the critical process is in fact the gener- ation of dislocations from grain boundaries which are stabilized by impurity atoms [57]. The details of this process are not known but a calculation by Suzuki and Nakanishi [58] indicates that the temperature dependence of $k$ for grain boundary dislocation gener ation may be between the extremes described above. For $Cu-1 \% Cd, k / G$ is between 0.14 and $0.22 ~m^{\frac{1}{2}}$ in the temperature range used and this can be comparedwith values of 0.28 for mild steel and 0.31 for $Al-3 \%$  Mg at room temperature [9]. In addition, the effect of the cadmium on the grain-size dependence of the yield strength seems to be carried over to the mechan- ical behaviour at higher strains: $k(\epsilon)$ for $Cu-Cd$ is also temperature dependent although the smaller values of $k(\epsilon)$ for $Cu$ are not. It is significant that a similar effect can be observed in mild steel in which decarburiza- tion eliminates the sharp yield point and also reduces k(e)[15]. The grain-size dependence of the flow stress in the homogeneous deformation region is now discussed.

### 4.5 The grain-size dependence of the flow stress
Interpretations of the Hall-Petch relation are more controversial for homogeneous deformation than for the lower yield stress. Armstrong et al. [9] considered the process underlying equation (2) was an extension of the events occurring at the lower yield stress: the flow stress was considered still to be controlled by the unpinning or generation of dislocation sources at the tip of a blocked slip band but $\sigma_{0}(\epsilon)$ was thought to reflect the extent of intragranular strain hardening. This interpretation gives a generally satisfactory de- scription of most experimental observations including the present results. However, valid objections can be raised against it. These stem from the fact that work hardening must be taken into account at higher strains and the simple picture of the critical process in discontinuous yielding becomes more complicated when applied to homogeneous deformation. For instance, during discontinuous yielding it is clear that there is a shortage of active dislocation sources lead- ing to a temporary instability in the stress-strain behaviour, but it is not self-evident that a similar pro- cess will be critical during homogeneous plastic flow. If the formation of a new slip band from a source becomes more difficult than the activation or gener- ation of the source, then it is the work-hardened structure that controls the flow stress and the simple interpretation of equation (2) breaks down. With plentiful sources, the flow stress may be determinedby strain hardening alone, leading to the idea that $k(\epsilon)$  may reflect a differential rate of strain hardening in different grain sizes. Meakin and Petch [56] devel- oped this idea for the plastic flow of $\alpha$ -brass, although they regarded the flow stress as still being controlled by source generation. They argued that if each slip band crosses a grain in order to generate new sources in a neighbouring grain the effective mean free path of dislocations is proportional to $d$ , so that a component of the flow stress of the form
$$\sigma^{\prime}=2 \alpha \bar{m}^{3 / 2} G b^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}} d^{-\frac{1}{2}}\qquad(8)$$
(with $\alpha \sim 0.2$ ) develops with increasing strain $\epsilon$ . This component increases the frictional resistance acting against the formation of new slip bands. Similar ex- pressions were derived by $Li$ [59] and Ashby [60] al though these authors considered that the flow stress could be entirely controlled by work hardening. We distinguish here between Li's ledge model of the lower yield point--involving dislocation generation from grain boundary ledges--and his equations describing homogeneous deformation. In Li's model the yield stress is given in terms of dislocation density and whilst no strain term enters into Li's equation (7), it has never been explained how the necessary disloca- tion density could be produced without giving a finite amount of plastic deformation. If a strain term is included in the ledge model then it becomes essen- tially the same as the work hardening model. Ashby [60] developed his model from a consideration of geometrically necessary dislocations. This gives a density
$$\rho_{G}=\bar{m} \epsilon /(4 d b), \quad(9)$$
 which together with a statistically stored dislocation density, $\rho_{s}$ , arising from random interactions between dislocations gives a flow stress
$$\tau=\alpha G b\left(\rho_{s}+\rho_{G}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \text {. }$$

Since $\rho_{s}$ is independent of $d$ , on this model the Hall Petch relation is obtained only as an approximation at low strains when equations 9 and 10 give a result similar to equation (8). It should be remarked that the component of dislocation density $\rho_{G}$ is strictly necess ary only if all the slip bands extend across the grain diameter. It may be argued that the other geometrical constraints in the deformation of polycrystals as dis- cussed by Ashby could be accounted for by the Taylor factor [9] which involves no grain-size depen- dence.

The essential results of all the work hardening models is that $k(\epsilon)$ should increase with strain, at least during the beginning of plastic deformation. This is not observed in Cu-Cd, although an increase in $k(\epsilon)$ is observed in Cu up to $2\%$ strain where the early stages of deformation are not obscured by discontinuous yielding. It is just possible that the flow stress of Cu at $2\%$ strain could be accounted for by work hardening alone. If equation 8 is taken as an upper limit, then $k(0.02)$ is predicted to be $0.22\ \text{MN/m}^{3/2}$—a larger value than is observed. It is not surprising that this extreme view is inconsistent with other observations: for example, in $\alpha$-brass where $k(\epsilon)$ increases with strain, the observed increment in $k(\epsilon)$ due to work hardening at $2\%$ strain [56] is only about $0.07\ \text{MN/m}^{3/2}$. Furthermore, it is difficult to understand how the presence of Cd could affect work hardening in the way shown in Fig. 6, especially since the grain-size-independent component of the flow stress seems to be insensitive to Cd (Fig. 5).

An important feature of the experimental results is the way in which $k(\epsilon)$ for Cu-Cd is maintained at a relatively high value. This is difficult to understand on the work hardening theory. But if the activation or creation of grain boundary sources is the controlling factor an equal difficulty arises because then $k(\epsilon)$ should still increase with strain as described by Meakin and Petch [56], equation (8). A constant or slowly decreasing $k(\epsilon)$ could be obtained on the Meakin-Petch model if the source generation component of $k(\epsilon)$ decreased coincidentally with an increase in the work hardening component. An explanation of this sort was put forward by Meakin and Petch for some of their results for $\alpha$-brass. However, the mode of slip in $\alpha$-brass is quite different from that expected in Cu-Cd which is probably more like that in copper. No detailed model of the grain-size dependence of the flow stress can be given at the present time but the following tentative explanation is put forward, taking account of recent studies of the development of slip lines in copper single crystals.

A detailed study of the slip traces in Cu single crystals deformed in *multiple* slip was made by Ambrosi and Schwinck [61]. They found that most of the deformation $(\sim 95\%)$ could be accounted for by fine slip on short slip lines. The average length of these slip lines decreased during deformation from $\sim 20\mu\text{m}$ at small strains to a limiting value of $\sim 2\ \mu\text{m}$ at $\sim 30\%$ strain. Thus, after moderate strains most of the slip lines in Cu single crystals were smaller than the grain diameters of the specimens tested in the present work. Longer, more prominent slip lines (glide bundles) were observed in the single crystal studies but it was concluded [61] that these accounted for only about $5\%$ of any strain increment. The prominent slip lines decreased in length from about $\sim 250\ \mu\text{m}$ at small strains to about $\sim 8\ \mu\text{m}$ at $30\%$ strain. Thus, over a fairly large range of strain, the prominent slip lines in the Cu single crystals are larger than the grain diameters used in the present work. Ambrosi and Schwinck thought that the prominent slip lines they observed formed as a result of slight misorientations in their specimens or because of bending during the tensile testing. Such long slip bands are therefore likely to form in individual grains in a polycrystalline aggregate. The present authors observed long slip bands extending across the grains in coarse grained Cu-Cd specimens; further, the visible slip bands produced by a $2\%$ strain increment were more prominent after $20\%$ strain than they were after small strains; thus it seems that some of the conclusions of Ambrosi and Schwinck can be applied to the deformation of polycrystals, although a similar quantitative study of slip in polycrystals would be extremely difficult.

In the light of these observations, it is suggested that the major part of the intragranular deformation occurs by slip on a fine scale as in the single crystals but a significant small fraction of slip occurs by slip in long glide bundles that traverse the grains in which they occur. It is further suggested that the grain-size dependence of the flow stress in Cu-$1\%$ Cd is controlled by the long slip bands and by the activation of grain boundary sources. It is not known for certain why $k(\epsilon)$ is smaller than $k$ although it should be noted that $k(\epsilon)$ could be affected not only by the strength of pinning [$\tau_c$ in equations (6) and (7)] but also by the stress intensity factor produced by the slip bands [62]. Perhaps $k(\epsilon)$ decreases with increasing strain because the stress intensity factor of the controlling slip lines increases with strain. Such an increase in stress intensity is possible for slip bands composed of edge dislocations [62]. Of course, slip cannot be propagated directly across a grain boundary because neighbouring grains do not in general have common slip traces at their boundaries, but, as Frank [53] has pointed out, this restriction means that slip can then be stimulated on a large number of paralleled planes by a blocked slip band in a neighbouring grain. This might be important in producing the fine-scale slip responsible for most of the deformation in a given strain increment. Long slip bands extending across the grain have also been observed by the authors in coarse-grain Cu polycrystals but presumably, for Cu, activation of grain boundary sources is easier than intragranular slip. The rapid decrease in $k(\epsilon)$ with strain in Cu then arises because slip is increasingly controlled by the intragranular cell structure which develops as the strain increases.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

1. Annealed copper-$1\%$ cadmium alloy specimens show a sharp yield point and discontinuous yielding over a wide range of temperature. Well-developed strain ageing was also observed in quenched specimens.

2. The yield and flow stress of the alloy obeys the Hall-Petch relation.

3. The grain-size-independent component of the flow stress is rather insensitive to the presence of Cd and the strengthening effect of cadmium arises mainly through the grain-size-dependent component.

4. The experimental results were shown to be quan- titatively consistent with established theories of dislo- cation pinning and solid solution hardening.

5. A qualitative explanation of the grain-size depen- dence of the flow stress has been given, which draws on the results of a recent study [61] of the slip lines formed in copper single crystals. Our explanation tends to follow the original model of Armstrong *et al.* [9] more closely than some later theories of grain- size dependence.
