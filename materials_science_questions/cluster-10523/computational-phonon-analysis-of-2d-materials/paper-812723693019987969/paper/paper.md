## ABSTRACT
The studies of the dimensional effect on the charge density wave (CDW) transition have attracted a lot of attention since the rise of 2D materials. In this paper, we synthesize high-quality single-crystal GdTe₃, a member of the layered rare-earth metal tritelluride family with CDW transitions, and systematically study the temperature-dependent Raman spectra of bulk and few-layer GdTe₃. Combining with first-principle calculations, the CDW and phonon Raman peaks are distinguished and characterized. We demonstrate that the CDW order can be enhanced in few-layer GdTe₃, and the CDW transition temperature increases from 377 K to 431 K as the thickness reduces from the bulk to 10 nm. We speculate that this enhancement of the CDW order in the GdTe₃ thin layer is likely due to the chemical pressure release. Our studies demonstrate that the dimensionality provides a valuable tuning parameter for manipulating the CDW properties of GdTe₃.

A charge density wave (CDW) is a periodic modulation of the electronic charge density,¹ which can occur in many quasi-low-dimensional materials.² CDW materials have potential applications in oscillators³,⁴ and memory storage.⁵ The dimensionality manipulation of the CDW phase transition has been enabled by the mechanical exfoliation method⁶ and investigated in many transition metal dichalcogenides recently.⁷⁻¹³ The first experimental study of thickness dependence of the CDW transition temperature ($T_{CDW}$) was performed on TiSe₂.¹² As the thickness decreases, $T_{CDW}$ increases. A similar behavior has been observed in NbSe₂⁷ thin layers. It was explained that the enhancement of the CDW order is due to the increasing electron-phonon coupling interaction. The thickness dependence of $T_{CDW}$ does not follow the same trend in different CDW materials. For instance, a reduction of $T_{CDW}$ was observed in thinner TaS₂ films.¹³ The thickness dependence of $T_{CDW}$ is even nonmonotonic in VSe₂,¹⁰,¹¹,¹⁴ which was attributed to the change of the fermi surface topology. These studies indicate that the thickness dependence of $T_{CDW}$ is material dependent. The mechanism behind the change of $T_{CDW}$ in systems with a reduced dimensionality is still not very clear and needs to be further studied.

The family of another quasi-two-dimensional CDW compound, rare-earth tritellurides RTe₃ (R:Y, La, Ce, Nd, Sm, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, and Tm), has attracted widespread attention in the past decade.¹⁵⁻¹⁷ The CDW gap and transition temperature can be tuned in a wide range by changing the so-called chemical pressure from LaTe₃ to TmTe₃.¹⁷ The compounds with light R atoms (Y, La, Ce, Nd, Sm, and Gd) possess one CDW order with a $T_{CDW}$ higher than 300 K, whereas those with heavy R atoms (Tb, Ho, Dy, Er, and Tm) undergo a second CDW transition at lower temperatures. Recent evidence from optical spectroscopy showed another CDW transition for the whole RTe₃ series.¹⁸ Some of the compounds have a long range magnetic order, coexisting with the CDW.¹⁹ CDW was found to compete with superconductivity,²⁰,²¹ and superconductivity can be induced in these materials by suppressing the CDW order under a high pressure.²²,²³ The interplay between antiferromagnetism, superconductivity, and CDW orders makes RTe₃ a series of materials of fundamental importance. Therefore, the dimensionality control of CDW properties in RTe₃ is particularly interesting.

In this work, we synthesize high-quality single-crystal GdTe₃, a member of the RTe₃ series, and fabricate few-layer GdTe₃ using the

mechanical exfoliation method. We study the temperature-dependent Raman spectroscopy of bulk and few-layer GdTe₃ systematically. The Raman peaks are characterized by combining with the first-principles calculations. $T_{\text{CDW}}$ increases prominently in few-layer GdTe₃ structures, suggesting a significant enhancement of the CDW order in the low dimensional RTe₃ series. We argue that the dimensional effect is equivalent to the chemical pressure release.

Single crystals of GdTe₃ were grown by using the flux method. The preparation, characterization, and Raman measurement methods for GdTe₃ are described in the supplementary material.

As shown in Fig. 1(d), the undistorted orthorhombic crystal structure (space group Cmcm) of GdTe₃ is composed of nearly planar Te layers that are separated by GdTe slabs and stacked along the b axis. The first-principles calculations were performed for this undistorted structure using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP)²⁴ based on the density functional theory with the Perdew-Burke- Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional²⁵ and projector-augmented- wave (PAW) type pseudopotentials.²⁶ The experimental lattice constants of $\text{a}=4.32\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, $\text{b}=25.57\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, and $\text{c}=4.33\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ were employed in the calculation [Fig. 1(d)].¹⁷ The atomic positions were relaxed in the primitive cell with $12\times12\times36$ k sampling in the Brillouin zone (BZ). The harmonic interatomic force constants (IFCs) were further obtained in the primitive cell with a real-space finite difference approach, and were then used to calculate the phonon frequencies at the $\Gamma$-point in the BZ.

Figure 1(e) shows the selected area diffraction (SAD) pattern of GdTe₃ taken at 183 K. The intense peaks correspond to reciprocal lattice points, and the patterns are consistent with the Cmcm space group of RTe₃ as previously assigned.²⁷ The pairs of satellite reflections (marked by arrows) correspond to the CDW superlattice peaks,²⁷ which are visible below $T_{\text{CDW}}$ (377 K for the bulk crystal¹⁷). The intensity of the superlattice signal increases with decreasing temperature. From the position of these peaks, we can obtain $\mathbf{q}=0.293c^{*}\approx2/7c^{*}$, consistent with the X-ray diffraction experiment.²⁸ Figure 1(f) shows the scanning tunneling microscope (STM) topographic image of the exposed Te surface of the cleaved GdTe₃ crystal.²⁹ The Te surface possesses a square lattice superimposed a series of parallel stripes, which also indicates the existence of a CDW state. Both SAD and STM show that the CDW occurs in the c direction, which is due to the "stripe" nature of GdTe₃.²³,³⁰

GdTe₃ thin layers can be obtained by mechanical exfoliation. Figure 1(a) shows an optical image of exfoliated GdTe₃ with different thicknesses, which was characterized by optical contrast when transferred onto a silicon substrate.³¹ Figure 1(b) shows the atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of the selected square area in (a). The flat surface that we observed is the ac plane of GdTe₃. The height of the thinnest part is about 10.2 nm, as shown in Fig. 1(c). These results demonstrate that GdTe₃ thin layers can be obtained by the mechanical exfoliation method. Since the thickness of two AB stacking layers is the lattice constant of the b axis, i.e., $25.57\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, the thinnest sample that we obtained consists of about 8 layers.

Figure 2 shows the Raman spectra of a bulk sample measured with XX and XY configurations at 30 K. The most intense peak for the XX configuration appears at $71.5\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, which can be attributed to the collective amplitude mode of the CDW state, since it has a strong temperature dependence [see Fig. 3(a) and the discussion below] and its position is close to the CDW amplitude mode of other RTe₃ materials.³²,³³ The CDW peak intensity ratio of XX to XY polarization is 8.7, and the polarization dependence of the CDW mode intensity is similar to the CDW peak of ErTe₃ at $71\ \text{cm}^{-1}$.³³

According to the orthorhombic symmetry, $4\text{A}_{g}+4\text{B}_{1g}+4\text{B}_{3g}$ modes are Raman active. However, as explained in Ref. 34, the $\text{B}_{1g}$ modes can also be observed due to the layer symmetry. The temperature dependent Raman spectra of bulk $\mathrm{GdTe}_{3}$ are presented in Fig. 3(a). At 20 K, the CDW peak saturates at $72.5\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. There is not much change of the Raman spectra from 20 to 110 K, except for a slight red shift of the CDW mode to $70.2\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. From 130 K, the CDW amplitude mode shows a progressive red shift increasing with temperature. Gradually, the CDW peak crosses over a phonon peak at about $56.0\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, and they share the same spectral weight at 230 K. Apart from the softening as T increases, the CDW mode is gradually broadened and its intensity is weakened. The temperature dependence of the $\mathrm{GdTe}_{3}$ CDW mode is consistent with that for $\mathrm{DyTe}_{3}.^{16,32}$ The lower and higher frequency ones of those two coupled modes are marked as P1 and P2, respectively. We extracted the frequencies of P1 and P2, and displayed in Fig. 4. Ideally, the frequency of the CDW amplitude mode $\omega_{\text{CDW}}$ follows the Ginzburg-Landau temperature dependence,
$$
\omega_{\mathrm{CDW}}=\omega_{0} \times\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{T}}{\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{CDW}}}\right)^{\beta}, \tag{1}
$$
where $\omega_{0}$ is the CDW amplitude mode frequency at a low temperature limit. $\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{CDW}}$ is the CDW transition temperature, and $\beta$ is a critical exponent. The CDW amplitude mode interacts with a neighboring phonon mode with a nearly temperature-independent frequency of $\omega_{\text{ph}}$. This coupling can be modelled by a $2\times 2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} \omega_{\text{CDW}} & \delta \\ \delta & \omega_{\text{ph}} \end{pmatrix}$, where $\delta$ characterizes the coupling strength. The two eigenvalues correspond to the renormalized frequencies measured from the Raman spectra. Furthermore, taking $\mathrm{T}_{\text{CDW}}$ = 377 K from Ref. 17, we fit the eigenvalues with the Raman spectra, and obtain the fitted parameters $\beta=0.256$ and $\delta=4.36\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, which are quite close to those for $\mathrm{DyTe}_{3}$, $\mathrm{HoTe}_{3}$, and $\mathrm{TbTe}_{3}.^{16,32}$ The fitting curves match very well with the experimental data as shown in Fig. 4.

To quantitatively study $T_{CDW}$ varying with the thickness of $GdTe_3$, the frequencies of the CDW and coupled phonon modes were also fitted for the thin layer samples by using the coupling model and shown in Fig. 4 (the details of the fitting are shown in the supplementary material).

The $T_{CDW}$ of all samples obtained from the fitting is summarized in Fig. 5. $T_{CDW}$ shows a clear increase for the samples thinner than 40 nm and reaches 431 K in a 10 nm film.

Reference 36 attributed $T_{CDW}$ increasing with decreasing thickness to the enhanced electron-phonon interactions due to reduced dielectric screening. Generally, the reduction of the dielectric screening happens in materials thinner than 10 nm, and the enhancement of electron-phonon coupling causes a blue shift of the CDW amplitude mode. $^{7}$ Since the samples in the present work are generally larger than 10 nm and the changes of the frequencies of the CDW modes at low temperatures are negligible, the increase in the $T_{CDW}$ in $GdTe_3$ thin samples should be caused by other reasons. The CDW transition mainly happens in the metallic Te layers in $RTe_3$. The Te layer suffers an internal compression named as "chemical pressure" by the RTe slab. $^{17}$ A larger atom leads to a smaller compression (i.e., larger lattice constant), and consequently a larger $T_{CDW}$. Externally applied pressure was shown to be equivalent to the chemical pressure. $^{37}$ We notice that the peak $c$ displays a blue shift when decreasing the sample thickness (see the supplementary material). This mode corresponds to the out-of-plane vibrations of Te atoms in opposite directions. $^{35}$ The blue shift is suggestive of lattice compression (stronger interaction) along the out-of-plane direction. Due to the Poisson effect, the in-plane Te layers should display expansion. The blue shift in thinner samples is also consistent with the change of the Raman peak at the same frequency with increasing lattice constant. $^{35}$ Therefore, we speculate that the dimensional effect is likely equivalent to the pressure effect. Chemical pressure release leads to lattice expansion in the Te layers. As a consequence, $T_{CDW}$ increases. The increased $T_{CDW}$ in thinned samples should be observable in other $RTe_3$ materials. In addition, considering that the smaller R atom has a larger pressure release, the increase in the $T_{CDW}$ should be more prominent for materials with smaller R atoms.

In conclusion, we investigated the thickness dependence of the CDW transition temperature by studying the evolution of the Raman spectrum in mechanically exfoliated flakes of $GdTe_3$. With the aid of the first principles calculation, the phonon and CDW modes were assigned for the Raman spectrum. All of the thin-layer samples display the same strong coupling behavior with a certain phonon mode as observed in the bulk sample, and the $T_{CDW}$ of samples with different thicknesses are extracted by fitting the temperature dependent frequencies of the CDW and coupled phonon modes with a coupling model. We found that $T_{CDW}$ shows a clear increase for samples thinner than 40 nm, and reaches 431 K for a 10 nm thick sample, 54 K higher than that for the bulk sample. We speculate that this dimensional effect originates from the chemical pressure release in the Te

layer where CDW occurs. Our findings demonstrate that manipulating $T_{CDW}$ in RTe₃ by varying the thickness is effective. The increased $T_{CDW}$ will help us to better utilize its properties above room temperature.
