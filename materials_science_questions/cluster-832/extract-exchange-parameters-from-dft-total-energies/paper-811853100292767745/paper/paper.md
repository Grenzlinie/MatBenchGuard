The ability to control spin and charge degrees of freedom of electrons in low-dimensional systems opens the way to a variety of applications in the field of spintronics. One of the best known examples is the spin-valve (SV) effect [1], which occurs in devices composed of a non-magnetic material separating two magnetic electrodes. The effect consists of induced changes in the resistance of the device by manipulating the relative orientation of the respective magnetizations. It is characterized by a magnetoresistance (MR), defined by the percentage resistance variation when the magnetization direction of the electrodes is switched between parallel (P) and anti-parallel (AP).

The SV effect results from the contrast between the interfacial scatterings experienced by electrons of opposite spin directions. Because of this contrast, the overall resistance depends on the relative orientation of the electrode magnetizations. This relies on the non-magnetic material having a spin-coherence length that exceeds the distance separating the electrodes. Carbon nanotubes (CNT) have spin-coherence lengths of the order of hundreds of nanometers [2], making them ideal candidates for the non-magnetic component of SV devices. In fact, after Tsukagoshi et al. [3] reported a MR of around 8% for CNT in contact with Co electrodes, numerous studies of nanotube-based spin valves have been carried out [4], culminating with the recent report of 61% [5].

Notwithstanding the gradual increase in the reported levels of MR, difficulties of working with CNT-based SV are (a) the limited capacity for injecting spins into the CNT and (b) the undesirable spin-flip scattering processes induced by the presence of magnetic impurities. The former is susceptible to resistivity mismatches between electrodes and CNT [6] and the latter mixes the two spin channels which shortens the spin-coherence length of the device. Our goal in this paper is to propose an alternative route to producing the SV effect in CNT that avoids these two limiting factors. We argue that the inclusion of magnetic impurities in nanotubes, rather than detrimental, may be surprisingly beneficial to the SV effect. Furthermore, because these impurities act as spin-dependent scatterers the need for magnetic electrodes to inject spins may be circumvented.

Magnetic impurities in an otherwise pristine crystalline material break the symmetry of the system with regard to the spatial and the spin degrees of freedom. However, it is possible to break the spatial symmetry without necessarily mixing the different spin channels if magnetically aligned impurities are introduced. In the case of CNT-based SV, this can only occur if magnetic impurities are coupled and remain so even when they are relatively far apart. This requires a long-range magnetic interaction that is known to occur when localized magnetic moments are surrounded by conduction electrons.

We have recently studied the electron-mediated coupling between magnetic impurities adsorbed to CNT [7–9] and shown that the magnetic moments of impurities can be aligned in either the P or the AP configuration. By defining the coupling between two impurities a distance D apart as $J(D)=E_{P}-E_{AP}$, where $E_{\alpha}$ is the total energy in the configuration $\alpha$, we know that positive (negative) values for J correspond to AP(P) alignment. Furthermore, it has been shown that the coupling $J(D)$ may decay abruptly as a function of D, depending on the adsorption location. In particular, we have shown with a simple analytical model that the coupling between two impurities adsorbed right above the hexagonal centre of the nanotube lattice decays as fast as $1/D^{5}$ [9]. Such a fast rate of decay has been reproduced by density functional theory results indicating the appropriateness of our model calculations. The fact that the vast majority of transition-metal atoms prefer to adsorb on this central location apparently suggests that the occurrence of long-range coupling between impurities on CNT is highly unlikely, unless they are extremely close together.

However, more long-ranged interactions decaying as $1/D$ have been predicted when magnetic impurities replace, rather than adsorb to, the carbon atoms of the CNT [7,9]. Motivated by recent reports pointing to the formation of localized magnetic moments when carbon-based materials are substitutionally doped with transition-metal atoms [10,12, 11], we investigate the nature of the magnetic coupling between these substitutional magnetic impurities to establish whether they can be used to improve the SV effect in CNT.

We start by considering two magnetic atoms (a distance D apart) substituting two carbon atoms of a CNT, as shown schematically in the inset of Fig. 1. Magnetism in these atoms is driven by an intra-atomic Hubbard-like interaction that, when treated through a self-consistent procedure, can be described by an effective spin- and position-dependent potential. In this way, the electronic structure of the entire system is well represented by a Hamiltonian in a basis of localized atomic orbitals. Furthermore, bearing in mind that the nanotube conduction electrons mediate the interaction between the magnetic moments, it is sufficient to treat the above Hamiltonian within a single-orbital description. As far as the magnetic atoms are considered, they are described by a fivefold degenerate d-band with the appropriate occupation to represent typical magnetic materials. With the exception of the amplitude value, the general features of the electron-mediated magnetic coupling like periodicity, sign and rate of decay are known to be well described by the single-band representation [13,14]. Because the main conclusions of this manuscript are essentially based on the sign of the coupling, it is sufficient to use this simplified description for the electronic structure. In such a basis, this tight-binding-like Hamiltonian is fully determined by the hopping integrals and their respective spin-dependent on-site potentials.

A direct way of calculating the magnetic coupling between two moments substitutionally embedded in a CNT is given by [7]

$$
J=-\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d E f(E) \operatorname{Im} \ln \left[1+4 V_{x}^{2} G_{a, b}^{\uparrow}(E) G_{b, a}^{\downarrow}(E)\right],
$$

where $f(E)$ is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function and $V_{x}$ represents the exchange splitting in the magnetic impurities. $G_{a, b}^{\sigma}(E)$ represents the single-particle Green function propagator between the magnetic atoms, labeled $a$ and $b$, for electrons of spin $\sigma$ and energy $E$ in the P configuration of the system.

It is instructive to distinguish between the two nonequivalent sites of the hexagonal CNT, hereafter referred to as black and white sub-lattices, as depicted in Fig. 1. We label the coupling $J_{\bullet \bullet}, J_{\circ \circ}$ or $J_{\bullet \circ}$, depending on whether the substitutional impurities occupy two black sites, two white sites, or one of each, respectively. It can be shown that the coupling between atomic spins occupying like sites are of same sign, but the sign is reversed when in different sub-lattices, i.e., $|J_{\bullet \bullet}| / J_{\bullet \bullet}=|J_{\circ \circ}| / J_{\circ \circ}=-|J_{\bullet \circ}| / J_{\bullet \circ}$. In other words, when $J_{\bullet \bullet}>0$ ($J_{\bullet \bullet}<0$) the coupling between different sub-lattices is $J_{\bullet \circ}<0$ ($J_{\bullet \circ}>0$). Fig. 1 shows one such example in which the coupling $J$ between two Ni atoms substitutionally placed on a (6,6) CNT is plotted as a function of $D$. The full circles representing $J_{\bullet \bullet}$ have opposite sign to the squares associated with $J_{\bullet \circ}$, which means that Ni atoms substitutionally located on the same sub-lattice prefer to adopt an AP alignment but favor a P ordering otherwise.

The knowledge of $J$ between a single pair of substitutional Ni impurities, depicted in Fig. 1, describes how their magnetizations are aligned but does not characterize the magnetic ordering of a truly disordered environment. In the case of atoms randomly distributed on a CNT, the total magnetization of the system and the local equilibrium spin directions may be found by using the Heisenberg Hamiltonian defined by $H=\sum_{k, \ell} J_{k, \ell} \vec{S}_{k} \cdot \vec{S}_{\ell}$. Here, the integers $k$ and $\ell$ label the magnetic atoms in the systems, $\vec{S}_{\beta}$ is the spin vector at atom $\beta$ and $J_{k, \ell}$ is the pairwise coupling between atoms $k$ and $\ell$. Monte Carlo methods implemented within the simulated annealing technique [15] give us the relative orientation of each one of the impurity magnetic moments. We can anticipate that the moments will not be fully aligned due to the competition between $J_{\bullet \bullet}$ and $J_{\bullet \circ}$ shown in Fig. 1. Because the impurities are on average equally split between black and white sites, it is easy to see that some degree of magnetic frustration is inevitable since it is impossible to accommodate moments that want to be AP on like sites and yet P on different sub-lattices.

With the magnetic orientations known, one can then proceed to calculate the transport properties of the system. This has been done within the Kubo formalism which provides closed-form expressions for the conductance of a nanoscopic system. In our case, the system consists of an infinitely long CNT doped with a small concentration of magnetic atoms in a finite region of the tube. Results for the conductance are averaged over 1500 configurations of disorder in order to achieve statistical significance. Not only can the overall conductance be obtained but we can also express it in terms of its separate spin components. In other words, the conductance $\Gamma$ can be written as $\Gamma=\Gamma_{\uparrow \uparrow}+\Gamma_{\downarrow \downarrow}-2 \Gamma_{\uparrow \downarrow}$, where $\Gamma_{\uparrow \uparrow}$ and $\Gamma_{\downarrow \downarrow}$ are the conductances for up- and down-spin electrons, respectively, and $\Gamma_{\uparrow \downarrow}$ is the so-called mixed conductance that describes the spin-flip processes occurring within the disordered region of the CNT. This latter component results from the fact that the magnetic orientations are not collinear, which in turn mixes the two spin channels. The MR values displayed in Fig. 2 are not particularly large when compared to the reported results observed in the traditional SV setup of CNT separating two magnetic electrodes [3-5]. Furthermore, they are also comparable to the MR values for granular systems [16]. This is not so surprising due to the fact that the magnetic frustration induces the non-collinear ordering of the magnetic moments, which in turn mixes the two spin channels in the conductance. Bearing in mind that the MR increases with the scattering contrast between the different spin channels, any spin mixing will have the opposite effect of making the conductances for the different spin directions more like each other. In fact, the top panel of Fig. 2 shows that $\Gamma_{\uparrow \uparrow}, \Gamma_{\downarrow \downarrow}$ and $\Gamma_{\uparrow \downarrow}$ are all comparable in magnitude, which significantly reduces the scattering contrast experienced by electrons of opposite spins.

If the spin mixing has such an undesirable effect on the MR, it is worth investigating whether the magnetic frustration can be avoided altogether. This can be done if the pairwise couplings between substitutional magnetic impurities have an opposite sign to those found for Ni. In other words, if $J_{\bullet \bullet}<0$ and $J_{\bullet \circ}>0$ the magnetic moments on like sites will be P at the same time that moments on different sublattices prefer the AP alignment. The magnetic ground state configuration in this case will have all atomic spins located on black sites aligned in one direction, and those on white sites pointing in the opposite direction. Therefore, a collinear configuration is favored, and the two conducting spin channels are completely independent. We must then look for impurities that will display a P alignment when located at like sites.

This search is shown in the inset of Fig. 3 where we calculate $J_{\bullet \bullet}$ for two substitutional impurities at a fixed separation as a function of the occupation $n$ of the impurity d-band. As previously mentioned, $J_{\bullet \bullet}$ does not change sign as a function of separation but it could as a function of $n$. In fact, we find sign reversals at $n=0.68$ and at $n=1.3$. This means that substitutional impurities whose d-band occupations lie within this range will not undergo magnetic frustration when randomly distributed on a metallic carbon nanotube lattice, and should consequently display different degrees of MR.

To test this idea we consider two typical values of $n$ corresponding to Ni and Mn, represented in the inset of Fig. 3a by a cross and a triangle, respectively [17]. According to the figure, the magnetic moments of randomly distributed Mn impurities should be collinear even in the absence of an external field. This suppresses the spin-flip scattering that occurs when moments are not aligned and should enhance the conductance contrast between up- and down-spin electrons, thus magnifying the MR. This is precisely what happens when we calculate the MR in the presence of substitutional Mn impurities in a metallic CNT. The bottom panel of Fig. 3 shows very large values for the MR (solid line) when plotted as a function of the Mn concentration. Even in the absence of any external aligning field, the mixed conductance vanishes. This means that the equilibrium state generated by the Heisenberg Hamiltonian is one in which all moments are collinear. Also shown are the total conductances $\bar{\Gamma}$ and $\Gamma$ for the system with and without an aligning field, respectively. Both conductances decay as power laws, each one of which with a distinct decaying power. This distinction in the decaying power is a direct reflection of the scattering contrast between the different spin channels. Such a clear contrast is absent in the frustrated regime, which explains the reduced values of MR found in Fig. 2.

With very distinct decaying rates for the conductances $\Gamma$ and $\bar{\Gamma}$, it is evident that beyond a certain concentration one of them (in this case $\Gamma$) will vanish before the other, giving rise to enormously large values of MR. Not only as a function of concentration do the conductances decay with different rates. Also as a function of the system size the conductances decay very differently. In this case the decay is not a power law but exponential, which is characteristic of localization effects. In other words, the scattering contrast experienced by up- and down-spin electrons induces localization lengths that are spin-dependent [18]. As a result, systems whose sizes are intermediate with respect to these localization lengths may lead to a situation in which $\Gamma \approx 0$ whereas $\bar{\Gamma}$ remains finite, which is the case depicted in Fig. 3.

Finally, to test the claims here made in regard to the MR enhancement, we have also carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations to verify the suitability of our model [11,19]. While it is impractical to use DFT to describe the magnetic ordering or the transport properties of heavily disordered CNT, we can obtain the magnetic coupling by evaluating total energy calculations in both the P and the AP configurations. For that we have considered two substitu-

tional atoms per unit cell and calculated the total energy in both configurations. This has been done for Ni as well as for Mn. To calculate $J_{\bullet \bullet}$ we firstly place the two impurities a small distance apart ($D=3a$) along the axial direction such that they both occupy black sites. The same is done to obtain $J_{\bullet \circ}$ after one of the impurities is moved to its nearest neighbour along the circumferential direction. Results for Ni are $J_{\bullet \bullet}=8.0 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{eV}$ and $J_{\bullet \circ}=-1.2 \times 10^{-2} \mathrm{eV}$. For Mn the corresponding results are $J_{\bullet \bullet}=-5.2 \times 10^{-1} \mathrm{eV}$ and $J_{\bullet \circ}=3.7 \times 10^{-2} \mathrm{eV}$. Note that the signs of all the couplings are exactly the same as those predicted by our results, shown in Figs. 1 and 2. It is worth noting that DFT total energy evaluations for other impurity separations are also consistent with our predictions. It also important to stress that the coupling sign is what determines whether or not magnetic frustration will take place.

In summary, we propose an alternative way to generate very large values of MR in CNT-based devices that does not depend on spin injection. In this case, the presence of magnetically coupled substitutional impurities, rather than detrimental, is surprisingly beneficial to the SV effect when their moments adopt a fully collinear alignment. This prevents the occurrence of magnetic frustration, enhances the scattering contrast between the different spin channels and amplifies the magnitude of the MR by many orders of magnitude. Furthermore, we predict that magnetic atoms whose d-band occupation $n$ lie in the range $0.68<n<1.2$ are the likely candidates to display such large values of MR [17], a prediction also confirmed by first-principle calculations for Mn atoms substitutionally inserted on CNT. These findings can not only prevent some of the key limitations experienced by earlier attempts of producing CNT-based SV devices but can also guide us in generating increasingly large values of MR, which in turn can be used to design more sensitive devices.
