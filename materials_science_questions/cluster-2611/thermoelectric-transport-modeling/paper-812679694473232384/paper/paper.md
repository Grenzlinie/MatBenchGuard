# Four-fold enhancement in the thermoelectric power factor of germanium selenide monolayer by adsorption of graphene quantum dot

Vaishali Sharma¹, Hardik L. Kagdada¹ and Prafulla K Jha¹,*

¹ Department of Physics, Faculty of Science, The Maharaja Sayajirao University of Baroda, Vadodara-390 002, India

## Abstract

The present study reports the electronic and thermoelectric properties of graphene quantum dot pyrene adsorbed germanium selenide monolayer using density functional theory calculations. The adsorption energy of 4x4 supercell of germanium selenide monolayer with graphene quantum dot is -0.92 eV suggesting a favorable binding between the germanium selenide monolayer and graphene quantum dot. Our calculations reveal that the Seebeck coefficient for both germanium selenide monolayer and graphene quantum dot adsorbed germanium selenide monolayer (GQD@GeSe monolayer) increases with a decrease in doping level while the value of Seebeck coefficient is highest at zero doping. The incorporation of graphene quantum dot increases the number of charge carriers in germanium selenide monolayer resulting in the amplified electrical conductivity from $0.13x10^{19}$ to $0.52x10^{19}\ (\Omega ms)^{-1}$ which leads to a very large thermoelectric power factor at room temperature. The power factor is enhanced from $1.17x10^{10}$ to $5.38x10^{10}$ W/mKs in germanium selenide by adsorption of graphene quantum dot on germanium selenide monolayer with doping level and temperature which can be used to generate more output power for the thermoelectric power generation. The present work contributes in understanding the design of germanium selenide monolayer with graphene quantum dot based hybrid structures for thermoelectric devices in the future.

**Keywords:** GeSe monolayer, graphene quantum dots, adsorption, thermoelectricity, power factor.

## I. Introduction

Highly efficient thermoelectric materials will play a key role in future owing to the world's increasing energy consumption and shortage of natural resources. Thermoelectric technology provides an effective direction in converting waste heat into electricity through the Seebeck effect [1]. The combination of semiconducting, electrical and thermal characteristics in thermoelectric materials permits their utilization in cooling and heating [2]. The thermoelectric performance or efficiency of a given material is classified by the dimensionless quantity called figure of merit (ZT), which depends on directly proportional parameters: Seebeck coefficient (S) and electrical conductivity ($\sigma$). However, ZT is inversely proportional to the thermal conductivity which further involves two parts: electronic thermal conductivity ($\kappa_{e}$) and lattice thermal conductivity ($\kappa_{l}$). High ZT and power factor can be achieved by enhancing the Seebeck coefficient which can be achieved by reducing the dimension of materials. The low dimensional materials lead to increased phonon scattering which further reduces the lattice thermal conductivity and finally improves the ZT [3]. In last few years, researchers have improved the ZT for low dimensional materials including two-dimensional (2D) heterostructures and zero dimensional materials. The thermal conductivity is lower in nanocomposites than their alloys counterparts (with similar chemical composition) [4]. For example, silicon, the ZT value of ~

0.01 in the case of bulk silicon enhances to 0.1 (about 100 times) in its nanowire form [5]. This increment is mainly attributed to the reduction of lattice thermal conductivity attained by reducing the dimension of silicon i.e. bringing it to nanowire form from its bulk form. The ZT of ~0.4 at room temperature is found in case of thin silicon membranes [6].

Among new materials under intense scrutiny, 2D materials now-a-days are being extensively explored for thermoelectric applications due to better performance than their bulk counterpart. The chemical functionalization, doping, defect and strain engineering, and formation of heterostructure have shown to significantly affect the thermoelectric properties of 2D materials [7]. Apart from the nanostructuring, doping, creating structural defects in graphene, its integration with polymer is shown affecting thermal conductivity and Seebeck coefficient [8]. A significantly high power factor is obtained in few layer $MoS_2$ at high carrier concentrations [9]. The conductivity of MO based MXenes ($M_{n+1}X_nT_x$, M is an early transition metal, X is carbon and/or nitrogen, $T_x$ represents the surface functional groups such as OH, O and/or F groups with n=1, 2 or 3) rapidly increases above 500K [10]. The thermoelectric performance of MXenes also increases by doping, controlling surface functional groups and/or making composite with semiconductors or polymers [10]. Recently, many studies are focused on chalcogenides such as SnSe, GeTe, GeSe etc. featuring them as a potential thermoelectric material. For instance, hole-doped SnSe crystals present high $ZT_{dev}$ (device) of ~1.34, and ranges between 0.7-2.0 in temperature range 300-773K which is attributed to the presence of manifold electronic valence bands [11]. Maximum ZT (0.7 at 1350K) is achieved in rhombohedral phase of bulk GeTe [12]. An enhanced Seebeck coefficient has been found in one dimensional GeSe [13]. A high ZT ranging from 0.8 to 2.5 between the temperature of 300K–800K is obtained in hole doped bulk GeSe through crystallographic b-axis [14]. Further, high bandgap 2D-chalcogenides such as SnSe, SnS, GeSe, GeTe and GeS have Seebeck coefficient twice to that of their bulk counterparts [15-16]. Shafique et al. theoretically predicted increased ZT values for SnSe (2.36, 2.46), SnS (1.75, 1.88), GeSe (1.99, 1.73) and GeS (1.85, 1.29) at 700 K in zigzag and armchair configurations which is greater than their bulk counterparts [15].

Bulk semiconductors comprising of heavy elements like PbTe have lower thermal conductivity due to the point defects present in them [17]. Accordingly, smaller the grain-size of n-type PbTe, higher the ZT which is due to increased potential barrier scattering [17]. However, the reduction in lattice thermal conductivity is not significant in nanomaterials, where the only

part which improves the ZT is the power factor evaluated through the product of $S^{2}$ and $\sigma$ [18]. It has been shown that the efficiency regulated by ZT is not the only considered parameter for practical applications, the output power is indeed equitably as important as the efficiency [19]. Contrarily, substantial output power is more important in case of free and unlimited heat source such as solar heat, waste heat from industries, automobiles etc. [19]. The Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity can be tuned independently in nanostructured materials by modifying their electronic density of states (DOS), which will further improve their power factor (PF) or output power. The electronic properties of $Bi_{2}Te_{3}$ were tuned by the inclusion of tin (Sn) to acquire higher PF [20]. The confinement significantly improved the conductivity of silicon nanowires leading in the increment of ~2x to 3x PF [21]. Recently, diketopyrrolopyrrole-based semiconductor (PDPP3T) have achieved excellent power factor of $276 \mu Wm^{-1}K^{-2}$ by maintaining the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity. The high mobility and low level of total dopant volume is responsible for such large power factor of $276 \mu W m^{-1}K^{-2}$ [22] and are directly associated with the output power density [19,23]. Liu et al. [23] studied two different materials PbSe and $Hf_{0.25}Zr_{0.75}NiSn$ having similar cubic crystalline structure at $T_{h}(500 \square)$ and $T_{c}(50 \square)$ with the 2mm leg length (L). The maximum value of $ZT$ and PF for the former is 1.3 and 21 $\mu W/cmK^{2}$, whereas for the latter, these values are 1 and $5 \mu W/cmK^{2}$. Their study showed that the PbSe has efficiency of ~11% with output power of $5.4 W/cm^{2}$. Nevertheless, the efficiency of $Hf_{0.25}Zr_{0.75}NiSn$ is 10% and output power is $14.4 W/cm^{2}$. Further concluding that enhancing the PF is valuable to increase not only ZT but also the output power for power generation applications. Therefore, output power is as important as efficiency since certain materials exhibit high $ZT$ yet low thermoelectric performance due to their less output power.

By reducing the materials dimensionality from traditional micrometer to nanometer, the relation between the confinement length (L) and the thermal de Broglie wavelength $(\Lambda)$ gained attention for defining the thermoelectric power factor of materials [24]. A zero dimensional (0D) quantum dots (QDs) may be ideal for phonon-blocking and electron transportation in thermoelectric applications [25]. Further, the sharp peaks amplified in electronic density of states of 0D QDs near Fermi energy will benefit in improving the power factor of considered materials when QDs are incorporated in them. Their electronic, optical and thermal properties of QDs can be independently tailored by playing with the size [26]. Additionally, GQDs enhance the power factor and ZT in GQDs-PbTe composites [27], $Bi_{2}Te_{3}$/GQDs hybrid sheets [28] and

GQDs/PEDOT:PSS films [29]. The hybrid structure with inclusion of GQDs with size analogous to or equivalent to mean free path of carriers will be a potential way to improve thermoelectric properties of a material. It is proposed that GeSe present thermoelectric properties equivalent to SnSe making them potential material for efficient thermoelectric properties [30]. Although, polycrystalline SnSe comprises high ZT and low thermal conductivity, the power factor is considerably less than single crystals [31]. To overcome this downside of SnSe, different chalcogenides were explored for thermoelectric applications. Among all chalcogenides, unfortunately, minimal works have been done to explore GeSe along with their nano and hybrid structures in order to attain high power factor for high output power.

In present work, the adsorption mechanism, electronic and thermoelectric properties, including band structure, density of states, Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity and power factor of monolayer GeSe and GQD@GeSe monolayer are investigated. The state-of-the-art in the present study is to propose a new model of single layer chalcogenide (GeSe) with zero-dimensional GQDs to get high thermoelectric power factor using density functional theory (DFT) and semi-empirical Boltzmann transport theory. The results unveil an evident enhancement in power factor after the adsorption of GQD. Quantum confinement effects in this study plays a lead role to improve the thermoelectric performance and further in the field of sustainable energy reducing environmental pollution. The manuscript is arranged as follows: Section "Computational methodology" includes a description of the computational details used for optimization and calculation of electronic and thermoelectric properties. Section "Results and Discussion" presents the results and discussion of the TE properties obtained from the calculations. Section "Conclusion" summarizes the main conclusions of present work.

## II. Computational methodology

The methodology in present calculation is based on density functional theory (DFT) and Boltzmann transport theory. In bulk phase, GeSe crystals exhibit a layered orthorhombic structure with two layers along the z-direction of the unit cell (total eight atoms; four Ge and four Se) [32]. To model monolayer structure of GeSe, we isolated one layer of the GeSe from its bulk counter-part by creating vacuum of $15$ Å in z-direction (total four atoms; two Ge and two Se atom). The lattice parameters and electronic properties for unit cell of GeSe monolayer were

reported in previous studies [15]. Size of monolayer in x-y plane of the unit cell is quite smaller than our considered GQD. Thus, to analyze the effect of GQD on thermoelectric power on GeSe monolayer, the unit cell of monolayer GeSe is extended four times in x and y direction, to model a 4x4 supercell containing 64 atoms. Initially, the extended monolayer of GeSe and GQD were not in its minimum energy state. To evaluate properties of a material, it is necessary to obtain ground state or minimum energy of corresponding material which is called the optimization of geometries. The calculation of ground state energy or optimization process is performed by varying the lattice parameters of GeSe monolayer along with relaxation of atomic positions to obtain ground state energy. Thus, we optimize the 4x4 pristine GeSe monolayer and GQD separately by solving the Kohn-Sham equation implemented in Quantum ESPRESSO (QE) code [33]. The minimization or optimization process requires the exchange correlation of electrons which is treated with generalized gradient approximation (GGA) proposed by Perdew-Burke- Ernzerhof (PBE) [34]. The interaction between electrons and ions were determined by the pseudopotential generated which was given by Martins and Troullier using plane wave self- consistent field (PWSCF) method [35]. In order to get better minimization of structure the plane wave basis expansion energy cut off is set to 100 Ry and Monkhorst-Pack special k-points grid of 3x3x1 to sample entire Brillouin zone (BZ) is used. The atomic relaxation of structures has been performed by the standard Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) algorithm [36] in a fully unconstrained manner with the force of about $10^{-3}$ eV/Å and energy accuracy of $10^{-4}$ eV. As discussed earlier, GeSe has layered structure, the interactions between layers were incorporated by long-range van der Waals (vdW) interactions where, the semi-empirical correction scheme of Grimme (DFT-D2) is considered in the calculations [37]. After minimization of GeSe monolayer and GQD, we proceed for the adsorption nature of GQD on GeSe monolayer by calculating adsorption energy. To understand adsorption mechanism of GQD on GeSe monolayer, the GQD was initially placed on GeSe monolayer. Again the whole system of GQD@GeSe monolayer was minimized using the same parameters as in monolayer GeSe. The adsorption energy $E_{ad}$ is calculated according to the following equation:

$$
E_{a d}=E_{(G e S e+G Q D)}-\left\{E_{(G e S e)}+E_{(G Q D)}\right\} \tag{1}
$$

Here, $E_{(GeSe+GQD)}$ is the total energy of system GeSe monolayer adsorbed with GQD. $E_{(GeSe)}$ is the total energy of the adsorbent surface GeSe monolayer, $E_{(GQD)}$ is the total energy of GQD which is obtained from their full optimized geometries. Negative value of $E_{ad}$ indicates the exergonic nature of the adsorption.

![](./images/812679694473232384_2.jpg)

Figure 1: Schematic of computer simulation used in present study to calculate electronic and thermoelectric properties.

The optimization of any structure is the process in which any crystal structure exhibits ground state or minimum energy configuration with maximum force of about $10^{-3}$ eV/Å, energy accuracy of at least $10^{-4}$ eV along with zero pressure.

After the structure optimization and study of adsorption mechanism the electronic transport properties for thermoelectric applications of GeSe and GQD@GeSe monolayer are evaluated. To get better understanding of implemented steps, the schematic of present calculations with corresponding input and output parameters are depicted in Fig. 1. To analyze electronic transport properties, the electronic band structure and total/projected density of states

(DOS/PDOS) were firstly studied to see the effect of GQD on GeSe monolayer within the framework of DFT as implemented in QE [33]. The electronic transport coefficients purely depend on the electronic bands structure. Therefore, electron energy eigen values for the calculation of electronic transport coefficients are utilized as they are fitted in Fourier expansion scheme in BoltzTraP package [38]. The semi-classical Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) is solved to evaluate the thermoelectric transport coefficients under the rigid body constant relaxation time ($\tau$) approximation (CRTA) [38]. Therefore, under CRTA, the conductivities $\sigma$ and $\kappa_e$ results in terms of relaxation time like $\sigma/\tau$ and $\kappa_e/\tau$. Seebeck coefficient under the assumption that $\tau$ is independent of direction, is considered to be independent of $\tau$ [38]. In the semi-classical transport theory, the electrical conductivity tensor can be defined as follows:

$$
\sigma_{\alpha \beta}(T, \mu)=\frac{1}{V} \int e^{2} v_{\alpha}(i, \boldsymbol{k}) v_{\beta}(i, \boldsymbol{k}) \tau(i, \boldsymbol{k})\left[\frac{-\partial f_{\mu}(T, \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}\right] d \epsilon \tag{2}
$$

Here, N is defined as the number of k-points in the reciprocal space, V is the reciprocal space volume. In the following manner Seebeck coefficient can be written as:

$$
S_{\alpha \beta}(T, \mu)=\frac{1}{e T} \frac{\int v_{\alpha}(i, \boldsymbol{k}) v_{\beta}(i, \boldsymbol{k})(\epsilon-\mu)\left[\frac{-\partial f_{\mu}(T, \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}\right] d \epsilon}{\int v_{\alpha}(i, \boldsymbol{k}) v_{\beta}(i, \boldsymbol{k})\left[\frac{-\partial f_{\mu}(T, \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}\right] d \epsilon} \tag{3}
$$

where e is the electron charge, $f$ is the Fermi distribution function, $\mu$ is the chemical potential amd T is the absolute temperature. The electronic thermal conductivity is calculated by using the Wiedemann-Franz law along with BoltzTraP distribution, written as:

$$
k_{\alpha \beta}(T, \mu)=\frac{1}{e^{2} T \Omega} \int \sigma_{\alpha \beta}(\epsilon)(\epsilon-\mu)^{2}\left[\frac{-\partial f_{\mu}(T, \epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}\right] d \epsilon \tag{4}
$$

Here, $k$ is the electronic thermal conductivity.

### III. Result and Discussions

#### A. Structural properties

The optimized structure of GeSe monolayer from our previous study [13] is used for present calculations, where a good agreement between optimized and previously reported lattice parameters is achieved [15]. The unit cell of GeSe monolayer comprising four atoms and a vacuum of 15 Å in the z-direction is used to avoid the interaction. Pyrene is a polyaromatic hydrocarbon treated as graphene quantum dots. They are closely analogous to the zero-


dimensional nanographenes [39]. It contains four fused benzene rings with the chemical formula $C_{16}H_{10}$. In order to estimate the effect of graphene quantum dots coverage, the adsorption of GQD has been explored with 2x2 and 4x4 supercells of GeSe monolayer. The calculation is primarily considered by placing a GQD above $3.5$ Å over a 2x2 supercell of GeSe monolayer.

![](./images/812679694473232384_3.jpg)

Figure 2: Optimized geometries of (a) GQD (pyrene) (b) 4x4 GeSe monolayer with their top and side view.

GQD is placed parallel to the GeSe monolayer in order to get the maximum effect of carbon and hydrogen atoms on Ge and Se atoms. After the optimization, it appears that GQD is slanted towards Ge atoms as can be seen from S1 (Supplementary Figure S1), decreasing the distance of $3.5$ Å to $3.2$ Å at the Ge site which may be due to the similar valence electronic configuration of carbon and germanium atoms. However, on the other side, distance between C and Se atoms increases from $3.5$ Å to $5.8$ Å which can be attributed to the fact that if GeSe is increased periodically along x and y-axis, there will be an interaction between the two periodic GQDs. Due to this interaction, the hydrogen (H) atoms of GQDs are repelled from each other leading to the inclination of GQDs towards GeSe which violates the zero-dimensionality of GQDs. The adsorption energy, in this case, is found to be -1.01eV. Therefore, the 4x4 supercell of GeSe monolayer is considered for further studies. Fig. 2(a-b) shows the optimized structure of GeSe monolayer and GQD (pyrene). The lattice parameter, "a" is $16.15$ Å and "b" is $16.12$ Å in fully 4x4 supercell of GeSe monolayer. The lattice parameters in the case of GQD@GeSe monolayer increase by 2.2 to 3.7%. Fig. 3 shows the optimized structure of GQD@GeSe monolayer. Here, the distance between two GQDs ($30$ Å) is sufficiently large to follow the zero- dimensionality of GQDs. The distances Se-Se and Ge-Ge increase along crystallographic a-

direction although it decreases in crystallographic b-direction after the adsorption of GQD. These results in the expansion of lattice parameters stated above.

![](./images/812679694473232384_4.jpg)

Figure 3: Optimized geometries of GQD@GeSe monolayer with top and side view

To investigate the nature of interaction between graphene quantum dot and GeSe monolayer, the adsorption energy ($E_{\text{ad}}$) for GQD@GeSe monolayer using Eq. (3) is calculated. The $E_{\text{ad}}$ of the considered system is -0.92 eV with the distance of ~3.4 Å after optimization. The adsorption energy clearly indicates the physisorption of GQD@GeSe monolayer. The change in adsorption energy results in the change in Seebeck coefficient as the adsorption energy is directly proportional to the maximum change in Seebeck coefficient. It can be attributed to the enhanced electron scattering in the GeSe monolayer due to physisorption of GQD [40]. Further, adsorption of GQD on GeSe monolayer incorporates the charge transfer between GQD and GeSe monolayer which will make a significant change in the thermoelectric transport coefficients. The energy values of HOMO-LUMO orbitals indicate the chemical activity of considered materials. Generally, the atom engaged by more densities of HOMO should have stronger capability to isolate an electron, although atoms have the capability to acquire an electron in case of more occupation of LUMO [41]. After the adsorption of GQD on GeSe monolayer, the HOMO-LUMO values shift towards the conduction band which shifts the Fermi level of GeSe monolayer towards the conduction band. This shift in the Fermi level indicates the n-type doping which

enhances the negative charge carriers in GeSe monolayer, further, it may lead to increase the electrical conductivity of GQD@GeSe monolayer [42].

[43] and others [13,15,44,45]. It is interesting to note that the GQD adsorption over GeSe monolayer does not significantly change the nature and the bandgap value of pristine GeSe monolayer. This may be due to the adsorption of GQD on GeSe monolayer. The contribution is discussed in detail in PDOS. There is a significant difference in the minima of conduction band (CBM) and maxima of valence band (VBM) in $\boldsymbol{S}$-$\boldsymbol{Y}$-$\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ direction of the BZ as compared to the $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$-$\boldsymbol{X}$-$\boldsymbol{S}$. This can be also seen from the total density of states (DOS) that near the Fermi level, towards the conduction band side, the first peak in DOS is almost similar for both GeSe and GQD@GeSe monolayers. However, DOS in valence band qualitatively shows the similar behavior near Fermi level. To understand this behavior and elaborate on the contribution of each atom in electronic properties, the partial density of states (PDOS) is analyzed. Fig. 5 represents the PDOS for GeSe monolayer and GQD@GeSe monolayer. In the PDOS of a monolayer of GeSe, the peak at 1.1 eV arises mainly due to the hybridization of Ge-4p and Se-3d orbitals attributed to empty shells in the electronic configuration of Ge and Se atoms, while at -1.5 eV, the dominant peak is observed due to the Se-4p orbitals.

![](./images/812679694473232384_6.jpg)

Figure 5: Projected density of states (PDOS) along with total DOS for (a) GeSe monolayer and (b) GQD@GeSe monolayer.

Presence of four valence electrons in outer most orbit of the selenium atom is responsible for the major contribution to the valence band in total DOS from the Se-4p orbitals. In conduction band, 2p orbital of carbon and 4p orbital of germanium atom plays a vital role in the total conduction band near the Fermi level. Similar nature of conduction and valence band in PDOS of GeSe monolayer was observed in previously reported studies [46]. The observed spikes between -5 eV to -15 eV in the DOS of GQD@GeSe monolayer can be attributed to the quantum confinement in all three directions. These kinds of spikes are common for the materials confined from all three dimensions [47]. Figure 6 represents the thermoelectric coefficients as a function of doping level (N) at 300 K. This figure clearly shows that the nature of Seebeck coefficient (S) with doping concentration for both GQD@GeSe monolayer and pristine GeSe monolayer is similar and can be due to similar band gap values in both cases. It is observed that the Seebeck coefficient for both pristine and GQD@GeSe monolayers increases as the doping level decreases. However, the value of Seebeck (S) is highest at zero doping.

![](./images/812679694473232384_7.jpg)

Figure 6: Calculated electronic transport coefficients: (a) Seebeck coefficient (b) electrical conductivity (c) electronic thermal conductivity and (d) power factor as a function of doping level (N).

The calculated Seebeck coefficient for GeSe at 300K is in good agreement with the previously reported study by M-F et al. for $10^{19}\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ carrier concentrations [44]. Seebeck coefficient is a tensor quantity which depends on its component in each direction and the electronic bandgap. The larger the bandgap the higher is value of Seebeck coefficient. The bandgap does not vary significantly with the adsorption of GQD and consequently will result only in slightly higher Seebeck coefficient as compared to GeSe monolayer. This slight higher value of Seebeck coefficient in GQD@GeSe (Fig. 6(a)) can be attributed to the band nature observed between S-Y-$\Gamma$ directions of Brillouin zone (BZ). The electrical conductivity for both cases with respect to relaxation time is presented in the Fig. 6(b). As the doping level increases the electrical conductivity increases due to the increase in charge carriers (either p-type or n-type) presented in the Fig. 6(c). There is almost 100% enhancement in the electrical conductivity of GQD@GeSe monolayer. This enhancement in Fig 6(b) will improve significantly the PF. The increment in electronic thermal conductivity is attributed to the Wiedemann-Franz law which shows that the electrical conductivity is directly proportional to the electronic thermal conductivity. Thus electronic thermal conductivity of GeSe monolayer also increases more than double after adsorption of GQD over GeSe monolayer (Fig 6(d)). Here, a similar type of enhanced effect of GQDs on electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient of poly3,4-ethylene- dioxythiophene:polystyrenesulphonate (PEDOT:PSS) is observed [29,42]. These results confirm the impact of graphene quantum dots on thermoelectric coefficients and also support the recent results [27,28,47]. This significant enhancement in electrical conductivity may improve the thermoelectric properties.

The electronic thermal conductivity as a function of doping level is explored in the present study. Qualitatively, both electrical and electronic thermal conductivities exhibit similar increasing nature with doping level attributed to the Wiedemann-Franz law which shows that the electrical conductivity is directly proportional to the electronic thermal conductivity. Thus electronic thermal conductivity of GeSe monolayer also increases more than double after adsorption of GQD@GeSe monolayer. Now, one of the most important thermoelectric parameter "power factor" is discussed which gives rise to the output power calculated with respect to doping level and is presented in the Fig. 6(d). The power factor ($\mathrm{S^{2}\sigma}$) is a combination of the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity. Here, the electrical conductivity with constant relaxation time is evaluated which gives power factor in terms of relaxation time ($\mathrm{S^{2}\sigma/\tau}$). A four-

fold enhancement in the power factor of GeSe monolayer after adsorption of graphene quantum dot over GeSe monolayer is observed. The highest power factor enhancement is achieved in the negative doping region attributed to a huge increment in the electrical conductivity after adsorption of GQD@GeSe monolayer. This will make a great application of GQD@GeSe monolayer towards the high thermoelectric performance devices. Our proposed structural model reveals that the proper adjustment of doping level leads to the increment in power factor.

To have an idea of temperature effect on above thermoelectric parameters, their temperature dependency for a fixed doping level is also analysed and presented in Fig. 7. As a function of temperature, the Seebeck coefficient initially increases up to 500 K for pristine and 650 K for GQD adsorbed GeSe monolayer. The maximum value of Seebeck coefficient is 122 μV/K at 500 K for GeSe monolayer, while, it is 139 μV/K at 600 K for GQD@GeSe monolayer. It is seen from the Fig. 7(a) that the nature of temperature dependent Seebeck coefficient for both is similar in the studied temperature region. However, a difference of about 20 μV/K is observed

Similar kind of behavior of Seebeck coefficient with temperature is observed in GQD capped PbTe nanoparticles [27]. In the case of electrical conductivity and electronic thermal conductivity for n-type doping with temperature shows a similar behavior ascribed to the Wiedemann-Franz law [48]. There are mainly two ways to increase the electrical conductivity of semiconducting materials, one is to increase the number of charge carriers and second is to increase the temperature of the semiconducting materials. Increasing the temperature of semiconducting materials leads to the reduction in the resistivity which enhances the electrical conductivity. In present case, electrical conductivity in the whole range of temperature increases with increasing temperature for both pristine and GQD@GeSe monolayer. Besides, GQD enhances the number of charge carriers in GeSe monolayer after its adsorption which results in the amplified electrical conductivity of around 290% in GQD@GeSe monolayer at room temperature. This increase in electrical conductivity also leads to the increase in the electronic thermal conductivity as a function of temperature which can be seen from Fig. 7(c). Such an amplified electrical conductivity leads to the very large thermoelectric power factor at room temperature. The calculated power factor for both structures reveal that the GQD@GeSe monolayer exhibits 380% higher power factor than pristine GeSe monolayer at room temperature (Fig. 7 (d)). A fairly reasonable agreement of power factor in-case of GeSe monolayer at different temperature with other reported values is found [44]. The state-of-the-art analysis of thermoelectric power factor reveals that proposed structural model of GeSe and GQD leads to four fold enhancement in thermoelectric power factor, which was not observed even for traditional thermoelectric materials such as $Bi_2Te_3$ [28]. Adsorption of graphene quantum dots on $Bi_2Te_3$ nanosheets decreases the power factor, which may result into the low output power. The novelty of this study shows that the power factor and hence the output power of binary chalcogenides may increase after adsorption of such quantum dots. Outcome of the present research may give a new direction to the scientific community towards the composition or nanostructuring of materials with graphene quantum dots in order to get better thermoelectric performance.


### IV. Conclusion

Evolving high-efficiency thermoelectric devices is expressed as an alternative source of sustainable energy and proposed to play a significant role in lowering environmental pollution. The exceptional potential of the thermoelectric devices to convert waste heat into electrical energy for power production shows great possibilities to accomplish a dream of low-carbon society. Moreover, they are highly reliable, vibration and noise-free source of energy. It is due to this aspect, research on the high-throughput thermoelectric materials is in progress from the past few years. In conclusion, the role of adsorption of the graphene quantum dots named pyrene on the thermoelectric properties of two-dimensional GeSe is investigated through the first-principles calculations based on density functional theory in combination with semi-classical Boltzmann transport theory. The suitable binding energy between the GeSe monolayer and pyrene (GQD) is found. The nature of interaction with the adsorption energy of -0.92 eV reveals physisorption of GQD over GeSe monolayer together with enhanced electron scattering. The adsorption of GQD over GeSe monolayer will further assist in enhancing the thermoelectric properties of GeSe monolayer. Minimal change in electronic bandgap from 1.06 eV to 1.03 eV reveals slightly enhanced Seebeck coefficient. Inclusion of GQD leads to increased electrical conductivity around 290% in pristine GeSe monolayer. Such a giant amplification in electrical conductivity leads to the significant escalation of thermoelectric power factor. The calculated power factor in terms of relaxation time for GeSe monolayer is 1.117E10 W/mK²s which further increases to 5.385E10 W/mK²s for GQD adsorbed GeSe monolayer at room temperature. Such enhancement is not observed in GQD adsorption on traditional Bi₂Te₃. This noteworthy behavior can be attributed to enriched hall mobility and charge carrier concentration. As a consequence, a promising power factor of GQD adsorbed GeSe monolayer probably gives large output power in the thermoelectric power generation. Hence, present study evidently provides a promising route for GQD-based hybrid thermoelectric materials which will be important for future development in thermoelectric applications. Finally, this study will be helpful for further investigations particularly at the experimental level.
