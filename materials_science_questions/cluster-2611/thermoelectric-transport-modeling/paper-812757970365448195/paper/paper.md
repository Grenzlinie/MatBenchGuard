## ABSTRACT

Impacts of phase transformation from regular wurtzite phase to a graphitic polymorph hexagonal phase under tensile loading, on the thermoelectric properties of Zinc oxide nanowires (ZnO NWs), which is oriented in the [0001] direction, are studied by combining the first-principles simulation with one- dimensional (1D) Boltzmann transport equation (BTE). Our results show that this phase transformation has greatly influenced the thermoelectric properties of the ZnO NWs. We also find that the largest value of figure of merit ZT achieved for hexagonal phase (H phase) is larger than that for wurtzite phase (W phase) in the temperature range (200 K-1000 K), which means that hexagonal phase may become the optimal choice for ZnO nanowires in thermoelectric applications.

### 1. Introduction

Zinc Oxide (ZnO) nanowires (NWs) have been attracting much attention due to its wide applications as energy generator and sen- sors. Through coupling piezoelectricity with semiconductor, ZnO nanowires arrays can convert mechanical energy into electrical en- ergy [1-4]. ZnO nanowires are also considered as very potential optoelectronic device [5-7]. Thermoelectric effect, which is able to convert waste heat into renewable electricity, is also an efficient way to solve the energy crisis. In order to evaluate the conversion efficiency of a thermoelectric material, we can use the dimen- sionless figure of merit $\text{ZT} (= S^2 \sigma T / \kappa)$, where $S$ is the Seebeck coefficient, $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity and $T$ is the abso- lute temperature. $\kappa = \kappa_e + \kappa_{ph}$ is the total thermal conductivity, where $\kappa_e$ is the thermal conductivity from electrons and $\kappa_{ph}$ is the thermal conductivity from phonons. However, it is not easy to in- crease the value of ZT, because the electronic-related thermoelec- tric quantities, that are $S$, $\sigma$ and $\kappa_e$, are interdependent. There are a lot of strategies to improve the value of ZT, which mainly focus on the reduction of $\kappa_{ph}$ arsing from phonon scatterings and keep- ing the electrical properties almost unchanged. ZnO nanowires are potential thermoelectric materials because of their high stability, large Seebeck coefficient and high electron mobility [8-10]. How- ever, the limit in the thermoelectric application for ZnO nanowires comes from its high thermal conductivity ($\kappa > 40$ W/m-K). The figure of merit ZT for pure bulk ZnO at room temperature is about $1.4 \times 10^{-4}$, which is extremely low and can almost be neglected [11]. Recently, some research groups have reported that the ther- moelectric performance in ZnO nanowires could be enhanced by doping Al, Ga, Sb atoms, etc. [12,13], since that the electrical con- ductivity of doped ZnO nanowires can be increased, while the thermal conductivity decreases because of the enhanced phonon- impurity scatterings. However, in addition to doping method, it is reported that a novel phase wurtzite (W-phase) can be transited into a graphitic polymorph (H-phase) in ZnO nanowires under ten- sile compression. Moreover, the electrical and thermal properties of ZnO nanowires are determined by this phase transfor- mation [14-17]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the effect of phase transformation on the thermoelectric properties of ZnO nanowires is still unknown.

In this work, we will investigate the thermoelectric properties of ZnO nanowires by combining first-principles calculation with the one-dimensional (1D) Boltzmann Transport Equation (BTE) [11, 18,19]. In particular, the impacts of phase transformation on the thermoelectric performance of ZnO nanowires are also explored.

The electronic band gap versus four wires in both W phase and H phase is shown in Fig. 2. Although the band gap of wires is underestimated by the DFT calculations, it is always able to predict the trends. Because of quantum confinement effects, the electronic band gaps for nanowires decrease when the wires become thicker [29]. For W phase, the band gaps energies for wire A, B, C, D are 1.99, 1.91, 1.82, 1.43 eV, respectively. However, wire A, B, C, D in H phase with gap energies are 1.63, 1.45, 1.49, 1.4 eV, respectively. Fig. 3(a) shows the band structures for wire D in W phase and H phase. The similar results can be obtained in other wires. Direct band gaps are found in all wires and the band gap of wire D in W phase is larger than that in H phase. Combined with one-dimensional (1D) Boltzmann Transport Equation (BTE), all the electron-related thermoelectric quantities including the electrical conductivity $\sigma$, electronic thermal conductivity $\kappa_{e}$ and Seebeck coefficient $S$, will be calculated from their electronic band structures [12,18,19].

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma & =\Lambda^{(0)} \\
\kappa_{e} & =\frac{1}{e^{2} T}\left[\Lambda^{(2)}-\Lambda^{(1)}\left(\Lambda^{(0)}\right)^{-1} \Lambda^{(1)}\right] \\
S & =\frac{1}{e T}\left(\Lambda^{(0)}\right)^{-1} \Lambda^{(1)} \\
A^{(n)} & =e^{2} \tau \frac{2}{m^{*}} \sum_{E_{k}} \Delta E\left[\frac{\beta \exp \left(\beta\left(E_{k}-\mu\right)\right)}{\left(1+\exp \left(\beta\left(E_{k}-\mu\right)\right)\right)^{2}}\right] \\
& \quad \times D\left(E_{k}\right) E_{k}\left(E_{k}-\mu\right)^{n}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $e$ is the charge of electrons, $T$ is the absolute temperature, $\tau$ is the electron relaxation time, $m^{*}$ is the effective mass of the electrons, $\Delta E$ is the energy interval; $E_{k}$ is the electron energy, $\mu$ is the electron chemical potential and $D(E_{k})$ is the density of states. $\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B} T}$, in which $k_{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant. In the calculation of thermoelectric properties, the relaxation time $\tau$ is an important quantity, which is related to the carrier concentration, operating temperature and atomic structure. Here, for simplicity, we only consider the effect of carrier concentration on the relaxation time in our calculation. In this work, we fit the calculated mobility to the measured electrical conductivity data of ZnO nanowires to obtain the value of $\tau$. However, there is no experimental data for ZnO nanowires with diameter from 1.4-1.75 nm and therefore we use the experimental data of ZnO nanowires, which are doped by Al element, with the diameter of 20-80 nm [31] $(\sigma=384.6~(\Omega \mathrm{cm})^{-1}, n=8.8 \times 10^{19} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$, $T=300 \mathrm{~K})$, and we can obtain $\tau=7.47 \times 10^{-15} \mathrm{~s}$. Then, we apply the dependence relation between the mobility and carrier concentration in ZnO thin film [32] to obtain the carrier concentration dependence of the relaxation time. For instance, for wire D in H phase, for $n=2.2 \times 10^{18}~(\mathrm{cm}^{-3})$, $\tau=1.07 \times 10^{-14}~\mathrm{s}$; for $n=6.5 \times 10^{18}~(\mathrm{cm}^{-3})$, $\tau=8.74 \times 10^{-15}~\mathrm{s}$. Then, the carrier concentration $(n)$ is defined as: $n=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} D(E-\mu) f(E-\mu) d E$, where $f(E)$ is the Fermi distribution function; The effect of doping is simulated by rigid-band approximation [33], which we assume that under a light doping, the shape of band structure does not change and the position of the Fermi level up or down is shifted for $n$-type or $p$-type systems, respectively.

The electrical conductivity $(\sigma)$ versus carrier concentration for wire D in both W phase and H phase in Fig. 3(b). The trends are similar in other wires. Here, for simplicity and convenience, we only consider $n$-type ZnO nanowires, since that in experiment, it is easy to obtain $n$-type ZnO nanowires by doping Al, Ga atoms. The electrical conductivity $\sigma$ increases when the carrier concentration increases [12,18,19,34]. In addition to carrier concentration, $\sigma$ also depends on this phase transformation. $\sigma$ for H phase is higher than that for W phase at the same carrier concentration. We can explain this phenomenon as follows: $\sigma$ is determined by the effective mass $(m^{*})$ of electrons in our work. $m^{*}$ can be computed by this equation: $m^{*}=\hbar^{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2} E}{\partial k_{z}^{2}}\right)^{-1}$ [12,18,19]. Here, we can calculate the value of $m^{*}$ of ZnO nanowires in W-phase and H-phase along the growth direction by using the lowest conduction band at Gamma point. We can give a detailed explanation from the shape of electronic band structures for these two wires. It is obvious that due to strain effect, the dispersion of the lowest conduction band for ZnO nanowires in H phase is much larger than that in W phase (see Fig. 3(a)), therefore, the effective mass of ZnO nanowires in H phase is lower than that of W phase, which could result in higher $\sigma$ of ZnO nanowires. Fig. 3(c) shows Seebeck coefficient $S$ versus carrier concentration for wire D. We can obtain relatively high value of $S$ from $200~\mu \mathrm{V} / \mathrm{K}$ to $600~\mu \mathrm{V} / \mathrm{K}$ by controlling carrier concentration. However, we can observe the opposite trend between Seebeck coefficient and the electrical conductivity. With the carrier concentration increasing, $S$ monotonically decreases in

both W phase and H phase. In addition to carrier concentration,
S is dependent on the electronic band gap [12,18,19,34]. For sim-
plicity, it is concluded that S is approximately proportion to the
electronic band gap. Therefore, we can see that ZnO nanowires in
W phase have a larger value of Seebeck coefficient at the same
carrier concentration, which is attributed to the larger band gap of
ZnO nanowires in W-phase. For example, for $n=1×10^{18}$ (cm⁻³),
S for W phase is 202 µV/K, while S for H phase is 180 µV/K.

In thermoelectric application, the power factor $(P=S^{2}\sigma)$ plays
an important role to determine the thermoelectric properties.
Fig. 3(d) shows the power factor versus carrier concentration for
wire D in both W phase and H phase. For each phase, there is al-
ways an optimal carrier concentration to achieve the largest value
of P. Moreover, among most range of carrier concentration, the
larger value of P is obtained for H phase compared with that for
W-phase. The maximum value of P ($P_{max}$) for ZnO nanowires in H
phase ($P_{max}=180.2$ µW/m-K²) when $n_{e}=6.5×10^{18}$ cm⁻³ and
$P_{max}=133.4$ µW/m-K² in W phase when $n_{e}=7.1×10^{18}$ cm⁻³.
We explain the physical reason for this phenomenon as follows:
the higher electrical conductivity of ZnO nanowires in H-phase due
to lower effective mass can offset their lower Seebeck coefficient.

The figure of merit ZT, is an important factor to determine the
thermoelectric conversion efficiency. In the calculation of ZT, the
total thermal conductivity contains both the electron and phonon
contribution. The electron thermal conductivity $\kappa_{e}$ can be calcu-
lated from Eq. (1). For wire D in W phase, $\kappa_{e}=0.062$ (W/m-K), for
$n=7.1×10^{18}$ (cm⁻³); while for the same wire in H phase, $\kappa_{e}=$
0.094 (W/m-K), for $n=6.5×10^{18}$ (cm⁻³). Therefore, it is found
that the thermal conductivity from electrons is much smaller than
the phonon thermal conductivity, which means that it could be
neglected. Thus, as the carrier concentration increases, the thermal
conductivity is nearly constant. In Ref. [17], A.J. Kulkarni and his
coworkers have obtained that the phonon thermal conductivity for
ZnO nanowires in H-phase with diameter about 1.9 nm is around
10.1 W/m-K, which is 20% higher than the same wire in W-phase
(8.3 W/m-K). Here, the diameter of wire D considered in this pa-
per is about from 1.4 nm to 1.75 nm, and therefore, we can use
their data of phonon thermal conductivity instead. Combined the
calculations of the power factor and the phonon thermal conduc-
tivity from Ref. [17], we obtain the thermoelectric figure of merit
ZT for wire D in both W phase and H phase. Fig. 4 shows the
relative value of ZT for wire D in both H and W phases versus car-
rier concentration. The achieved value of ZT for wire D in H phase
is higher than that in W-phase when $n_{e}\leq1.5×10^{19}$ cm⁻³. The
maximum electron concentration obtained with doping method
reaches about in order of $10^{20}$ (cm⁻³) [35]. Furthermore, the max-
imum value of ZT obtained for ZnO nanowires in H phase is about
1.1 times larger than that in W phase when the electron carrier
concentration is $n_{e}=6.5×10^{18}$ cm⁻³.

At last, we also discuss the temperature-dependent behavior of ZT, which is important for practical applications. The relative value of the maximum ZT for H phase and W phase (Max(ZT − H)/Max(ZT − W)) versus temperature is shown in Fig. 6. We can see that this relative value firstly decreases and then in- creases with the temperature increasing. The maxima of (Max(ZT − H)/Max(ZT − W)), which is about 1.18, is achieved at $T=1000$ K. ## 4. Conclusion
In summary, we have studied the influence of phase transfor- mation on the thermoelectric properties of ZnO nanowires. Our results show that the thermoelectric performance is strongly de- pendent on the phase transformation. We found that the largest value of ZT at room temperature is achieved in ZnO wires in hexagonal (H) phase, which is 1.1 times larger than that in wurtzite phase. We also investigated the temperature dependence of the thermoelectric properties of ZnO nanowires for both H phase and W phase. The maxima of Max(ZT − H)/Max(ZT − W) can reach 1.18 at $T=1000$ K, which is 7% increment comparing with that at room temperature. We also find that the ZnO nanowires in hexagonal phase may become an optimal choice for thermoelec- tric performance, and may provide a possible approach to tune the thermoelectric properties of ZnO nanowires.
