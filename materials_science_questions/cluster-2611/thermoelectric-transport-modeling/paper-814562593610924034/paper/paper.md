# Effects of doping and planar defects on the thermoelectric properties of InAs nanowires†

Seong Gi Jeon,ⁿᵇ Dong Woo Park,ᵇᶜ Ho Sun Shin,ᵇ Hyun Min Park,ᵇ Si Young Choi,ᵈ Sang Jun Lee,*^ᵇ Jin Yu*^ᵃ and Jae Yong Song*^ᵇ*

Undoped InAs and Si-doped InAs nanowires with stacking faults and twins were synthesized by catalyst-free molecular beam epitaxy and their thermoelectric enhancements due to planar defects were experimentally and theoretically demonstrated. The Seebeck coefficients, electrical resistivities, and thermal conductivities of the Si-doped and undoped InAs nanowires were measured using a micro-fabricated thermoelectric measurement platform over the temperature range of 50 to 300 K. The Si-doping increased electrical conductivity from $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ to $7.8 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{S}\ \text{m}^{-1}$, due to the increase in carrier concentration from $2 \times 10^{17}$ to $8 \times 10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, and then decreased the thermopower from $-216$ to $-81\ \mu\text{V}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ at 300 K, in agreement with the two-band model based on the Boltzman transport theory. Phonon scattering, caused by planar defects such as surfaces, twins, and stacking-fault boundaries, suppressed the lattice thermal conductivity below $3\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ following the Callaway model. The planar defect-induced phonon scattering as well as the optimization of carrier concentration is very effective at enhancing the thermoelectric properties of InAs nanowires and is expected to be utilized for improving the thermoelectric properties of other thermoelectric materials.

## Introduction

Thermoelectric (TE) materials have attracted increasing interest for use in environmentally-friendly and sustainable energy conversion, based on their ability to directly convert heat into electricity, and *vice versa*. Following the prediction that the dimensionless figure-of-merit $ZT$ ($ZT = S^{2}\sigma T/\lambda$, $S$: Seebeck coefficient, $\sigma$: electrical conductivity, $\lambda$: thermal conductivity, and $T$: operating temperature) could be dramatically enhanced by quantum confinement and size effects,¹ many researchers have attempted to enhance the $ZT$ using nanoengineered materials, such as superlattices,²·³ nanowires (NWs),⁴·⁵ nanotubes,⁶ and nanostructured bulk materials.⁷ Unfortunately, the $ZT$ enhancement has been limited to below approximately 2.6,⁸ because $S$, $\sigma$, and $\lambda$ are strongly coupled to each other through carrier concentration.⁹ Recently, in order to separately control the transport of phonons and electrons, internal defects such as nanograins, nanoprecipitates, and twins have been utilized for $ZT$ enhancements based on phonon scattering at the internal boundaries.¹⁰⁻¹²

InAs NWs are known to be promising candidates for use as high performance single-NW transistors due to their high carrier mobility.¹³·¹⁴ Therefore, its transport phenomena and parameters have been well investigated. Recently, it was reported that InAs NWs have an excellent TE property, *i.e.*, the Seebeck coefficient increases dramatically up to several $\text{mV}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ when the Fermi level is controlled with an external electric field.¹⁵·¹⁶ Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, the effect of InAs NW carrier concentration on TE properties ($S$, $\sigma$, and $\lambda$) has not yet been reported. InAs NWs with stacking faults between wurtzite (WZ) and zinc blende (ZB) phases are known to have lower thermal conductivities ($2$ to $3\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$)¹⁷·¹⁸ than that ($30\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) of bulk InAs,¹⁹ which was believed to be due to the phonon scattering at surfaces of InAs NWs. However, the thermal conductivities of InAs NWs with stacking faults were still lower than those ($5$ and $9\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) of pure WZ and ZB InAs NWs at 300 K.²⁰ Therefore, the stacking faults in InAs NWs might play a role of lowering the thermal conductivity, like the twins in $\text{Bi}_{2}\text{Te}_{3}$ NWs.¹²

In this study, we synthesized undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs with stacking faults and twins, in order to investigate the effects of carrier concentration and planar defects on the TE properties with temperature, because Si is well-known as an n-type dopant for InAs. The carrier concentration and mobility determined by gate-effected transport measurement were correlated with the $ZT$s of both the doped and undoped InAs

## Experimental
### Synthesis of InAs nanowires
Undoped and Si-doped InAs single crystalline NWs were grown on Si(111) substrates *via* catalyst-free MBE (Molecular beam epitaxy) deposition. The growth rate of InAs NWs was 0.1 nm s⁻¹ at 703 K. The indium was supplied at a beam-equivalent pressure of $3 \times 10^{-8}$ torr, and V/III ratio was set to be 400 by varying arsenic flux. Si-doped InAs NWs were grown by simultaneously supplying Si flux with the nominal concentration of $1 \times 10^{19}$ cm⁻³ at two-dimensional growth. The diameters of undoped and Si-doped NWs were approximately $114 \pm 11$ nm and $97 \pm 8$ nm, respectively. The lengths of both types of InAs NWs were measured to be approximately 10 μm.

### Microstructure analyses
The morphology, microstructure, and crystal structure of the InAs NWs were investigated using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi, S4800), an aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (CS-STEM, JEOL ARM200F). For TEM analyses, the NWs on Si substrates were sonicated in ethanol solution and dispersed in the solution. Then, the NWs-containing solution was dropped onto a lacey-type TEM grid.

### Measurement of thermoelectric properties
The $S$ and $\sigma$ of NWs were measured using a membrane-type microfabricated thermoelectric measurement platform (MTMP) (see Fig. S1(a), ESI†), while the $k$ of NWs were evaluated using a bridge-type MTMP shown in Fig. 1(a). A micromanipulator based on the optical microscope was employed to transfer the InAs NWs onto the MTMPs.¹² To remove the native oxide at the NW surface, the NWs were dipped into 0.23 M HF aqueous solution for 15 seconds prior to metallization deposition of 15 nm-thick Ni and 100 nm-thick Al layers as ohmic contacts of electrodes.

Electrical conductivities were measured by a four-point probe method. The linear $I$–$V$ curves of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs indicate the ohmic behavior over the wide temperature range (see Fig. S1(b), ESI†). The electrical conductivities and Seebeck coefficients were measured in the temperature range of 50 K to 300 K at steps of 50 K. In the same temperature range, the thermal conductivities were evaluated under a steady-state of heat flow. First, the thermal conductance of the MTMP including NWs (Fig. 1(b)) was measured and then we measured the thermal conductance of the MTMP without the NWs (Fig. 1(c)). Consequently, the difference between the two thermal conductances established the thermal conductance of the NWs, which was converted into thermal conductivity according to calculation with the cross-sectional area and length of the NWs. It is noted that thermal conductivity measurements were performed for three InAs NWs in order to increase the measurement signal-to-noise of the thermal conductance, as shown in Fig. 1(a). All measurements were carried out in a dark chamber under a high vacuum of <10⁻⁶ torr. A more detailed report on the procedures of sample preparation and TE measurements can be found in the previous work.¹²

## Results and discussions

### Microstructure of InAs nanowires

The undoped InAs NWs, which were composed of zinc blende (ZB) and würtzite (WZ) layered structures, grew along the [0001] WZ-longitudinal direction, as shown in Fig. 1(d) and (e). In Fig. 1(e), the atomic columns of In and As can be distinguished as In atoms (brighter column) and As atoms (darker column) by Z-contrast of atomic number difference. It is noted that the NW included planar defects, such as stacking faults between ZB and WZ layers (indicated by yellow dotted lines) and twins (indicated by red arrows), normal to the [0001] WZ-growth direction. This finding was consistent with a previous study, where planar defects were found in InAs NWs grown by MOCVD. $^{22}$ The Si-doped InAs NWs had very similar planar defects to that of the undoped InAs NWs, *i.e.*, layered stacking faults and twins normal to the [0001] WZ-growth direction (see Fig. S2, ESI$\dagger$). It is also noted that the undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs exhibited a smooth surface (see Fig. 1(d) and S2, ESI$\dagger$) unlike a previous report of surface morphology changing with Si content. $^{23}$ This might be attributed to the very small amount of Si atoms substituting In atoms in the InAs crystal structure. $^{24}$

### Measured Seebeck coefficients and electrical conductivities

Fig. 2(a) shows the variations in electrical conductivities and Seebeck coefficients of both NWs with temperature. The electrical conductivity of undoped InAs NW was $1.0 \times 10^{4}$ S m$^{-1}$ at 300 K and slightly decreased to $0.8 \times 10^{4}$ S m$^{-1}$ with a temperature decrease to 50 K, reflecting a semiconducting behaviour. On the other hand, Si-doped InAs NWs showed electrical conductivity of $7.6 \times 10^{4}$ S m$^{-1}$ at 300 K and increased up to $7.8 \times 10^{4}$ S m$^{-1}$ at 50 K with a decrease in temperature, indicating a typical metallic behaviour. The Si-doping is presumed to significantly increase the carrier concentration in InAs NWs.

As shown in Fig. 2(a), the Seebeck coefficients of each InAs NW had negative sign, indicating an n-type semiconductor. At 300 K, the Seebeck coefficients were $-216$ $\mu$V K$^{-1}$ for the undoped InAs NW, and $-81$ $\mu$V K$^{-1}$ for the Si-doped InAs NW, respectively. The Seebeck coefficients decreased with a decrease in temperature, indicating a diffusive thermopower. The thermopower factor, $S^{2}\sigma$, was calculated to be $4.9 \times 10^{-4}$ W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ for the Si-doped NWs while that of undoped InAs was $4.5 \times 10^{-4}$ W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$ at 300 K. As the Si-doped InAs NW showed a metallic behaviour as a degenerate semiconductor, its carrier concentration was derived from the slopes of Seebeck coefficient ($S$) vs. temperature ($T$) shown in Fig. 2(a), according to the Mott equation, eqn (1),$^{25,26}$

$$
\frac{S}{T}=-\left.\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\text{B}}^{2}}{3|q|}\frac{\partial\ln\sigma(E)}{(\partial E)}\right|_{E_{\text{F}}}=-\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\text{B}}^{2}m_{\text{e}}^{*}}{\left(3\pi^{2}\right)^{2/3}\hbar^{2}|q|}\frac{1}{\left(n^{2/3}\right)} \quad (1)
$$

where $k_{\text{B}}$, $q$, $h$, $\sigma(E)$, $E_{\text{F}}$, $m_{\text{e}}^{*}$, and $n$ denote the Boltzmann constant, electric charge, Plank constant, electrical conductivity, Fermi energy, the effective mass of electrons, and carrier concentration, respectively. The carrier concentration was estimated to be $8.38 \times 10^{17}$ cm$^{-3}$ for the electron effective mass ($0.023m_{0}$) of InAs. $m_{0}$ denotes free electron rest mass.

### Field effect properties of InAs nanowires

Fig. 2(b) shows the variations of source-drain current ($I_{\text{DS}}$) with gating voltage ($V_{\text{G}}$) for a single NW FET device at 300 K, as shown in the inset of the SEM image. The field-effect carrier mobilities ($\mu_{\text{FET}}$) were calculated from the measured transconductance ($g_{\text{m}}$), according to eqn (2),$^{27}$

$$
\mu_{\text{FET}}=\frac{L_{\text{Ch}}^{2}}{CV_{\text{DS}}}g_{\text{m}} \quad (2)
$$

where $L_{\text{Ch}}$, $C$, and $V_{\text{DS}}$ denote channel length, capacitance of the dielectric SiO$_2$ layer, and source-drain voltage, respectively. The

gating voltage $(V_{\mathrm{G}})$ was swept from $-10 \mathrm{~V}$ to $+10 \mathrm{~V}$ at a fixed $V_{\mathrm{DS}}$ of $10 \mathrm{mV}$. The $\mu_{\mathrm{FET}}$ was determined from the linear slope of the $I_{\mathrm{DS}}-V_{\mathrm{G}}$ curve shown in Fig. 2(b), according to eqn (2). The $\mu_{\mathrm{FET}}$ was found to be $1920 \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ for the undoped InAs NW and $1510 \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ for the Si-doped InAs NW at $300 \mathrm{~K}$, respectively.

The carrier concentration of undoped InAs NW was evaluated using eqn (3), $^{27}$

$$
n=\frac{V_{\mathrm{th}} C}{q \pi\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{2} L_{\mathrm{Ch}}}
\tag{3}
$$

here, $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ is a threshold voltage of the $I_{\mathrm{DS}}-V_{\mathrm{G}}$ curve and $d$ denotes NW diameter. The calculated carrier concentration of undoped InAs NW (non-degenerate semiconductor) was $2.6 \times 10^{17} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ ($V_{\text {th }}=-5.8 \mathrm{~V}$ shown in Fig. 2(b)).

### Calculated Seebeck coefficients

The highly increased carrier concentration in Si-doped InAs NW is presumed to lead to the higher electrical conductivity and the $20 \%$-reduction of $\mu_{\mathrm{FET}}$ due to carrier scattering. And the Si doped InAs NWs had lower Seebeck coefficient with higher carrier concentration, as shown in Fig. 2(a), in agreement with the Pisarenko relation. The Si-doping effects on the Seebeck coefficient of InAs NWs were analysed by the Boltzmann transport equation. We used a bipolar transport theory in relaxation time approximation, which assumes bipolar conduction by electrons and holes in parabolic bands, as follows, $^{28}$

$$
\sigma=\sum_{i=\mathrm{e}, \mathrm{p}}\left[n_{i} q \mu_{i}\right]
\tag{4}
$$

$$
n_{i}=\frac{4 \pi}{h^{3}}\left(2 m_{i}^{*}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}} F_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\eta_{i}\right)
\tag{5}
$$

$$
F_{j}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{j} \mathrm{~d} x}{\mathrm{e}^{x-\eta}+1}
\tag{6}
$$

here, $n_{i}$, and $\mu_{i}$ are the carrier concentration and carrier mobility, respectively. The e and p denote electron and hole, respectively. The carrier concentration $\left(n_{i}\right)$ is expressed by eqn (5). Here, $h$ and $\eta_{i}$ are the Plank constant and reduced Fermi energy $(\eta_{\mathrm{e}}=E_{\mathrm{F}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{p}}=-(E_{\mathrm{F}}+E_{\mathrm{g}})/k_{\mathrm{B}}T)$, respectively, where $E_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the band gap. $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ is defined zero at conduction band minimum. $F_{j}(\eta_{i})$ is the Fermi-Dirac integral of the order, $j$, defined as eqn (6).

The Seebeck coefficient $(S_{i})$ can be written by eqn (7),

$$
S_{i}=-\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{e}}\left(\frac{\left(r+\frac{5}{2}\right) F_{\left(r+\frac{3}{2}\right)}\left(\eta_{i}\right)}{\left(r+\frac{3}{2}\right) F_{\left(r+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(\eta_{i}\right)}-\eta_{i}\right)
\tag{7}
$$

where $r$ is taken to be $-1 / 2$. Each parameter of intrinsic bulk InAs was used as $E_{\mathrm{F}}=0.354 \mathrm{eV}, m_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}=0.023 m_{0}, m_{\mathrm{p}}^{*}=0.41 m_{0}, \mu_{\mathrm{e}}=$ $4 \times 10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$, and $\mu_{\mathrm{p}}=500 \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ for calculations. $^{29,30}$

In Fig. 3(a), the calculated Seebeck coefficients are plotted as a function of electrical conductivities at $300 \mathrm{~K}$ (dotted line), following the two-band model expressed by eqn (4)-(7). The experimental data of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs in this study are shown together with the literature data in Fig. 3. In the intrinsic region $(\sigma<10^{4} \mathrm{~S} \mathrm{~m}^{-1})$, it is expected that a small $\sigma$-variation causes large change of Seebeck coefficient. This is in agreement with the literature data of InAs NWs inside the calculated result (dotted line).

The Seebeck coefficients of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs seem to match with the calculated result in the highly degenerate regime (blue open circle) as well as the less degenerate regime (red open square). Based on eqn (4) and (7), the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity of InAs NWs are plotted as a function of Fermi energies, as shown in Fig. 3(b). The Fermi energies corresponded to be $-5 \mathrm{meV}$ and $80 \mathrm{meV}$ for the measured Seebeck coefficients $(-216$ and -81 $\mu \mathrm{V} \mathrm{K}^{-1}$) of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs, respectively. And the electrical conductivities were estimated to be $3.5 \times 10^{4}$ and $2.6 \times 10^{5} \mathrm{~S} \mathrm{~m}^{-1}$ at each Fermi energy of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs, respectively, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3(b). The estimated electrical conductivities, which were calculated for the intrinsic bulk InAs, exhibited higher values than those $(1.0 \times 10^{4}$ and $7.6 \times 10^{4} \mathrm{~S} \mathrm{~m}^{-1}$) of the measured electrical conductivities. As the reasons, this might be due to suppression of carrier mobility caused by boundary scattering of the NWs. $^{28,31}$

### Measured thermal conductivities

As shown in Fig. 4(a), the total thermal conductivities ($k_{\text{Total}}$) of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs varied slightly in the range of 2.6 to $3.2\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ with temperature. The measured $k_{\text{Total}}$'s were comparable to the previous report of 2 to $3\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for the CVD-grown InAs NWs containing planar defects, $^{17,18}$ and $4\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for InAs nanoribbons. $^{32}$ The $k_{\text{Total}}$ is composed of electronic thermal conductivity ($k_{\text{Electro}}$) and lattice thermal conductivity ($k_{\text{Lattice}}$). The $k_{\text{Electro}}$ was calculated from the Wiedemann-Franz law, as shown in Fig. 4(a). The Lorentz number of $2.45 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W}\ \Omega\ \text{K}^{-2}$ was used. Although the Lorentz number depends upon the composition and degeneracy, $^{7}$ it was presumed to be still valid for the rough estimation of the electronic contribution to thermal conductivity. As expected, the $k_{\text{Electro}}$ of Si-doped InAs NW was much higher than that of undoped InAs NW due to higher carrier concentration. By substracting $k_{\text{Electro}}$ from $k_{\text{Total}}$, the $k_{\text{Lattice}}$'s for the Si-doped and undoped NWs were plotted in Fig. 4(a). It is well-known that structural defects cause phonon scattering and reduce lattice thermal conductivity. $^{12,33}$ Considering the planar defects of twins and stacking faults shown in Fig. 1(e), lattice thermal conductivity is expected to be lowered by phonon scattering at the planar defects.

### Calculated thermal conductivities

The effects of planar defects on lattice thermal conductivity were analysed by the Callaway model, *i.e.*, eqn (8), as follows, $^{34}$

$$
k_{\mathrm{L}}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}}{2 \pi^{3} \nu}\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{\hbar}\right)^{3} \int_{0}^{\theta_{\mathrm{D}} / T} \frac{x^{4} \mathrm{e}^{x}}{\tau^{-1}\left(\mathrm{e}^{x}-1\right)^{2}} \mathrm{~d} x
\tag{8}
$$

here, $\nu$, $\theta_{\text{D}}$, and $\tau$ denote sound velocity, Debye temperature, and phonon relaxation time, respectively. If each scattering process is independent of each other, $\tau^{-1}$ can be expressed by eqn (9), as follows,

$$
\tau^{-1}=\tau_{\mathrm{U}}{ }^{-1}+\tau_{\mathrm{b}}{ }^{-1}+\tau_{\mathrm{i}}{ }^{-1}=B \mathrm{e}^{-b / T} \omega^{2} T+\frac{\nu}{F L}+A \omega^{4}
\tag{9}
$$

here, $\tau_{\text{U}}$, $\tau_{\text{b}}$, $\tau_{\text{i}}$, and $\omega$ denote phonon-phonon umklapp scattering, boundary scattering, and impurity scattering, and phonon frequency $((x k_{\text{B}} T)/h)$, respectively. $L$ and $F$ denote the NW thickness and the boundary scattering parameter at the NW surface. $^{35}$ Each parameter was used, such as $\theta_{\text{D}} = 114.57\ \text{K}$, $B = 6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{s}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, $b = 64\ \text{K}$, $A = 0.8 \times 10^{-44}\ \text{s}^{3}$, $\nu = 2540\ \text{m}\ \text{s}^{-1}$, and $F = 1$. $^{35,36}$

Fig. 4 (a) Variations of thermal conductivities of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs with temperature. (b) The lattice thermal conductivity with temperature simulated by Callaway model, for InAs NWs with planar defects and without planar defects.

The calculated and measured lattice thermal conductivities are shown as a function of temperature in Fig. 4(b). The thermal conductivity $(7.6\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1})$ calculated for InAs NW with 110 nm thickness at 300 K existed between that $(5\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1})$ of WZ and that $(9\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1})$ of ZB NWs, $^{20}$ but was still larger than the measured thermal conductivities (approximately $3\ \text{W}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$), as shown by the solid line in Fig. 4(b). Notably, the dashed line representing the thermal conductivities calculated for the $L$ of 28 nm agreed well with the experimental results. That implies that additional phonon scattering mechanisms, as well as the surface scattering, were involved in lowering the lattice thermal conductivity. It is presumed that the twins in the ZB phase and stacking faults between the ZB and WZ phases caused more phonon scattering. $^{37}$ Previously, it was reported that twins (number density of $0.1\ \text{nm}^{-1}$) aligned in the longitudinal direction effectively lowered the lattice thermal conductivity of $\text{B}_{2}\text{Te}_{3}$ NW by 30%, approximately. $^{12}$ The planar defects aligned in a vertical direction to the phonon propagation would lower the lattice thermal conductivity more effectively than those aligned in the direction of phonon propagation. Therefore, 110 nm-thick InAs NW exhibited the lattice thermal conductivity that 28 nm-thick NW without planar defects would have. Thus, in present study, the vertical planar defects with higher number density of $0.5\ \text{nm}^{-1}$ lowered the thermal conductivity by 60%, approximately, as shown in Fig. 4(b).

Fig. 4(a) shows that the lattice thermal conductivities of Si-doped InAs NW were slightly lower than those of undoped InAs NW over the entire temperature range, despite similar planar defects. This might be due to more electron-phonon scattering for Si-doped InAs NW with higher carrier concentration.

### Thermoelectric figure-of-merit of InAs nanowires

The $ZT$ values of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs parabolically increased with temperature, as shown in Fig. 5. At 300 K, the average value of $ZT$ of the Si-doped InAs NWs was evaluated to be 0.051 larger than that (0.040) of the undoped InAs NWs. This was due to the highly increased electrical conductivity, despite the lower Seebeck coefficient of the Si-doped InAs NW, following the two-band model shown in Fig. 3(a). It is noted that the $ZT$ (0.051) for the Si-doped InAs NW at 300 K was much

The $ZT$ enhancement in the present study is ascribed to higher thermopower factor as well as low thermal conductivity. The higher thermopower factor might be related to the catalyst-free MBE growth, unlike the gold-catalytic growth of InAs NWs in CVD growth. The gold catalyst might be introduced as an impurity in semiconductors $^{38}$ and significantly affect the transport properties. In the literature, the InAs NWs were reported to include similar planar defects such as stacking faults and twins, however the effects of planar defects on the thermal conductivity of InAs NWs were not investigated. $^{17,18,32}$ Therefore, the lowered thermal conductivity is believed to be due to the phonon scattering at the planar defects as well as surfaces of NWs. This suggests that the WZ-ZB mixed phase in InAs NWs is favourable for the $ZT$ enhancement due to the phonon scattering at the stacking faults.

## Conclusion

We investigated the thermoelectric properties of undoped and Si-doped InAs NWs grown by catalyst-free MBE process. The Seebeck coefficients, electrical conductivities, carrier mobilities, and thermal conductivities were measured, to perform a full $ZT$-characterization using the microfabricated devices. It was experimentally and theoretically observed that the Si-doping in InAs NW did not degrade the high mobility and Seebeck coefficient very much, and rather increased the electrical conductivity significantly, more leading to $ZT$ enhancement. We demonstrated that the vertically-aligned planar defects (such as stacking faults and twins) frequently observed in InAs NWs can effectively lower the lattice thermal conductivity and contribute to the $ZT$ enhancement. Such planar defects with a high number density as well as NW surface of with high surface-to-volume ratio are expected to be used for $ZT$ enhancement of other thermoelectric materials.
