## ABSTRACT

We report on measurements of the thermoelectric power of $NiMn_2O_4$ spinels over the temperature range from $100\ ^\circ$C up to $860\ ^\circ$C. The thermopower (Seebeck coefficient $S$) of samples with different thermal history (slow cooled or quenched from different temperatures) was measured upon heating up to the upper temperature of the spinel stability field in air and upon subsequent cooling in air. All samples exhibit a negative Seebeck coefficient at $T < 680\ ^\circ$C, and a cross-over to p-type behavior is observed at higher temperature. Thermopower data were evaluated using the modified Heikes equation considering spin and orbital degeneracy, and the concentrations of $Mn^{3+}$ and $Mn^{4+}$ on octahedral position (B-site) were calculated. A model of the temperature-dependent cation distribution of $NiMn_2O_4$ was derived. The results are discussed in terms of cation redistribution kinetics and electron configurations of Mn ions.

### 1. Introduction

$NiMn_2O_4$ and substituted oxides represent the dominating materials family for NTC-thermistors used for temperature sensors and inrush current limiters [1]. The manganite spinels exhibit semi-conducting charge transport behavior based on a hopping-type motion of charge carriers with a thermally activated mobility. Due to high demands regarding the reliability of temperature sensors, a mayor challenge for the development of a new generation of these passive components is the reduction of the resistance drift, commonly associated with this type of NTC-thermistors upon operation. In order to achieve better long-term stability of the resistivity of NTC-type oxides, a fundamental understanding of the aging phenomena is required. However, a generally accepted theory on the origin and mechanisms of aging of NTC spinel ceramics is still lacking. Possible reasons include changes in the bulk chemistry of thermistors, i.e. non-equilibrium cation distribution, or partial oxidation and formation of cation-deficient defect spinels upon cooling [2-4].

$NiMn_2O_4$crystallizes in the cubic spinel structure (general formula $AB_2O_4$) with space group $Fd-3m$ (No. 227). In this structure type, oxygen ions form a cubic close packing and cations reside on tetrahedral $8a$ sites (A-sites) and octahedral $16d$ positions (B-sites). The position of oxygen anions on $32e$ is characterized by the oxygen u-parameter. The unit cell contains eight formula units with half of the octahedral interstices filled with B-cations, whereas one eight of the tetrahedral positions are filled with A-cations [5]. The cation distribution in the spinel structure of $NiMn_2O_4$ is dominated by the large octahedral preference of $Ni^{2+}$ ions and the disproportionation reaction of $2\ Mn^{3+}$ into $Mn^{2+}$ and $Mn^{4+}$. Many experimental studies were performed to elucidate the cation distribution of nickel manganite with the early period dating back to the 1950's. Based on XRD experiments and magnetic measurements, Baltzer [6] proposed a simple inverse cation distribution $Mn^{2+}[Ni^{2+}Mn^{4+}]O_4$ (brackets denote cations on octahedral B-sites). Boucher et al. concluded from neutron diffraction experiments that a partially inverse distribution $Ni^{2+}_{1-\nu}Mn^{3+}_{\nu}[Ni^{2+}_{\nu}Mn^{3+}_{2-\nu}]O_4$ exists with a temperature dependent degree of inversion, e.g. with $\nu=0.74$ for quenched and $\nu=0.93$ for slow cooled samples, respectively [7]. Many other studies using different experimental techniques have shown that the cation inversion is coupled with a disproportionation of manganese [8-12], hence the cation distribution of $NiMn_2O_4$ can be described by the general formula:

$$
\left(Ni_{1-\nu}^{2+}Mn_{\nu}^{3+}Mn_{p}^{2+}\right)_{A}\left[Ni_{\nu}^{2+}Mn_{2-\nu-p}^{3+}Mn_{p}^{4+}\right]_{B}O_{4} \tag{1}
$$

with the degree of inversion $\nu$ and the disproportionation parameter $p$. Larsen et al. found $\nu=1$ and $p=0.65$ based on a combination of XRD, electrical and magnetic measurements [9]. Macklen determined $\nu=p=0.65$ at $900^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ based on thermogravimetry and electrical conductivity data [10]. Gillot et al. reported a $\nu=0.88$ and $p=0.78$ from neutron diffraction studies [11]. Töpfer et al. reported $\nu=p=0.90$ using photoelectron spectroscopy [12]. According to the phase diagram of the system $\mathrm{NiO}-\mathrm{Mn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ in air, $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ is stable between $730^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $900^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ only [13]. At higher temperatures the spinel decomposes upon formation of $\mathrm{NiO}$ and a Mn-rich spinel, whereas below $700^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ decomposition into $\mathrm{NiMnO}_{3}$ and $\alpha-\mathrm{Mn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ takes place. $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ is metastable at room temperature, and standard cooling rates allow to preserve a single spinel phase down to room temperature. Experimental determination of the cation distribution of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$, however, was performed at room temperature in most cases, with samples quenched from sintering temperature, or cooled down with different cooling rates. However, during cooling, the spinel may change its cation distribution due to temperature-dependent degrees of inversion $\nu$ and disproportionation $p$. Moreover, during cooling the spinel may start to decompose into the ilmenite and bixbyite phases, or, as an initial stage, may form a cation-deficient defect spinel through weak oxidation. Taking this into account, the cation distribution of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ measured at room temperature, critically depends on the thermal history of the samples. In-situ measurements at high temperature, i.e. within the stability range of the compound, are scarce.

Measurements of the thermopower (Seebeck coefficient $S$) have been used to derive information on the cation distribution in spinels, e.g. for V-spinels [14], $\mathrm{Fe}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ [15], or $\mathrm{Mn}_{3} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ [16]. Some reports on the Seebeck coefficient of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ have already been published. Brabers et al. measured negative Seebeck coefficients between $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $600^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and observed a change in activation energy at about $400^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, which they interpreted as the onset of cation redistribution processes [8]. Thermopower measurements on $\mathrm{Ni}_{0.98} \mathrm{Mn}_{2.02} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ confirmed a negative Seebeck coefficient almost independent on temperature below about $400^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, and a stronger temperature dependence between $400^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $550^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ [17]. For a sample sintered at $1000^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, annealed at $800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in air and quenched, an almost temperature independent thermopower of $S=-110 \mu \mathrm{V} / \mathrm{K}$ was measured between $30^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $150^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, and a cation distribution with $\nu=p=0.85$ was derived [18]. Recently, studies on the thermopower of Cu-substituted nickel manganite spinels revealed an decrease of the negative Seebeck coefficient of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ with temperature, and even a change of sign to p-type at about $770^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ [19]. Since the cation distribution in $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ is a function of temperature, the synthesis and cooling conditions of the samples determine their cation distribution at room temperature and hence the magnitude of the Seebeck coefficient $S$. In most studies, the thermal history of the samples is not very clear. Moreover, $S(T)$ has been measured from room temperature up to medium temperatures only (e.g. up to $770^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in Ref. [19]), i.e. in a temperature range, where $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ is metastable and, according to the phase diagram, $\mathrm{NiMnO}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{Mn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ represent the equilibrium phases [13].

In this study, we report on thermopower measurements of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ samples with different thermal history (quenched and slow cooled). We measured the Seebeck coefficient $S$ from $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ up to $860^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, i.e. up to the higher temperature stability limit of this spinel in air. The Seebeck coefficients were used to calculate a temperature-dependent $\left[\mathrm{Mn}_{\mathrm{B}}^{4+}\right] /\left[\mathrm{Mn}_{\mathrm{B}}^{3+}\right]$ ratio, and to derive a model of the temperature-dependent cation distribution of this NTC thermistor material.

## 2. Material and methods

$\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ was prepared using the oxalate precursor route. $\mathrm{Mn}\left(\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{COOC}\right)_{2} * 4 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{NiCO}_{3}$ were dissolved in an acetic acid solution. An oxalic acid solution with a slight excess of oxalate was added to precipitate Ni-Mn-oxalate mixed crystals at $25^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. Thermal decomposition of the oxalates was performed at $350^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for five hours. Pellets with diameter of $10 \mathrm{~mm}$ were fabricated by uniaxial pressing. All samples were sintered at $1150^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for one hour in oxygen atmosphere, and reoxidized for $40 \mathrm{~h}$ at $850^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in air. The samples were subsequently heated in a vertical tube furnace to various temperatures $\left(750^{\circ} \mathrm{C}, 800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}, 850^{\circ} \mathrm{C}\right)$ for $20 \mathrm{~h}$ and finally quenched in water (samples Q750, Q800, Q850). One set of samples was heated to $900^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for $20 \mathrm{~h}$ and slowly cooled with $5 \mathrm{~K} / \mathrm{min}$ (sample SC900).

The phase composition of all samples was studied by X-ray diffraction (XRD, $\mathrm{CuK}_{\alpha}$ radiation) with a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker AXS, Karlsruhe, Germany). The Rietveld refinement was performed using software Topas Version 6 (Bruker AXS).

BET specific surface area (SSA) was determined from nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms measured at liquid nitrogen temperature using a Nova 3200e Quantachrome analyzer. The particle size $d_{B E T}$ was estimated using the relation $d_{B E T}=6 /(\rho \cdot \mathrm{SSA})$ (with density $\rho$, calculated from cubic lattice parameters) assuming spherical particles. The average oxidation state of manganese was determined by dissolving the samples in an excess of $0.1 \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{VO}^{2+}$ solution and titration with $0.1 \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{KMnO}_{4}$ solution. According to the formula $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+} \mathrm{Mn}_{2}^{2+k} \mathrm{O}_{3+k}$ with $\mathrm{k}$ as redox equivalent. Values of $\mathrm{k}=1$ correspond to the formation of a homogeneous spinel phase with an average Mn oxidation state of 3.00, whereas a $\mathrm{k}<1$ or $\mathrm{k}>1$ indicates formation of multi-phase phase assemblies, or defect spinels, respectively. The Seebeck coefficient $S$ was measured between $100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $860^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ during heating and cooling with a rate of $1 \mathrm{~K} / \mathrm{min}$ with a Linseis LSR3 (Linseis Messgeräte GmbH, Selb, Germany) using a multigradient method. Values of the Seebeck coefficient were calculated using linear regression of nine measurements at the same temperature with different temperature gradients, errors are obtained from linear regression $(3 \sigma)$.

## 3. Results

The oxide powder obtained from the thermal decomposition of the oxalate precursor at $350^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ exhibits a large specific area of $250 \mathrm{~m}^{2} /$ g, which corresponds to a particle diameter of about $d_{B E T}=5 \mathrm{~nm}$. XRD has shown that this powder consists of a cubic defect spinel phase as a result of oxalate decomposition [20]. The powder compacts were sintered at $1150^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in oxygen to reach sufficient densification. This temperature is somewhat above the upper stability limit of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$ $\left(1100^{\circ} \mathrm{C}\right.$ in oxygen, or $950^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in air [13]), and the spinel decomposes above that temperature upon formation of $\mathrm{NiO}$ and a Mn-rich spinel according to:

$$
\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4} \rightleftarrows x \mathrm{NiO}+\frac{(3-x)}{3} \mathrm{Ni}_{\frac{(3-3 x)}{(3-x)}} \mathrm{Mn}_{\frac{6}{(3-x)}} \mathrm{O}_{4}+\frac{x}{6} \mathrm{O}_{2} \tag{2}
$$

as already reported in ref. [21]. Next, the samples were annealed at $850^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in air for complete reoxidation and reformation of a single spinel phase. Eventually, a last anneal was performed at temperatures between $750^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $900^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in air, i.e. within the stability range of $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}$, in order to equilibrate the cation distribution at a particular temperature, and finally the samples were quenched or slowly cooled. The bulk samples have densities of $>90 \%$. X-ray diffraction reveals that all samples are single-phase cubic spinels (Fig. 1). The lattice parameters of the quenched samples (Table 1) show a slight reduction with decreasing equilibration temperature, and that of the slowly cooled sample consistently exhibits the smallest lattice parameter of $8.3935 \AA$. This small variation of the cubic lattice parameter seems to reflect the temperature-dependent character of the cation distribution. In addition, measurements of the redox equivalents of the spinel samples by chemical titration result in values of $k=1$ (Table 1) within the experimental error, confirming that the quenched and cooled samples represent single-phase spinels with composition $\mathrm{NiMn}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4.00}$ and an average Mn oxidation state of +3.00. At 100 °C, the Seebeck coefficients of the quenched samples are very similar ($S = -88\ \mu$V/K), whereas the cooled sample (SC900) shows a more negative value of $S = -187\ \mu$V/K. We may note that the values of the Seebeck coefficients of the quenched samples are close to those reported earlier on similar quenched samples, measured in a limited temperature range up to 150 °C only [18]. The absolute values of the Seebeck coefficient of the slow cooled sample decreases continuously upon heating, and change sign at 680 °C. At higher temperatures, the positive values of $S$ continue to grow with lower rate, and seem to saturate at $T > 800$ °C. However, the general trend of the variation of $S(T)$ of all samples during cooling is similar. Moreover, we note that the $S(T)$ characteristics of the slow cooled sample SC900 are reversible during the heating/cooling measurements. This indicates that this sample seems to represent an equilibrium cation distribution, as well in the metastable state (from room temperature up to 730 °C) as well as in the thermodynamically stable state (730–900 °C in air [13]) of $NiMn_2O_4$.

### 4. Discussion

For oxides with charge transport via hopping of small polarons, the Seebeck coefficient in the high-temperature limit is given by the Heikes formula [22].

$$
S = -\frac{k_B}{e} \left[ \frac{S_T}{k} + \ln\left( \frac{1 - c}{c} \right) \right] \tag{4}
$$

![](./images/812510522040647681_2.jpg)

Fig. 2. Seebeck coefficient vs. temperature of quenched and slow cooled samples of $NiMn_2O_4$, measured on heating (A) and cooling (B).

with $c = n/N$, where $N$ denotes the number of available sites and $n$ is the number of charge carriers. The term $S_T/k$ denotes the entropy of transport, which may be neglected in the case of small polaron hopping [15]. If charge carriers are electrons with spin $S = 1/2$, spin degeneracy is to be considered. Chaikin and Beni introduced a factor $\beta$ to take into account the spin degeneracy [23].

$$
S=-\frac{k_{B}}{e}\left[\ln \left(\beta \frac{1-c}{c}\right)\right] \tag{5}
$$

It was noted by Doumerc [24], that for a system with mixed-valent cations, the degeneracy factor $\beta$ should consider the spin degeneracies of all cations included within the charge hopping process and not only that of one electron, and therefore a spin degeneracy $\beta=\frac{2 S_{n+1}}{2 S_{n+1}+1}$ was proposed with the total spins of cations $M^{n+}$ and $M^{(n+1)+}$, respectively. From a careful theoretical analysis of the Seebeck coefficient of perovskite oxides it was concluded, that in addition to spin degeneracy, the orbital degeneracy, i.e. the degeneracy of orbitals which are incorporated in the hopping of charge carriers in a given crystallographic environment and crystal field splitting, must be considered [25]. Koshibae et al. emphasized the influence of the number of configurations $g_i$of $i$ ions to the Seebeck coefficient [26-28]:

$$
S=-\frac{k_{B}}{e}\left[\ln \left(\frac{g_{e}}{g_{h}} \frac{1-c}{c}\right)\right] \tag{6}
$$

with $g_e$ and $g_h$ as electronic degeneracies of the cations acting as electron donors $e$ (e.g. $Co^{3+}$, $Mn^{3+}$), or electron acceptors $h$ (holes, $Co^{4+}$, $Mn^{4+}$), respectively. Both, spin and orbital degeneracies ($g_{spin}$, $g_{orbit}$) are included in the electronic degeneracy $g$.

In Mn-spinels, e.g. $NiMn_2O_4$, electronic conduction is governed by hopping of charge carriers between $Mn^{3+}$ and $Mn^{4+}$on octahedral B-sites, hence the term $(1-c)/c$ in Eq. (6) corresponds to the ratio $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$, consequently, the Seebeck coefficient $S$ can be used to calculate the ratio $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$. Since the magnetic ordering in $NiMn_2O_4$ sets in below $T_N=90$ K [29] and the measurements discussed here are performed at higher temperatures, appropriate electronic degeneracies $g_e/g_h$ must be considered. Kobayashi [28] pointed out, that dynamic or static distortions of Jahn-Teller active ions might contribute to the absolute Seebeck coefficient. For the electronic configurations of the Mn-ions, high-spin states are assumed, and for the Jahn-Teller-active $Mn^{3+}$ ions two different configurations need to be considered (Fig. 3).

$Mn^{3+}$ ions with $t_{2g}^3e_g^1$ configuration are Jahn-Teller- active, and $e_g$ level splitting should be considered with absence of orbital degeneracy. The absence of a tetragonal distortion of the spinel structure of $NiMn_2O_4$ indicates that no static, cooperative Jahn-Teller distortions are present. Cooperative distortions of the spinel lattice with lowering of symmetry appear in manganese spinels, if the $Mn^{3+}$ concentration on B-sites exceeds a critical limit of about 50-65% [30-33]. However, locally a Jahn-Teller effect might be active and could lift the orbital degeneracy, leading to a total degeneracy of $\frac{g_e}{g_h}=\frac{5}{4}$. On the other hand, if the Jahn-Teller effect of $Mn^{3+}$ would not be able to lift the orbital degeneracy, a total degeneracy of $\frac{g_e}{g_h}=\frac{10}{4}$ would be present. With this, we follow the discussion of Kobayashi et al. [28], who have argued for a $g = 5$ for $Mn^{3+}$ ions in $LiMn_2O_4$ and selected perovskites. For $LiMn_2O_4$ at temperatures of $T > 1000$ K, however, they assume a transition into another state of $Mn^{3+}$ with the orbital degeneracy recovered ($g = 10$), based on the observation of a change in the slope of the Seebeck coefficient vs. temperature curve at high temperatures. A recent study on the thermopower of $LiMn_2O_4$ reports a high-temperature Seebeck coefficient which is consistent with an electronic structure for $Mn^{3+}$ with $g = 10$ and orbital degeneracy [34]. This illustrates, that a generally accepted treatment of the electronic degeneracy of Jahn-Teller active $Mn^{3+}$ ions in manganese spinels is lacking. In the following, we have calculated $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ ratios (Fig. 4) based on both degeneracy ratios of 5/4 and 10/4, and we will discuss the impact on the spinel cation distribution below.

Fig. 3. Electronic degeneracy of $Mn^{3+}$ and $Mn^{4+}$ in an octahedral crystal field and high-spin state with and without Jahn-Teller distortion. The total spin, number of configurations ($g_{spin}$ and $g_{orbit}$) and electronic degeneracy $g$ are given.

It may be noted, that the quenched samples exhibit very similar Seebeck coefficients and thus similar $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ ratios (Fig. 4A). This points to the fact, that quenching the samples was not successful in freezing-in the cation or charge distribution at the particular quench temperature. It might be speculated, that a certain cation distribution might be frozen in during quenching, since cation redistribution kinetics involving exchange of cations within the crystal structure are slow, whereas charge distribution kinetics (Mn disproportionation reaction) are fast enough to change the $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ ratio in the samples during cooling/quenching. The distribution equilibria of the three quenched samples seem to be frozen in at about the same temperature, corresponding to a similar intermediate non-equilibrium cation/charge distribution state. This intermediate state is not identical with the high-temperature equilibrium configuration, but also strongly differs from the cation/charge distribution of the slowly cooled sample, which has a much higher ratio $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ at room temperature (Fig. 4A).

During heating, the $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ ratio of the quenched samples starts to increase at above $310\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. Since we have measured the redox equivalents $k$ of the four samples at room temperature by redox titration (Table 1), we can exclude a different average Mn oxidation state. The differences in Mn oxidation state ratios, as probed by thermopower measurements, refer to the ratio of $Mn^{4+}$ and $Mn^{3+}$-concentrations on octahedral B-sites of the spinel structure. Hence, the changes in $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$observed in Fig. 4A, originate from changes in the cation and charge distribution parameters $\nu$ and $p$, respectively, rather than from oxidation/reduction phenomena associated with the formation of defect spinels. During heating of the quenched samples, the ratio $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ approaches and coincides with that of the slowly cooled sample between $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $500\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, and hence reaches the equilibrium cation/charge distribution. Upon further heating, all samples represent the temperature-dependent equilibrium states. During cooling (Fig. 4B) all samples exhibit a similar increase of the ratio $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ from $870\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ down to about $600\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ (see dotted line, Fig. 4B). Below that temperature a steep increase of $[Mn_{\beta}^{4+}]/[Mn_{\beta}^{3+}]$ is observed down to

about $400\ ^\circ$C, followed by a less pronounced, but significant increase of $[Mn_B^{4+}]/[Mn_B^{3+}]$ at lower temperature (Fig. 4B). As mentioned above, the $S(T)$ and thus the ratio $[Mn_B^{4+}]/[Mn_B^{3+}]$ of sample SC900 are almost identical during heating and cooling, representing an equilibrium cation/charge distribution. The further discussion is, hence, focused on the cation distribution of that sample.

To further discuss the different changes of $[Mn_B^{4+}]/[Mn_B^{3+}]$ and cation distributions with temperature, we present an Arrhenius-type plot of the Seebeck coefficient of the sample SC900 during cooling (Fig. 5). We note a linear variation of $S$ vs. $1000/T$ in the high-temperature range between $730\ ^\circ$C and $860\ ^\circ$C with an activation energy $E_S$ = 0.113 eV, whereas a second linear region with $E_S$ = 0.226 eV is found between $400\ ^\circ$C and $640\ ^\circ$C. An activation energy of about 0.060 eV is observed in the low-temperature region from $100\ ^\circ$C to $320\ ^\circ$C. A similar behavior of $S(1/T)$ with two different regions in the temperature range 80–600 °C and activation energies of 0.27 eV and 0.06 eV, respectively, has been reported in Ref. [8].

The differences in activation energies might be interpreted by different mechanisms associated with motion of ions and/or electrons between the different crystallographic sites of the spinel structure. At low temperature, cation exchange between different lattice sites does not take place, and the disproportionation reaction of the Mn ions, expressed by the parameter $p$ in Eq. (1), seems to dominate. Disproportionation involves the exchange of electrons with a small activation energy only, which is consistent with the small value of $E_S$ = 0.06 eV observed for the temperature range below $400\ ^\circ$C. Above that temperature a change of the slope of the $S$ vs. $1/T$ curve signals the onset of a temperature dependent cation distribution. A change of the degree of inversion involves exchange of ions between A- and B-sites, which is associated with a larger activation energy. The small activation energy of 0.11 eV observed in between $720\ ^\circ$C and $860\ ^\circ$C might originate from the fact, that cation exchange processes are less likely in $NiMn_2O_4$ at high temperature, and that electron exchange between $Mn^{3+}$ and $Mn^{4+}$ ions on B-sites is the prevailing mechanism. This would imply a less temperature dependent inversion parameter $\nu$ in this high-temperature region.

Finally, starting from the ratio $[Mn_B^{4+}]/[Mn_B^{3+}]$ we calculated an effective cation exchange parameter $p'$ using Eq. (1). Normally, the inversion parameter $\nu$ and the disproportionation parameter $p$ should be determined independently. However, here in the analysis of Seebeck data it is assumed, that the cation inversion and disproportionation parameters $\nu$ and $p$, respectively, are equal ($\nu = p \cong p'$). Consequently, $p'$ does not represent a parameter purely describing the disproportionation equilibrium, but rather a mix of cation inversion and Mn disproportionation. The variation of $p'$ vs. temperature (Fig. 6) and the resulting cation distributions are calculated using both degeneracy ratios 5/4 and 10/4, respectively (Tables 2 and 3).

The cation exchange parameter $p'$ decreases linearly up to 400 °C (Fig. 6), and between 400 °C and 640 °C a larger change is observed. Finally, above 640 °C, $p'$ decreases less steeply (Fig. 6). The absolute numbers of the effective cation exchange parameters $p'$, however, differ, depending which degeneracy ratio has been used (Fig. 6). At 110 °C the slow cooled sample SC900 has a $p'$ =0.92 ($g$ = 5/4), or $p'$ =0.86 ($g$ = 10/4), respectively, corresponding to a spinel with high degrees of inversion and disproportionation (Tables 2 and 3). Here we find large/low concentrations of $Mn^{4+}$/$Mn^{3+}$ on B-sites, respectively, in accordance with n-type charge transport behavior. At 860 °C, which is the upper temperature limit of the measurements discussed in this study, the resulting cation distributions exhibit larger differences (Tables 2 and 3). With a total degeneracy ratio of 5/4, a concentration of $Mn_B^{3+}$=0.88 is found with a corresponding $Mn_B^{4+}$=0.56 and an inversion of $Ni_B^{2+}$=0.56. Using a degeneracy ratio 10/4, a large concentration of $Mn_B^{3+}$=1.22 and a very small inversion of $Ni_B^{2+}$=0.39 results. Such cation distributions with high levels of $Mn^{3+}$ on B-sites are consistent with the observed p-type conduction at these temperatures. Large concentrations of $Mn^{3+}$ on octahedral B-sites are known to cause a cooperative Jahn-Teller distortion and, consequently, the formation of a tetragonal spinel with lower symmetry [33] would be expected, which is not the case. The large concentration of octahedral $Mn^{3+}$ calculated using a degeneracy of 10/4 is too large (76% of Mn on B-sites) to be consistent with the presence of a cubic spinel structure. The small $Ni_B^{2+}$=0.39 obtained for degeneracy of 10/4 appears unrealistic as well bearing the large octahedral site preference of $Ni^{2+}$ ($d^8$) in mind. In the literature, inversion parameters were shown to be large, e.g. $\nu$=0.74…0.93 [7], or $\nu$=$p$=0.65 at 900 °C [10], or $\nu$=$p$=0.90 [12]. Finally, the ratio $[Mn_B^{4+}]/[Mn_B^{3+}]$ is found to be close to one at temperatures of about 600 °C or 400 °C for degeneracy ratios of 5/4 and 10/4, respectively (Fig. 4). Since the n-p transition of the Seebeck coefficient is observed at around 680 °C (Fig. 2), the degeneracy of 5/4 seems more realistic.

It seems difficult to decide between both cation distribution models discussed here. For reasons discussed above, we prefer the distribution model derived from thermopower data using a degeneracy ratio of 5/4 (Fig. 6, Table 2). Both models include a large concentration of $Mn^{3+}$ on octahedral B-sites. Since we do not observe any cooperative tetragonal structural distortion (and a cubic to tetragonal phase transition has not been reported to the best of the authors knowledge), a cooperative Jahn-Teller distortion is excluded. Locally, a (non-cooperative) Jahn-Teller effect at $Mn^{3+}$ on B-sites will be operative with a total degeneracy of 5/4 excluding orbital degeneracy (Fig. 3). In order to unequivocally determine the in-situ cation distribution in $NiMn_2O_4$, it would be desirable to combine the data on Mn disproportionation accessible via Seebeck coefficient measurements (as discussed here), with an independent determination of the temperature-dependent cation inversion, e.g. using in-situ neutron diffraction. This is ongoing work and will be reported somewhere soon.

### 5. Conclusion
The results of this Seebeck coefficient study of $NiMn_2O_4$ demonstrate, that the thermal history and the cooling conditions of the samples have significant impact on the resulting thermoelectric power and thus cation/charge distribution. Samples which were quenched from an annealing temperature exhibit Seebeck coefficients at 100 °C, which are significantly different from the Seebeck coefficients at the annealing temperature, as well as from the values at 100 °C of slowly cooled samples. The quenched samples manifest an intermediate cation/charge distribution state, which is dictated by the cooling process and the cation and charge exchange kinetics.

Using the measured Seebeck coefficients and a modified Heikes formula we calculated the temperature variation of the $[Mn_B^{4+}]/[Mn_B^{3+}]$ ratio over a wide temperature range from 100 °C up to 860 °C. Further evaluation of the data of a slow cooled sample using two different electronic degeneracy factors allowed to calculate an effective cation exchange parameter $p'$. It is shown that using the degeneracy factors of 10/4 and 5/4, plausible models of the cation distribution in $NiMn_2O_4$ for the temperature range from about

$100$–$860\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$ are obtained, which are characterized by large cation inversion, a Mn disproportionation reaction being largely temperature dependent, and a change of sign from n- to p-type charge transport at about $680\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$. Further investigations using in-situ neutron diffraction and combination with the results of this thermopower study will allow to derive precise information on the temperature-dependent cation distribution in $\text{NiMn}_2\text{O}_4$.
