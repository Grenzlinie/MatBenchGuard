# Ab Initio Study of Semi-classic Transport Coefficients of $\text{SnO}_2$ Thermoelectric Material

## Abstract

The electronic structure and semi-classic transport coefficients of $\text{SnO}_2$ are investigated. The calculations are carried out by using the density functional theory and Boltzmann transport equations. The exchange-correlation potential is treated by the Tran–Blaha modified Becke–Johnson potential (Tb-mBJ) approximation. The calculated band gap value of $\text{SnO}_2$ is found to be in good agreement with experimental one. Thermoelectric properties reveal that Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity and power factor are strongly dependent on the doping level and temperature. A doping approach is proposed to enhance the thermoelectric properties of $\text{SnO}_2$. The factor of merit suggests that $\text{SnO}_2$ could be a good thermoelectric material.

**Keywords:** Density Functional Theory, Boltzmann Transport Equations, Thermoelectric Oxide Semiconductors, Structure Electronic, Thermoelectric Properties.

*Corresponding author:* a.slassi@yahoo.fr , a.slassi22@gmail.com

### Introduction

The search on thermoelectric materials with high energy conversion efficiency, non toxic and low cost have received a great attention because of their applications to directly convert thermal energy into electricity (Seebeck effect) and vice versa (Peltier effect) [1]. The performance of such materials thermoelectric is related to the dimensionless figure of merit:

$$
ZT = \frac{\sigma S^{2}T}{k_{e} + k_{l}} \tag{1}
$$

Where $T$ is absolute temperature, $\sigma$ is electrical conductivity, $S$ is the Seebeck coefficient (or thermopower), $k_{e}$ the electrical thermal conductivity, $k_{l}$ is the lattice thermal conductivity and, $S^{2}\sigma$, is usually referred to as the thermoelectric "power factor". It's clear from this dimensionless figure; a good thermoelectric material should have together, high Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity with at the same time low thermal conductivity. On the other hand, the efficiency of a thermoelectric material is also determined by averages over the operating temperature range. Among the high $ZT$ materials have been used to date, PbTe [2-4] is the most efficient for a power generation application at high temperature, whereas $\mathrm{Bi_{2}Te_{3}}$-based materials [2-4] are renowned for refrigeration around the room temperature. However, these alloy materials known the some application limitations due mainly to their high cost, toxicity and low stability at large temperature game. In any case, because of the very high temperature which can be achieved with concentration sunlight, it is interesting to develop thermoelectric materials for high-temperature applications. A considerable attention has been focused on metal oxides, which have a high resistance against degradation and are thermodynamically stable at high temperature range [5, 6].

$\mathrm{SnO_{2}}$ has been found in several applications such as transparent electrode, sensor and photo catalytic [7-9]. Recently, some investigations have shown that $\mathrm{SnO_{2}}$ has interesting thermoelectric properties [10-12]. Saadeddin et al. [10] have reported that $\mathrm{SnO_{2}}$ ceramics codoped with $\mathrm{Sb^{+5}}$ and $\mathrm{Zn^{+2}}$ showed a high electrical conductivity. Morgan et al. [11] reassured the Seebeck coefficients ranged from 50 to $220\ \mu.V.K^{-1}$ as a function of the temperature. Tsubota et al. [12] obtained a maximum figure of merit $ZT$ of $2.4×10^{-5}K^{-1}$ for the $\mathrm{Sn_{0.98}Ti_{0.01}Sb_{0.01}O_{2}}$ sample, which is high as compared to other metal oxides.

The thermoelectric properties of various materials have been calculated by using density functional theory (DFT) and Boltzmann equations transport [13-16]. One of the shortcomings of the conventional DFT is to seriously underestimate the fundamental band gap of

semiconductors including $SnO_2$, which is due mainly to exchange-correlation term. Many theoretical studies have been investigated recently to correct the band gap computational such as the hybrid function [17-18] and GW methods [19-20]. However, these methods lead to very expensive calculation. Recently, the modified version of semi-local Becke-Johnson (BJ) [21] exchange-correlation potential that proposed by Tran and Blaha TB-mbj [22] which is found to be a reasonable alternative of GW or hybrid functional, achieving a compared correction with a low cost of calculation.

Through this work, we use these methods (TB-mBJ and GGA) in other to study the lattice constants, electronic and thermoelectric properties of bulk $SnO_2$.

## Computational details

In our calculations, we have used the first-principle study based on density functional theory with full-potential linearized augmented plane-wave (FP-LAPW) method. This method is implemented in WIEN2K package [23, 24]. The exchange and correlation energy has been described by generalized gradient approximation GGA [25] for structural calculations, and TB-mBj approximation [22] for electronic, optical and transport proprieties calculations. The The radii of the muffin tin atomic spheres $R_{MT}$ of Sn and O atoms have been 2.3 and 1.6 Bohr, respectively. Inside the atomic spheres the maximum value of angular momentum $l_{max}$=10 has been taken for the wave function expansion, while charge density has been Fourier expanded up to $G_{max}$=10 $(Ryd)^{1/2}$, where $G_{max}$ is the largest vector in the Fourier expansion. The cutoff parameter $R_{MT} × K_{MAX}$ has been set to 7.0 where $R_{MT}$ denotes the smallest muffin tin radius of atoms and $K_{MAX}$ is the maximum value of the reciprocal lattice vectors used in the plane wave expansion. The integrals over the Brillouin zone have been performed with 1000 k-points. We have used a self-consistent criterion of the total energy with a precision of 0.0001 Ry.

To obtain the transport properties of $SnO_2$, the calculated band structure data from DFT have been fitted to Boltzmann package that is based on semi-classic Boltzmann theory and the rigid band approach [26-27]. It follows from these approaches that, the dependence of the conductivity on transport distribution can be given by:

$$
\sigma_{\alpha \beta}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i, k} \sigma_{\alpha \beta}(i, k) \frac{\delta\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i, k}\right)}{\delta(\varepsilon)} \tag{6}
$$

In the above relation, N denotes the number of k-points that are sampled in the BZ and $\varepsilon_{i,k}$ is the band structure which is considered as a rigid band. The $k$-dependent transport tensor is read as:

$$
\sigma_{\alpha \beta}(i, k)=e^{2} \tau_{i, k} v_{\alpha}(i, k) v_{\beta}(i, k)
\tag{7}
$$

In this equation, i and k stand for the band index and wave vector, respectively, and $\tau$ denotes the relaxation time, $v_{\alpha}(i, k)$ is $\alpha$ component of the group velocities, while $e$ is the electron charge.

By integrating the transport distribution over energy, the electrical conductivity can be then written as a function of the temperature, $T$, and the chemical potential, $\mu$, via the following equations:

$$
\sigma_{\alpha \beta}(T, \mu)=\frac{1}{\Omega} \int \sigma_{\alpha \beta}\left[-\frac{\partial f_{\mu}(T, \varepsilon)}{\partial \varepsilon}\right] d \varepsilon
\tag{8}
$$

$$
v_{\alpha \beta}(T, \mu)=\frac{1}{e T \Omega} \int \sigma_{\alpha \beta}(\varepsilon)(\varepsilon-\mu)\left[-\frac{\partial f_{\mu}(T, \varepsilon)}{\partial \varepsilon}\right] d \varepsilon
\tag{9}
$$

$$
S=\sigma^{-1} v
\tag{10}
$$

Where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ stand for the tensor indices, $\Omega$, $\mu$, and $f$ denote the volume of unit cell, Fermi level of carriers, and the carrier Fermi-Dirac distribution function, respectively, and $S$ is seebeck coefficient.

The one of shortcomings of the semi-classic Boltzmann equation is to describe exactly the carrier scattering mechanisms in a solid. For this reason the relaxation time approximation is adopted to overcome such complexity, where the relaxation time is treated as energy-independent constant. These approaches have been demonstrated to be a reasonable good approximation for evaluating the optimal doping level of several bulk thermoelectric materials [28-30].

## 3. Results and discussions
### 3.1 Lattice parameters

Tin oxide ($\text{SnO}_2$) crystallizes in rutile-type structure with space group 136, the Wyckoff of Sn atoms at position (0, 0, 0) and (1/2, 1/2, 1/2) and four O atoms at position +-(u, u, 0; 1/2+u, 1/2-u, 1/2) in each primitive cell. The experimental cell parameters are $a=b=0.4737\ nm$, $c=0.3142\ nm$ and $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=90^\circ$ with ideal $u=0.306$ [31]. The each Sn atom is in the central site of an octhedron, which is formed by four rectangular basal O atoms ($\text{O}_1$) and two vertex

O atoms ($O_2$). In order to get the relaxed lattice parameters, the total energy is, firstly, calculated versus reduced volume and $c/a$ ratio of $SnO_2$ unit cell. Then, the total energies are fitted as a function of the volume and $c/a$ ratio, as shown in Figure 1.

According to results, the relaxed lattice constants for $SnO_2$ are $a= 0.48$ and $c= 0.327\ nm$, which are close to experimental values and in agreement with other theoretical calculations [32, 33]. Indeed, these obtained results reflect the reliability of the calculation methods used.

### 3.2 Structure electronic
The width of band gap and sharp of the density of states near the band edges are main parameters affecting the thermoelectric properties. Hence, we perform the electronic structure, as our first criterion. Similar results have been found in the previous theoretical calculation [32-33]. Figure 2 shows the band structure of pure $SnO_2$ calculated by employing TB-mBJ along the Z-A-X-G-Z-R-X-G path in the first Brillouin zone, where the Fermi level is referred to be zero. Here the value of the band gap is defined as a difference between the valence band maximum (VBM) and the conduction band minimum (CBM). It's observed that, the conduction band maximum and the valance band minimum localise at the same $\Gamma$-point indicating that $SnO_2$ belong to direct band gap semiconductor family, which confirms the previous predicted results. The calculated band gap value is 3.1 eV representing an underestimate of 10% as compared to experimental value one (Eg=3.4 eV). Moreover, this result is still much improved as compared to PBE that gives only 1.22 eV (underestimated by 60%). Furthermore, The Fermi level is localised in the band gap means that the pure $SnO_2$ is non-conductive material. Overall, the mBJ gives band gap results sufficiently accurate for describing the physical properties of $SnO_2$ compound. Hereafter, we will demonstrate only the results obtained by Tb- mBJ approach. On the other hand, it is known, from the band structure theory that the effective mass of carriers depends basically on the band edge curvatures. One can see from Figure 2 that the curvature of the valance band is flatter as compared to conduction band. This indicates that the effective mass of holes is heavier than the electrons. In fact, the p-type $SnO_2$ as thermoelectric material may be more suitable if it is possible to make it.

We plot in Figure 3 the total and partial densities of states of pure $SnO_2$, including the Sn and O, where the Fermi level is referred to be zero. It's shown clearly that, the valence band in range 0 to -7 $eV$ is formed mainly by O-2p state with small contribution of Sn-s, p and d, states while the conduction band is dominated by Sn-s and Sn-p states.

### 3.3 Electrical transport properties

We use the rigid band approximation (RBA) [26-27] to evaluate the electrical transport properties of $SnO_2$ compound. In the RBA, doping in the $SnO_2$ semiconductor is assumed not to change the band structure of materials but to shift the Fermi level up or down. In this work the Fermi level is fixed to middle of band gap, so shift of the chemical potential to down produces a p-type doping and correspond to negative $(\mu-\mu_0)$, or inversely to up produces a n-type doping and the corresponding to positive $(\mu-\mu_0)$. This is a reasonably good approximation widely used for theoretical study of thermoelectric materials. Indeed, getting theoretically the optimal doping level at which factor of merit achieves a maximum value is helpful to orient the experiment works.

Here, in our study we focus on both $n$-type and $p$-type doping, it is worth noting that the depending of direction transport properties on anisotropy doesn't discuss in this study. We only calculate the average of electrical transport properties; note that electrical properties transports in each direction can be deferent owing the anisotropy in tetragonal rutile structure.

The dependence of average electrical transport properties on doping level at three temperatures 600, 900 and 1200 $K$ are shown in Figures 4-8. The wide doping level range allows us to find the optimal electrical thermoelectric properties and the carrier concentration correspondent. Firstly, we expose the average thermo power of $SnO_2$. It's emphasizing the fact that a high merit factor cannot be obtained without a high $S$, on the other hand the Seebeck coefficient is independent of relaxation time therefor we can obtained it directly from the electronic structure. As shown in Figure 4, the Seebeck coefficient exhibits two big peaks when the chemical potential is near to the middle of band gap due mainly to low carrier concentration around this doping region, one peak for the p-type and the other for n-type doping. These peaks are localized at doping level of -0.115 $\mu eV$ with a value of +2391 $\mu V/K$ for p-type case and at +0.115 $\mu eV$ with a value of -2336 $\mu V/K$ for n-type case, which indicates that they are accessible in the intrinsic limit (Fermi level inside band gap). Moreover, we also see that the curve of Seebeck coefficient continues to increase at low doping level, pointing out that there is no bipolar conduction owing to the large band gap of $SnO_2$ semiconductor. Furthermore, over the doping level of $\pm0.115$ $\mu eV$; we can see an increase in the thermo-power which indicates the onset of bipolar conduction at those ranges.

Now let's discuss the thermal conductivity. To get an efficient thermoelectric material, its thermal conductivity should be powerless as much as possible. Indeed, the thermal

conductivity is defined as a sum of two contributions ($k$=$k_e$+$k_l$), $k_e$ is the electronic contribution and $k_l$ is the phonon contribution. Figure 5 gives the calculated the electron thermal conductivity $k_e$ (in units of $\tau$) as a function of doping level at temperatures 600, 900 and 1200 $K$. The lowest values of the electron thermal conductivity are found in the doping level between -1 and +1 $\mu eV$, which indicates that a high factor of merit can be achieved in this doping region. Outside this range, the electronic thermal conductivity is dramatically increased which is undesirable for attaining a maximum efficiency. On the other hand, the thermal conductivity for p-type doping is larger than for n-type.

Another important parameter in the thermoelectric performance is the electrical conductivity, which should be high for a good thermoelectric material. However, the electrical conductivity is substantially calculated with respect to the relaxation time within semi-classical Boltzmann equations. The reduced electrical conductivity $\sigma/\tau$ could be calculated as a function of $n$ and $T$ from electronic structure, but it is not possible to calculate $\sigma$ itself without getting the value of relaxation time. So, we first consider variation of $\sigma/\tau$ with Fermi level at three different temperatures. It is observed a significantly change in the electrical conductivity with temperature for our focus temperature range, because of the semi-conductivity behaviour of $SnO_2$. Thus, as shown in Figure 6, the electrical conductivity is increased exponentially with the level doping, indicating an increase in the carrier charge concentration. Moreover, it is found that the reduced electrical conductivity ($\sigma/\tau$) weakly depends on the temperature over the doping range from -1.9 $\mu eV$ to +1.9 $\mu eV$.

The relaxation time depends on the nuances for overcome this problem, we have used the module proposed by Ong et al. [13] and experimental data reported by Toshiki Tsubota et al. [34], who made measurements on ceramic $SnO_2$. They report a value experimental thermo power of $S = -175\ \mu V/K$ and electrical conductivity of $\sigma= 3.53x10^2\ (\Omega m)^{-1}$. By comparing with the calculated $S\ (T,\ n)$ (Fig) and $\sigma/\tau$ (Figu), we obtain values $n= 3.95\times10^{19}\ cm^{-3}$ and $\tau= 7.36\times10^{-16} s$ for this particular sample at 900 $K$. In this regime, the experimental data from this sample and others follow an approximate electron-phonon $T$ dependence $\sigma\alpha T^{-1}$. For the doping dependence, we therefore take a standard electron-phonon from, $\tau\alpha n^{-1/3}$. This yields $\tau$=$2.25\times10^{-6}T^{-1}n^{-1/3}$ with $\tau$ in $s$, $T$ in $K$, and $n$ in $cm^{-3}$. New, after estimating the value of relaxation time we can calculate the exact electrical thermoelectric properties of $SnO_2$. As mentioned, we calculate $\sigma$ as $(\sigma/\tau)$$\times$$\tau$. The resulting average conductivity is shown in Figure 6. We can see that the electrical conductivity increases with doping level due to increasing in the

carrier concentrations. As temperature depends on the electrical, we can see also that the electrical conductivity increases with temperature.

The correspondent power factor $\sigma \times S^{2}/\tau$, obtained from the calculated $S$ ($T$, $n$) and $\sigma/\tau$ as above, is given in Figure 7. The power factor achieves it maximum, for n-type doping, when the chemical potential shift about of $1.63\ eV$ up to conduction band, this value of chemical potential correspondent to add of $8.77 \times 10^{19}\ electrons/cm^{-3}$. The peak power factor is only weakly $T$ dependent with a value $(\sigma S^{2})_{max} \sim 0.0000876\ W/mK^{2}$. However, the peak shifts to low doping level with increasing of temperature. For p-type doping, we can see the similar behaviour, the power factor attains a maximum value of $0.00021\ W/mK^{2}$ at doping level around of $-1.62\ \mu eV$.

Next we analyse the factor of merit, so long as we cannot get the lattice thermal conductivity contribution by using Boltzmann transport equations we will discuss only the electronic part of the factor of merit $(ZT)_{e}= S^{2}\sigma T/k_{e}$, which depends only on electronic properties. Figure 8 illustrates the factor of merit as a function of doping level at three temperatures 600, 900 and $1200\ K$. the factor of merit of $SnO_{2}$ compound reaches a value around the unity which is extremely useful for thermoelectric applications. Besides, the values of the factor of merit for n-type and p-type doping are quantitatively equal, which suggests that the $SnO_{2}$ can be used as an n-type or p-type thermoelectric material. This is very interesting for fabricating a thermoelectric junction p-n based $SnO_{2}$ material. On the other hand, a negligible difference in the predicted behaviour of $ZT$ is seen when the temperature goes from 600 to $1200\ K$, indicating that $SnO_{2}$ material could be used at various temperature range applications.

## Conclusion

In this paper, the density functional theory and Boltzmann transport equations have been used to investigate the different thermoelectric properties of $SnO_{2}$. To produce accurate results, the Tran–Blaha modified Becke–Johnson potential (Tb-mBJ) approximation has been used for exchange-correlation potential. Our results about band gap have showed an agreement with experimental measurement for pure $SnO_{2}$. The Seebeck coefficient has been decreased with doping level and temperature, while the electrical conductivity has been increased. Importantly, the power factor could be obtained at optimal doping level at high functional temperatures. Besides, the factor of merit can achieve a value around the unity for both n-type and p-type doping and in only the intrinsic limit. Finally, the results suggest that low cost $SnO_{2}$ material could be found in the thermoelectric device applications.
