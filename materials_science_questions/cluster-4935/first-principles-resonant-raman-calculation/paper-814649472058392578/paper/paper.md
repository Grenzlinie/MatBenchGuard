## ABSTRACT

Light scattering in quantum liquid helium-3 may involve a unique mechanism - the creation and annihilation of atom excitations across the Fermi level. The density of states of particle-hole excitations in the low-energy limit is strongly enhanced as compared to that of collective excitations of phonons in helium-3. This makes possible to directly observe Fermi excitations in the resonant Raman scattering (RRS) by $^3$He droplets doped by impurity molecules. The RRS spectra essentially depend on the excitation frequency. In case of excitation in the anti-Stokes side of absorption the first order RRS is directly determined by the particle-hole excitations in the vicinity of the impurity molecule and the contribution of phonons mainly given by the localized spherical vibration. The calculations are made for a $^3$He droplet doped by a glyoxal molecule.

## 1. Introduction

The direct study of excitations of nuclear motion in helium nano-droplets by means of inelastic Raman scattering of light is severely hampered by the very small polarizability of helium atoms. However, the insertion of a chromophore molecule (CM) into the droplet, providing an efficient virtual electronic transition for light scattering, may allow to observe the droplet vibrational spectrum via the electron-phonon coupling of the CM electronic transition to the phonon excitations of the surrounding He liquid. A substantial amplification of the Raman scattering process can be obtained for an incident light frequency close to the resonance with a CM electronic transition. In particular resonance enhancement appears to be necessary in the special case of fermionic $^3$He droplets, considered in the present study, where the sharp features of collective phonon excitations are superimposed to the continuum of single particle-hole excitations across the Fermi level [1]. Therefore, the interaction of optical electrons of CM with nuclei (usually called as vibronic interaction) in $^3$He droplets responsible for the resonance Raman scattering is unusual: it includes, in addition to the interaction with collective excitations (phonons), also the interaction with the continuum of single particle-hole excitations. Resonance Raman scattering is also requested under the special experimental conditions where the target is actually a beam of nanometric droplets, each one containing just one single CM. The available density of the beams should be sufficient to get measurable resonance Raman signal from molecules imbedded to the droplets.

An additional possibility to enhance the Raman scattering by He droplets was recently demonstrated by experiments of Gomez et al. [2] and Thaler et al. [3] on the growth of metal nanowires inside superfluid $^4$He droplets: analogously to SERS (surface enhanced Raman scattering) on rough metallic surfaces, the metallic nanowires allow one to strongly enlarge the cross-section of the Raman scattering of molecules in He droplets due to the contribution of surface plasmon polaritons.

$^3$He droplets are produced in a free jet expansion of a nominally pure $^3$He gas ($<10^{-5}$ impurities) through a $5\ \mu$m diameter nozzle from a source kept at a stagnation pressure of the order of 20 bar and temperatures in the range of 7-10 K [4]. With a suitable choice of these parameters the average size of the liquid droplets can be controlled. In previous absorption experiments [1] typical liquid droplet diameters were around $80\ \mathring{A}$, corresponding to about $5\times10^3$ atoms [5]. The temperature of the flying $^3$He droplets decreases due to evaporation until it stabilizes at about 0.15 K. At a certain distance from the nozzle ($\sim$10 cm) the droplet beam passes through a cell containing the CM vapor at a pressure of about $10^{-5}$ torr. A pressure of this size, combined with a suitable path length inside the cell permits the pick-up, on average, of a single molecule per droplet. Differently from what happens in all other materials, inelastic light scattering from a Fermi liquid like a $^3$He droplet is shown to involve predominantly the creation or annihilation of single particle-hole excitations across the Fermi level, besides collective phonon excitations. Unlike phonon excitations whose density of states (DOS) decreases in the low-energy limit and vanishes at zero energy, the spectrum of particle-hole excitations remains finite in the low-energy limit, due to the finite DOS of quasi-particles and quasi-holes at the Fermi level. This remains true also in nanometric droplets, whose size is however large enough to keep the atom level separation at the Fermi level orders of magnitude smaller than the experimental resolution. Thus in the low-energy limit a predominance of Fermi particle-hole excitations over phonon excitations is also expected to occur in resonant Raman spectra.

Fermi excitations have been observed in the absorption spectrum of doped helium-3 droplets, as shown experimentally in [1] and interpreted theoretically in [1,6,7]. It was found that Fermi excitations essentially affect the spectrum in the vicinity of the zero-phonon line (ZPL) by causing: (i) the disappearance of the dip (existing for helium-4 droplets) between the zero-phonon line and the phonon tail; (ii) a $0.5\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ broadening and strong asymmetry of the line. Moreover, also the observed long tail, forming the background to the sharp localized phonon peak at $8\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ and extending up to $\sim40\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ on the blue side, was assigned to Fermi excitations.

The aim of the present article is to point out the advantage of resonant Raman scattering for the detection of Fermi excitations. In resonant Raman scattering, the spectrum of fermionic and phonon excitations can be directly observed at proper vibronic excitations. In case of a Raman exciting frequency on the anti-Stokes (red) side of the molecule absorption line the shape of the first order Raman scattering spectrum follows that of the response function, i.e. it is directly determined by the excitation of particle-hole pairs simultaneously with phonons.

### 2. Basic equations

To calculate the optical spectra of the $^3$He Fermi liquid in case of excitation of an embedded impurity molecule we use the theory of resonant secondary radiation [8], here generalized in order to take into account Fermi excitations.

The full secondary radiation, whether luminescence, or hot luminescence, or resonant inelastic and elastic (Rayleigh) scattering, is described as [8]

$$
W(\omega_0,\Omega)=\frac{B}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\mu\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau d\tau' e^{i(\Omega-\omega_0)\mu-i\omega_0(\tau'-\tau)}e^{-\gamma(\tau+\tau')}A(\mu,\tau,\tau') \tag{1}
$$

where $\omega_0$ and $\Omega$ are the frequencies of excitation and scattered light, respectively,

$$
A(\mu,\tau,\tau')=\left\langle e^{i\tau'H_2}e^{i\mu H_1}e^{-i\tau H_2}e^{-i(\mu+\tau'-\tau)}\right\rangle \tag{2a}
$$

$$
B=\frac{\omega_0^4}{2\pi c^3}|\langle 1|\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{M}|2\rangle\langle 2|\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{M}|1\rangle|^2 \tag{2b}
$$

$\langle...\rangle$ means the quantum-statistical average, $\mathbf{e}$ denotes the polarization vector of the electric field of the scattered light, $\mathbf{E}$ the electric field of the incident light, $\mathbf{M}$ the dipole moment of the electrons of the impurity center, $H_1$ and $H_2=H_1+V$ denote the Hamiltonians of the doped helium-3 droplet in the ground and excited electronic state of the impurity molecule, respectively, $\gamma$ is the inverse radiative lifetime of the excited electronic state.

By using the technique of time-ordered operators [9],

$$
\exp\left(itH_2\right)=\hat{T}\exp\left(i\int_{0}^{t}dt'V(t')\right)\exp\left(itH_1\right) \tag{3}
$$

where $\hat{T}$ is the time-ordering operator, the time-dependent potential $\hat{V}(t)=e^{itH_1}\hat{V}e^{-itH_1}$ accounts for the interaction of the CM electron performing the transition and the Fermi excitations of the surrounding droplet. The coupling mechanism providing the Fourier transform of $V(t)$ is discussed below in Section 3. By applying the cumulant expansion up to the second-order, one obtains [8]

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(\mu,\tau,\tau')&=e^{iE_0(\tau'-\tau)}\exp\left[g(\mu)+g(\mu+\tau'-\tau)+g(\tau')+g(-\tau)\right.\\
&\quad\left.-g(\mu+\tau')-g(\mu-\tau)\right]
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
$$

Here $E_0$ is the energy of the electronic excitation, and

$$
g(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\left(e^{it\omega}-1\right)\varphi(\omega)/\omega^2 \tag{5}
$$

is determined from the response function $\varphi(\omega)$. This is in turn the Fourier transform of the potential-potential correlation function,

$$
\varphi(\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt e^{-i\omega t}\left(\left\langle\hat{V}(t)\hat{V}(0)\right\rangle-\langle V\rangle^2\right). \tag{6}
$$

The potential-potential correlation function contains all the information on the particle-hole (PH) and collective phonon dynamics.

The Fourier transform of $\exp(g(t))$ determines the absorption spectrum [8]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(\omega_0)&=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt e^{-it(\omega_0-E_0)-\gamma|t|+g(t)}\\
&=e^{-S}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt e^{-it(\omega_0-E_0)-\gamma|t|}\exp\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega e^{i\omega t}\varphi(\omega)/\omega^2\right],
\end{aligned} \tag{7}
$$

where

$$
S\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\varphi(\omega)/\omega^2 \tag{8}
$$

is the total Huang-Rhys exponent giving the average number of elementary excitations created by the electronic transition.

The $k$th order resonance scattering is determined by the $k$th term in the expansion of the second exponential defining the correlation function $A(\mu,\tau,\tau')$, Eq. (4), with respect to its argument

$$
\begin{aligned}
&g(\mu)+g(\mu+\tau'-\tau)-g(\mu+\tau')-g(\mu-\tau)\\
&\quad=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\varphi(\omega)}{\omega^2}e^{i\omega\mu}\left(e^{i\omega\tau'}-1\right)\left(e^{-i\omega\tau}-1\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{9}
$$

The zero-order spectrum corresponds to the Rayleigh line

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_0(\Omega)&=B\delta(\Omega-\omega_0)\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau e^{i(E_0-\omega_0)\tau-\gamma\tau+g(\tau)}\right|^2\\
&=B\delta(\Omega-\omega_0)|\Phi(\omega_0)|^2
\end{aligned} \tag{10}
$$

where

$$
\Phi(\omega_0)=P\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{I(\omega)}{\omega-\omega_0}-i\pi I(\omega_0) \tag{11}
$$

is the complex refraction index.

The first order resonant Raman spectrum is determined by

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_1(\Omega)&=\frac{B}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\varphi(\omega)}{\omega^2}\\
&\quad\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\mu e^{i(\Omega-\omega_0+\omega)\mu}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau'e^{i(E_0-\omega_0)\tau'-\gamma\tau'+g(\tau')}(e^{i\omega\tau'}-1)\right|^2
\end{aligned} \tag{12}
$$

Please cite this article in press as: I. Tehver et al., Chem. Phys. (2015), http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.chemphys.2015.02.015

Substitution of Eqs. (5) and (11) into (12) finally allows to express the first order resonant Raman scattering as [8]

$$
W_{1}\left(\omega_{0}, \Omega\right)=B \frac{\varphi\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)}{\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)^{2}}\left|\Phi(\Omega)-\Phi\left(\omega_{0}\right)\right|^{2}
\tag{13}
$$

The second order Raman spectrum is then given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{2}\left(\omega_{0}, \Omega\right)= & \frac{1}{2} B \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d x \frac{\varphi(x) \varphi\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega-x\right)}{x^{2}\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega-x\right)^{2}} \\
& \times\left|\Phi\left(\omega_{0}\right)+\Phi\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)-\Phi\left(\omega_{0}-x\right)-\Phi\left(\omega_{0}+x-\Omega\right)\right|^{2}
\tag{14}
\end{aligned}
$$

and similarly one can derive the higher order Raman spectra.

When $|\omega_{0}-E_{0}|$ is large with respect the excitation energies of the Fermi liquid, say much larger than its Fermi energy $E_{F}$, Eq. (9) can be expanded in powers of $\tau$ and $\tau^{\prime}$ up to the lowest-order non-zero terms. This yields a simpler form for the $k$th order Raman scattering:

$$
W_{k}\left(\omega_{0}, \Omega\right)=\frac{B}{k!} \varphi_{k}\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)\left|\int_{0}^{\infty} \tau^{k} e^{i\left(E_{0}-\omega_{0}\right) \tau-\gamma \tau+g(\tau)} d \tau\right|^{2},
\tag{15}
$$

where $\varphi_{k}\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)$ denotes the $k$th order convolution of the response function

$$
\varphi_{k}\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d x \varphi(x) \varphi_{k-1}\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega-x\right)
\tag{16}
$$

with $\varphi_{1}\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)=\varphi\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)$.

Thus, in direct proximity of the Rayleigh line the shape of the spectrum is determined by the sum of $\varphi\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)$ and its foldings. However, this approximation does not hold if the incident frequency is close to a narrow ZPL of the absorption spectrum (with the width being smaller than or comparable to the width of the spectral function $\varphi(\omega)$ ). In this case large times $\tau^{\prime} \gtrsim 1 \gamma$ give an essential contribution to the integral in Eq. (12) and the RRS spectra depend essentially on the excitation frequency. Eqs. (11)-(13) permit to calculate such dependence of the resonant Raman spectra if the frequency dependence of $\varphi(\omega)$ and the absorption spectra are known. As one can see, the response function $\varphi\left(\omega_{0}-\Omega\right)$, the spectrum of excitation of single particle-hole pairs and phonons, manifests itself directly in the shape of the Raman spectrum, Eq. (13), whereas in the absorption spectrum (7) it only occurs indirectly, via the Fourier transform of $\exp (g(t))$.

## 3. Contribution of the Fermi sea

For pure particle-hole excitations $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ are written in the following manner [6,7]:

$$
H_{1}=\sum_{i}\left(\varepsilon_{p i} \hat{a}_{i}^{+} \hat{a}_{i}+\varepsilon_{h i} \hat{b}_{i}^{+} \hat{b}_{i}\right)
\tag{17}
$$

$$
H_{2}=H_{1}+V_{q}
\tag{18}
$$

$$
V_{q} \equiv \sum_{i j} V_{i j}\left(\hat{a}_{i}^{+} \hat{a}_{j}+\hat{a}_{i}^{+} \hat{b}_{-j}^{+}+\hat{b}_{-i} \hat{a}_{j}+\hat{b}_{-i} \hat{b}_{-j}^{+}\right)
\tag{19}
$$

where $\hat{a}_{i}^{+}$and $\hat{a}_{i}$ are the creation and the annihilation operators of a quasi (q)-particle of energy $\varepsilon_{p i}=E_{i}-E_{F} \equiv \varepsilon_{i}$ above the Fermi level $E_{F} ; \hat{b}_{-i}^{+}$and $\hat{b}_{-i}$ are the creation and annihilation operators of a q-hole of energy $\varepsilon_{h i}=E_{F}-E_{i}=-\varepsilon_{i}$ below the Fermi level. Since the annihilation operator of a particle with the energy $E_{i}<E_{F}$ is equivalent to the creation operator of the corresponding hole of energy $\varepsilon_{h i}=E_{F}-E_{i}$, both energies $\varepsilon_{p i}$ and $\varepsilon_{h i}$ are defined positive. Four possible transitions are accounted for by Eq. (19), two across $\left(\hat{a}_{i}^{+} \hat{b}_{-j}^{+}, \hat{b}_{-i} \hat{a}_{j}\right)$ and two either above $\left(\hat{a}_{i}^{+} \hat{a}_{j}\right)$ or below $\left(\hat{b}_{-i} \hat{b}_{-j}^{+}\right)$the Fermi level.

The Fourier transform $V_{q}$ of the potential is represented as a combination of the matrix elements $V_{i j}$ which couple the CM dipole-active electronic transition to the elementary atom excitations between states of indices $i$ and $j$. The latter, assuming a spherical symmetry of the atom density around the CM, are spherical waves, $i \rightarrow(k, \ell m), j \rightarrow\left(k^{\prime}, \ell^{\prime} m^{\prime}\right)$, where $k, k^{\prime}$ are radial wave vectors and $\ell m, \ell^{\prime} m^{\prime}$ angular quantum numbers. Since in the ${ }^{3} \mathrm{He}$ Fermi liquid the CM rotations are completely damped and orientation is random [10,11] it can be assumed, as previously done for adsorption processes [1], that no angular momentum is transferred to Fermi excitations and, moreover, that only spherical $(\ell=m=0)$ excitations couple to the electronic transition, so that $V_{i j} \rightarrow V_{k 00, k^{\prime} 00}$. In this way the matrix elements are independent of the shape of the molecule, which greatly simplifies the discussion, and only the change in radial wave vector $k^{\prime}-k \equiv Q$ is considered.

The electronic wavefunctions of the molecule, and therefore the electron transition energy, depend on the nearest neighbor ${ }^{3} \mathrm{He}$ atom positions through the overlap with the wave functions of the He atoms. Such dependence can then be derived from their orthogonality conditions [12]. The procedure has been illustrated in two previous papers [1,7]. A salient point is that the dependence of the matrix elements on the radial momentum transfer $q$ can be conveniently transformed into an approximate dependence on the energy transfer $\mathcal{E}$. The approximation is actually the matching at the cross-over energy $\mathcal{E}_{c o}=2 m k_{F}^{2} /\left(m_{F}^{*}\right)^{2}$ between the low-energy limit, where $Q \ll k_{F}$, and the high-energy limit, where $Q \cong(2 m \mathcal{E})^{1 / 2}$ and $Q \gg k_{F}$, with $k_{F}$ the Fermi wave vector and $m_{F}^{*}$ the ${ }^{3} \mathrm{He}$ atom effective mass [7]. In this way it is found:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|V_{k 00, k^{\prime} 00}\right|^{2} & \cong \frac{V_{0}^{2}}{\left(1+Q^{2} a_{0}^{2} / 4\right)^{4}} \\
& =V_{0}^{2} \cdot \begin{cases}{\left[1+\left(m_{F}^{*} \mathcal{E} a_{0} / 2 k_{F}\right)^{2}\right]^{-4},} & \mathcal{E}<E_{c o} \\
{\left[1+\left(m \mathcal{E} a_{0}^{2} / 4\right)^{2}\right]^{-4},} & \mathcal{E} \geq E_{c o}\end{cases} \equiv V_{0}^{2} \varsigma(\mathcal{E})
\tag{20}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $V_{0} \cong 20 f_{1} \rho_{1} a_{0}^{4}, a_{0}$ is the Bohr radius and $\rho_{1}$ is the atom density in the first atomic shell surrounding the CM [13]. The matrix element of the potential $V_{k 00, k^{\prime} 00}$ is proportional to the inelastic part of the transition dipole matrix element calculated in Ref. [1] via a proportionality constant $f_{1} / e$, where $e$ is the electron charge and $f_{1}$ is the force exerted by the electronic transition on the atoms of the first shell, analogous to the electron-phonon coupling force in ordinary Raman scattering. Eq. (20) reduces the squared potential matrix element to a constant multiplied by a continuous cut-off function $\varsigma(\mathcal{E})$ which tends quadratically to 1 for $\mathcal{E} \rightarrow 0$ and drops rapidly to zero as $\mathcal{E}^{-4}$ for $\mathcal{E} \rightarrow \infty$.

The potential-potential correlation function of Fermi quasi-particles and quasi-holes takes the comparatively simple form [7]

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle\hat{V}_{q}(t) \hat{V}_{q}(0)\right\rangle= & \alpha \iint d \varepsilon d \varepsilon^{\prime}\left\{\varsigma\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\left[\bar{p}_{\varepsilon}\left(1-\bar{p}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right) e^{-i t\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)}\right.\right. \\
& \left.+\left(1-\bar{h}_{\varepsilon}\right) \bar{h}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}} e^{i t\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)}\right]+\varsigma\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\left[\bar{p}_{\varepsilon} \bar{h}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}} e^{-i t\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)}\right. \\
& \left.\left.+\left(1-\bar{h}_{\varepsilon}\right)\left(1-\bar{p}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right) e^{i t\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)}\right]\right\}
\tag{21}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\alpha$ is a dimensionless coupling strength, $\bar{p}_{k}$ and $\bar{h}_{k}$ are the Fermi occupation numbers of quasi-particles and quasi-holes, respectively. In Eq. (21) the first two terms in the curly brackets represent the simultaneous creation of a quasiparticle (q-particle or q-hole) and annihilation of another quasiparticle of the same

type, while the last two terms represent the simultaneous creation of a quasi-particle and a quasi-hole.

For the explicit evaluation of the particle-hole (PH) contribution $\varphi_{PH}(E)$ to the total $\varphi(E)$ we note that the energy of annihilated quasi-particles $\varepsilon'$ is smaller than $k_B T$. Thus $\varsigma(\varepsilon+\varepsilon') \approx \varsigma(\varepsilon-\varepsilon')$, which allows, for a positive energy $E$ and after replacing the occupation numbers in Eq. (21) with their temperature-dependent expressions, to explicitly obtain the particle-hole response function $\varphi_{PH}(E)$ as [7]

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_{P H}(E)= & \alpha \varsigma(E) \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{d \varepsilon}{\left(1+e^{-\beta \varepsilon}\right)\left(1+e^{\beta(\varepsilon-E)}\right)}[1-\Theta\left(\varepsilon-E_{F}\right) \Theta(E-\varepsilon) \\
& \left.+\Theta(\varepsilon-E) \Theta\left(E_{F}-\varepsilon\right)\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\beta=1 / k_{B} T$, and $E_{F}$ is the Fermi energy. The calculation gives [7]

$$
\varphi_{P H}(E)=\frac{\alpha \varsigma(E)}{1-e^{-\beta E}}\left[E+\beta^{-1} \ln \frac{1+e^{-\beta E_{F}}}{1+e^{\beta\left(E-E_{F}\right)}}\right]
$$

For $E \ll k T$ the PH response function is $\varphi_{P H}(E) \approx \alpha k T$ and is therefore finite at $E=0$ and finite temperature. The actual behavior of $\varphi_{P H}(E)$ at zero temperature and at $T=0.15 \mathrm{~K}$, the latter corresponding to the actual experimental droplet temperature [1,7], is shown in Fig. 1 for a coupling strength $\alpha=1$, and a Fermi energy $E_{F}=4.21 \mathrm{~K}$. The smoothly decreasing tail for positive energy transfers (blue side) is a manifestation of PH excitations, as actually observed in the absorption spectrum of the glyoxal molecule in helium-3 droplets [1,6,7].

### 4. Phonon contribution

The single PH and collective excitations of the Fermi liquid are actually entangled in the global response function $\varphi(E)$, though the good spectral separation in the $(Q, E)$ plane of PH and collective excitations allows for the additivity $\varphi(E) \cong \varphi_{P H}(E)+\varphi_{p h o n}(E)$ where $\varphi_{p h o n}(E)$ is the collective excitation (phonon) response function. The latter has to include the local dynamics within the first ${ }^{3} \mathrm{He}$ solvation shell, generated by the first giant Friedel oscillation [13] (inset of Fig. 2), besides the quasi-continuum of the droplet bulk vibrational spectrum. The main features of the local dynamics, as experimentally found in the absorption spectrum (Fig. 2) [1,7], consist of a small but very sharp peak at $E_{L P}=8.05 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and a quasi-continuum extending up to about $20 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ (two downward arrows in Fig. 2), well above the bulk maxon energy of $\sim 11 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, all neatly appearing on top of the smooth PH wing. The maximum local frequency is well above the bulk maxon energy of $\sim 11 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. Actually the bulk phonons, though included in the calculations, do not show distinct features in the absorption spectrum due to the shielding of the first shell [7].

## 5. Resonant Raman spectrum

The first order Raman spectra of Fermionic helium-3 droplets doped by glyoxal molecule is calculated by using Eq. (13) with the response function $\varphi(\omega)=\varphi_{PH}+\varphi_{phon}$ introduced in the previous Sections and an efficient fast Fourier-transform code. As it follows from Eq. (13), due to the factor $|\Phi(\Omega)-\Phi(\omega_0)|^2/(\omega_0-\Omega)^2$ the shape of the Raman spectrum is remarkably dependent on the excitation frequency $\omega_0$ if the excitation occurs in the blue wing of (and not too far from) the ZPL.

The peaks at Raman frequencies $30\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (left) and $50\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (right) describe the enhancement of the Raman scattering due to the resonance of the scattered light with the zero-phonon radiative transition from the excited state to the ground state. The exact correspondence of the scattered light frequency with the frequency of the radiative zero-phonon transition occurs thanks to the excitation of the Fermi liquid; in both cases the tuning is due to a single PH excitation (no second-order processes are considered here), and the peak intensity rapidly decreases with increasing of excitation frequency due to the drop of the PH tail at larger frequencies.

On the other hand by exciting the resonant Raman scattering in the anti-Stokes (red) side of ZPL, the shape of the first order Raman spectrum follows approximately the shape of the response function $\varphi(E)$, as clearly seen in Fig. 5. In any case, as appears from the present analysis, notably from the comparison of Figs. 4 and 5 with Fig. 2, the response function of a Fermi liquid droplet as probed via the spectroscopy of a chromophore molecule would be far better resolved by means of RRS than by direct absorption measurements.

## 6. Concluding remarks

The unusual vibronic interaction of molecules in $^3$He Fermi liquid droplet was probed via measurements of the vibronic absorption spectrum of the glyoxal molecule at $T=0.15$ K. It was found that the broadened ZPL and the observed decay to the blue tail extending to $\sim40$ cm⁻¹ can be attributed to Fermi excitations. In the theoretical description [1,6,7] of the observed sideband in $^3$He droplets the coupling of the molecule to the single particle-hole and collective excitations of the doped Fermionic droplet was taken into account. The simulation provided a very good description of the observed spectrum.

However, the response function of vibronic excitation reveals itself in absorption spectrum in an indirect way, via the integrated $\exp(g(t))$ factor. For a direct determination of the local PH and collective excitation spectrum of a Fermi liquid surrounding a probe molecule resonant Raman scattering measurements are shown to be the most appropriate. It is shown that by choosing the excitation energy in the anti-Stokes (red) side of the molecule absorption line, the shape of the first order Raman spectrum substantially follows that of the local response function, including however both single PH and collective phonon excitations.

The development during the last decade of intense pulsed He droplet beams [16] actually allows for a sufficient density of droplets, and therefore of CM scattering centers, to make a resonant Raman spectroscopy of $^3$He droplet Fermi excitations possible. This can be estimated as follows. The liquid $^3$He flow (molar volume of $37$ cm³/mole at zero pressure and $T=0.15$ K [17]) through a $5$ $\mu$m dia. orifice at a typical speed of 400 m/s [16] carries $1.7\times10^{20}$ atoms/s. For a pulsed supersonic beam aperture of $2.5\times10^{-3}$ sr and a liquid jet fragmentation into an average droplet size of 1000 atoms [16], at 20 cm from the orifice, where the beam cross section is $1$ cm², there are about $0.32\times10^{13}$ droplets/cm³, i.e., the same concentration of CM scattering centers for a unitary pick-up. This concentration of less than $10^{-11}$ M is still three orders of magnitude below the sensitivity of resonant Raman scattering ($\sim10^{-8}$ M) [18]. Working with larger droplet densities (smaller droplet size), a shorter distance from the source orifice, and in a backscattering geometry with the incident light anti-parallel to the beam so as to elongate the scattering volume, may help attaining the detection threshold even in $^3$He droplets, where the formation or insertion of metal nano-particles for surface-enhanced Raman scattering can hardly be proposed.

Finally we note that the observation of resonance Raman scattering of flying $^3$He droplets is facilitated by a possibility of using strong laser excitation. Indeed, in case of laser beam with the power 10 W ($\sim6\times10^{19}$ photons/s) and aperture 1 cm only two and half photons in average will hit one droplet moving across with the speed 400 m/s; these photons will not harm the droplet. Using $10^{-24}$ cm² for the cross-section of the resonance Raman scattering and taking into account that $\sim1\%$ of droplets are doped by CMs one gets $\sim10^6$ scattered photons per second, which is possible to detect using modern technique.
