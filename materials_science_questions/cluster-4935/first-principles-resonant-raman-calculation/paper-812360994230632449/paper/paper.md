## Abstract

The experimental Raman spectra of some polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) belonging to the $D_{2h}$ symmetry point group, recorded with different exciting laser energies, are presented and compared with simulations obtained by quantum chemical methods in the framework of Albrecht's theory restricted to the A term. It is shown that resonance with the $L_{a}$ state enhances the activity of the low frequencies in the D-band region, while high frequency components of the D-band are preferentially enhanced by resonance with the $B_{a}$ state. Moreover, low frequency vibrations, in the region of acoustic phonons, are identified both by computations and experimentally, which are characteristic fingerprints of the size and shape of the $D_{2h}$ symmetry PAHs.

## 1. Introduction

The Raman spectra of several polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) of well-defined size and structure have been investigated over the last few years both experimentally and theoretically [1–4]. The results obtained from the basis of a *molecular approach* for the study and the interpretation of the Raman spectra of carbon-based materials containing graphitic domains [5,6]. This approach was inspired by the fact that the Raman spectra of PAHs show a very characteristic pattern, similar to that found for several carbon materials. In particular, the Raman spectra of PAHs show two strong (sometimes structured) bands at about 1600 and $1300\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. These lines can be immediately correlated with the so-called G and D bands which are characteristic of the Raman spectra of carbon materials containing $\mathrm{sp}^{2}$ carbon domains [7–9]. Thanks to systematic calculations of vibrational normal modes and Raman [1–3] or resonance Raman [4] activities for several PAHs, we have recently proved that the observed correlation originates from the presence (both in PAHs and in graphitic materials) of characteristic normal vibrations whose Raman activity is particularly strong.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations predicted one strong Raman band in the $1600-\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ region (G-band) of PAHs belonging to $D_{6h}$ symmetry [1–3]. The corresponding vibration is doubly degenerate ($\mathrm{E}_{2\mathrm{g}}$ symmetry species) and its associated nuclear displacements are sketched in Fig. 1(a). A comparison of these eigenvectors with those associated with the only one Raman active phonon of a perfect 2-dimensional graphite sheet, is also reported in Fig. 1(b). This vibration, hereafter referred to as the R mode, can be described as the oscillation of the condensed rings between an aromatic and a quinoidic structure [10]. When the

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Experimental

The powder samples of three PAHs (Fig. 3), were analyzed without any further treatment. To collect the Raman and FT-Raman spectra a small amount of powder was placed on a microscope slide glass. Micro Raman spectra were recorded with a Modular XY Dilor Raman spectrometer coupled to an Olympus BH2 microscope. Three different exciting laser lines were used: the 457.9 nm and 514.5 nm of an $\text{Ar}^{+}$ laser Spectra Physics 2030, and the 632.8-nm of a HeNe Melles Griot 05-LHP-927 laser. As it can be inferred from Fig. 4, the characteristic electronic absorptions of these molecules are close to the wavelengths of the laser used. A $50\times$ microscope objective was used to focus the laser beam on the sample and to collect the scattered light. ### 2.2. Off-resonance and resonance Raman intensities

For a Raman transition between two states $|i\rangle$ and $|f\rangle$,
the intensity is proportional to the square of the transi-
tion polarizability $[\alpha_{\rho\sigma}]_{fi}$ [17]. If the Born-Oppenheimer
approximation is invoked, the vibronic states $|i\rangle$, $|f\rangle$ and
the intermediate state $|r\rangle$ are formed by products of pure
vibrational and pure electronic states. If we assume that
the system is initially and finally in the ground electronic
state $|g\rangle$ we may write $|i\rangle=|{\bf g}n\rangle=|g\rangle|{\bf n}\rangle$, $|f\rangle=|{\bf g}m\rangle=$
$|g\rangle|{\bf m}\rangle$ and $|r\rangle=|e{\bf v}\rangle=|e\rangle|{\bf v}\rangle$, and the transition polar-
izability can be written as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{f i}^{\rho \sigma}= & \frac{1}{\hbar} \sum_{e \mathbf{v}}\left[\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{m}\left|\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{g e}\right| \mathbf{v}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathbf{v}\left|\left[\mu^{\sigma}\right]_{e g}\right| \mathbf{n}\right\rangle}{\omega_{e \mathbf{v}}-\omega_{g \mathbf{n}}-\omega_{l}+\mathrm{i} \Gamma_{e \mathbf{v}}}\right. \\
& \left.\times \frac{\left\langle\mathbf{m}\left|\left[\mu^{\sigma}\right]_{g e}\right| \mathbf{v}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathbf{v}\left|\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{e g}\right| \mathbf{n}\right\rangle}{\omega_{e \mathbf{v}}-\omega_{g \mathbf{m}}+\omega_{l}+\mathrm{i} \Gamma_{e \mathbf{v}}}\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{e\mathbf{v}}$ is a damping factor associated with the
$|g\rangle\leftarrow|e\rangle$ electronic transition and $[\mu^{\rho}]_{ge}$ is the pure
electronic transition moment. The sum in Eq. (1) ex-
tends to all electronic $|e\rangle$ and vibrational $|{\bf v}\rangle$ states.
Notice that $|{\bf v}\rangle$ indicates a multidimensional vibrational
state. $\hbar\omega_{g{\bf n},{\bf m}}$ and $\hbar\omega_{e{\bf v}}$ are the vibronic energies of the
initial, final and intermediate states, and $\hbar\omega_{l}$ is the in-
cident photon energy. Under the conditions for which
the Born-Oppenheimer approximation is valid, the
electronic and vibrational parts of the integrals in Eq.
(1) can be separated:

$$
\langle\mathbf{m}|\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{g e}| \mathbf{v}\rangle=\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{g e}^{0}\langle\mathbf{m} | \mathbf{v}\rangle+\sum_{k}\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{g e}^{\prime}\langle\mathbf{m}|Q_{k}| \mathbf{v}\rangle,
\tag{2}
$$

where the sum runs over the set of $k$ normal coordinates
$Q_k$ and $[\mu^\rho]_{ge}'=(\partial[\mu^\rho]_{ge}/\partial Q_k)_0$. In the Herzberg-Teller
[18] perturbation description of vibronic coupling, the
derivative of the transition dipole-moment can be ex-
pressed in terms of the variation $h_{es}^k$ of the Hamiltonian
with respect to the normal coordinate $Q_k$, which can mix
the state $|e\rangle$ with other states $|s\rangle$ and the analogous $h_{gs}^k$
terms. Substitution of Eq. (2) in (1) leads to an expres-
sion than can be grouped in four terms, that are labeled
the A, B, C and D [17]. The latter three terms contain
expansions that include the $h_{es}^k$ and $h_{gs}^k$ vibronic coupling
parameters, while the A term is given simply by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{1}{\hbar} \sum_{e}\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{g e}^{0}\left[\mu^{\sigma}\right]_{e g}^{0} \sum_{\mathbf{v}}\left[\frac{\langle\mathbf{m} | \mathbf{v}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v} | \mathbf{n}\rangle}{\omega_{e \mathbf{v}}-\omega_{g \mathbf{n}}-\omega_{l}+\mathrm{i} \Gamma_{e \mathbf{v}}}\right. \\
& \left.\quad \times \frac{\langle\mathbf{m} | \mathbf{v}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v} | \mathbf{n}\rangle}{\omega_{e \mathbf{v}}-\omega_{g \mathbf{m}}+\omega_{l}+\mathrm{i} \Gamma_{e \mathbf{v}}}\right]=\alpha_{f i}^{\rho \sigma} \quad \text { (A term). }
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$

In this study, we restrict the attention to the contribu-
tion of the above term, that is, we consider only the
Franck-Condon effects and ignore vibronic coupling.
The reason for this is that, although the Raman spectra
of PAHs show, in general, bands that are due to vib-
ronic coupling mechanisms, these effects are localized on
a limited spectral region, namely the G-band region,
where intensity modulations with the shape of the PAHs
are minor. In addition, the lower symmetry of the $D_{2h}$
PAHs studied in this work, reduces further the impor-
tance of vibronic coupling effects by increasing the
number of totally symmetric vibrations (TS), i.e., the
main type of vibrations that gain intensity through the A
term.

If we neglect vibronic coupling effects, Eq. (3) is
completely general, and can be used to calculate either
resonance and off-resonance Raman intensities. For a

given electronic transition, $[\mu^{\rho}]_{ge}^{0}$ and $[\mu^{\sigma}]_{eg}^{0}$ are constants and the modulation of vibronic intensities occurs because of the vibrational overlaps.

The multi-dimensional vibrational overlap integrals in Eq. (3) can be expanded in terms of the contributions from non-totally symmetric (NTS) and TS vibrations as follows:
$$
\langle\mathbf{m}|\mathbf{v}\rangle=\prod_{l}^{\mathrm{NTS}}\left\langle m_{l} \mid v_{l}\right\rangle \prod_{k}^{\mathrm{TS}}\left\langle m_{k} \mid v_{k}\right\rangle,\qquad(4)
$$

NTS modes can contribute to vibrational overlaps (other than the $\langle 0|0\rangle$) in case of large frequency variations upon excitations, but generally these effects are negligible. Thus, to a good approximation, according to the Franck-Condon mechanism only TS vibrational modes can contribute to Raman bands. In other words, we can restrict the calculation of vibrational integrals to TS modes since for the $l$th NTS vibration $|n_{l}\rangle=|v_{l}\rangle=|m_{l}\rangle=0$. The latter TS vibrational overlap integrals are readily evaluated by further adopting the harmonic approximation, and by assuming identical vibrational frequencies and normal coordinates (no Dushinsky rotation [19]) in the ground and excited electronic states. Analytical [20] or recurrence formulas [17,21,22] can be easily employed to this end. Vibrational overlap integrals are expressed in terms of the dimensionless displacement parameters $B_{k}$ [23] that can be calculated for each $k$th TS vibration according to the following relation:
$$
B_{k}=\left(\frac{\omega_{k}}{\hbar}\right)^{1 / 2} Q_{k}^{e, g} ; \quad Q_{k}^{e, g}=\left[\mathbf{x}_{g}-\mathbf{x}_{e}\right] \mathbf{M}^{1 / 2} \mathbf{L}_{k},\qquad(5)
$$
where $Q_{k}^{e,g}$ represents the projection of the geometry change upon $|g\rangle \to |e\rangle$ electronic excitation, expressed in Cartesian coordinates, onto the normal coordinate $Q_{k}$ [24]. In the above formula $\mathbf{x}_{g}$ ($\mathbf{x}_{e}$) is the $3N$-dimensional vector of the equilibrium Cartesian coordinates computed for the ground (excited) state, $\mathbf{M}$ is the $3N \times 3N$ diagonal matrix of the atomic masses, and $\mathbf{L}_{k}$ is the $3N$ vector describing the normal coordinate $Q_{k}$ in terms of mass-weighted Cartesian coordinates.

For a fundamental band of a TS mode $k$ in the Raman spectrum, the product of multi-dimensional vibrational overlap integrals in Eq. (3) reads:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\mathbf{1}|\mathbf{v}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{0}\rangle & =\left\langle 1_{k} \mid v_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle v_{k} \mid 0_{k}\right\rangle \prod_{p \neq k}^{\mathrm{TS}}\left\langle 0_{p} \mid v_{p}\right\rangle\left\langle v_{p} \mid 0_{p}\right\rangle \\
& =\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\left(B_{k}^{2} / 2\right)}}{\sqrt{2}} \frac{2 v_{k} B_{k}^{\left(2 v_{k}-1\right)}-B_{k}^{\left(2 v_{k}+1\right)}}{2^{v_{k}} v_{k}!} \prod_{p \neq k}^{\mathrm{TS}} \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\left(B_{p}^{2} / 2\right)} B_{p}^{2 v_{p}}}{2^{v_{p}} v_{p}!}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that Eq. (3) can be drastically simplified if only one excited electronic state $|e\rangle$ contributes to the Raman scattering and if we assume that the sum running over the vibrational states is dominated by $\mathbf{v}=0$. These conditions are satisfied, for instance, if we are in perfect resonance with a 0-0 electronic transition, well separated in energy from other dipole-allowed electronic transitions. In this case, the contribution from the A term simplifies to:
$$
\left(\alpha_{f i}^{\rho \sigma}\right)_{k}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left[\mu^{\rho}\right]_{g e}^{0}\left[\mu^{\sigma}\right]_{e g}^{0} \frac{\frac{B_{k}}{\sqrt{2}} \exp \left(-\sum_{p}^{\mathrm{TS}} \frac{B_{p}^{2}}{2}\right)}{\omega_{e 0}-\omega_{g 0}-\omega_{l}+\mathrm{i} \Gamma_{e v}},\qquad(7)
$$
where the second term in Eq. (3) has been neglected because of the resonance condition. The above relation shows that to evaluate Raman intensities the computation of the $B_{k}$ parameters is essential. Indeed $(\alpha_{f i})_{k} \propto B_{k}$ and the Raman intensity of the $k$-th fundamental vibration is proportional to $B_{k}^{2}/2$. The evaluation of the $B_{k}$ parameters, in turn, requires the determination of equilibrium structures of the ground state and of the excited electronic states that can contribute, through their non-negligible transition dipole moments, to the sum in Eq. (3). Relation (7) also shows that, under the conditions discussed above and, specifically, for fundamentals of TS vibrations, the effect of the remaining vibrations in the multi-dimensional approach is simply to scale the $(\alpha_{f i})_{k}$ polarizabilities by the factor $\exp\left(-\sum_{p}^{\mathrm{TS}} \frac{B_{p}^{2}}{2}\right)$.

The simplifications discussed above will be useful to rationalize in a simple way the computed and observed Raman intensities presented in Section 4. The simulated spectra were however computed by using the more complete Eq. (3) and the following expression of the intensity as a function of the polarizability tensor $\alpha_{k}$ [16]:
$$
\frac{I_{k}}{I_{\text {laser }}}=\frac{8 \pi}{9 c^{4}}\left(\omega_{\text {laser }}-\omega_{k}\right)^{4} \alpha_{k}^{2} ; \quad \alpha_{k}^{2} \equiv \sum_{\rho \sigma}\left\|\alpha_{k}^{\rho \sigma}\right\|^{2}.\qquad(8)
$$

According to Eq. (3), two main parameters ($B_{k}$ and $[\mu_{ge}]^{0}$) are responsible for intensity modulations with respect to the laser light employed. Different excited states can enhance different vibrational modes through the $B_{k}$ values, which are strictly related to the geometry change upon excitation to a specific excited state. Furthermore, each excited state will contribute with a weight related to its transition dipole moment $[\mu_{ge}]^{0}$.

The simulated spectra were plotted using a Lorentzian lineshape with a bandwidth of $10 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and $\Gamma_{e}$ (Eq. (3)) was fixed at $500 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The large value of $\Gamma_{e}$, which is consistent with the broad envelope observed in absorption spectra, takes into account not only the intrinsic lifetime of the excited state but also inhomogeneous broadening along with solid-state effects.

The molecular parameters required to evaluate the A term were obtained with the semi-empirical quantum consistent force field for pi electrons (QCFF/PI) Hamiltonian [25], already used in several spectroscopical studies on conjugated carbon systems [4,26-28]. In particular, for each PAH we determined the equilibrium

structure of the ground state and of the dipole-allowed excited states directly involved in resonance Raman phenomena (see Section 3) along with the ground state vibrational frequencies and normal modes. Accordingly, the displacement parameters $B_k$ were obtained for the selected dipole-allowed electronic transitions.

## 3. Electronic excited states and absorption spectra

The electronic absorption spectra of PAHs in the UV–Vis region are rationalized in terms of electronic transitions to four dipole-allowed excited states labelled ${\rm L_b}$, ${\rm L_a}$, ${\rm B_b}$ and ${\rm B_a}$ in Platt's notation [29]. By definition, the $a$ states have nodes, in transition densities, through the bonds and the $b$ states through the atoms. From a theoretical point of view, these four states are described by single electronic excitations and are dominated by the following four: $(\mathrm{H} \rightarrow \mathrm{L}+1),(\mathrm{H}-1 \rightarrow \mathrm{L}),\ (\mathrm{H} \rightarrow \mathrm{L})$ and $(\mathrm{H}-1 \rightarrow \mathrm{L}+1)$. Owing to their single excitation nature, the excited states of the three PAHs studied in this work were computed by using configuration interactions restricted to single excitations (CIS) and the ${\rm L_b}$, ${\rm L_a}$, ${\rm B_b}$ and ${\rm B_a}$ states were identified as indicated in Table 1. The ${\rm L_b}$ and ${\rm L_a}$ states are dipole-forbidden for ${\rm D_{6h}}$ symmetry PAHs but become allowed in ${\rm D_{2h}}$ symmetry. ### 4. Discussion of the Raman spectra

We have recently shown that the Raman spectra of $D_{6h}$ PAHs such as C42 or C114, are weakly sensitive to the variation of excitation energy $\omega_{\mathrm{laser}}$ in the re- gion of the lowest dipole-allowed transition [4]. In contrast, the changes observed in the Raman spectra of $D_{2h}$ symmetry PAHs presented here are more

significant and interesting. The spectra show a more complex D-band region which is laser sensitive (see Figs. 6-8). The intensity modulation observed upon changing the laser energy can be rationalized in terms of the symmetry lowering compared to $D_{6h}$ PAHs. Indeed, as it will be seen below, the observed variation of Raman spectra is greater for $D_{2h}$ molecules whose $L_X/L_Y$ ratio is larger, as expected owing to a more remarkable symmetry breaking as compared with $D_{6h}$ symmetry PAHs.

Symmetry lowering acts on vibrations and electronic states. It increases the number of TS vibrational modes and as a consequence, the number of Raman active transitions that can be observed and computed in a narrow spectral region. In addition, other electronic excited states $(L_a, L_b)$, beside the $B_a, B_b$ states, become dipole-allowed and can contribute, to the sum in Eq. (3), although calculations indicate that the $L_b$ state plays a negligible role. The above two factors will be taken into account in the following discussion.

The experimental Raman spectra of C114 (see Fig. 6) show the presence of at least three bands whose relative intensities in the D region are very sensitive to the laser energy. This increase is so large that the spectrum obtained with the 457.9 nm laser shows a D-band stronger than the G-band. In addition, the experimental spectra show that, when the laser energy increases, the G peak becomes structured. The G-band of the spectra obtained with infrared (1064 nm) and red (632.8 nm) excitations appears as made up by a single peak whereas the G-band obtained with green (514.5 nm) and blue (457.9 nm) lasers is made up by a clear doublet.

Recalling that the Raman intensity is roughly proportional to $B_k^2$ (Section 2.2), this implies that the computed 1320 cm$^{-1}$ band (which we assign to the observed 1338 cm$^{-1}$ band), is very weak for excitation wavelength of 632.8 nm. This state has slightly lower $B_k$ values for the modes at 1259, 1269, 1277 and 1288 cm$^{-1}$, and a significant displacement parameter for the modes at 1320 and 1314 cm$^{-1}$. Thus, the observed, increased activity of the 1338 cm$^{-1}$ band is nicely explained by the calculations in terms of increased displacement parameters when the light approaches resonance with the $B_a$ state. In other words, the Raman activity in the D-band region moves from lower to higher frequencies as the laser energy increases and approaches the $B_a$ state. As it will be shown, this trend occurs for all the PAHs studied in this work.

With regard to the G-band region, we notice that QCFF/PI overestimates the frequency of the active band. Frequency overestimate has no effect, however, on the ability of the method to predict the Raman activity, since the latter depends on the magnitude of the $B_k$ parameters. The activity is, indeed, reproduced accurately for the lowest two spectra in Fig. 6. However, the simulations do not account for the doublet in the experimental spectra obtained with green and blue excitation lights (Fig. 6). Notice that, in contrast with $D_{6h}$ symmetry PAHs [4], activity is predicted in this region by Albrecht's A term, owing to the fact that for $D_{2h}$ symmetry PAHs there is a TS mode that correlates with one component of the $E_{2g}$ mode of $D_{6h}$ symmetry PAHs (see Section 1). Thus, the origin of the doublet observed in the highest two spectra in Fig. 6 can be ascribed to the activity of both the $A_g$ and $B_{1g}$ components which correlate with the $E_{2g}$ mode of $D_{6h}$ PAHs (see Fig. 1). Since the present simulations of Raman intensities are restricted to the activity of TS modes, only the activity of the longitudinal R mode ($A_g$) appears in the simulated spectra. Non-totally symmetric modes (NTS) gain intensity through higher terms in Albrecht's expansion [16], owing the existence of non-negligible vibronic couplings between electronic excited states, mediated by vibrations of appropriate symmetry [17]. In this case, the additional activity observed with green and blue laser lights is probably due to the coupling of the $B_a$ and $B_b$ states through the $B_{1g}$ mode. The frequency of the $B_{1g}$ component of the doublet (1617 cm$^{-1}$), extracted from the experimental spectra, is higher than the $A_g$ component (1602 cm$^{-1}$), in nice agreement with QCFF/PI computed frequencies (1699 cm$^{-1}$ ($B_{1g}$) and 1681 cm$^{-1}$ ($A_g$), see Fig. 1(c)) and with previous DFT calculations [3].

As in the case of C114, the experimental Raman spectra of C78 (see Fig. 7) show a redistribution of the

intensity from low to high frequencies within the D re-
gion by increasing the laser energy. The frequency of the
G-band is not sensitive to the laser energy, but when
using the green laser its intensity reaches a maximum
relative to the D-band, a trend nicely reproduced by the
simulations depicted in Fig. 7. The intensity modulation
in the G-band region can be explained by considering
the energy level scheme in Fig. 5 along with the $B_k$ values
collected in Table 3. It is seen that the green laser falls in
the middle between the $L_a$ and the $B_a$ states while red
and blue lights are almost in resonance with the $L_a$ and
$B_a$ states, respectively. The computed $B_k$ parameter of
the $1682$-cm$^{-1}$ mode, which is assigned to the observed
$1607$ cm$^{-1}$ band, is larger for the $L_a$ than for the $B_a$ and
$B_b$ states, and this explains the reduced activity of this
band computed for excitation in the blue, as compared
with excitation in the red. In addition, the green light
appears to be in the most appropriate energetic position
to sum up the effects of enhancement of the $1682$-cm$^{-1}$
band from both the $L_a$ and $B_a$ states. A contribution to
the intensity of the G-band from a NTS mode (similarly
to C114) cannot be ruled out although the experimental
spectra do not show evidence of doublets in this case.
Notice, however, that the frequency of the $B_{1g}$ compo-
nent might be closer to the $A_g$ component owing to the
reduced $L_X/L_Y$ ratio as compared to C114. In addition,
according to the mechanism proposed in the previous
subsection, the activity of the NTS component is ex-
pected to become substantial as the excitation light ap-
proaches resonance with the $B_a$ state, that is, only for
the blue light (see Fig. 5).

The simulation of the intensity redistribution in the
D-band region (Fig. 7) with respect to the three excita-
tion energies employed, shows the correct trend (i.e.,
enhancement of the higher frequency side of the D-band
region while approaching the $B_a$ state resonance). In-
spection of the displacement parameters in Table 3
shows indeed that modes with frequencies smaller than
$1286$ cm$^{-1}$ are preferentially enhanced by resonance
with the $L_a$ state, while modes with frequencies higher
than $1286$ cm$^{-1}$ are preferentially enhanced by reso-
nance with the $B_a$ and $B_b$ states. Nevertheless, the
agreement is only qualitative, since the $1286$-cm$^{-1}$ mode
always dominates the simulated spectra, similarly to $D_{6h}$
symmetry PAHs. In this sense, it seems that QCFF/PI
underestimates the perturbation that leads to
$D_{6h} \rightarrow D_{2h}$ symmetry breaking in C78, and predicts its
Raman activity still very similar to a $D_{6h}$ PAH of the
same size. The observed bands are tentatively assigned
in the last column of Table 3.

## 5. Raman activity in the low frequency region

The computed $B_k$ parameters reported in Tables 2–4 show that Raman activities are expected, beside the D and G-band regions, also in the very low frequency region (100–400 cm⁻¹), especially when the laser light is in resonance or pre-resonance with the Lₐ and Bₐ states. Prompted by these predictions, we recorded the Raman spectra also in this spectral region and the resulting measured spectra are compared with the simulated spectra in Fig. 9. Because of the fixed experimental excitation wavelengths, the pre-resonance or quasi-resonance conditions were not exactly the same for the three experimental spectra recorded in the low frequency region. Nevertheless, the light employed was always close to the excitation energy of the Lₐ state which, hence, dominated the intensity distribution. For this reason, the simulations were carried out, for simplicity, by assuming perfect resonance (0–0 transition) with such electronic state. In addition, to enhance the intensity of the low frequency modes, the simulations reported in Fig. 9 (dashed lines) were obtained with a damping factor $\Gamma_e = 100$ cm⁻¹, although, as discussed in Section 2.2, a larger value is more consistent with inhomogeneous sources of broadening and solid-state effects. For the above reasons, the simulated intensities in Fig. 9 must be taken as qualitative. The scope is simply to show that some activity is expected, on the basis of computed parameters, in the low frequency region, activity which is indeed observed in the experimental Raman spectra measured in pre- or quasi-resonance with the Lₐ state. In particular, the computed frequencies match almost perfectly the experimental frequencies (see the assignment proposed in the last column of Tables 2–4). These signals are very specific for a given molecule of a given size and allow to directly identify the molecule itself. Inspection of the vibrational eigenvectors associated with these Raman transitions allows to assign the strongest experimental lines to longitudinal and transversal modes which describe an in-plane expansion of the whole molecule along its two principal axes, respectively (see Fig. 10). These modes correlate with the in-plane acoustic phonons in the limit of the infinite graphene sheet. As in the case of the D peak, the active vibrations are TS and they shift toward lower frequencies as the dimension of the molecule increases. ## 6. Conclusions

We have presented a combined experimental and computational study of the pre-resonance and resonance Raman modulation of vibrational activities for three PAHs of $D_{2h}$ symmetry. The most significant intensity redistribution occurs in the D-band region and both experimental and simulated spectra show that resonance with the $L_a$ state enhances the activity of modes in the low frequency region of the D-band while high fre- quency vibrations are preferentially activated by reso- nance with the $B_a$ state. The redistribution of intensities is more substantial for PAHs characterized by a larger $L_X/L_Y$ ratio, that is by a more remarkable symmetry breaking (with respect to the $D_{6h}$ symmetry point group). The calculations follow the same trend but un- derestimate the symmetry breaking effects. This results in a quantitative description of the Raman intensity redistribution for C114 but only qualitative agreement in the case of C60.

In the G-band region, the model employed in this work to simulate Raman intensities (Albrecht's A term) accounts for most of the observed intensity, but activity of NTS modes is clearly identified in the case of C114 and C60.

Finally, low frequency vibrations which become Ra- man active in pre-resonance or resonance with the $L_a$ or $B_a$ states have been identified both experimentally and by the simulations. These frequencies can be con- sidered experimental markers to identify in a simple and precise way the structure and dimension of the PAH investigated.
