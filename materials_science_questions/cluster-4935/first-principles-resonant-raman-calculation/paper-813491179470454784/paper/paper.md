## ABSTRACT

This work focuses on a bundle of two van der Waals-coupled single wall carbon nanotubes. The non-resonant Raman spectra of inhomogeneous dimers of single walled of carbon nanotubes are calculated in the framework of spectral moments method, together with a bond-polarizability model. The evolution of the low frequency Raman-active modes as a function of the nanotube diameters is discussed. Effects of van der Waals coupling on charge transfer processes are also investigated. The obtained results are compared to experimental Raman data measured on dimer sample.

## Introduction

The importance of developing new types of energy is evident from the fact that the global energy consumption has been accelerated at an alarming rate due to the rapid economic worldwide expansion, increase in world population, and ever increasing human reliance on energy based appliances. Many researchers have devoted their efforts to study nanomaterials such as carbon nanotubes [1] and graphene [2] due to their various remarkable chemical, physical and mechanical properties [3,4]. Carbon nanotube and derivatives offer exciting possibilities for photovoltaic with hydrogen storage applications [3,4] because of the ability to engineer and tailor their optical and electronic properties. In fact, carbon nano-materials and nanotechnologies have been demonstrated to be an enabling technology for creating high-performance energy-conversion and storage devices [5,6].

Carbon nanotubes represent a new class of materials for investigating fundamental one dimensional (1D) physics and for exploring nanoelectronics and molecular electronics [7]. Among the many interesting properties exhibited by nanotubes, electronic properties are arguably the most significant one. Double-walled carbon nanotubes and dimers of SCNT provide unique model systems for the study of the charge transfer process and van der Waals coupling effects at the nanoscale [9]. Dimers of two identical (non-identical) SCNTs, the so-called homogeneous (inhomogeneous) dimers, have been recently synthesized using the chemical vapor deposition method [10]. The dimer structure of two free-standing SCNTs (15,11) and (16,12) is clearly observed from transmission electron microscopy pictures image [10] (Fig. 1). Dimers are characterized by weak van der Waals interactions between the neighboring carbons of its two constituting SCNTs. Three electronic configurations are considered: Sc–Sc, Sc–M and M–M, where Sc(resp.M) stands for semiconducting (resp.metallic) SCNT.

Raman spectroscopy is known as a powerful tool for studying the vibrational properties of carbon nanotubes [11], especially the dependence of these properties as a function of the tube diameter and chirality [12–14]. For long time, it has been theoretically demonstrated a one-to-one correspondence between the atomic structure of a SCNT, especially its diameter, and the frequency of a low-frequency Raman active mode, the so-called radial breathing mode (RBM).

Recently, it was stated theoretically, and confirmed experimentally, that the RBM frequency vs Diameter (d) relationship can be expressed as: $\omega_{RBM}=228/d\sqrt{1+Cd^{2}}$ with $C = 0$ for individual environmental-free SCNTs [15], and $C \neq 0$ for SCNTs in interaction with an environment such as SCNTs sitting on a substrate, surrounded by different surfactants, or in bundles (Ref. [16] and references therein). In SCNT bundles, the origin of the C term mainly results from competition between the intertube interactions leading to an upshift of the radial breathing mode (called radial breathing-like mode (RBLM) in SCNT bundle) of about $8-16\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, depending on the tube diameter, with respect to the RBM of isolated SCNTs [17,18].

In a previous paper, using the spectral moments method (SMM) [19,20] in the framework of the bond-polarization theory, the polarized Raman spectra of SCNT bundle and DWNTs have been calculated. The dependence of the Raman spectra with the diameter, chirality and length was investigated. In this theoretical work, we are first interested in the Raman spectra of dimers. The results of calculations concerning the low-frequency breathing like modes (BLMs) and hight-frequency tangential-like modes (TLM) of dimers of SCNTs are compared with the few available experimental Raman data. Next, we report the results of the calculated band structure and the electronic density of states (eDOS) for isolated SCNT dimers.

# Models and method

A dimer consists of two free-standing SCNTs. The optimal inter-SCNT gap was calculated by minimization of the van der Waals interactions between adjacent SCNTs. A Lennard-Jones potential, $U_{LJ}(r)=4\varepsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12}-(\sigma/r)^{6}]$, was considered to describe the SCNT–SCNT interactions. In all our calculations, we use the parameters $\varepsilon = 2.964$ meV and $\sigma = 0.3407$ nm. For the different dimers investigated, the intertube interaction energy was minimized with respect to the interlayer separation, the relative angle of rotation of the layers around the tube axis and their relative translation along the tube axis. The optimal intertube gap is found to be around 0.34 nm.

The dynamical matrix of dimers is built block-by-block from the dynamical matrices of each constituting SCNTs: the matrix of the two SCNTs form the bi-diagonal block of the dimer one, while the matrix associated to the SCNT–SCNT interactions form the off-diagonal block. Intratube interactions are represented by the force constants model introduced by Rubio et al. [21] and previously used by our group to study the nonresonant Raman spectra of SCNTs [22].

To enhance the Raman response of dimers, we use the empirical nonresonant bond polarizability model in which the polarization is only modulated by the nearest-neighbor bonds [23]. The empirical parameters used in this study are taken from Ref. [7]. The usual method to calculate the Raman response requires a direct diagonalization of the dynamical matrix of the system. However, when the system under study contains a large number of atoms, the dynamical matrix is very large and its diagonalization fails or requires long computing time. In such cases, the SMM appears as a very powerful method as it allows to compute directly the Raman spectra without diagonalizing the dynamical matrix [20].

The eDOS calculations are based on the microscopic tight-binding Hamiltonian which includes the coupling of the carbon $\pi$ orbitals within a nanotube [24,25]. The coupling between nanotubes in a dimer is described by an Hamiltonian previously used to study the local density of states of semiconductor-metal configuration of a double-wall carbon nanotubes [26].

# Results and discussion

## Non-resonant Raman spectrum

In this section, we report the calculated polarized Raman spectra for infinite inhomogeneous dimers of SCNTs. The calculations are performed on infinite systems obtained by applying periodic boundary conditions on unit cells of the

considered nanotubes constituting dimer. We mainly discuss the ZZ polarized Raman spectra for which the polarization direction of the incident and scattered light are along the z direction.

Recent Raman experiment have been performed on (15,11)-(16,12) dimer sample. In order to understand the measured data, studies of dimers with different structure parameters have been done. In real experiments, the chiralities of the two nanotubes of a dimer have numerous possible combinations. A great advantage of the spectral moments method is to allow the calculations of the Raman spectrum for all SCNT chirality combinations in dimers, especially with a significant number of atoms. Samples containing more than 20,000 atoms are treated.

To investigate the dependence of the Raman spectra of dimers as a function of the diameter of the nanotubes, four chiral-chiral dimers have been considered: (7,3)-(8,4), (11,7)-(12,8), (15,11)-(16,12) and (19,15)-(20,16) with diameters close to 6.96-8.29, 12.31-13.65, 17.70-19.06 and 23.12-24.47 Å, respectively. The chiral angles of these tubes are close to $25^\circ$. The calculated ZZ Raman spectra, in the Breathing Mode (panel A) and tangential mode (panel B) regions, are shown in Fig. 2.

In the low frequency region, the ZZ polarized Raman spectrum of the (15-11)-(16-12) dimer displays four peaks located at 102 (BM4), 118 (BM3), 125 (BM2) and $128\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (BM1). All these modes result from the coupling of all the radial breathing modes of isolated tubes calculated at 125 and $116\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ for (15,11) and (16,12), respectively. The eigenvector displacements of the radial breathing-like modes (RBLMs) of (10,0)-(9,0) dimer are given in Fig. 3 (the arrows indicate the magnitude and direction of carbon atom displacements). We have found that BLM2 (resp.BLM3) mainly corresponds to the coupling of the RBM of isolated (10,0) (resp.(9,0)) tube. The BLM1 and BLM4 peaks are assigned to hybridation between radial breathing modes and the degenerate $E_n$ modes of individual SCNTs.

The comparison between the spectra in Fig. 2 shows that hybridation between the breathing modes increases with increasing the diameters of the tubes. For the small diameter dimer (7,3)-(8,4), the Raman spectrum displays two peaks located at $268\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ and $318\ \text{cm}^{-1}$. These modes correspond to the coupling of the RBM of isolated (8,4) and (7,3) tubes, meaning that the van der Waals interactions between tubes have no significant effect on Raman-active modes. When the diameter of dimer increases, we state that additional RBLMs appear around the RBM of isolated tubes constituting dimer.

The presence of additional modes as compared to the two expected RBM is attributed to the lowering of the symmetry in dimers [27,28]. It is tempting to assign the lower-frequency pair of peaks (102 and $118\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) to vibrations of the (16,12) tube and the higher-frequency pair (125 and $128\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) to the vibrations of (15,11) one. In Fig. 3, we present the vibrations of the three modes (BM2 ($118\ \text{cm}^{-1}$), BM3 ($125\ \text{cm}^{-1}$), BM4 ($128\ \text{cm}^{-1}$)) in (15,11)-(16,12). We observe that BM3 (resp. BM2) correspond to the radial breathing mode of the (16, 12) (resp.(15,11)) tube.

In the TLM region, the ZZ polarized Raman spectra show four peaks denoted by TM1, TM2, TM3 and TM4. The first two modes correspond to the Transverse-Optical modes (TO), whereas, the two other ones correspond to Longitudinal-Optical modes (LO) of the semiconducting carbon nanotubes. ## Conclusion

We presented in this paper a Raman study of van der Waals coupled carbon nanostructures for a dimer of SCNTs. The Raman spectrum of an individual index-identified inhomo- geneous dimer is reported. In agreement with experimental data, the spectrum displays four BM peaks: a lower-frequency pair of peaks around RBM position of the (16, 12) SCNT and a higher-frequency pair of peaks around RBM position of the (15,11) SCNT. The splitting of the modes is due to the lowering of the symmetry in dimers induced by van der Waals in- teractions. From electronic properties study of a Sc-Sc dimer, the charge transfer effect caused by this interface is stated. Let us note that work on Sc-M and M-M SCNT dimer interfaces are in progress.
