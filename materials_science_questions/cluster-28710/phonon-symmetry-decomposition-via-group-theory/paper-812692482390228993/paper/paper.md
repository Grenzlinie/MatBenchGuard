Abstract. NbS₃ type I is commensurately modulated and the structure is known. NbS₃ type
II is incommensurately modulated and the structure has not been determined. Starting from
a superspace-group description of NbS₃ type I, a proposal is presented for the modulation
unit cell and superspace group for NbS₃ type II which meets the following criteria: (i) the
basic structure unit cell is approximately a simple multiple of the unit cell of the NbS₃ type
I basic structure; (ii) the Fermi level, deduced for a simple bonding electron count, is in
agreement with the observed incommensurate superlattice spot positions; and (iii) the
modulation superspace group and the corresponding dualistic notation is given by

$$\mathrm{A} \begin{array}{cc}
\mathrm{P} 2_{1} / \mathrm{m} & \mathrm{m} \mathrm{P} 2_{1} / \mathrm{m} \\
1 & \overline{1}
\end{array} \stackrel{\mathrm{m}}{=} \begin{array}{c} 
\\
c
\end{array} \mathrm{~B} 2 / \mathrm{m}$$

and leads to extinction rules that are the same as those deduced from electron diffraction
patterns. A normal-mode analysis, which is independent of the above, has also been carried
out. This approach leads to extinction rules and modulation symmetry conditions that are
identical to those deduced from the superspace-group analysis. A phase transition that
occurs in the type-II modulation structure at about 350 K is interpreted on the basis of the
crystallographic description.

## 1. Introduction

NbS₃ belongs to the family of transition-metal trichalcogenides MX₃ (M = Nb, Ta, Ti,
Zr or Hf; X = S, Se or Te). The structures of these compounds are all based on trigonal
prisms of chalcogen atoms with a metal atom near the centre of each prism. These prisms
are stacked base-to-base to give extended columnar cages containing linear chains of
metal atoms. There has been widespread interest in these compounds in recent years
because in several cases the metal-atom chains undergo a Peierls distortion giving rise
to a charge-density wave (CDW) in the crystal. This CDW may move under the action of an
electric field and give rise to non-linear conductivity effects, which have been extensively
studied (Monceau 1985).

The structures of most of the $MX_3$ compounds have been determined by x-ray diffraction. Adjacent columns are displaced by one-half of a prism height for the simplest $MX_3$ structure, $ZrSe_3$ (Krönert and Plieth 1965). The columns are interconnected via M-X inter-column bonds. The bicapped trigonal prisms $MX_{6+2}$ are generally of at least two slightly different types with triangular bases that are equilateral or isosceles-like (Whangbo 1986), with corresponding $(X^{2-})_3$ and $X^{2-}(X_2)^{2-}$ oxidation states of the chalcogen atoms. The unit-cell sizes depend on the number of column types and their stacking arrangement so that the unit cell may contain from two columns $(ZrSe_3)$ to 24 columns (orthorhombic $TaS_3$) (Meerschaut and Rouxel 1986).

This paper concerns $NbS_3$, for which several polytypes have been reported (Monceau 1985), although only type I ($NbS_3$-I) and type II ($NbS_3$-II) have been confirmed and investigated in some detail. The $NbS_3$-I structure was determined by Rijnsdorp and Jellinek (1978) and shown to be a superstructure of the simple $ZrSe_3$ structure. The Nb and S atoms, which would occupy the 2e positions in the undistorted parent-structure space group $P2_1/m$ with $Z = 2$, undergo minor shifts that result in Nb-Nb pairs. Thus a commensurate CDW distortion gives rise to the space group $P\overline{1}$ ($Z = 4$) with both Nb and S in 2i positions. Neglecting the slight deformation, the chemical formula can, in analogy with $ZrSe_3$, be written $Nb^{4+}(S_2)^{2-}S^{2-}$. This was confirmed by x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements (Rijnsdorp 1978, Endo *et al* 1981, Meerschaut 1983). Bullet (1980) calculated the electronic structure of $NbS_3$-I and showed that the Nb-Nb pairing stabilises the structure by creating a 0.5 eV semiconducting gap. Stacking faults with Burgers vectors parallel to the trigonal columns were first observed in this material by Cornelissens *et al* (1978) using electron microscopy. Diffuse x-ray scattering in $NbS_3$-I was attributed to stacking disorder by Iwazumi *et al* (1986).

$NbS_3$-II was first reported by Cornelissens *et al* (1978), who showed that electron diffraction patterns from these crystals contained doublets of incommensurate satellites close to $\frac{1}{3}c^*$ ($c$ is the axis of the columns). The satellites were observed to be strongly streaked perpendicular to the $c^*$ axis. It was shown later (Boswell and Prodan 1980) that the positions of the satellites were unchanged on cooling to 100 K and that the spots could be explained on the basis of two overlapping periodic distortions. In a more detailed electron diffraction study, Roucau (1983) indexed the diffraction patterns on the basis of a subcell that was an enlargement of the $NbS_3$-I monoclinic cell. The parameters used were $a = 9.9$ Å, $b = 3.4$ Å, $c = 18.3$ Å and $\beta = 97^\circ$. The distortion was attributed to two wavevectors, $\boldsymbol{q}_1$ ($\frac{1}{2}a^*, 0.297b^*, 0$) and $\boldsymbol{q}_2$ ($\frac{1}{2}a^*, 0.353b^*, 0$). It was reported by Monceau (1985) that one of the doublet pairs disappeared on heating at about 350 K.

Although the structure of commensurately modulated $NbS_3$-I has been determined by x-ray methods, that of the incommensurately modulated $NbS_3$-II is at present unknown. This is due to problems associated with the very small fibre cross sections obtained for this polytype and the fact that these crystals contain very high densities of defects (see the following section). However, we have deduced a plausible arrangement of the columns and the symmetry of the modulated structure of $NbS_3$-II. This result has been attained in several steps, combining experimental data and theoretical deductions as follows: (i) The known modulated structure of $NbS_3$-I has been described using the superspace-group description (de Wolff *et al* 1981, Yamamoto *et al* 1985) and also in terms of the dualistic interpretation (de Wolff 1984) where the space groups of both the basic structure and modulation are given separately. (ii) The reciprocal lattice of $NbS_3$-II has been deduced from electron diffraction data. (iii) Using Wilson's approach to the relationship between bands, bonds and charge-density waves in the $NbSe_3$ family

of compounds (Wilson 1979), the arrangement of columns in the $NbS_3$-II unit cell has been deduced. (iv) The superspace-group description of $NbS_3$-II has been determined.

Normal-mode analysis (e.g. Pérez-Mato *et al* 1986) has also been carried out and it is shown that the extinctions and symmetry of the modulated $NbS_3$-II structure deduced from this approach are in good agreement with that from the superspace-group analysis.

Projections of both the basic and modulation structures of $NbS_3$-I are shown in figure 7. For the basic structure, figure 7(a), the atoms are shown, whereas for the modulation structure, figure 7(b), positions of equal phasing of the modulation wave in adjacent columns are shown. Trigonal prisms are outlined by broken lines for reference. Unspecti- fied shifts of the Nb atoms, as indicated by small bars in figure 7(b), are different for neighbouring Nb atoms in the same column. The lattice parameters we have used are those of Rijnsdorp and Jellinek (1978) which, after a change of axes, become $a=9.14 \mathring{A}, b=4.96 \mathring{A}, c=6.73 \mathring{A}$ and $\gamma=97.2^{\circ}$. The shifts of Nb and S atoms due to the modulation are not necessarily directed along the chain direction and in general the modulation can take on any anharmonic form.

The structure is such that the energy difference in the two positions appears to be minimal and we believe that this is the reason for the large numbers of faults in planes parallel to the $c$ axis reported for $NbS_3$-I.

### 3.2. $NbS_3$-II
The crystallographic description of $NbS_3$-II is much more complicated than that of NbS-I because the modulation is incommensurate and, more seriously, the basic struc- ture is not known. In spite of these limitations, we propose below a unit cell and superspace group for modulated $NbS_3$-II that is in good agreement with the current experimental data. It was shown previously by Roucau (1983) that a simple multiple of the $NbS_3$-I unit cell gave a reasonable fit for indexing his electron diffraction data for $NbS_3$-II. We have chosen a different cell, which is also an approximate multiple of the $NbS_3$-I cell, for the reasons given below.

The structure of $NbS_3$-I is similar to that of $NbSe_3$ for which Wilson (1979) has carried out an analysis of the relationship between structure, bonding and charge-density waves. For $NbS_3$-II there are several possible arrangements of the columns in the unit cell. Using Wilson's approach, we have selected the eight-column basic cell shown in figure 8 for the modulated structure of $NbS_3$-II. Of the eight trigonal-prism columns, two are approximately equilateral and six are isosceles. This structure is based on the $NbS_3$-I basic unit cell and we have taken the parameters to be approximately $a=9.1 \mathring{A}, b=$ $\times 4.96=19.9 \mathring{A}, c=3.4 \mathring{A}$ and $\gamma=97^{\circ}$. We note that van Smaalen and de Boer (1987) have recently carried out a preliminary x-ray analysis of $NbS_3$-II and found $a=9.6 \mathring{A}$, $b=3.36 \mathring{A}, c=18.7 \mathring{A}$ and $\beta=98^{\circ}$, which are in reasonable agreement with the values we have used. The unit cell shown in figure 8(a) was also chosen to have the same symmetry as the $NbS_3$-I basic structure, viz. $P2_1/m$.

In the case of NbS₃-II we propose that only one modulation (and its higher harmonics) can take place along the six equivalent isosceles- like columns and the equilateral triangles are not modulated. Thus the eight $Nb^{5+}$ atoms contribute 40 electrons, of which 36 are used to bond with the six $(S_{2})^{2-}S^{2-}$ plus two $3S^{2-}$ anions. ## 4. Normal-mode symmetry analysis

We examine here the symmetry properties of $\mathrm{NbS}_{3}$ normal vibrational modes, their corresponding atomic displacements and the resulting extinction rules for satellite reflections. This approach (Grout *et al* 1975, Burns and Glazer 1978, Moncton *et al* 1977, Iizumi *et al* 1977, Pérez-Mato *et al* 1986), basèd on some general works (Maradudin and Vosko 1968, Kovalev 1965, Bradley and Cracknell 1972), was recently developed to predict extinction rules for satellite reflections by Withers and Bird (1986) and by Withers *et al* (1987).

### 4.1. Symmetry analysis of normal modes

Regarding the basic-structure unit cells of both polytypes it is enough to consider the symmetry properties of two non-equivalent atoms in $2 \mathrm{e}$ positions, for which the displacement eigenvector $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{q}, j)$ (for given wavevector $\boldsymbol{q}$ and mode $j$ ) has six generally complex components:
$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{q}, j)=\left(\hat{e}_{1 x}^{\boldsymbol{q} j}, \hat{e}_{1 y}^{\boldsymbol{q} j}, \hat{e}_{1 z}^{\boldsymbol{q} j} ; \hat{q}_{2 x}^{\boldsymbol{q} j}, \hat{q}_{2 y}^{\boldsymbol{q} j}, \hat{e}_{2 z}^{\boldsymbol{q} j}\right). \tag{1}
$$

The atomic displacement of the $k$ th atom in the $l$ th unit cell $\left(\boldsymbol{l}=l_{x} \boldsymbol{a}+l_{y} \boldsymbol{b}+l_{z} \boldsymbol{c}\right)$ is given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{u}_{l k}=\operatorname{Re} & \sum_{\alpha=x, y, z} \sum_{n} \hat{e}_{k \alpha}^{n \boldsymbol{q} j} \exp (\mathrm{i} n \boldsymbol{q} \boldsymbol{l}) \\
& =\sum_{\alpha=x, y, z} \sum_{n} \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k \alpha}(n \boldsymbol{q}, j) \cos \left(n \boldsymbol{q} \boldsymbol{l}+\theta_{k \alpha}\right)
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$
where $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k \alpha}(n \boldsymbol{q}, j) \exp \left(\mathrm{i} \theta_{k \alpha}\right)=\hat{e}_{k \alpha}^{n \boldsymbol{q} j}$ and the summation is taken over $n$, if higher harmonics are present.

The decomposition of the representation $T(\boldsymbol{q}, h_i)$ acting in the six-dimensional displacement space is given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= 3Z_1 & \text{at the point } Z\ (\mathrm{NbS}_3\text{-I}) \\
T &= 3V_1 + 3V_2 & \text{on the line } V\ (\mathrm{NbS}_3\text{-II}).
\end{aligned} \tag{3}
$$

There are no additional degeneracies for these modes caused by time reversal (Maradudin and Vosko 1968, Grout *et al* 1975, Burns and Glazer 1978, Moncton *et al* 1977). The two phonon eigenvectors for doubly degenerate mode $Z_1$ ($\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(Z_1,1)$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(Z_1,2)$) and the ones for modes $V_1$ and $V_2$ ($\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(V_1,1)$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(V_2,1)$) should obey the following transformation rules (Maradudin and Vosko 1968):
$$
T(\boldsymbol{q}, h_i)\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{q}, j, s) = \sum_{s'=1}^{f_j} \hat{\tau}_{s's}^j(\boldsymbol{q}, h_i) \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{q}, j, s') \tag{4}
$$
where $f_j$ is the dimension of irreducible representation matrices $\hat{\tau}^j$ and $s$ denotes different eigenvectors belonging to the same mode (in our case $Z_1$).

\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(V_{1}, 1) &=(a_{1}, b_{1}, c_{1} ;-\omega_{2} a_{1},-\omega_{2} b_{1}, \omega_{2} c_{1}) \\
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(V_{2}, 1) &=(a_{2}, b_{2}, c_{2} ; \omega_{2} a_{2}, \omega_{2} b_{2},-\omega_{2} c_{2})
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
$$
again with complex $a_{i}, b_{i}, c_{i}$ values and $\omega_{2}=\exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol{q}_{V} \cdot \boldsymbol{c} / 2)=\exp (\mathrm{i} q \pi)$.

Thus, it is obvious that in the first case the eigenvectors are strongly interconnected.

## 4.2. Extinction conditions on satellite reflections
In terms of atomic displacement parameters $\varepsilon_{k \alpha}$ and $\theta_{k \alpha}$ of (2) the structure factor $F(\hat{\boldsymbol{G}}+m \boldsymbol{q})$ for the satellite $m \boldsymbol{q}$ of the main spot $\boldsymbol{G}$ can be expresed (Withers and Bird 1986, Withers *et al* 1987) as

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(\boldsymbol{G}+m \boldsymbol{q})= & \sum_{k} \sum_{\substack{m_{1}, m_{2}, m_{3} \\
m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=m}} f_{k}(\boldsymbol{G}+m \boldsymbol{q}) \exp \left[-\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{G}+m \boldsymbol{q}) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_{k}\right] \\
& \times \exp \left\{\mathrm{i}\left[m_{1}\left(\theta_{k x}-\pi / 2\right)+m_{2}\left(\theta_{k y}-\pi / 2\right)+m_{3}\left(\theta_{k z}-\pi / 2\right)\right]\right\} \\
& \times J_{m_{1}}\left((\boldsymbol{G}+m \boldsymbol{q}) \cdot \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k x}\right) J_{m_{2}}\left((\boldsymbol{G}+m \boldsymbol{q}) \cdot \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k y}\right) J_{m_{3}}\left((\boldsymbol{G}+m \boldsymbol{q}) \cdot \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k z}\right)
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

where $J_{m}(Y)$ is a Bessel function of order $m$.

Using this equation and the eigenvectors $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{q}, j, s)$ of (5) and (6), the extinction rules are obtained as follows:

In the case of $\mathrm{NbS}_{3}$-I, i.e. doubly degenerate mode $Z_{1}$, there are no extinction conditions, if both eigenvectors are considered simultaneously (as a result of 'sticking' together normal modes $\Lambda_{i}$ at the point $Z$ of the Brillouin zone boundary). Speaking strictly, we have indistinguishable extinction conditions:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
F\left(00 l+\frac{1}{2} c^{*}\right)=0 & \text { unless } l=2 n \text { for } \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(Z_{1}, 1\right) \\
F\left(00 l+\frac{1}{2} c^{*}\right)=0 & \text { unless } l=2 n+1 \text { for } \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(Z_{1}, 2\right).
\end{array}
\tag{8}
$$

For $\mathrm{NbS}_{3}$-II a larger unit cell $2 \boldsymbol{a} \times \boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c}$ with a corresponding wavevector $\boldsymbol{q}=(0,0, q)$ can be considered instead of the small one $\boldsymbol{a} \times \boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c}$ with $\boldsymbol{q}_{V}=(0.5,0, q)$. Thus, the new eigenvectors $\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}(\boldsymbol{q}, j)$ have two components each:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(V_{1}, 1\right)=\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(V_{1}, 1\right),-1 \times \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(V_{1}, 1\right)\right) \\
& \tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(V_{2}, 1\right)=\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(V_{2}, 1\right),-1 \times \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(V_{2}, 1\right)\right)
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

connected with displacements $\boldsymbol{u}_{(000) k}$ and $\boldsymbol{u}_{(100) k}$ (equation (2)) for the old $\boldsymbol{a} \times \boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c}$ unit cell and $-1=\cos (\boldsymbol{q}_{V} \times(100))$. Again, by putting these components into (7), extinction rules can be calculated. However, the same result is obtained by the following consideration.

Let an arbitrary atom $k$ within the unit cell $\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime} \times \boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c}$, with $\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}=2 \boldsymbol{a}$, be at a position $\boldsymbol{r}_{k}=x \boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}+y \boldsymbol{b}+z \boldsymbol{c}$ with associated phonon eigenvector components $(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k x}, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k y}, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k z})$. This atom is shifted under translation $\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime} / 2$ into $(x+\frac{1}{2}) \boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}+y \boldsymbol{b}+z \boldsymbol{c}$ and the associated phonon eigenvector components are transformed into $-1 \times(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k x}, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k y}, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{k z})$ for both cases of $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$ symmetry. Substituting the atomic positions $\boldsymbol{r}_{k}$ and associated eigenvector components back into (7), one can see that the contribution to $F(\boldsymbol{G}+1 \times \boldsymbol{q})$, of any particular term in the summation over all $m_{i}$, has to be proportional to $1+\exp [\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime} / 2) \cdot \boldsymbol{G}](-1)^{m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}}$. Thus for the matrix reflection $h k l$ and for $m_{1}+m_{2}+$

$m_3 = m = 1$, this becomes

$1 - \exp(\mathrm{i}\pi h)$
for a satellite reflection

$1 + \exp(\mathrm{i}\pi h)$
for a main reflection.

Hence, the following extinction rules are obtained:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(h k l) & =0 & & \text { unless } h=2 n \\
F(h k l+\bar{q}) & =0 & & \text { unless } h=2 n+1
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
$$

for both modes $V_1$ and $V_2$.

There are no additional extinction rules, which could distinguish between these two modes $V_1$ and $V_2$.

### 4.3. Interpretation of electron diffraction data

The absence of extinction rules in the case of $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-I, based on the $Z_1$ mode analysis, as well as the extinction conditions for $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-II, resulting from the analysis of the $V_1$ and $V_2$ modes, are consistent with the available x-ray and electron diffraction patterns. The first- and second-order incommensurate satellites of $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-II at $\boldsymbol{q}_\delta=c^* / 3(1+\delta)$ and (after folding into the first Brillouin zone) at $\boldsymbol{q}_{2 \delta}=c^* / 3(1-2 \delta)$, respectively, transform as first-order structure factors of two primary distortion waves $(m=1, \boldsymbol{q}_\delta$ and $m=1, \boldsymbol{q}_{2 \delta})$ and not as first- and second-order structure factors of the primary distortion $(m=1, \boldsymbol{q}_\delta$ and $m=2, \boldsymbol{q}_\delta)$.

## 5. Superspace (dualistic) versus normal-mode symmetry analysis

Two independent approaches were used in previous sections to describe the modulated structures of $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-I and $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-II. The first was a general crystallographic and structural analysis, while the second was based on the theory of phase transitions (Pérez-Mato et al 1984). The fact that both approaches give the same extinction rules for the superlattice reflections lends support to the modulation structure that we have proposed for $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-II.

### 5.1. $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-I

The crystal structure of $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-I can be described either by

$$
\mathrm{P} \begin{array}{c}
\mathrm{P} 2_1 / \mathrm{m} \\
1 \quad \mathrm{~T}
\end{array} \stackrel{\mathrm{m}}{=} \begin{gathered}
\mathrm{P} 2_1 / \mathrm{m} \\
\mathrm{c} \quad \mathrm{P} 2 / \mathrm{m}
\end{gathered}
$$

in the superspace group (dualistic) notation or by the periodic distortion wave of the doubly degenerate $Z_1$ mode. The basic structure is the same in both cases.

Both approaches result in the absence of extinction conditions (in agreement with x-ray and electron diffraction experiments). However, $\mathrm{NbS}_3$-I is commensurate and hence must be regarded as a special case when considering the atomic displacement field. The results given by the superspace-group approach are consistent with the ordinary space-group description of Rijnsdorp and Jellinek (1978), presuming that a secondary modulation with $\boldsymbol{q}=2 \boldsymbol{q} \cdot \boldsymbol{c}^*$ is present. However, in the case of a commensurate modulation,

different three-dimensional structures can be obtained as sections through the super- crystal for different values of the internal coordinate $e_{4}$ (van Smaalen 1987). The super crystal, described in our case by

$$
\mathrm{P} \begin{gathered}
\mathrm{P} 2_{1} / \mathrm{m} \\
1 \quad \overline{1}
\end{gathered}
$$

corresponds to the ordinary space group $P \overline{1}$ for the chosen modulation unit-cell origin (i.e. for the given value of $a_{4}$ ), as given in figure 7. (However, a shifted origin by $\frac{1}{2} a_{4}$ may change the space group into $\mathrm{Pm}$, with two different trigonal prismatic columns per unit cell.)

Thus, while in the superspace-group approach a fit with the x-ray refinement of Rijnsdorp and Jellinek (1978) can be achieved by a proper choice of the unit-cell origin and presuming a secondary modulation is present (the element $\left(\begin{array}{l}2 \\ 1\end{array} 1\right)$ relates displacements of sites 1 and 2 in such a way that they have the same amplitude but a phase shift of $\pm \omega_{1}$ ), the superposition of displacements, given by two $Z_{1}$ eigenvectors, results in different amplitudes at the two sites considered. A fit, however, can be obtained if instead of $Z_{1}$ the two modes $\Lambda_{1}$ and $\Lambda_{2}$ (corresponding to $\hat{e}(Z_{1}, 1)$ and $\hat{e}(Z_{1}, 2)$ ) on line $\Lambda=(0,0, q)$ are considered. From these two modes, 'stuck' together at $Z=(0,0,0.5)$, the first fits the superspace-group description.

### 5.2. $NbS_{3}-II$
The structure of $NbS_{3}$-II is described in the superspace-group approach by

$$
\mathrm{A} \begin{gathered}
\mathrm{P} 2_{1} / \mathrm{m} \\
1 \quad \overline{1}
\end{gathered} \equiv \begin{gathered}
\mathrm{m} \mathrm{P} 2_{1} / \mathrm{m} \\
c \mathrm{~B} 2 / \mathrm{m}
\end{gathered}
$$

and in the normal-mode-analysis approach by the $V_{1}$ or $V_{2}$ symmetry. As for $NbS_{3}$-I the structure unit cells are the same in both descriptions. The generating operator $\left(\begin{array}{l}2 \\ 1\end{array} 1\right)$ in

$$
\mathrm{A} \begin{gathered}
\mathrm{P} 2_{1} / \mathrm{m} \\
1 \quad \overline{1}
\end{gathered}
$$

corresponds to the only non-trivial element (2) of the little co-group $\hat{G}_{k}$, leading to the same conclusions regarding the symmetry and phase relationships in the modulation functions.

Consequently, the displacement waves with $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$ symmetry correspond to the possible modulation stackings obtained from superspace groups, of which the one in figure $8(b)$ was chosen. Further, both approaches (the first through the operation $\left(\begin{array}{c}m \\ 1\end{array}\right)$ and the second through the $\hat{e}^{*}(-q, j)=\hat{e}(q, j)$ equivalence, where $\hat{e}(-q, j)$ is obtained from equation (4.12) of Maradudin and Vosko (1965)) divided the modulation functions according to parity into two groups, from which the first contains the $\hat{x}$ - and $\hat{y}$ - and the second the $\hat{z}$-polarised functions.

## 6. The phase transition in the modulation structure of $NbS_{3}$-II
It was first reported by Monceau (1985) that a phase transition occurs in the modulation structure of $NbS_{3}$-II at about $350 ~K$. This transition is evidenced by the disappearance

of all the weaker components of the superlattice doublets in electron diffraction patterns (figure 6). It also shows as a change of slope in a log(resistivity) versus $1/T$ graph and has a minimum in a plot of $E_c$ versus $T$ where $E_c$ is the maximum voltage for which Ohm's law is obeyed (Monceau 1985).

The transformation results in the extinction of all the satellite spots that we have attributed to $G_{M2}$. We have noted above that the most likely interpretation of $G_{M2}$ is that it corresponds to a second harmonic of $G_{M1}$. It is known that strong anharmonic effects give rise to higher-order satellites (Bak 1982, Wilson 1979, 1982, Fleming et al 1978, Bird and Withers 1986). We propose that the higher harmonics are associated with a discommensurate modulation such that $2.84c$ modulation fits to the $17c$ spacing as a commensurate $3c$ distortion with a $4\pi/3$ phase shift after five modulation wavelengths, as shown schematically in figure 9(c). Above $T_c$ the modulation becomes incommensurate (figure 9(b)) and $G_{M2}$ disappears. Thus we believe that a discommensuration array exists in the crystals at room temperature and is eliminated at about 350 K on heating.
