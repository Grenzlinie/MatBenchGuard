# The First and Second Neighbour $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ Pairs in Ruby $\star$

## I. Group Theoretical Treatment

J. Heber

Institut für Technische Physik der Technischen Hochschule
Darmstadt, West-Germany

Received October 29, accepted November 14, 1975

**Abstract.** The irreducible representations of the states of the first and second nearest neighbour $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ pairs in ruby were deduced from the known states of the single ions by the group theoretical method of induction. The summetry selection rules and polarizations for electric dipole radiation are discussed.

---

### Introduction

The first and second nearest neighbour (1.n.n. and 2.n.n.) $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ pairs in ruby are of special interest, because their exchange coupling determines the magnetic ordering of the well known isomorphic antiferro- magnet $\text{Cr}_{2}\text{O}_{3}$. Both pair types show well resolved optical spectra in the near infrared and ultraviolet regions. Thus they offer the possibility to investigate the exchange interaction between two $3d$-ions with the well known precision of optical spectroscopy. This is the reason why several authors [1-6] studied the pair spectra of ruby. But, for a successful analysis of the optical spectra, it is necessary to know the sym- metry properties of the electronic states in question. Therefore we deduce in this paper the symmetry properties of the $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ pair states starting from the known electronic states of the single ions. Mathemat- ically, the problem is to find the characters of the irreducible representations under which the pair eigen- functions transform in the given crystal symmetry. This can be done by the method of induction [7] applying the site symmetry operations on zero-order eigenfunctions of the pairs and decomposing the found representations into their irreducible parts.

---

### The Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian of a $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ pair can be represented as

$$\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1}^{0}+\mathscr{H}_{2}^{0}+\mathscr{H}_{12}+\mathscr{H}_{\text{so}}.$$

Here, $\mathscr{H}_{1}^{0}+\mathscr{H}_{2}^{0}$ is the operator of the ions in the crystal field without magnetic interactions.

$$\mathscr{H}_{12}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{r_{ij}}$$
is the electrostatic interaction be- tween the three $3d$-electrons at the sites 1 and 2 and

$$\mathscr{H}_{\text{so}}=-\frac{1}{2m_{\text{e}}^{2}c^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{6}\mathbf{s}_{k}\cdot(\mathbf{p}_{k}\times\mathbf{grad}\ U(\mathbf{r}_{k}))\approx\sum_{k=1}^{6}\zeta(\mathbf{r}_{k})\mathbf{s}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{l}_{k}$$

is the spin-orbit interaction of the $3d$ electrons. ($\mathbf{s}=$spin, $\mathbf{p}=$linear momentum, $U(\mathbf{r})=$electrostatic potential energy, $\mathbf{l}=$angular momentum, $\zeta=$spin-orbit coupling constant of the $3d$ electrons.) The Hamil- tonian has two exchange symmetries, first with respect to electron exchange, and second to site exchange $1\leftrightarrow2$. The combination of these two exchange symmetries gives rise to a third exchange symmetry, the excitation exchange. The simultaneous exchange of the electrons and sites means that the electrons remain at their nuclei and change only the eigenfunctions, if these be different at both sites. If we consider the nuclei as fixed in the crystal lattice, only the electron and

the excitation exchange are physically realizable and must be taken into account in constructing the eigen- functions. Together with the interaction Hamiltonian $\mathscr{H}_{12}$ they give rise to two different splittings. The splitting due to the electron exchange is determined by the well known "exchange integrals" of the form

$$
J_{i j}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}}\left\langle\varphi_{1}^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}\right) \varphi_{2}^{\beta}\left(\mathbf{r}_{j}\right)\left|\frac{1}{r_{i j}}\right| \varphi_{1}^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{r}_{j}\right) \varphi_{2}^{\beta}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)\right\rangle.
$$

The splitting due to the excitation exchange is given by integrals of the form

$$
A_{i j}=\frac{e^{2}}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}}\left\langle\varphi_{1}^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}\right) \varphi_{2}^{\beta}\left(\mathbf{r}_{j}\right)\left|\frac{1}{r_{i j}}\right| \varphi_{1}^{\beta}\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}\right) \varphi_{2}^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{r}_{j}\right)\right\rangle.
$$

For larger distances between the two nuclei we get $A_{i j}>J_{i j}$. This can easily be seen from the exchange integrals. In the excitation exchange integral the eigen- functions of the electrons $i$ or $j$ are to be taken at the same nucleus, whereas in the electron exchange integral - at different nuclei. Therefore, with increasing distance and decreasing overlap of the eigenfunctions at different nuclei the effects due to electron exchange tend faster to zero than the effects due to excitation exchange. Therefore it is convinient to split $\mathscr{H}_{12}$ into two parts: one, $\mathscr{H}_{12}^{\prime}$, responsible only for the "classical" excitation exchange and a second, $\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ex}}$, responsible for the "quantummechanical" electron exchange.

$$
\mathscr{H}_{12}=\mathscr{H}_{12}^{\prime}+\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathscr{H}_{12}^{\prime}-\sum_{i \neq j}^{3} J_{i j} \mathbf{s}_{i} \cdot \mathbf{s}_{j}.
$$

Fig.1. The 1.n.n. $Cr^{3+}$ pairs in ruby. $\odot: Cr^{3+}$ ions, $O: O^{2-}$ ions, forming nearly an octahedron with the $Cr^{3+}$ ion in its center. Here, for the electron exchange part, the usual Heisen- berg exchange form is adopted. The Hamiltonian of the pair can then be written in the form

$$
\mathscr{H}=\left(\mathscr{H}_{1}^{0}+\mathscr{H}_{2}^{0}\right)+\mathscr{H}_{12}^{\prime}+\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{ex}}+\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{so}}.
$$

The order of the sum gives the order of the magnitude of its parts for the $Cr^{3+}$ pairs in question. Some un certainty exists for the last two terms, $\zeta(3 d)$ and $J_{i j}$ being of the same order of magnitude of $10^{2} ~cm^{-1}$ [8-10]. But, from the analysis of the pair spectra, there exists some evidence that the total spin of the pairs remains a relatively good quantum number, so it seems to be meaningfull to treat $\mathscr{H}_{so}$ as the smallest and last perturbation. The reason may be that $\mathscr{H}_{ex}$ , from its physical origin, is a part of $\mathscr{H}_{12}$ , which clearly dominates the spin-orbit coupling $\mathscr{H}_{so}$ for the given pair distances. In our group theoretical treatment we want to consider the terms of the Hamiltonian in the above order from left to right.

## The First Nearest Neighbour Pairs

### 1. Orbital States

One isolated $Cr^{3+}$ ion has the full symmetry $\mathscr{L}_{0}$ of its site in the crystal. If we substitute one regular neighbour ion by a second impurity, in our case by a second $Cr^{3+}$ ion, the site symmetry of the ion reduces to a lower symmetry $\mathscr{L}_{1}$ . This is just the symmetry of the zero order Hamiltonians $\mathscr{H}_{1}^{0}$ and $\mathscr{H}_{2}^{0}$ . If we couple both ions to a pair, the pair has a new site symmetry $\mathscr{L}_{2}$ , related to its center of gravity, which is usually of higher order than $\mathscr{L}_{1}$ because of the additional symmetry operations transforming the ions one into the other. Fig. 1 demonstrates the situation for the 1.n.n. pairs in ruby. The $C_{3}$ axis is the threefold crystallographic axis of the crystal. The exact site symmetry of the isolated $Cr^{3+}$ ions is $\mathscr{L}_{0}=C_{3}$ , but, to a relatively good approximation, it can be taken as $O_{h}$ . Accordingly, the site symmetry of the pair is $\mathscr{L}_{2}=D_{3}$ or $D_{3 h}$ . Let us consider the higher symmetries $\mathscr{L}_{0}=O_{h}$ and $\mathscr{L}_{2}=D_{3 h}$ . The results can easily be modi fied later for the lower symmetry case. Then the starting site symmetry for both $Cr^{3+}$ ions to form a pair is $\mathscr{L}_{1}=C_{3 v}$ . The common symmetry elements with the isolated ion symmetry $\mathscr{L}_{0}=O_{h}$ are $E, C_{3}$ , and $\sigma_{d}=\sigma_{v}$ . (For the notation of the group symbols see, for instance, [11,12].) In $C_{3 v}$ the octahedral orbital states of the isolated $Cr^{3+}$ ion (Fig. 2) split in the following way.

$$
\begin{array}{cc}
\mathscr{L}_{0}=O_{h} \quad \longrightarrow & \mathscr{L}_{1}=C_{3 v} \\
A_{2 g} & A_{2} \\
E_{g} & E \\
T_{1 g} & A_{2}+E \\

Now from the given irreducible representations of the eigenstates of the two $Cr^{3+}$ ions in $C_{3 v}$ we have to find the possible resultant irreducible representations of the pair eigenstates in $D_{3 h}$. This we want to do by using the method of induction. We take a set of pair eigenfunctions, for instance the simple product eigenfunctions of the zeroth order Hamiltonian, and apply on them all group operations of $D_{3 h}$. The resultant transformation matrix forms a general representation of the group $D_{3 h}$, which easily can be decomposed into the wanted irreducible representations.
To do this we must look at the transformation properties of the product eigenfunctions of the pair under the symmetry operations of $D_{3 h}$. As can readily be shown the symmetry group $\mathscr{L}_{2}=D_{3 h}$ can be decomposed into its subgroup $C_{3 v}$, the site symmetry $\mathscr{L}_{1}$ of the pair partners, and a left coset [13], for instance $\sigma_{h} \cdot C_{3 v}$ (Table 1).

$$
D_{3 h}=C_{3 v}+\sigma_{h} \cdot C_{3 v}.
$$

Therefore any operation $L_{2}$ of $\mathscr{L}_{2}=D_{3 h}$ can be represented as a known operation $L_{1}$ of $\mathscr{L}_{1}=C_{3 v}$ on the single ions times a reflection $\sigma_{h}$ which interchanges the ions.

$$
L_{2}=\left(\sigma_{h}\right)^{r} \cdot L_{1} ; \quad r=0,1.
$$

Applying the operations $L_{2}$ on the product eigenfunctions of the pair we have to distinguish between two possibilities:
i) both ions are in equal or degenerate eigenstates,
ii) both ions are in nondegenerate eigenstates.
i) In the first case no excitation exchange can occur.
The product eigenfunctions are given by $\varphi_{1}^{k_{1}} \cdot \varphi_{2}^{k_{2}}$, where $k$ enumerates the degenerate eigenfunctions $\varphi$. Because $\sigma_{h}$ commutes with all operations of $L_{1}$ the transformation of the pairs under any operation $L_{2}$ is given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
& L_{2}\left(\varphi_{1}^{k_{1}} \varphi_{2}^{k_{2}}\right)=\left(L_{1} \sigma_{h}^{r}\right)\left(\varphi_{1}^{k_{1}} \varphi_{2}^{k_{2}}\right) \\
& =L_{1}\left(\varphi_{1+r}^{k_{1}} \varphi_{2+r}^{k_{2}}\right)=\sum_{k_{1}^{\prime}, k_{2}^{\prime}}\left(\alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{1}^{\prime} k_{1}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{2}^{\prime} k_{2}}\right)\left(\varphi_{1+r}^{k_{1}^{\prime}} \varphi_{2+r}^{k_{2}^{\prime}}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
$j$ enumerates the representations of $L_{1}, \varphi_{r+2}=\varphi_{r}$. For our purposes, only the character of the transformation matrix is of interest. It is given by the sum over all coefficients for which the left and right side eigenfunctions have the same indices. The sum is to extend over all left side functions. I.e., we have to take $k_{1}^{\prime}=k_{1+r}$, $k_{2}^{\prime}=k_{2+r}$.

$$
\chi\left(\sigma_{h}^{r} L_{1}\right)=\sum_{k_{1}, k_{2}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{1+r} k_{1}} \cdot \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{2+r} k_{2}}.
$$

Now we must consider separately the two possibilities:
a) $r=0$;
$$
\chi\left(L_{2}=L_{1}\right)=\sum_{k_{1}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{1} k_{1}} \cdot \sum_{k_{2}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{2} k_{2}}=\chi_{j}^{2}\left(L_{1}\right).
$$
b) $r=1$;
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \chi\left(L_{2}=\sigma_{h} \cdot L_{1}\right)=\sum_{k_{2}} \sum_{k_{1}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{2} k_{1}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}\right)^{k_{1} k_{2}} \\
& =\sum_{k_{2}} \alpha_{j}\left(L_{1}^{2}\right)^{k_{2} k_{2}}=\chi_{j}\left(L_{1}^{2}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The last two formulae give the characters of the representations of the 1.n.n. pairs under any operation $L_{2}$, if the pair partners are in degenerate states and transform like the $j$-th representation in $\mathscr{L}_{1}$. The

ii) When the pair ions are in nondegenerate states excitation exchange takes place. Therefore the product eigenfunctions have the form $\varphi_{n}^{k_{1}} \cdot \psi_{m}^{k_{2}}$ where $n \neq m$ enumerate the ion sites and can have the values 1 or 2. They transform under any operation $L_{2}$ as follows

$$
\begin{aligned}
& L_{2}(\varphi_{n}^{k_{1}} \psi_{m}^{k_{2}})=(\sigma_{h}^{r} L_{1})(\varphi_{n}^{k_{1}} \psi_{m}^{k_{2}}) \\
& =\sum_{k_{1}^{\prime} k_{2}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{j}(L_{1})^{k_{1} k_{1}^{\prime}} \alpha_{i}(L_{1})^{k_{2} k_{2}^{\prime}}) \cdot \{\sigma_{h}^{r}(\varphi_{n}^{k_{1}} \psi_{m}^{k_{2}})\} \\
& =\sum_{k_{1}^{\prime}, k_{2}^{\prime}} \sum_{\substack{n^{\prime} m^{\prime} \\
n^{\prime} \neq m^{\prime}}}(\alpha_{j}(L_{1})^{k_{1} k_{1}^{\prime}} \alpha_{i}(L_{1})^{k_{2} k_{2}^{\prime}} \beta^{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, n m})(\varphi_{n^{\prime}}^{k_{1}^{\prime}} \psi_{m^{\prime}}^{k_{2}^{\prime}}).
\end{aligned}
$$

$\sigma_{h}^{r}$ can be commuted with the matrix coefficients $\alpha$ because they are only numbers. The doubly indexed matrix $\parallel \beta^{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, n m}\parallel$ represents the operation $\sigma_{h}^{r}$.

$$
\parallel \beta^{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, n m}\parallel = \begin{vmatrix}
\beta^{12,12} & \beta^{12,21} \\
\beta^{21,12} & \beta^{21,21} \\
\end{vmatrix}.
$$

For $r = 0$ the matrix is the unity matrix. Its elements are of the form $\beta^{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, n m} = \delta_{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, n m}$. For $r = 1$ all functions change their site. The matrix contains only elements in the second diagonal. They have the form

$$
\beta^{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, n m} = \delta_{n^{\prime} m^{\prime}, m n}.
$$

The character of the transformation of the product functions is given again as the sum over the coefficients for which the right and left side eigenfunctions are equal, i.e. we have to take $k_{1}^{\prime} = k_{1}$, $n^{\prime} = n$, $k_{2}^{\prime} = k_{2}$, and $m^{\prime} = m$. The sum extends over all indices of the leftside functions. As before, we must distinguish the two cases $r = 0$, and $r = 1$.

a) $r = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \chi(L_{2}=L_{1}) = \sum_{\substack{n m \\
n \neq m}} \delta_{n m, n m} \cdot \sum_{k_{2}} \alpha_{i}(L_{1})^{k_{2} k_{2}} \cdot \sum_{k_{1}} \alpha_{j}(L_{1})^{k_{1} k_{1}} \\
& = 2\chi_{i}(L_{1}) \cdot \chi_{j}(L_{1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The character is two times that of the direct product.

b) $r = 1$:

$$
\chi(L_{2}=\sigma_{h} \cdot L_{1}) = \sum_{\substack{n m \\
n \neq m}} \delta_{n m, m n} \cdot \chi_{i}(L_{1}) \cdot \chi_{j}(L_{1}) = 0.
$$

The characters are all zero.

### 3. Optical Transitions
An optical transition between two states is allowed by symmetry, if the direct product of the representations of the initial state, the transition operator, and the final state contains the identical representation: $\Gamma_i \times \Gamma_{\text{op}} \times \Gamma_f \supset A_'1$ [13]. We want to consider only transitions due to electric dipole radiation. The symmetry-allowed transitions and their polarizations are summarized in Table 3. For small spin-orbit coupling we can, as in atomic spectroscopy, distinguish between two types of transitions, spin-allowed and spin-forbidden ones.

i) The spin-allowed transitions are transitions between pure product states of spin and orbital functions. They obey the spin selection rule
$$
\Delta S=0,\quad \Delta M_s=0.
$$

These transition are the most intensive ones. The symmetry selection rule is active between the orbital states only.

ii) The spin-forbidden transitions with
$$
\Delta S \neq 0,\quad \Delta M_s \neq 0
$$
are possible only due to spin mixing by the spin-orbit interaction. They are allowed only in a higher approach of perturbation theory and are therefore expected to be much less intensive. The symmetry selection rules are valid for the coupled spin-orbit states.

## Concluding Remark
In our considerations we have taken into account only geometrical symmetry arguments and the ques- tion arises, do there exist some restrictions to the number of pair states due to the Pauli exclusion principle. Such restrictions do not exist, because we started our considerations with single ion eigenfunc- tions satisfying the Pauli principle and located at different sites in the coordinate space. But, in this case, the site number of the center of the ion eigen- functions can be taken into account as a new additional quantum number, so the exclusion principle, requiring all electrons to be in states with different quantum numbers, cannot give rise to any restrictions.
