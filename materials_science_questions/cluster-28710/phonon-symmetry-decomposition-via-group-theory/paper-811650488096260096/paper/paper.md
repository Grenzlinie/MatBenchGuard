## ABSTRACT

Laser ultrasonics was applied to the manufacturing control of the integrity (no failure) of coated spherical particles designed for High Temperature Reactors (HTR). This control is of major importance, since the coating of the nuclear fuel kernel is designed to prevent from the diffusion of fission products outside the particle during reactor operation. The SiC layer composing the coating is particularly important, since this layer must be an impenetrable barrier for fission products. The integrity of the SiC shell (no crack within the shell) can be assessed by the ultrasonic vibration spectrum of the HTR particle, which is significantly changed, compared to the reference spectrum of a defect-free particle. Spheroidal vibration modes of defect-free dummy particles with a zirconium dioxide ($ZrO_2$) core were observed in the 2-5 MHz range. A theoretical analysis is presented to account for the observed vibration spectra of defect-free or cracked HTR particles.

## 1. Introduction

The conformity control of manufactured components is essential in many industrial sectors. This control is particularly crucial for components designed to contain the nuclear fuel in nuclear reactors, since these components are designed to prevent from the diffusion of fission products in the environment during reactor operation. In the 1960-1970s, a generation of nuclear reactors was designed in order to reduce the quantity and the radiotoxicity of waste in nuclear plants. In this generation - the High Temperature Reactors (HTR) - the nuclear fuel is conditioned as spherical beads of about 0.5 mm in diameter that are coated by several layers of materials to ensure the perfect confinement of fission products. The fuel core is surrounded by four spherical layers: (i) a porous pyrocarbon layer (PPyC), (ii) a dense internal pyrocarbon layer (IPyC), (iii) a silicon carbide layer (SiC) and (iv) an outer shell of dense pyrocarbon (OPyC) [1]. A composite HTR coated particle is designed to have a diameter of about 1 mm (Fig. 1). The coating layers are deposited on the fuel kernels in a chemical vapor deposition (CVD) process using a fluidized-bed furnace. Pyrocarbon or pyrolytic carbon layers are obtained by the cracking of hydrocarbon gases and silicon carbide is deposited by the decomposition of methyltrichlorosilane (MTS) in hydrogen. The fluidization of the kernels is ensured by an argon flow. The control of conformity of the SiC shell is of paramount importance because this layer guarantees the solidity of the confinement shells. Moreover, the SiC shell has a major role of being an impenetrable barrier for fission products. So the integrity of the SiC shell must be controlled on each HTR particle. Unfortunately, the inspection of the SiC shell cannot be done before the whole fabrication process is finished. Therefore, it is no longer possible to control directly the SiC shell, due to the presence of the outer pyrocarbon layer (OPyC). However, X-ray radiography can be used to detect a crack hidden in the structure but the technique is not fast enough to be applied for on-line control of HTR particles. A fast and reliable non-destructive method of control is desirable to detect a possible crack in the particle. Ultrasonics can be such a non-destructive technique. It is frequently used in industry for crack detection and it is compatible with fast testing [2]. Ultrasonics was applied to control ceramic balls of several millimeters in diameter, using a piezoelectric transducer for the excitation of ultrasound, and the vibrations were detected by the non-contact interferometric technique [3]. However, the excitation by a piezoelectric transducer requires a precise control of the force that is applied by the transducer onto the tested object, because the resonance frequencies and the quality factors of normal modes depend on that force [4-6]. In order to overcome this problem, we applied the laser ultrasonic method, which is an all-optical technique using lasers both to generate and to detect ultrasonic vibrations [5-9]. This

## 2. Vibration modes of dummy HTR particles

### 2.1. Experiment

The laser ultrasonics-RUS setup used for the control of the coated particles is presented in Fig. 2. During the measurement, the spherical particle was held on a support, which consists in a spherical bowl whose radius of curvature is slightly greater than the radius of the HTR particle. A hole was drilled at the bottom of the bowl, so that the particle rests on a contact ring of about 0.2 mm. The resulting contact force is the weight of the particle. For a secure handling of the particle on its support after the measurement, the particle may be jammed against the hole by an air flow. The ultrasonic vibrations were excited by a Q-switched Nd:YAG laser at 1064 nm. Two kinds of pulsed lasers were used: one with 50 ns pulses duration and 300 µJ energy and another with 0.6 ns pulses duration and 10 µJ energy. The tested particle was irradiated by a laser beam on an area of 0.5 mm in diameter. In this condition, the incident power density is under the ablation threshold. Hence, the vibrations of HTR particles could be excited by repeated laser pulses without damaging the outer pyrocarbon shell.

A stabilized homodyne interferometer sensitive to out-of-plane displacements was used to measure the vibrations. The probe beam of the interferometer, at a wavelength of 532 nm, was strongly focused at the surface of the particle by an aspherical lens of 8 mm focal length and 0.5 numerical aperture. The resulting spot at the focus had a diameter of about 1 µm. The average sensitivity of the interferometer could reach 10 mV/nm for 30 mW of incident power on the particle.

Fig. 3 shows a typical vibration signal measured on a dummy HTR particle composed of a zirconium dioxide core (ZrO₂). Signal averaging was used to improve the signal to noise ratio. Fig. 4 shows the vibration spectrum calculated by a fast Fourier transform (FFT) from the digital signal shown in Fig. 3, recorded during 1 ms, and thus leading to a spectral resolution of 1 kHz. The lowest measured eigenfrequencies are around 2 MHz. Fig. 3 shows that vibration modes are weakly damped. Indeed, the quality factors of most vibration modes were typically found in the range of 1000–3000. Eigenfrequencies appear dispatched within several multiplets that are separated by large frequency gaps. Such a characteristic spectrum can be explained by modeling the mechanical behavior of a HTR particle.

Manufactured HTR particles are fairly spherical objects that can be modeled with relatively good accuracy as spherical multilayered structures composed of homogeneous and isotropic materials. Spherical symmetry greatly simplifies modal analysis of HTR particles. Symmetry and group theory are commonly used to describe the normal modes of a symmetric molecule. The point group of an object, which is the set of all geometric operations that leave the object unchanged, such as rotations and reflection planes. A free multilayered spherical structure is depicted by the point group of a sphere in three dimensions, which is usually denoted O(3). This point group contains all the rotations about any axes going through the center C of the sphere and the reflection planes containing C. In addition, this point group contains the inversion denoted i – which transforms the position vector $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{CM}$ into $-\mathbf{r}$ – and the identity E. Group theory predicts that each normal mode of a symmetrical structure can be associated to a particular irreducible representation of the group [10]. A representation associates to each element of the group an inversible square matrix of dimension $d \times d$, so that the set of matrices constitutes also a group, which is isomorphic to the point group. The dimension $d$ of the matrices is the dimension of the representation. A representation is said irreducible if no representation of lower dimension can be found. A particular normal mode is always associated to an irreducible representation and the dimension of the representation determines

the modal degeneracy. From the group theory standpoint, there is no fundamental difference between a spherical multilayered object and a homogeneous isotropic ball. Hence, the spectroscopic labeling of the normal modes of a multilayered spherical object and a homogeneous ball are identical. Therefore, a short recall of the main results pertaining to the vibration modes of a homogeneous sphere is made hereafter. This problem was formally solved by Lamb [11] and the eigenfrequencies and modal shapes were calculated analytically by Sato and Usami [12]. The normal vibration modes of a spherical structure belong either to spheroidal modes (S) or to torsional modes (T). The distinction between spheroidal and torsional modes arises from the polar or axial property of the displacement vector field $\mathbf{u}$ [13]. Axial and polar vectors are distinguished according to their transformation by a symmetry plane. A vector field $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_{//} + \mathbf{u}_{\perp}$, where $\mathbf{u}_{//}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{\perp}$ are respectively the parallel and perpendicular components relative to the symmetry plane is axial (polar) if under reflection, the vector $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_{//} + \mathbf{u}_{\perp}$ is transformed into the vector: $\mathbf{u}' = -\mathbf{u}_{//} + \mathbf{u}_{\perp} (\mathbf{u}' = \mathbf{u}_{//} - \mathbf{u}_{\perp})$. In the spherical coordinates denoted $(r, \theta, \varphi)$, it was demonstrated for an isotropic and homogeneous elastic medium that the general solution of the elastic wave equation for the displacement vector $\mathbf{u}$ can be expressed in the form [13]:

$$
\mathbf{u} = \nabla \Psi_1 + \nabla \wedge \Psi_2 + \nabla \wedge \nabla \wedge \Psi_3. \tag{1}
$$

where $\Psi_{1}=\left[A_{1} \mathrm{j}_{L}\left(k_{\ell} r\right)+B_{1} \mathrm{n}_{L}\left(k_{\ell} r\right)\right] \cdot \mathrm{Y}_{L}^{\mathrm{m}}(\theta, \varphi)$ is the scalar potential associated to a longitudinal spherical acoustic wave of angular frequency $\omega$ and wave vector $k_{\ell}=\omega / c_{\ell}$, with longitudinal acoustic velocity $c_{\ell}$. The scalar potential $\Psi_{1}$ verifies the Helmholtz equation $\nabla^{2} \Psi_{1}+k_{\ell}^{2} \Psi_{1}=0$. The functions $\mathrm{j}_{L}\left(k_{\ell} r\right)$ and $\mathrm{n}_{L}\left(k_{\ell} r\right)$ are the spherical Bessel functions of first and second kind, respectively. The $(2 L+1)$ spherical harmonic functions $\mathrm{Y}_{L}^{\mathrm{m}}(\theta, \varphi)$ - the integers $L$ and $m$ are such that $L \geqslant 0$ and $|m| \leqslant L$ - are the basis functions of an irreducible representation of dimension $2 L+1$ for the $\mathrm{O}(3)$ point group [10]. The orthonormal basis for the spherical coordinates system is denoted $\left(\mathbf{e}_{r}, \mathbf{e}_{\theta}, \boldsymbol{e}_{\varphi}\right)$. The two polar vector potentials $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{2}=r \Psi_{2} \mathbf{e}_{r}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{3}=r \Psi_{3} \mathbf{e}_{r}$ are associated to the two transverse spherical acoustic waves, with the wave vector $k_{t}=\omega / c_{t}$ and the transverse acoustic velocity $c_{t}$. The two scalar potentials $\Psi_{2}$ and $\Psi_{3}$ are solutions of the same scalar Helmholtz equation $\nabla^{2} \Psi+k_{t}^{2} \Psi=0$. Thus, the two particular solutions for these scalar potentials are $\Psi_{2}=$ $\left[\mathrm{A}_{2} \mathrm{j}_{L}\left(\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{t}} \mathrm{r}\right)+\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{L}}\left(\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{t}} \mathrm{r}\right)\right] \cdot \mathrm{Y}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{m}}(\theta, \varphi)$ and $\Psi_{3}=\left[\mathrm{A}_{3} \mathrm{j}_{\mathrm{L}}\left(\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{t}} \mathrm{r}\right)+\mathrm{B}_{3} \mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{L}}\left(\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{t}} \mathrm{r}\right)\right] \cdot$ $\mathrm{Y}_{L}^{\mathrm{m}}(\theta, \varphi)$. The second and third terms in Eq. (1) are calculated by application of the curl operator $\nabla \wedge$, which switches the polar and axial characters of vector fields. The polar-axial switch is illustrated in the Maxwell-Faraday equation $\nabla \wedge \mathbf{E}=-\partial \mathbf{B} / \partial t$, where the magnetic induction field $\mathbf{B}$ is axial and the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is polar. The application of the double curl operator $\nabla \wedge \nabla \wedge$ to the polar vector potential $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{3}$ gives also a polar vector. As the gradient of the scalar field $\Psi_{1}$ is a polar vector, the $\nabla \wedge \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{2}$ is the only axial vector. Thus, in order to preserve the polar or axial homogeneity of the displacement vector $\mathbf{u}$ for a normal vibration mode, the expression of the displacement field must be either:

$$
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{S}}=\nabla \Psi_{1}+\nabla \wedge \nabla \wedge \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{3},
$$

which expresses the displacement field of spheroidal modes,

or

$$
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{T}}=\nabla \wedge \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{2},
$$

which express the torsional modes. A torsional mode involves only a transverse spherical acoustic wave. By contrast, a spheroidal mode is a superposition of a longitudinal spherical wave and a transverse spherical wave. The spheroidal and torsional modes are labeled $S_{L}$ and $T_{L}$ respectively, where the integer $L$ determines the spherical harmonics $Y_{L}^{m}(\theta, \varphi)$ and the modal degeneracy $2 L+1$. When $L=0$, the spherical harmonic function $Y_{0}^{0}(\theta, \varphi)$ is a constant function, so $\nabla \wedge \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{2}=0$ and $\nabla \wedge \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{3}=0$. In consequence, there is no $T_{0}$ mode and the $S_{0}$ modes involve only spherical waves. The $S_{0}$ mode of lowest frequency is commonly named the "breathing mode" of the sphere [14]. An additional integer number $n$ must be used to sequence $S_{L}$ or $T_{L}$ modes in the frequency scale. The spectroscopic notation $n S_{L}$ or $n T_{L}$ is generally adopted to label the vibration modes of a sphere [4]. Starting the numbering by $n=1$, the integer $n-1$ is then interpreted as the number of spherical nodal surfaces in the displacement field. Then, for a given value of $\mathrm{L}$, the fundamental $S_{L}$ mode is noted $1 S_{L}$. The rotation and the translation of a free sphere are trivial solutions of the wave equation with zero eigenfrequencies, which have to be labeled $1 \mathrm{~T}_{1}$ or $1 \mathrm{~S}_{1}$, respectively, but cannot be considered as vibration modes. The first vibrations modes of $T_{1}$ or $S_{1}$ types are in fact the $2 T_{1}$ and $2 S_{1}$ modes.

Another feature of a vibration mode of a spherical object emerges from the transformation of the displacement vector field $\mathbf{u}$ under inversion. This transformation is determined by both considering the transformation of the spherical harmonics $Y_{L}^{m}(\theta, \varphi)$ and the gradient operator $\nabla$ under inversion. Since $Y_{L}^{m}(\pi-\theta$, $\varphi+\pi)=(-1)^{L} Y_{L}^{m}(\theta, \varphi)$ [13], the three scalar potentials transform as $\Psi_{i} \rightarrow(-1)^{L} \Psi_{i}$. Moreover, the operator $\nabla$ transforms as $\nabla \rightarrow$ $-\nabla$, under inversion. Hence, Eqs. (2) and (3) lead to the transformations: $\mathbf{u}\left(S_{L}\right) \rightarrow-(-1)^{L} \mathbf{u}\left(S_{L}\right)$ and $\mathbf{u}\left(T_{L}\right) \rightarrow(-1)^{L} \mathbf{u}\left(T_{L}\right)$. Then, the symmetric modes are $S_{L(\text { even })}$ and $T_{L(\text { odd })}$ and the anti-symmetric modes are $S_{L(\text { odd })}$ and $T_{L(\text { even })}$. The symmetric and anti-symmetric classification arises directly by considering the two-element Abelian sub-group $(E, i)$, which has only two one-dimensional irreducible representations. The inversion symmetry will be important to consider in Section 3.2 in order to predict the lifting of the modal degeneracy under the presence of a defect.

### 2.2.2. Finite element modeling of a HTR particle

Group theory can predict the symmetry properties of modal shapes but the eigenfrequencies have to be calculated by solving the wave equation. We assumed that each material is elastically isotropic and thus characterized by a density, a Young modulus and a Poisson coefficient. Boundary conditions were determined by assuming ideal bonding between layers and zero stress at the outer surface. Calculations were carried out using finite element modeling with the parameters of Table 1, which depicts a typical HTR particle of $1 \mathrm{~mm}$ diameter. The calculated eigenfrequencies are presented in Table 2. Indeed, a weak numerical degeneracy lifting was observed. This fact can be attributed to imperfection of the mesh, which does not perfectly reproduce the spherical symmetry of the particle. By diminishing the element size, the degeneracy of the calculated eigenfrequencies can be more and more fairly reproduced, at the expense of an increase in computing time. Indeed, the accuracy of the calculations is not only determined by the size of the elements in the mesh but also by the choice of the interpolation functions. With quadratic tetrahedral finite elements of reasonable size, the calculated multiplet widths remain under $2 \mathrm{kHz}$ for modes under $5 \mathrm{MHz}$, which is far smaller than the gaps between successive multiplets. Consequently, we present in Table 2 only the mean eigenfrequency for each multiplet. The inspection of modal shapes allowed us to determine the spheroidal or torsional nature of the mode. In Fig. 5, the $1 S_{2}$ spheroidal mode and the $1 \mathrm{~T}_{2}$ torsional mode are represented. For the latter mode, the outer surface of the modal shape is a sphere with a constant radius. For spheroidal modes, the shape generally deviates from spherical except for $S_{0}$ modes which involve an isotropic radial deformation.

For a given set of mechanical parameters, the particle diameter was adjusted to fit roughly the experimental frequencies of Fig. 4. When the thickness of each layer is multiplied by a number $\alpha$, the particle diameter is also multiplied by $\alpha$, and then all eigenfrequencies are divided by $\alpha$. This procedure of fitting is justified by the fact that in a batch, the mechanical parameters may be homogeneous but the thickness of each layer may be a little dispersed, so the eigenfrequencies are roughly correlated with the size of the particles. Table 2 shows that, with a particle of 1.20 $\mathrm{mm}$, the calculated spectrum fits rather satisfactorily the measured eigenfrequencies that are displayed in Fig. 4. Deviations of the calculated eigenfrequencies from the experimental ones are less than $10 \%$. The greatest discrepancy is nevertheless for the $1 \mathrm{~S}_{4}$ multiplet, where the experimental frequency is $f_{\exp } \approx 3.7 \mathrm{MHz}$ and the calculated frequency is $f_{\text {calc }} \approx 3.4 \mathrm{MHz}$. In reality, the parameters of the tested particle were not all accurately known. At the end of the fabrication process, some differences may occur between the true and the specified values. Fortunately, some parameters can be determined by X-ray radiography, which is a non-destructive method, thus reducing the number of unknown parameters [15,16]. Consequently, only the mechanical parameters which have a strong effect on the eigenfrequencies can be determined with a satisfactory precision by fitting the calculated spectrum with the experimental eigenfrequencies. The majority of experimental spectra show that the multiplet about 2 MHz is composed with a maximum of three lines. In Fig. 4, the 2S₁ multiplet shows two strong lines and a weak and broad line. This observation is in agreement with the threefold degeneracy of 2S₁ modes. The degeneracy lifting arises essentially because of tiny deviations from the perfect spherical symmetry. The 2 MHz triplet cannot be attributed to the 2T₁ modes. The first reason is that the configuration of the pump laser has not the required symmetry for the excitation of transverse waves and thus torsional modes. Secondly, our interferometer is not sensitive to transverse displacements. So, for the mentioned reasons, the detection of a 2T₁ mode is much more unlikely than a 2S₁ mode. Therefore the 2 MHz triplet can be surely attributed to 2S₁ spheroidal modes. The 2S₁ modal shape, not presented here, shows that the ZrO₂ core moves in one direction almost as a rigid body whereas the rigid outer shells (IPyC/SiC/OPyC) move in the opposite direction. To give a simple picture of this antisymmetric normal mode, the relatively smooth buffer layer (PPyC) plays the role of a spring which binds two almost rigid bodies: the core and outer shells, like a dipole. The zero momentum of the particle is conserved during the vibrations. The modal shape of a 2S₁ mode exhibits a symmetry axis. Clearly, because of the spherical symmetry of the particle, the 2S₁ eigenfrequency does not depend upon the direction of this symmetry axis. So, the three independent directions in space form a modal basis for the threefold degenerate 2S₁ modes. Similarly, the modal shape of a 2T₁ mode shows that the ZrO₂ core rotates around one axis in one direction while the outer shells rotate rigidly in the opposite direction around the same axis, thus conserving at any time a zero angular momentum during the oscillations. The threefold degeneracy of the 2T₁ modes is related to the existence of three perpendicular independent rotation axes. The simulation indicates that the second multiplet around 2.5 MHz can be attributed to the quadripolar fivefold degenerate 1S₂ modes, whose model shape is represented in Fig. 5. This supposition is confirmed by the observation of experimental spectra which indicate that this multiplet contains no more than five frequency lines. The 1S₂ modal shape shows that this mode essentially involves the deformations of the outer shells, whereas the rigid core undergoes nearly no deformation. The third and fourth multiplets are respectively 1S₃ and 1S₄ modes, which involve, as for the 1S₂ modes, flexions of the outer shells. We conclude that the experimental spectrum in Fig. 4 displays only eigenfrequencies of spheroidal modes. The detected multiplets appear in the sequence: 2S₁, 1S₂, 1S₃ and 1S₄, corresponding to frequencies around: 2 MHz, 2.5 MHz, 3.0 MHz and 3.7 MHz respectively. In addition, the spectral width of each multiplet increases through the series: 2S₁, 1S₂, 1S₃ and 1S₄. The 1T₂ torsional eigenmodes are expected between 1S₃ and 1S₄ modes, but they are not observed in Fig. 4. The modal shape of a particular 1T₂ mode is represented in Fig. 5, which shows that the outer shell is divided into two hemispheres that rotate in opposite directions. The equatorial plane is a nodal plane for the displacement vector, but it is also the area where the materials are strongly sheared.

Note that the modal shapes could be determined by scanning the probe beam along the surface of the particle, so that the eigenmodes could be identified unambiguously [20].

## 3. Detection of an embedded crack

### 3.1. Experiment

Detecting a crack in the SiC shell is very important, since this shell plays a fundamental role in the confinement of fission products. We demonstrate the detection of a crack in the SiC shell by laser ultrasonics. The test was carried out on dummy HTR particles with a ZrO₂ core. An artificial crack of about 50 μm wide and 500 μm long was created in a series of selected HTR particles. After fabrication, some particles that have almost identical diameters were selected from the batch. This selection results in a low dispersion of vibration spectra through the series. This dispersion can be appreciated by evaluating the intervals between the "forbidden gaps", where no eigenfrequency is detected in a defect-free particle, and denoted G₀, G₁, …, G₄ in Fig. 6. The selected particles were introduced in an oven and heated in air in order to burn the outer pyrocarbon shell (OPyC). After removing the OPyC shell, an artificial crack was made in the SiC shell by cutting the SiC shell along a meridian plane by a laser beam. In the last step of the process, the cracked particles were placed again in the fluidized-bed furnace in order to restore the dense pyrocarbon outer shell. At the end of the process, the cracks were filled with dense pyrocarbon and the internally damaged particles looked completely sound from visual inspection. The size of crack could be evaluated after the process on some particles of the series by X-ray radiography [15,16].

The appearance of many eigenfrequencies in the "forbidden gaps" is the major indicator of the presence of the defect. The spectrum of the cracked particle displays also an increase in multiplet widths. In addition, eigenfrequencies tends to be down-shifted by the presence of a crack. The sensitivity of the method is really satisfactory. Indeed, cracks of smaller dimensions ($10\ \mu$m wide and $500\ \mu$m long) can be detected without doubt.

### 3.2. Modal analysis of a cracked particle

#### 3.2.1. Symmetry considerations

Symmetry considerations can help us to analyze how a defect may change the vibration spectrum of a HTR particle. The presence of a defect in a spherical particle generally reduces the number of symmetry operations. The group of symmetry of a damaged spherical particle becomes a sub-group of the point group O(3). This situation occurs, for example, if there is an inclusion, a crack in a layer, or a local disbonding between two layers. The presence of defects in spherical balls generally leads to the degeneracy lifting of spheroidal and torsional modes [17]. When a meridian crack is present in a spherical structure, the number of symmetry operations is drastically reduced. The only remaining symmetry operations, apart from the identity operation denoted E, are: the 2-fold rotation $C_2$ around the symmetry axis that intersects the center of the crack, the $\sigma(zx)$ mirror plane that contains the line of the crack and the $\sigma(yz)$ mirror plane which is perpendicular to the zx-plane (Fig. 7). All these elements constitute the point group denoted 2 m in Hermann-Mauguin notation and $C_{2v}$ in Schönflies notation.

Degeneracy lifting can be predicted using the character table of the 2 m point group, which is shown in Table 3. The characters are denoted $\chi_i(R)$, where R represents the classes (column headers of Table 3) of the point group and the subscript i represents a particular irreducible representation (irrep). The existence of only one-dimensional irreducible representations in the 2 m group demonstrates that the 2L+1-fold degeneracy of each $S_L$ or $T_L$ multiplet is completely lifted by the presence of a crack in the SiC shell. Eigenmodes of a cracked particle are labeled: $A_1$, $A_2$, $B_1$ and $B_2$, according to the irreducible representations of the 2 m point group. The character table indicates also the symmetry properties of the displacement field for each mode, since it is transformed either as $\mathbf{u} \rightarrow -\mathbf{u}$ or $\mathbf{u} \rightarrow \mathbf{u}$. For example, the modal shape of an $A_1$ mode must be invariant upon all symmetry element of the point group 2 m. The displacement of $A_1$ modes has the same symmetry properties as a translation along the z-axis. The $A_2$ mode is similar to a rotation $R_z$ around the z-axis.

To determine the splitting of the $S_L$ and $T_L$ modes under the 2 m symmetry, it is necessary to determine first the characters $\chi(R)$ of $S_L$ and $T_L$ modes for the class of twofold rotations $C_2$ and for the class of reflection planes $\sigma$ in the O(3) group. The representation associated to $S_L$ or $T_L$ modes have both the dimension 2L+1. Thus, these latter representations are reducible in the 2 m point group. The character for the class of rotations of angle $\alpha$ is $\chi(\alpha)=\sin[(L + 1/2)\alpha]/\sin(\alpha/2)$ [10]. In particular, for a twofold rotation: $\chi(C_2)=(-1)^L$. To determine the character of the class of reflection planes, one has to consider the direct product of groups: O(3)=SO(3) $\times$ (E, i), where SO(3) is the sub-group of O(3) containing only the rotations. Such a direct product can be constructed as each element of the point group (E, i) commutes with all the rotations of O(3). Thus, the class of reflection planes is generated by the product of the $C_2$ class with the inversion: $\sigma=C_2 \times i$. Consequently, the character for a reflection plane is: $\chi(\sigma)=\chi(C_2)\cdot\chi(i)$. As $\chi^{T_L,S_L}(C_2)=(-1)^L$, $\chi^{T_L}(i)=-(-1)^L$ and $\chi^{S_L}(i)=(-1)^L$ (Section 2.2.1), one obtains: $\chi^{T_L}(\sigma)=-1$, $\chi^{S_L}(\sigma)=1$. ### 3.2.2. Finite element modeling of a cracked particle
Finite element modeling was used to calculate the normal modes of a HTR particle modified by a crack inside the SiC shell. The size of the crack was $50\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ wide and $500\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ long in the SiC shell. This size corresponds to the cracked particles used in the test. The identification of each mode was made through the observation of the symmetry properties of the modal shape. The finite element calculation of the $2S_1$ multiplet width leads to a value of about 30 kHz, which is rather weak. In comparison, the experimental $2S_1$ multiplet width displayed in Fig. 6 is about 60 kHz. The calculated $1S_2$ multiplet width is around 250 kHz, as it can be shown in Fig. 8. In Fig. 6, we estimate the experimental $1S_2$ multiplet width at about 350 kHz. Modal shapes of the perturbed $1S_2$ modes are not considerably changed compared to the unperturbed $1S_2$ modes (Figs. 8). The finite element calculation of the multiplet widths for the $1S_3$, $1T_2$ and $1S_4$ are respectively: 250 kHz, 100 kHz and 100 kHz.

As all the multiplets are completely split into isolated eigenfrequencies, the number of eigenfrequencies is expected to increase drastically in the experimental spectrum. The simulation shows that most eigenfrequencies are down-shifted. This latter effect is understood as a consequence of a loss of rigidity of the confinement shells due to the presence of the crack. Modal shapes of perturbed $1S_2$ mode are not deeply changed by the crack (Figs. 8 and 9). Conversely, modal shapes of perturbed torsional $1T_2$ modes are more affected by the crack as it is shown for the $\mathrm{A}_1$ mode in Fig. 9. Indeed, this perturbed torsional modes does not longer appear as a pure torsional mode, since the deformation of the outer shell displays a significant radial displacement. To account for the emergence of radial components in the perturbed torsional modes, we may consider the perturbed torsional modes as "mixture" of torsional and spheroidal modes, or torsional-spheroidal hybrid modes. Such hybrid modes are observed in anisotropic spherical balls [18]. In the latter case, the displacement vector of some modes of anisotropic spheres is explicitly expressed by a combination of spheroidal and torsional modes. The closeness of $1T_2$ eigenfrequency with the $1S_3$ and $1S_4$ eigenfrequencies strongly favors the coupling of $1T_2$ modes with the adjacent spheroidal modes $1S_3$ and $1S_4$. Consequently, because of their significant radial displacement, some hybrid modes are more easily detected by an interferometer sensitive to out-of-plane displacements. The lines around 3.6 MHz between the $1S_3$ and $1S_4$ eigenfrequencies in Fig. 6 could be due to such perturbed torsional modes. The closeness of $2T_1$ and $2S_1$ eigenfrequencies probably favors a $2T_1$-$2S_1$ hybridization, which may be viewed as a coupling between the translation and the rotation motion of the core-shell system. The eigenfrequency around 1.65 MHz, which is close to the $2T_1$ eigenfrequency, could be such a hybrid mode.

### 4. Conclusion

The laser ultrasonics RUS technique was applied to the testing of HTR particles. The characteristic vibration spectrum was measured for a defect-free particle. Eigenfrequencies are in the range of 2–5 MHz. Rather high quality factors were measured for these modes. A crack of 50 µm wide and 500 µm long in the SiC shell, hidden by the outer pyrocarbon shell, was detected by the alteration of the vibration spectrum of the particle. The effect of the crack was simulated in order to understand the modifications of the vibration spectrum induced by the crack. The presence of a crack in the SiC shell of a spherical HTR particle has major effects on the vibration spectrum: full degeneracy lifting and torsional-spheroidal mode coupling. The method would also be useful for the detection of other kind of defects, such as local disbonding of layers. In addition, the knowledge of the eigenfrequencies of a defect-free particle can be exploited for the evaluation of the Young modulii of the SiC and PPyC layers.
