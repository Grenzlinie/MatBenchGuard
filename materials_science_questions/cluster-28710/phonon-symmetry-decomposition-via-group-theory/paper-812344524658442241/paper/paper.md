# Irreducible Representations, Symmetry Coordinates, and the Secular Equation for Line Groups*

MARVIN C. TOBIN†

Olin Mathieson Chemical Corporation, New Haven, Connecticut

The theory of irreducible representations of one-dimensional space groups is reviewed. Explicit directions for setting up representations are given and illustrated by examples. Symmetry coordinates and factoring of the secular equation for chain molecules are discussed.

## INTRODUCTION

The use of group theory to treat the molecular dynamics of chain molecules has so far been limited to the derivation of selection rules for spectroscopically active fundamentals and overtones (1). This may be done from a knowledge of the character table of the factor group of the one-dimensional space group ("line-group") describing the molecular symmetry. However, a more involved analysis, such as a normal coordinate analysis, is greatly facilitated if all of the representation matrices for the line group are available. While many authors (2) have given character tables for space groups, none have given explicit directions for obtaining the representation matrices. This paper presents, in a form useful to molecular spectroscopists, methods of deriving representation matrices for the one-dimensional space groups used in treating polymer chains. The matrix representations for several typical line groups are derived. Also the factoring of the secular equation and the symmetry coordinates for polymer molecules will be discussed.

## LINE GROUPS

A polymer molecular is generated by propagation of a one-dimensional repeat unit along the chain axis. This fact is expressed by including translations as well as point operations in the group describing the symmetry of the molecule. Mixed point-translational operations, glide reflections or screw rotations, may occur as well. The pure translational operations constitute an Abelian invariant subgroup

to the line group (3). The line group may be expanded in cosets based on the trans- lation group. Thus

$$
L=A_{1} T+A_{2} T+\cdots A_{n} T. \tag{1}
$$

Here $T$ is the subgroup of pure translations, and the $A_{i}$ are glide reflections, screw rotations, or point operations. In geometrical terms, Eq. (1) states that any cov- ering operation of a line group may be considered as the product of a standard point operation, glide reflection, or screw rotation with a pure translation. The same is true of the matrices in any representation of the line group. The number of "coset representatives," $A_{i}$ , is given by the number of symmetry operations associated with a single repeat unit. The factor group based on the translational subgroup will always be isomorphic to one of the point groups. Its irreducible representations are irreducible representations of the line group in which the translational matrices $T$ are all unit matrices.

Now, let us suppose that we have somehow obtained an irreducible nonfactor group representation of the line group, expressed in the form (1). It will be found that the matrices $T$ will be (generally, reducible) representations of the transla tional subgroup. If the line group contains no glide planes or screw axes, the matrices $A_{1}, A_{2}, \cdots, A_{n}$ will constitute a (generally, reducible) representation of the factor group. If the line group contains glide planes or screw axes, the last statement will no longer hold. However, one may select from those matrices $A$ corresponding to point operations, representations for point subgroups to the line-group factor group. It will also be found that some power of a glide reflection or screw rotation matrix equals a translation.

Our problem thus reduces to the simpler problem of finding matrices for the translational operations, $T$ , and for a small number of coset representatives, $A_{1}, A_{2}, \cdots, A_{n} .$

Winston and Halford (2) have shown that the irreducible representations of aone-dimensional translation group may be given by the expressions:

$$
\begin{gathered}
(T)=e^{2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}}=e^{2 \pi i n l / N}, \\
\mathbf{k}=n \mathbf{b} / N, \mathbf{t}=l \mathbf{d}, 0 \leqslant n, l<N \text { or }-\frac{N}{2}<n, l<\frac{N}{2}.
\end{gathered} \tag{2}
$$

Here $d$ is the one-dimensional lattice vector, $b$ is a vector such that $b \cdot d=1$ , and N is the number of repeat units in the chain. (The translational group is assumed to be finite and cyclic, so that the $(N+1)$ th translation is taken as equivalent to the first.) To generate the $n$ th of the $N$ irreducible representation, we assign a value to $n$ and run through the values of $l$ . The fifth representation of the translational group, for example, reads:

$$
\begin{array}{cccc} 
& T_{0} & T_{1} & T_{2} \\
R_{5} & 1 & e^{2 \pi i 5 \cdot 1 / N} & e^{2 \pi i 5 \cdot 2 / N} \cdots.
\end{array} \tag{3}
$$

Since the translational subgroup is Abelian, we may chose the translational matrices in line group irreducible representations to be diagonal.

# IRREDUCIBLE REPRESENTATIONS OF LINE GROUP

In order to complete the foregoing discussion, we need some results obtained by Seitz (4) on the reduction of space groups.

Let us suppose that we have a set, $f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})$, of basis functions for a line group. The subscript $i$ indicates that there may be more than one function characterized by the vector $\mathbf{k}$, although with the exception noted below, these will belong to different irreducible representations. According to the foregoing discussion,
$$
T_{\boldsymbol{t}} f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})=e^{2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}} f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}) \tag{4}
$$

If we consider the effect of a point operation, glide reflection or screw rotation on $\mathbf{k}$, either $A \mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}$, or $A \mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{k}$. First, Seitz showed that if $A \mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}$, then
$$
A f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})=a f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}), \tag{5}
$$
where $a$ is a matrix element of an irreducible representation. For line groups, if $A \mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{k}$, then
$$
A f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})=a f_{i}(-\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}). \tag{6}
$$
Since $f_{i}(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})$ and $f_{i}(-\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})$ arise from each other by symmetry operations, they must belong to the same irreducible representation of the line group.

Second, Seitz showed that the assemblage of operations $A T$ of a line group, for which $A \mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}$, constitutes a group. Furthermore, the basis functions for irreducible representations of this subgroup are basis functions for irreducible representations of the line group. To get a complete set of basis functions for the line group, we operate on the functions for the subgroup with any of the symmetry operations which send $\mathbf{k}$ into $-\mathbf{k}$.

Naturally, one does not often have available a set of basis functions for a subgroup or a line group. However, the foregoing results are useful in that they tell us what the irreducible representations must be like. For simplicity, we will first consider those line groups which contain no greater-than-two-fold rotation or screw axes. If the group contains no operations which send $\mathbf{k}$ into $-\mathbf{k}$, the translational matrices have the form $(e^{2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}})$ when $k \neq 0, \mathbf{b} / 2$. If the group contains such operations, then the translational matrices have the form
$$
\left\|\begin{array}{cc}
e^{2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}} & 0 \\
0 & e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}}
\end{array}\right\|.
$$

A set of vectors $(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k})$ which arise from each other by the group operations is called a "star." The group which leaves these invariant is here designated the "star group." It is clear from the foregoing that an irreducible representation of a line group may be characterized by the star $(\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k})$, and an irreducible

representation of the star group [Winston and Halford (2)]. Given an irreducible representation of the line group, we may generate all the representations charac- terized by a given star group representation by assigning all possible values to $(\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k})$. When $(\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k})=(0,0)$, the irreducible representations become re- ducible to factor group representations.

A complication arises when $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{b}/2$, so that $A\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}$ and $e^{2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{t}}=e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{t}}=(-1)^l$. In groups containing glide planes or screw axes, several functions with the same $\mathbf{k}$ may belong to the same irreducible representation when $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{b}/2$. The method for obtaining these "Brillouin zone boundary representations" [see Brillouin (3)] is indicated in the examples.

To prove completeness, we note that the line group is of order 8 N. ## FACTORING OF THE SECULAR EQUATIONS

A chain molecule whose unit cell contains $n$ atoms and which contains $N$ repeat units will give rise to $3nN$ normal modes. Of these, $3n$ will be factor-group modes, four of which will have zero frequency. Transferring to a symmetry coordinate system will cause the secular equation to factor into blocks along the diagonal. Most of these will differ from each other only in the vector $\mathbf{k}$.

We will consider the polyethylene chain as an example (1). This molecule contains six atoms per repeat unit. The first $18 \times 18$ diagonal block contains all modes having factor group symmetry. This block is broken down by the line group symmetry into two $1 \times 1$, two $2 \times 2$, and four $3 \times 3$ blocks. The next $(N - 2)$ $18 \times 18$ blocks break down into two $4 \times 4$ and two $5 \times 5$ blocks each. Of these $4(N - 2)$ blocks, one need solve only one $4 \times 4$ and one $5 \times 5$ block for the normal frequencies. The normal frequencies will be found to depend on $\mathbf{k}$, so that all of the remaining normal frequencies may be found from one $4 \times 4$ and one $5 \times 5$ block by varying $\mathbf{k}$.

The last $18 \times 18$ block, in which $\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{b}/2$, will split into two $4 \times 4$ and two $5 \times 5$ blocks. Due to degeneracy, only one $4 \times 4$ and one $5 \times 5$ block need be solved for the frequencies.

It is therefore necessary to have symmetry coordinates for translations and rotations which will reduce to zero when $\mathbf{k}=0$. It may be possible to choose these by inspection. Otherwise, the symmetry coordinates for the factor group may be set up in a Cartesian coordinate system, made orthogonal to translations and rotations (6) and then transformed to internal coordinates (7). Since the line group operations do not mix symmetry coordinates with different $\mathbf{k}$, it is very easy to set these up using a conventional formula (Ref. 7, p. 119, Eq. (7)). As an example, let us set up the $(\mathbf{k}, A_{1})$ C--C stretching coordinate for polyethylene. The $(-\mathbf{k}, A_{1})$ member of the pair $(\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k})A_{1}$ may be obtained from this by applying the inversion operation. Let us label the two C--C bonds in the first repeat unit $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$, the two C--C bonds in the second repeat unit $S_{2}$ and $S_{3}$, etc. Then, remembering that $T S_{1}=S_{3}, \bar{C}_{2}S_{1}=S_{2}$, etc., we get, neglecting the normalization factor $1/\sqrt{2N}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\mathbf{k}, A_{1}}^{\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}}= & \left(S_{1}+S_{2}\right)+e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{1}}\left(S_{3}+S_{4}\right)+e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{2}}\left(S_{4}+S_{5}\right) \\
& \cdots e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{N-1}}\left(S_{2 N-1}+S_{2 N}\right)+e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{0}}\left(S_{2}+S_{3}\right) \\
& +e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{t}_{1}\right)}\left(S_{4}+S_{5}\right)+\cdots e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{t}_{N-1}\right)}\left(S_{2 N}+S_{1}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

To show that this is a true symmetry coordinate, we apply the line group operations to it and generate the representation matrix elements. For instance,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{C}_{2} S_{\mathbf{k}, A_{1}}^{\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}}= & \left(S_{2}+S_{3}\right)+e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{1}}\left(S_{4}+S_{5}\right) \\
& +\cdots+e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{0}}\left(S_{3}+S_{4}\right)+\cdots \\
= & e^{2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{0}} S_{\mathbf{k}, A_{1}}^{\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Again, taking $\sigma_{v}$ such that $S_{2} \to S_{1}, S_{1} \to S_{2}, S_{3} \to S_{2 N}, S_{4} \to S_{2 N-1}$, etc., and remembering that $e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{N-1}}=e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot\left(-\mathbf{t}_{1}\right)}=e^{2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{1}}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{v} S_{\mathbf{k}, A_{1}}^{\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}}= & \left(S_{2}+S_{1}\right)+e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{t}_{1}}\left(S_{2 N}+S_{2 N-1}\right)+\cdots e^{-2 \pi i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{0}} \\
& \left(S_{1}+S_{2 N}\right)+\cdots=S_{-\mathbf{k}, A_{1}}^{\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
