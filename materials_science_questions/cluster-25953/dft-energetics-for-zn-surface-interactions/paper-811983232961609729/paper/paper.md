# Self-Assembled Alkanethiol Monolayers on a Zn Substrate: Structure and Organization
C. Nogues† and P. Lang*

ITODYS (CNRS UMR 7086), University Paris 7, 1 rue Guy de la Brosse, 75005 Paris, France

Received January 22, 2007. In Final Form: March 19, 2007

This work concerns a first step toward the use of self-assembled monolayers derived from thiols as coupling layers between a zinc surface and organic coatings. The adsorption, structure, and aging of alkanethiol monolayers on zinc substrates have been studied by contact angle measurements, infrared spectroscopy, and electrochemistry. The thiols self-assemble on the zinc surface to form a highly hydrophobic monolayer. The molecules are well organized with very few gauche defects, oriented nearly normal to the surface, and protect the zinc from oxidation in a neutral aqueous medium.

## Introduction
Over the past 20 years, self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) of various chain lengths and terminal groups have been extensively studied. $^{1-3}$ SAMs give strongly bound and densely packed organic molecules on various surfaces. The character of the bond created between the SAM and the substrate is governed by both the nature of the surface and the reactive group of the molecules. In particular, thiol moieties $^{4}$ have a high affinity toward metals (oxidation state 0) such as $Au^{5-10}$ or $Ag,^{11,12}Pt,^{13-18}Cu,^{19-23}$ $Al,^{24,25}Fe,^{26,27}Ni,^{28-30}Ti,^{31}$ and $Zn.^{32,33}$ More recently, thiols have been adsorbed onto nonmetallic substrates: GaAs, $^{34}$ InP, $^{35}$ ITO, $^{36-38}$ and ZnSe. $^{39}$ Because of their intrinsic properties, SAMs provide an original path for surface modification and open up new perspectives for applications in materials science. For instance, surface wettability can be controlled by the adsorption of a specific SAM to give an antifouling coating. $^{40}$ It is important to control the surface energy and the chemical reactivity of a modified surface because these factors strongly influence the adhesion and structure of deposited organic thin films. In addition, it has been shown that SAMs provide protection against the corrosion of oxidizable metals such as $Fe^{26,27}$ and $Cu.^{19-23,41}$

Zinc used as a pure metal or in an alloy is very important in the domain of surfaces and coatings. It can form a passivation layer against corrosion and is easily deposited electrochemically in the metallic state from aqueous media. $^{42,43}$ However, the stabilization of such galvanized substrates under corrosive conditions and the further fixation of various coatings on these surfaces remains a serious problem. As a possible solution, we have exploited the self-assembly of alkanethiols on zinc, which give a barrier against ions, dioxygen, and water diffusion and then protection against corrosion. Furthermore, the thiols can act as a grafting agents if they possess a second suitable terminal function that is able to bind chemically or electrochemically to a subsequent layer. Bipolar molecules such as $\text{HS}-(\text{CH}_{2})_{n}-\text{Y}$ have already been tailored and used successfully to graft conducting polymers/oligomers to noble metals$^{44}$ but also to $\text{Al}^{25}$ and $\text{Ti}^{31}$ and to improve the adhesion of polyisoprene to $\text{Zn}$ wire. $^{45}$

In this work, we study the adsorption of SAMs on a metallic zinc surface through thiol chemisorption at atmospheric pressure. We characterized the self-assembly and the structure of the alkanethiol on zinc as a function of the alkyl chain length and the adsorption time. The resulting layers are densely packed, and the molecules are increasingly organized on the surface for longer adsorption times. The sulfur−zinc bond is strong enough to resist ultrasonic treatment. In addition, the stability of the layer under corrosive conditions, as studied by electrochemistry, reveals that the value of the oxidation potential of zinc increases significantly.

## Experimental Section

### Preparation of Zn Substrates.
Two different zinc samples were used for the adsorption of self-assembling monolayers.

For contact angle measurements and electrochemistry, zinc was electrodeposited from 1 M aqueous solutions of $\text{ZnSO}_4$ (Aldrich, 99.99%) adjusted to pH 2.5 with $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$. The platinum electrode was a spherical polyoriented single crystal. To remove the single-crystal properties of the platinum surface prior to electrodeposition, it was cycled in 0.5 M $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ (Suprapur, Merck) between −0.7 and 0.8 V versus $\text{Hg/HgSO}_4$, sat. $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$ (sweep rate 500 mV·s⁻¹). Metallic zinc was then deposited on the platinum with sonication⁵⁹ by scanning the potential to −1.7 V (50 mV·s⁻¹), which was maintained until the reduction charge reached $2.1\ \text{C·cm}^{-2}$ (ca. $1\ \mu\text{m}$ thickness of zinc). The current density was ca. $j\approx50\ \text{mA·cm}^{-2}$. For infrared spectroscopy, a Zn sheet (Goodfellows, 99.99%) ($1\times4$ cm²) was polished with abrasive paper (4000 Struers). Ultrapure water for solutions and for rinsing was taken directly from an Elgstat UHQ II ($18\ \text{M}\Omega\text{·cm}$) water purification system. Sonication was performed in a type T-460H Bioblock-Scientific ultrasonic bath at a frequency of 35 kHz and a power of 35 W.

### Preparation of Monolayers.
All vessels were carefully cleaned by immersion in hot sulfuric acid (Ultrapure, Merck) overnight and then rinsed thoroughly with ultrapure water. $n$-Decanethiol (DT), $n$-octadodecanethiol (ODT), $\text{CH}_3(\text{CH}_9)-\text{SH}$, and $\text{CH}_3(\text{CH}_{17})-\text{SH}$ (Aldrich, 99%) were used as received.

Just after Zn electroplating, the spherical electrode was rapidly rinsed with argon-saturated acetonitrile (distilled under argon) and transferred to argon-purged neat thiol for different adsorption times ranging from a few seconds to 48 h. Because $n$-octadecanethiol is solid at room temperature, it is melted at $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ prior to and during adsorption.

For the spectroscopic study, the zinc sheets are first polished in neat thiol in a glovebox under argon. After 10 min, the substrates are immersed in thiol for a few seconds up to 48 h.

All Zn samples were subsequently rinsed twice with dichloromethane (Aldrich, 99%) in the ultrasonic bath for 2 min to remove molecules that were not strongly grafted.

## Results

### Monolayer Organization and Structure. Contact Angle Measurements.
Contact angles of the monolayers as a function of the adsorption time were measured for zinc electrodeposited on a platinum single crystal, making it possible to perform the measurements in a controlled atmosphere and resulting in very reproducible data. We compared these results with contact angles measured on a flat polycrystalline-treated zinc surface.

Figure 1a,b reports the contact angles measured as a function of adsorption time for n-decanethiol (DT) and n-octadecanethiol (ODT) adsorbed on electrodeposited zinc. Both thiols give rise to a highly hydrophobic surface after 1 min of incubation. For long adsorption times, the contact angle is 123 ± 3° for DT and 129° ± 3° for ODT. For both monolayers, saturation is reached after 5 min. At shorter adsorption times, lower contact angles are found for DT (60 ± 4°) than for ODT (103 ± 4°). Therefore, the time required for the monolayer to be complete is shorter for the longer alkyl chain.

We report in Table 1 the values for planar substrates and values found in the literature for the same thiols on various metals.

The contact angles measured on an electrodeposited zinc surface are significantly higher than on a flat polycrystalline substrate. The electrodeposition of zinc leads to roughness that is likely to trap air during the immersion of the electrode in water. It was shown that on very hydrophobic, rough substrates air bubbles can be trapped in hollows, thereby increasing the contact angle, which is a phenomenon called superhydrophobicity.⁴⁶ On the flat zinc substrate, the contact angles are still very high and are similar to those measured on gold and silver (Table 1). SAMs of alkanethiols on these two metals are dense and highly organized. We conclude that the zinc surface allows the formation of dense SAMs of CH₃(CH₂)₉−SH and CH₃-(CH₂)₁₇−SH. In addition, high contact angles found after sonication indicate that the molecules are strongly bound to the zinc surface.

To minimize the oxidation of the zinc surface before the adsorption of the monolayer, the polycrystalline zinc substrate was polished in neat thiol under argon for 10 min and directly immersed in the thiol for 48 h. Therefore, the frequencies of $v_{\mathrm{a}}\mathrm{CH}_2$ (2919 cm⁻¹) and $v_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{CH}_2$ (2850 cm⁻¹) of $\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_{17}-SH}$ adsorbed on polycrystalline zinc demonstrate that the monolayer is densely packed and organized. Furthermore, the small peak widths at half-height (fwhh), 16.5 and 12 cm⁻¹ of the $v_{\mathrm{a}}\mathrm{CH}_2$ and $v_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{CH}_2$ peaks, respectively, confirm that most of the alkyl chains are in the trans conformation, with broader peaks being associated with disordered gauche-rich chains. $^{25,26}$ In addition, the frequency of the $v_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{CH}_3$ vibration of the monolayer is shifted toward higher energies than the corresponding vibration of the molecules in the liquid. This is also characteristic of monolayer organization. The intensities of the methyl vibrations are stronger than the methylene vibrations although there is one methyl for nine methylenes. This is an indication of a highly oriented monolayer with the molecules nearly normal to the surface.

$n$-Octadecanethiol. The qualitative characteristics of the spectrum for an ODT monolayer adsorbed on a zinc surface are similar to those described for a DT monolayer. Therefore, there is no significant influence of the chain length on the molecular packing and organization on the zinc surface. This is in agreement with the literature on thiol SAMs adsorbed on various metals and in which a length of $n = 9$ to 10 is sufficient to obtain close packing and self-assembly. $^{4,47}$

Quantitative Determination of the Molecular Orientation. The selection rule for metallic surfaces implies that the infrared-active modes are those having a component of the vibrational transition dipole moment normal to the surface, $M_{iz}$.

Therefore, the integrated intensity $I$ of a band corresponding to vibrational mode $i$ is proportional to $M_{iz}^2$

$$
I(M_i)\#\ (M_i \cdot \mathbf{E})^2\#\ M_{iz}^2 \tag{1}
$$

in which $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field normal to the surface of the metal. Introducing the absorption intensity $A_i$ for vibrational mode $i$ in the bulk isotropic product, we get for two vibrations $i$ and $j$

$$
\frac{I_{iz}}{I_{jz}} = \left(\frac{M_{iz}}{M_{jz}}\right)^2 = \left[\frac{F_i(\alpha, \beta)}{F_j(\alpha, \beta)}\right]^2\left(\frac{M_i}{M_j}\right)^2 = \left[\frac{F_i(\alpha, \beta)}{F_j(\alpha, \beta)}\right]^2\left(\frac{A_i}{A_j}\right)^2 \tag{2}
$$

where $F_i$ is a function depending on the angle between $M_i$ and $\mathbf{E}$.

$$
F_i(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{M_i}{M_{iz}} = \cos(M_i, \mathbf{E}) \tag{3}
$$

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the tilt and twist angles, respectively (Figure 3).

Evaluation of Orientation Angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Table 3 indicates the direction of the transition dipole moment and the value of $F_{ij}$ for the $\mathrm{C-H}$ bond stretching. The twist angle $\beta$ and the tilt angle $\alpha$ are calculated from the ratio between the relative integrated intensities of methyl and methylene vibrations of the molecules adsorbed on the surface ($I$) and of the neat liquid ($A$). $^{47,48}$ The relative integrated intensities of the methyl and methylene groups determined in the monolayer ($I$) and in the isotropic liquid thiol ($A$) give the monolayer orientation.

The twist angle $\beta$ is directly deduced from the $v_{\mathrm{a}}\mathrm{CH}_2$ and $v_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{CH}_2$ intensities and by using the selected angular functions

$$
\beta = \arctan \sqrt{\frac{I(v_{\mathrm{a}}\mathrm{CH}_2) A(v_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{CH}_2)}{I(v_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{CH}_2) A(v_{\mathrm{a}}\mathrm{CH}_2)}}, 0 \leq \beta \leq \pi/2 \tag{4}
$$

The values of $\beta$ (Table 4) are similar for the two thiols and are close to the values found in the literature. $^{4-11,48}$

Two solution couples are found for each of the chosen vibrations (Supporting Information). The differences between the positive angles ($\alpha_2^+ - \alpha_1^+$) are very small whatever the thiol length, whereas the negative ones are quite different. As has been shown, $^{48,49}$ this difference is related to the presence of gauche defects and should be zero for an all-trans chain. Introducing a number of gauche defects into the calculation should give the same value for $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_1$. As we show now, the true solution can be found on the basis of physical considerations.

Evaluation of Gauche Defects. In the SAMs, gauche defects correspond to the rotation of the $CH_3$ group around the outermost $CH_2-CH_2$ of the $CH_3$ group. It is generally assumed that at room temperature the gauche defects are located only at the extremity of the alkyl chain. $^{4,50,51}$ In our case, the latter assumption seems very likely in view of the highly organized nature of the monolayer.

Figure 4 clearly shows the influence that a gauche defect located at the extremity of an alkanethiol adsorbed on a surface has on $M_{iz}$. For a small positive value of $\alpha$ and for an odd number of $CH_2$ groups, the projected transition moment $M_{iz}$ of the in-plane $v_sCH_3$ decreases when a trans conformation is changed to a gauche defect. The opposite behavior is observed for the in-plane $v_a$$CH_3$. For both conformations, the $M_{iz}$ component of the out-of-plane $v_aCH_3$ depends only on the twist angle $\beta$. As a consequence, the calculated values of $\alpha_1^{\pm}$ and $\alpha_2^{\pm}$ using the intensities of $v_aCH_3$ and $v_sCH_3$ vibrations vary with the presence of gauche defects, and their difference expresses the number of gauche defects.

Table 6 gives the results for gauche defects in both monolayers for solutions $\alpha^+(\alpha_1^+, \alpha_2^+)$ and $\alpha^-$ ($\alpha_1^-, \alpha_2^-$)

The number of gauche defects for both monolayers and for positive tilt angles does not exceed 1%. This is in agreement with the position and width of the $\nu C-H$ bands observed in the IR spectra, which showed that the monolayers are highly organized. On the contrary, the percentage of gauche defects extracted from the negative values of the tilts is higher than 50%, which is not compatible with the same IR spectra. Therefore, the negative solution of the tilt angle has no physical meaning and can be rejected. We consider that the tilt angle of the molecules on the surface is $+14.5^\circ$ for $CH_3(CH_2)_9-SH$ and $+18.5^\circ$ for the $CH_3(CH_2)_{17}-SH$ monolayer with less than 1% gauche defects. Gauche defects are very rare and are independent of the alkyl chain length. The positive angle indicates that the metal-sulfur- carbon angle has increased compared to its value for a zero tilt angle of the molecule.

Blocking Electooxidation by Thiol Monolayers. Because of the self-organization properties of the $\text{CH}_3(\text{CH}_2)_9-\text{SH}$ and $\text{CH}_3-(\text{CH}_2)_{17}-\text{SH}$ monolayers on the zinc surface, it is interesting to study their ability to block zinc oxidation in an aqueous chloride solution at pH 7. Indeed, we expect that surface oxidation is sensitive to defects in the layer. Each defect allows oxidation of the metal. The propagation of the oxide at the interface between the zinc surface and the monolayer is expected to affect the organic layer dramatically.

Starting from the rest potential ($-1.3$ V), potential sweep voltammetry was monitored to $-0.95$ V/SCE. Figure 5 shows the first current-potential curve obtained with a DT monolayer adsorbed for 10 min and 16 and 48 h on zinc. For comparison, the first current-potential curve of bare zinc is shown. Figure 6 shows the current intensity measured at $-0.95$V/SCE at increasing adsorption time.

The current rises quickly from $-1.1$ V/SCE for the bare electrode whereas it increases slowly from $-1.07$, $-1.05$, and $-1$ V/SCE for adsorption times of 10 min and 16 and 48 h, respectively. Therefore, with a dense monolayer, the oxidation potential is increased by ca. 100 mV. Figure 6 demonstrates that the current decreases dramatically for zinc treated with a monolayer compared to bare zinc. After a few seconds, the oxidation current falls to a quasi-plateau where the current decreases very slowly to reach after 48 h a minimum value that is 20 times lower than for untreated zinc. The longer the adsorption time, the more efficient the blocking.

The influence of the chain length is clearly evidenced in Figure 7, and this appears to be an important parameter in hindering oxidation. The oxidation current measured at $-0.95$ V is about $1000\ \mu\text{A·cm}^{-2}$ for DT and $50\ \mu\text{A·cm}^{-2}$ for ODT when both thiols are adsorbed for 10 min. For an adsorption time of 48 h, the values of the current at the same potential decrease again by 1 order of magnitude. We conclude that an alkanethiol monolayer adsorbed on the zinc surface acts as an efficient barrier toward ion, dioxygen, and water diffusion with a decrease in current by 3 orders of magnitude compared to that for an untreated zinc substrate.

### Discussion

#### Tilt Angle, Organization of SAMs, and Nature of the Zn−S Bond.
The value and sign of the tilt angle of the molecules within a monolayer adsorbed on a metallic substrate depend on several parameters: (i) van der Waals interactions between alkyl chains; (ii) interatomic distances in the lattice or the distance between the anchor points of the molecules to the substrate; (iii) nature and geometry of the sulfur−metal bond; and (iv) interfacial energy of the outermost part of the monolayer with the external medium from which the monolayer was deposited.

Let us now consider the influence of the two first parameters. As was shown in the case of physisorbed monolayers of the Langmuir type or of crystallized alkanes, the system energy passes through a minimum for tilt angles of 0 and $30^{\circ},^{4,54}$ which give a better interlocking between the methylene groups of two neighboring molecules. If we consider the constraint added by the distance between two anchor points $d_{\text{GG}}$ (nm) (A), the molecular chain tilts toward the normal in order to optimize the VdW interactions (by approaching their next-nearest neighbor as closely as possible ($\sim$0.43 nm)). We get approximately

$$
\cos \alpha \approx \frac{0.43\ \text{(nm)}}{d_{\text{GG}}\ \text{(nm)}} \tag{5}
$$

Comparing the experimental values to the theoretical one according to the previous equation (Table 7), the agreement appears to be quite good except for Zn substrate. However, ignorance of the superficial crystalline structure of the Zn substrate makes it impossible to discuss the S−S distance further. Indeed, XPS has shown that the thiol is mainly bonded to Zn-(II).$^{55}$Nevertheless, we observe that the tilt angle is particularly small with respect to the Zn−Zn distance in the metallic state. It has been pointed out that the chain density$^{11,56}$ may be higher on oxidizable metals: the initial high coverage of oxygen atoms leads upon substitution by thiol to denser chain packing.

The sign of $\alpha$ and the very low number of gauche defects of alkanethiol/Zn clearly show a similarity between Zn, Ag, and

Cu substrates, which behave very differently from monolayers on gold. Laibinis¹⁰ and Porter⁵,¹¹ have noted that the direction of the tilt for alkanethiols CH₃(CH₂)ₙ−SH depends on the parity of n for Ag and Cu but not for gold. These observations have been rationalized mainly by considering the nature of the sulfur−metal bond related to the value of the M−S−C angle. For gold, the covalent nature of the sulfur−metal bond would impose a rigid and fixed geometry of MSC bonds (120° for the MSC angle) and a constant sign of α whatever the parity of n. In other cases, the Ag−S or Cu−S bond would be more ionic and the MSC angle would be more flexible, with the sign and value of the tilt angle being adjusted to optimize nearest-neighbor interactions and interfacial energy at the chain termini. Then for Ag and Cu and for long chains (n > 9), the tilt direction is positive for odd n and negative for even n. The alternation of the sign prevents the end methyl orientation changing with n parity. This weakly rigid metal−sulfur bond induces few gauche defects. In our case, the Zn substrate also shows a positive value of α with odd n and few gauche defects. The formation of an ionic bond of Zn (and Zn (II)) with the thiolate could perhaps explain the similarity with the Ag and Cu substrates. Moreover, we notice that these metals are all oxidizable and more electropositive than gold.

## Conclusions
We have shown that alkanethiols form dense, self-assembled monolayers on a Zn surface.

Their hydrophobicity is higher for the longer alkyl chain length and for a long adsorption time (48 h). The tilt angle of the molecules is +14° for n-decanethiol and +18° for n-dodecanethiol, according to our sign conventions. For both monolayers, the number of molecules having a gauche form is negligible (less than 1% of the total number of molecules within the layer). The self-organization and density of the monolayers increase the oxidation potential by 100 mV under the conditions studied, which implies some protection of the substrate.

The value and sign of the tilt angle, as well as the number of gauche defects in the adsorbed molecules, indicate that Zn behaves similarly to Ag and Cu but is different from gold. These results can be accounted for by the absence of a rigid bond between the Zn substrate and the thiolate, which is associated with the ionic character as for Cu and Ag.
