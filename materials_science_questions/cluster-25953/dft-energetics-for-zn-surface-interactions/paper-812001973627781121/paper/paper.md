# Crystal Quality of InN Thin Films Grown on ZnO Substrate by Radio-Frequency Molecular Beam Epitaxy

SATORU OHUCHI $^{1,2}$ and TOSHIYUKI TAKIZAWA $^{1}$

1.—Semiconductor Device Research Center, Semiconductor Company, Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. Ltd., Osaka 569–1193, Japan. 2.—E-mail: ohuchi.s@jp.panasonic.com

The growth of InN on {0001} ZnO substrates by radio-frequency, plasma-assisted, molecular-beam epitaxy (RF-MBE) has been experimentally investigated. The reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) pattern quickly recovered to a $1\times1$ streak pattern as the InN growth was started on nitridated ZnO substrates, whereas the RHEED pattern of the ZnO substrate was spotty because of plasma damage induced by nitridation. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the (0002) InN rocking curve was estimated to be around 150 arcsec from x-ray diffraction (XRD). Furthermore, we observed a remarkable feature from our experiments; namely, the crystal quality of InN does not seem to depend on the surface polarity of the ZnO substrate, while it is well known that InN growth on GaN has strong polarity dependence. To investigate this tendency, we have also investigated the surface stability of adatoms, In and N, on Zn- and O-face ZnO surfaces using a first-principles technique. From the theoretical study, N adsorption is more stable on ZnO surfaces of both polarities compared with In adsorption. Accordingly, the preferential initiation by N adatoms onto both ZnO surfaces can explain the unique style of InN growth on ZnO substrates.

Key words: InN, ZnO, molecular-beam epitaxy (MBE), polarity, first-principles calculation

## INTRODUCTION

Indium nitride has been rigorously studied because its bandgap was redefined to be around $0.6$–$0.7$ eV. $^{1–5}$ This narrow bandgap property of InN extends the available wavelength range toward the near infrared-red (IR) region by alloying with other nitride semiconductors (GaN and AlN) and suggests new device applications, such as solar cells, IR light-emitting diodes, and so on. Furthermore, InN has been predicted to have excellent electric properties compared with other nitride semiconductors $^{6}$ and has a potential to realize ultrahigh-speed electronic devices that would reach the terahertz region. $^{7}$

However, thin InN films of device quality have not yet been obtained because of the difficulties in growth; for example, the InN crystal easily decomposes at very low temperature (around $550^\circ\text{C}$), $^{8}$ the window of In/N ratio for successful growth is narrow, and the most serious problem is that it lacks convenient substrates for lattice matching.

Therefore, it is very important to choose a proper substrate to realize high-quality InN. Mainly two substrates, sapphire ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) and GaN, are well used in recent research. $^{3,8–10}$ Table I shows the lattice mismatch of each substrate for InN growth. Sapphire is most widely used; however, it has a very large lattice mismatch such that a low-temperature buffer layer is essential to remove the crystal stress. On the other hand, although GaN has relatively small lattice mismatch compared with sapphire, the properties of InN grown on GaN strongly depend on the polarity of the GaN (that is, Ga- or N-face GaN). Matsuda et al. have demonstrated that good crystal quality of InN can be achieved only on a N-face GaN substrate at high temperature. $^{9}$

In this study, we propose ZnO as a suitable substrate for InN growth. As already shown in Table I, ZnO also has a small lattice mismatch to InN. Accordingly, there exists a potential to make high-quality

## EXPERIMENTS

The undoped ZnO substrates we used were $1 \times 1$ cm² squares. Both Zn-face (0001) and O-face (000–1) substrates were prepared to study the InN property dependence on the substrate polarity. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of ω-scan (0002) rocking curves of ZnO substrates was measured to be approximately 80–100 arcsec by x-ray diffraction (XRD). Orientation-independent scratches with depths of around 1–2 Å were found on ZnO substrates by atomic force microscopy (AFM).

All InN films were grown directly on ZnO substrates by radio-frequency, plasma-assisted, molecular-beam epitaxy (RF-MBE) without any buffer layer. The RF-MBE system we employed was equipped with a cryopump and an ion pump that evacuates the growth chamber down to $2 \times 10^{-10}$ torr. A radical cell and an effusion cell were employed as N and In sources, respectively.

Figure 1 shows a diagram of the growth procedure. First, thermal cleaning of ZnO substrates was performed at the high temperature of $720^\circ$C for 30 min. Then, the ZnO substrate was exposed to the nitrogen radical flux from the RF-plasma cell for the surface nitridation at the temperature of $550^\circ$C for 60 min. The nitridation of the substrate is a useful method to achieve a uniform layer for III-nitride growth in RF-MBE.¹⁰,¹¹ After that, an InN film was grown at $550^\circ$C for 60 min. During the growth procedure (Fig. 1), in-situ reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) patterns were observed. The RF-plasma source was operated under the condition of a RF power of 290 W with an N₂ gas flow of 1 sccm.

![](./images/812001973627781121_1.jpg)

Fig. 1. Growth procedure of InN on ZnO substrates.

The beam equivalent pressure of In flux was fixed to be $8 \times 10^{-8}$ torr during the InN growth. This flux condition is equal or slightly larger than that for a 1:1 stoichiometry condition for InN growth. These conditions were applied to all InN growth on both Zn- and O-face ZnO substrates. The thickness of InN films on both substrates was around 150 nm under the growth conditions mentioned earlier.

After the growth, ex-situ characterization was performed by optical microscopy, XRD, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), AFM, and Hall measurements.

## RESULTS

### Growth Mode in RF-MBE

Figure 2 shows the RHEED patterns at each point (Fig. 1a–d) of the growth procedure. This figure shows InN growth on a Zn-face ZnO substrate. As shown in Fig. 2a, the RHEED pattern of the ZnO substrate remained a $1 \times 1$ streak pattern during thermal cleaning. It indicates that the ZnO substrate possesses atomically flat surfaces. After the surface nitridation, however, the pattern changed from streak to spotty (Fig. 2b), indicating a three-dimensional ZnO surface. According to AFM, the roughness of the nitridated ZnO substrates was around 6 Å. As the shutter of the In effusion cell was opened to grow InN, the RHEED pattern quickly recovered from spotty to a $1 \times 1$ streak pattern. Figure 2c shows the RHEED pattern of a 7.5-nm-thick InN surface after 3 min as the In shutter was opened. The spacing width of the RHEED streaks was reduced from that of ZnO, corresponding to the spacing width of RHEED streaks for well-relaxed InN grown on sapphire substrates. This observation suggests that the nitridation-induced rough ZnO surface was quickly buried and covered by a smooth InN layer during the initial growth. After the InN growth, the RHEED pattern was a sharp $1 \times 1$ streak, and no surface reconstruction pattern was observed according to Fig. 2d. We could not observe any changes in InN growth on O-face ZnO substrates compared with the case of the Zn-face.

## XRD Measurements

Figure 4a and b shows the XRD ω-scan (0002) rocking curves of InN grown on Zn- and O-face ZnO substrates, respectively. The XRD measurement shows the FWHM (mosaicity) to be 200–300 arcsec for each InN film, regardless of ZnO polarity. We suppose that the crystal quality of InN depends mainly on the quality of ZnO itself rather than polarity, while GaN polarity strongly affects the InN quality. From the ω-2θ scan, it was confirmed that the InN films we obtained are single crystal with wurtzite structure, oriented InN [11–20]//ZnO [11–20]. In addition, one of the samples we made showed a FWHM as small as 150 arcsec, which might be the smallest value as far as we know.

### Electric Properties

After the removal of In droplets, electrical mea- surements were performed. Hall mobilities and car- rier densities of InN films grown on Zn- and O-face ZnO substrates were measured at room tempera- ture and were found to be almost the same, around $250\ \text{cm}^2/\text{Vs}$ and $1\text{--}2 \times 10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$, respectively. Ac- cordingly, there seems to be no electronic property dependence on surface polarity of ZnO substrates.

### DISCUSSION

In the previous section, we mentioned that InN can be grown successfully on *both* Zn- and O-face ZnO substrates with the same growth conditions, in contrast to the strong polarity dependence on GaN substrates.⁹ One of the reasons for this can be found from the surface stability of the incident atoms (that is, In and N atoms on Zn). For example, in the case of GaN growth on sapphire ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) substrates, there are several kinds of atomic bonds, such as Ga-O, Ga-Al, N-Al, and N-O bonds. Among these atomic bonds, Onozu et al. show that the formation of the Al-N bond is most energetically stable on the sapphire substrate, using molecular dynamics cal- culations.¹² Thus, to answer why almost the same crystalline and electronic properties are shown for InN films grown on both ZnO surfaces as shown in the preceding experimental results, the initial InN growth on ZnO was estimated using first-principles calculations.

In this calculation, we employ the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package,¹³⁻¹⁵ which is based on the Kohn–Sham density-functional theory using a plane- wave basis set. Ultrasoft Vanderbilt pseudopoten- tials¹⁶ are employed using the generic gradient approximation for the exchange-correlation func- tional. For the total energy calculation, a $4 \times 4 \times 1$ Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh¹⁷ is used. The basis set cutoff energy is set at 20 Ry.

These surface models were fully relaxed using molecular dynamics before adsorption. The calculation procedure of surface stability is as follows. (1) The In or N adatom is set at one of the mesh points above the ZnO surface (Fig. 6b). (2) The structural relaxation of the adatom in each model is done only along the z-axis, while Zn and O atoms are fixed and not relaxed. (3) Finally, the total energy is calculated to estimate the stability of each model. The approximations that Zn and O atoms are fixed and relaxation of adatoms only along the z-axis are very effective to save computational resources. However, it is not sufficient to reveal the phenomenon at the initial InN growth because adatoms will affect the atomic coordination of the ZnO substrate. Nevertheless, this model can explain the growth initiation of InN on ZnO substrate approximately.

Figure 7a–d shows the contour maps of total energies of four models: N adatom with Zn-face, N adatom with O-face, In adatom with Zn-face, and In adatom with O-face model, respectively. Figure 7a shows the result of N adatom with the Zn-face model. Red and blue areas indicate N adatoms are unstable and stable at these points, respectively. From this result, it can be said that a N adatom tends to stay on Zn top surface atoms, rather than on O atoms. The minimum energy at the most stable point in Fig. 7a can be estimated as $-21.65$ eV. By the same manner, the minimum energies of the N adatom with O-face, In adatom with Zn-face, and In adatom with O-face model were calculated as $-21.75$ eV, $-20.08$ eV, and $-20.05$ eV, respectively.

From the viewpoint of ZnO polarity, we summarize the calculation results. For Zn-face models, the total energy with N adatoms is approximately 1.6 eV lower than that with In adatoms. This suggests that N atoms are more easily adsorbed on Zn-face ZnO compared with In atoms. Similarly, for the O-face, N atoms are more easily adsorbed on the O-face than In atoms because of a 1.7 eV lower total energy with N adatoms than with In adatoms. Consequently, a N adsorption layer might be formed just on ZnO surfaces preferentially, whichever Zn- or O-face is used. Therefore, we suppose that it might be a reason why InN can be grown on both surfaces of ZnO, and we observed no properties differences between InN layers grown on Zn-and O-face ZnO substrates.

# CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, InN films were grown on Zn- and O-face ZnO substrates by RF-MBE. It was observed that the ZnO substrate roughened by surface nitridation was quickly smoothed by the initial growth of InN. The TEM observation showed a thin intermediate region between InN and ZnO and an atomically smooth InN layer above. All our measurements suggested that InN films grown on Zn- or O-face ZnO substrates show almost the same properties in contrast to the strong dependence on the polarity of GaN substrates. The initial growth of InN on each

surface ZnO substrate was estimated using first-principles calculations, which showed that N adatoms are more easily adsorbed on both ZnO surfaces than In adatoms, regardless of ZnO polarity.
