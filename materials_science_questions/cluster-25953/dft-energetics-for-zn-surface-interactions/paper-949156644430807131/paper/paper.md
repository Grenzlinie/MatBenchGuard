# Designing Chemically Replaced Interfacial Layer via Unveiling the Influence of Zn Crystal Facets for Practical Zn-Metal Anodes

Jung Been Park, Changhoon Choi, Sang Won Jung, Byeong Chan Min, Jong Hyun Park, and Dong-Wan Kim*

Zn metal anodes (ZMAs) undergo irregular deposition and unfavorable side reactions, which hinders the practical application of aqueous rechargeable Zn metal batteries (ARZMBs). Chemical replacement reaction (CRR) strategies can achieve stable ZMAs, but the effect of the crystal facets of metallic Zn as reductants remains poorly understood. In this study, based on the observation that preferentially exposed Zn crystal facets affect the surface characteristics of chemically replaced layers in Sn-based CRR, a multifunctional Sn-based interfacial layer (ZnTCF@Sn) is designed on the Zn with textured crystal facets using a novel two-step CRR process. ZnTCF@Sn simultaneously provides abundant zincophilic sites and high surface energy and homogenizes the distribution of current/$Zn^{2+}$ flux, resulting in fast electrochemical kinetics and dendrite-free deposition. Furthermore, the uniform Sn coverage on the ZnTCF@Sn surface inhibits side reactions and enhances reversibility during Zn deposition/dissolution. Thus, the ZnTCF@Sn achieves exceptional cyclability over 1200 h even under harsh operating conditions with a cumulative capacity of 24 Ah cm$^{-2}$. This study contributes to the development of practical ARZMBs by providing new insights into the effect of the Zn crystal facets on the surface modification of ZMAs through various CRRs.

## 1. Introduction
Organic electrolyte-based Li-ion batteries are being extensively researched for sustainable energy storage.$^{[1]}$ However, the limited reserves of Li and flammability of organic electrolytes hinder their utilization in grid-scale energy storage systems.$^{[2]}$ Therefore, various aqueous rechargeable battery systems, such as Mg,$^{[3]}$ Al,$^{[4]}$ and Zn-metal batteries,$^{[5]}$ have emerged as promising alternatives owing to the abundance of metallic anode materials, simple manufacturing processes, and non-flammable characteristics of these battery systems, resulting in cost effectiveness, eco-friendliness, and improved safety. Among these, aqueous rechargeable Zn-metal batteries (ARZMBs) have attracted intensive interest owing to their low electrochemical potential ($-$0.76 V vs the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)), high theoretical specific capacity (820 mAh g$^{-1}$ and 5855 mAh cm$^{-3}$), and outstanding water compatibility of Zn-metal anodes (ZMAs).$^{[6]}$ Nevertheless, ZMAs may undergo reckless parasitic reactions, including Zn corrosion, hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), and dendritic growth of Zn deposits for repetitive Zn deposition/dissolution.$^{[7]}$ The dendrite growth on Zn surfaces with inevitable scratches and protrusions is primarily attributed to the increased local current density resulting from the irregular distribution of the electric field and $Zn^{2+}$ flux based on the "tip-effect."$^{[8]}$ Moreover, the relatively weak binding energy (low Zn affinity) between the metallic Zn surface and Zn deposits generates sluggish deposition kinetics and uncontrollable 2D diffusion of $Zn^{2+}$, which exacerbates the gradual dendrite prevalence on the Zn surface.$^{[9]}$ Owing to the inherently lower standard electrode potential of $Zn^{2+}$/Zn than $H^{+}$/$H_2$ (0 V vs SHE), competitive HER, a parasitic reaction, induces the formation of $H_2$ gas, local pH changes, and insulating byproducts, such as $Zn_4SO_4(OH)_6\cdot xH_2O$.$^{[10]}$ These chronic problems can interfere with each other, causing an inescapable short circuit, low Coulombic efficiency (CE), and sluggish electrochemical kinetics.$^{[11]}$ Thus, such an inherent Achilles' heel of ZMA is fundamentally associated with the surface features of ZMA. Alleviating these issues requires modifying the ZMA surface.

However, to construct ideal EULs, comprehensive requirements should be incorporated: First, from a crystallographic perspective, the atomic configuration of the basal surface influences the surface energy and affinity between the Zn nuclei and the basal plane. Specifically, a high Zn affinity can enhance Zn deposition kinetics by lowering the Zn nucleation/growth energy barrier, $^{[19]}$ and a surface energy increase can lead to preferential Zn deposition with a high planar packing density. $^{[20]}$ Therefore, to simultaneously achieve superior electrochemical kinetics and stable Zn deposition behavior, Zn affinity and surface energy should be considered. Second, designing an anticorrosive composition of EULs, that exhibits high chemical stability and hydrogen evolution overpotential, is crucial for suppressing the competitive HER. $^{[7 b]}$ Third, the EULs require morphological engineering to provide abundant zincophilic active sites to enable not only the uniform distribution of the electric field/Zn $^{2+}$ flux but also $Zn^{2+}$ transport/diffusion, even under high current density conditions. $^{[21]}$ Otherwise, EULs with a relatively restricted active surface can hardly withstand the intensified local current densities as the current density increases. Therefore, Zn deposition behavior is difficult to regulate. $^{[22]}$ Fourth, the layers commonly fabricated by ex situ methods using spin-coating or doctor blading techniques inevitably experience weak adhesion with metallic Zn and local inhomogeneity. $^{[18 a, 23]}$ However, the in situ fabrication of EULs can reduce interfacial resistance and ensure a high bonding strength of EULs and their uniformity on the ZMA surface. Ultimately, the aforementioned considerations, including crystallographic, compositional, and morphological perspectives and chemical/mechanical stability, are required in the ideal EUL design to achieve high-performance ARZMBs. However, studies reporting ideal EULs are scarce.

Therefore, using a novel two-step chemical replacement reaction (CRR), we developed a multifunctional, architectural Sn-based EUL on the Zn, with a textured crystal facet (ZnTCF@Sn). The influence of the crystal facets of metallic Zn as a reductant in the CRR for developing EUL was revealed for the first time. Sn as the material consisting of EUL was selected owing to its high hydrogen evolution overpotential, excellent Zn affinity, high conductivity, and higher reduction potential in comparison with that of Zn (-0.14 V and -0.76 V vs SHE, respectively). $^{[24]}$ Crystal facet engineering of metallic Zn induces a nanosized stalagmitelike ZnTCF@Sn surface, providing abundant zincophilic active sites. It also allows the optimal exposure of crystal facets with a similar ratio of $Sn_{(100)}$ and $Sn_{(101)}$, leading to the lateral growth and fast deposition kinetics of $Zn^{2+}$. Compared with bare Zn, ZnTCF@Sn showed high wettability, an ultralow overpotential for Zn nucleation/growth, and a remarkable antiparasitic reaction. Despite the extremely high current density $(20 mA cm^{-2})$ in symmetric cells, ZnTCF@Sn achieved remarkable stability and a prolonged lifespan over 1200 h with a low overpotential. Furthermore, even in practical $Zn/MnO_{2}$ full-cell tests with a low negative-to-positive electrode capacity ratio (N/P ratio) of ≈10.88, the ZnTCF@Sn full-cell exhibited improved cyclability and rate performance.

## 2. Results and Discussion
### 2.1. Design of ZnTCF@Sn and the Influence of Preferential Zn Crystal Facets in CRR
It has been well known that chemical species with lower redox potentials can spontaneously induce rapid CRRs of chemical species possessing higher redox potentials in solutions. $^{[25]}$ However, although EULs of various compositions (In, $^{[10 b]} Cu,^{[17 b]}$  $Ag,^{[26]}$ and $Sb^{[27]}$ ) on the ZMA surface through CRRs have been studied, in-depth studies on the influence of Zn crystal facets as reducing agents on CRRs have not been reported. In this study, we devised a novel two-step CRR process using $HCl$ and $SnCl_{4}$ solutions, and clarified the correlation between the suggested CRRs and exposed Zn crystal facets showing distinctive characteristics using theoretical and experimental evidence. Figure 1a shows a schematic of the stepwise ZnTCF@Sn fabrication process. First, bare Zn was polished to expose a fresh surface by eliminating the native oxide layer (mainly composed of ZnO and other Zn-containing species), thereby disturbing the CRR between Zn and other oxidizing species. $^{[28]}$ In the first step, the polished bare $Zn$ (pBZn) with the preferred $Zn_{(002)}$ crystal facet changes to bumpy Zn with a textured crystal facet (ZnTCF), which predominantly exposes the $Zn_{(101)}$ crystal facet via intentional CRR between metallic Zn and the HCl solution (redox potential $H^{+}/H^{0}$ :0 V vs SHE), following Equation (1)
$$\mathrm{Zn}^{0}+2 \mathrm{H}^{+} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn}^{2+}+\mathrm{H}_{2}\tag{1}$$

In the second step, due to the highest binding energy and affiliation between $Zn_{(101)}$ facet and $Sn$, compared to $Zn_{(002)}$ and $Zn_{(100)}$ facets, ZnTCF with $Zn_{(101)}$-dominant exposure undergoes an intense CRR with $Sn^{4+}$ in aqueous $SnCl_{4}$ solution (redox potential of $Sn^{4+}/Sn^{0}$: +0.1 V vs SHE) in a short time, as shown in Equation (2), inducing the formation of a novel architectural stalagmitelike ZnTCF@Sn with Sn crystal facets composed of $Sn_{(101)}$ and $Sn_{(200)}$ in a similar intensity ratio.
$$2 \mathrm{Zn}^{0}+\mathrm{Sn}^{4+} \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{Zn}^{2+}+\mathrm{Sn}^{0}\tag{2}$$

Gradual changes in the morphology and crystal facets of ZMA were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The wrinkled-surface pBZn with many abrasions shows an inversion of the predominant facet from $Zn_{(101)}$ to $Zn_{(002)}$ corresponding to $43.12^{\circ}$ and $36.15^{\circ}$, respectively (Figure 1b,c and Figures S1 and S2, Supporting Information), resulting from the basal plane slip mechanism preferentially texturing with $Zn_{(002)}$, induced by mechanical polishing with sandpaper. $^{[29]}$ After the first CRR between HCl and pBZn, known as chemical etching, the surface of the metallic Zn changed from silver-white to pale white (insets in Figure 1b,d). Although a dilute HCl solution is commonly used to eliminate native oxides on the surface of metallic materials, in our case, the intentional application of chemical etching to expose the crystal facet of $Zn_{(101)}$ resulted in the formation of hillocks and nanogrooves, which can serve as evidence of the preferential etching of grain boundaries and the predominant exposure of the $Zn_{(101)}$ crystal facet, respectively. $^{[30]}$ In addition, based on the terrace-step-kink model, $^{[31]}$ in strong acidic HCl solution, atoms on the relatively stable $Zn_{(002)}$ facet with high coordination number (CN) of 6 can be oxidized to form terraces, which produce adatoms, kink, and steps with low coordination numbers. After 1 min (Figure S5, Supporting Information), small size stalagmite-like spikes and nanosized tips began to form along the texture of ZnTCF, and after 3 min (Figure 1f and Figure S6, Supporting Information), the surface of ZnTCF entirely turned into longer spikes than those of the initial stage. Moreover, in the side-view SEM and EDS images of ZnTCF@Sn milled by a focused ion beam (Figure 1i), spikes with a height and width of $\approx 1 \mu m$ and 300-400 nm, respectively, are observed, and they are covered by a layer of Sn with an increasing density gradient from the bottom to the top. ZnTCF@Sn also displayed two newly detected XRD peaks of the tetragonal Sn crystal (PDF#04-0673) corresponding to $Sn_{(101)}$ and $Sn_{(200)}$, with similar intensities and a slight decrease in the $Zn_{(101)}$ peak (Figure 1g). Quantitative analysis of the dynamic texturing alteration during ZnTCF@Sn formation under various mechanical and chemical stimuli (Figure 1j) showed that the intensity ratio $I_{Zn_{(101)}} / I_{Zn_{(002)}}$ for bare Zn, pBZn, ZnTCF, and ZnTCF@Sn was 1.86, 0.82,10.0, and 7.69, respectively, and the intensity ratio $I_{Sn_{(200)}} / I_{Sn_{(101)}}$ of ZnTCF@Sn was almost 0.99, which implies that reactivity toward mechanical and chemical stimuli varied according to distinct crystallographic facets. The presence of Sn in ZnTCF@Sn was further analyzed by the survey scan of X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Figure S8a,b, Supporting Information). In the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Sn 3d, ZnTCF@Sn exhibited two characteristic peaks at 484.88 ( $SnO_{2} 3 d_{3 / 2})$ and 487.28(Sn $3 d_{5 / 2})$ eV, which were absent in pBZn (Figure S8c,d, Supporting Information). $^{[32]}$ Although absent in the XRD pattern of ZnTCF@Sn, $SnO_{2}$ peaks originating from natural oxidation on the surface of metallic Sn, which prevents deeper oxidation into the Sn layer in the atmosphere, $^{[33]}$ were observed in the high-resolution XPS spectrum of ZnTCF@Sn. When the second CRR time was further extended to 5 min, the spiky architecture of ZnTCF@Sn collapsed structurally due to excess Sn (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Especially, the as-developed ZnTCF@Sn exhibits a strong mechanical integrity between Sn and Zn without conspicuous exfoliation and weight loss, even under dynamic mechanical deformations (bending, folding, and rolling) and ultrasonication (Figure S10a,b, Supporting Information). Also, after mechanical deformations and ultrasonication, as shown in Figure S10c (Supporting Information), the ZnTCF@Sn still maintain fresh ZnTCF@Sn surface without detachment of unique spikes and cracks on the surface.

The influence of exposed crystal facets on the ZnTCF@Sn formation mechanism was determined by theoretical calculations and experiments involving the direct application of pBZn to the second CRR. The morphological and compositional changes in pBZn over the reaction time were verified by SEM and EDS elemental mapping images (Figure S12a, Supporting Information). ### 2.2. Investigation of ZnTCF@Sn Surface Properties via Theoretical/Experimental Approaches
Crystallographic facets with different atomic structures possess distinct surface characteristics that affect Zn deposition behav- ior and kinetics. $^{[29 a, 35]}$ The binding energies of Zn atoms on the crystal facets $(E_{b / Zn - crystal facets })$ related to Zn affinity were first eval uated by DFT calculations for different crystal facets $(Zn_{(002)}$ , $Zn_{(100)}, Zn_{(101)}, Sn_{(200)}$ , and $Sn_{(101)})$ , which are the preferentially exposed facets of pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn according to the XRD re- sults (Figure 1c,g). The $Sn_{(101)}$ facets have the highest $E_{b / Zn-Sn_{(101)}}$ of $-2.23 eV$ than other crystal facets $(E_{b / Zn-Zn_{(002)}}:-0.52 eV$ , $E_{b / Zn-Zn_{(100)}}:-0.90 eV, E_{b / Zn-Zn_{(101)}}:-1.43 eV$ , and $E_{b / Zn-Sn_{(200)}}$ : -0.92 eV) (Figure 2a). The collective influence of the exposed facets on $E_{b}$ was assessed using the average $E_{b}(E_{b, avg. })$ values of pBZn, ZnTCF, pBZn@Sn (where pBZn has undergone a di- rect 2nd CRR for 7 min, Figure S12 in the Supporting Infor- mation), and ZnTCF@Sn (Figure 2b) calculated from the XRD peak intensity ratios shown in Figure 1c,e,g, and Figure S12b and Tables S1 and S2 (Supporting Information). ZnTCF@Sn ex- hibits a higher $E_{b, avg. }(-1.58 eV)$ than the others (pBZn: $-0.86 eV$ , ZnTCF: $-1.28 eV$ , and pBZn@Sn: $-1.44 eV$ ), which implies that the ZnTCF@Sn can not only reduce the energy barrier for Zn nucleation but also restrict the lateral diffusion of Zn atoms, lead- ing to the facile and antidendritic deposition of $Zn.^{[17 a, 27]}$ The zincophilic surface characteristics of ZnTCF@Sn were experi- mentally verified through the nucleation overpotential (NOP, the difference between the initial soaring voltage and steady-state voltage) and growth overpotential (GOP, corresponding to the steady-state voltage) of $Zn^{2+}$ in symmetric cells. As displayed in Figure 2c and Figure S13 (Supporting Information), ZnTCF@Sn shows extremely low NOP/GOP values of $\approx 0.5$ and $\approx 10 mV$ , respectively, compared to those of pBZn $(\approx 35 / 41 mV)$ , ZnTCF $(\approx 20 / 28 mV)$ , and pBZn@Sn $(\approx 12 / 23 mV)$ at a current density of $2 mA cm^{-2}$ . Even at an increased current density $(5 mA cm^{-2})$ , theNOP and GOP of ZnTCF@Sn ( $\approx 1.0$ and $\approx 18 mV$ , respectively) remained low without notable alterations, whereas those of pBZn(NOP: $\approx 83 mV$ , GOP: $\approx 51 mV$ ) increased substantially. This agrees with the high $E_{b, avg. }$ for ZnTCF@Sn, obtained from DFTcalculations. The low deposition overpotential (NOP and GOP) of ZnTCF@Sn with high Zn affinity indicates that ZnTCF@Sn facilitates homogenous Zn deposition while improving the Zn deposition kinetics. $^{[36]}$ The improved Zn deposition kinetics was further investigated using the electrochemical impedance spec- troscopy and the $Zn^{2+}$ transference number $(t_{Zn^{2+}})$ in symmetric cells (Figures S14 and S15, Supporting Information). The pBZn symmetric cell exhibited a high charge-transfer resistance $(R_{ct})$ of $197.8 \Omega$ , but ZnTCF@Sn cell delivered a remarkably reduced $R_{ct}$  of $1.53 \Omega$ (Figure S14 and Table S3, Supporting Information) at initial state. Moreover, the $t_{Zn^{2+}}$ of ZnTCF@Sn $(\approx 0.61)$ was signif icantly higher than that of pBZn $(\approx 0.30)$ (Figure S15, SupportingInformation), indicating that ZnTCF@Sn can mitigate the $Zn^{2+}$  concentration gradient, which causes a strong electric field andaccelerates dendrite growth through elevated ion transport. $^{[9,37]}$  Thus, the introduction of ZnTCF@Sn can facilitate more effi- cient charge transfer and ion transport processes at the interface between the electrolyte and ZnTCF@Sn, and guide highly stable Zn deposition.

The crystallographic facets also affect another key factor for stable Zn deposition without dendrite growth, the surface en- ergy $(E_{s})$ . The $E_{s}$ value of $Sn_{(200)}$ obtained from DFT calculation(Figure 2d) displays the highest surface energy of $5.02 eV nm^{-2}$ among all the representative crystallographic facets $(Zn_{(002)}$ : $1.62 eV nm^{-2}, Zn_{(100)}: 4.53 eV nm^{-2}, Zn_{(101)}: 4.17 eV nm^{-2}$ , and $Sn_{(101)}: 2.60 eV nm^{-2})$ . The average $E_{s}(E_{s, avg. })$ for ZnTCF@Sn, obtained in the same manner as $E_{b, avg. }$ was $3.81 eV nm^{-2}$ , which is higher than those of pBZn $(2.69 eV nm^{-2})$ . The high $E_{s}$ of ZnTCF@Sn results in its increased surface wettability, strength of capillary actions, and preferential Zn nucleation sites because surfaces with a high $E_{s}$ tend to minimize the energy through in teractions with other ions and molecules, unlike surfaces with low surface energies. $^{[38]}$ To confirm the correlation between the surface wettability and enhanced $E_{s}$ of ZnTCF@Sn, we mea sured the dynamic contact angle between the electrodes and a 2 M $ZnSO_{4}$ electrolyte at $25^{\circ} C$ . ZnTCF exhibits an ini- tially low contact angle $(30.4^{\circ})$ , but low electrolyte hygroscopicity over time (Figure S16a, Supporting Information). The pBZn@Sn(Figure S16b, Supporting Information), where $Sn_{(200)}$ is prefer entially exposed, exhibits a lower contact angle $(50.3^{\circ})$ than that of pBZn $(83.6^{\circ})$ , but did not show the lowest contact angle cor responding to the highest $E_{s, avg. }$ value (Figure S17, Supporting Information). This may be due to the locally covered Sn parti- cles exposing the hydrophobic $Zn_{(002)}$ -preferring pBZn (Figure S12, Supporting Information). Morphological alteration of ZnTCF@Sn can also play a pivotal role in the amelioration of Zn deposition behavior and enhanced electrochemical kinetics. The surface areas of pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn were quantitatively compared using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and the corresponding ratio of the increment in the interfacial area $(A_{\text{i}})$ to the projected area $(A_{\text{p}})$ (denoted as Sdr (\%)) (Figure S18, Supporting Information). $^{[40]}$ The Sdr value of ZnTCF@Sn (64.0%), which is more than sixfold higher than that of pBZn (10.1%), indicates that ZnTCF@Sn can provide more electrochemically active zincophilic nucleation sites for $\text{Zn}^{2+}$. As evidenced by this, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve of the ZnTCF@Sn asymmetric cell (ZnTCF@Sn/stainless steel (SS)) shows an approximately nine times larger electrochemical active area than that of the pBZn/SS cell (Figure S19, Supporting Information). To further reveal the effect of the stalagmite-like architecture of ZnTCF@Sn, we simulated the distribution of the current density and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ flux at the interface between the electrodes (pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn) and the aqueous electrolyte $(2\ \text{M}\ \text{ZnSO}_{4})$ using a COMSOL finite element simulation. The geometries of the simplified 2D simulation cells are served in Figure S20 (Supporting Information). $^{[41]}$ Previous studies have shown that homogenous current and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ flux distribution can guide uniform Zn deposition and inhibit dendrite growth. $^{[42]}$ As seen in Figure 2f,g, pBZn exhibits an inhomogenous distribution of current density and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ flux, concentrated at "hot spots," where $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ preferentially but nonuniformly accumulates due to the rough surface and few nucleation sites, $^{[8,43]}$ possibly inducing a continuous accumulation of Zn deposits and inevitably facilitating the growth of the Zn protrusions and dendrites. On the contrary, in ZnTCF@Sn, the current density and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ flux are uniformly distributed. "Hot spots" are absent owing to numerous nucleation sites, which suppress the formation of Zn protrusions and dendrites. In addition, the current density and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ flux in the internal holes between the spikes of ZnTCF@Sn were relatively larger than those at the tips of the spikes, implying that $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ is preferentially deposited in the internal holes at the initial step of Zn deposition. The evolution of surface morphology before and after Zn deposition was compared using CLSM images to visually observe the change in the Zn deposition behavior of ZnTCF@Sn. The surface roughness parameters, such as root-mean-square height $(S_{\text{q}})$, arithmetic mean height $(S_{\text{a}})$, and altitude intercept $(S_{\text{z}})$ are listed in Table S4 (Supporting Information) to quantitatively represent the uniformity of the Zn deposits. Before Zn deposition (Figure 2h), the $S_{\text{z}}$ of both pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn was the same at $5.89\ \mu\text{m}$, but ZnTCF@Sn showed slightly larger surface roughness $(S_{\text{q}}$: $0.631\ \mu\text{m}$ and $S_{\text{a}}$: $0.509\ \mu\text{m}$) than pBZn ($S_{\text{q}}$: $0.270\ \mu\text{m}$ and $S_{\text{a}}$: $0.168\ \mu\text{m}$) due to the intense CRRs. However, after a Zn deposition of $5\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ at a current density of $2\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure 2i), the deposited pBZn exhibited nonuniform and large Zn protrusions with $S_{\text{q}}$, $S_{\text{a}}$, and $S_{\text{z}}$ of 4.85, 4.14, and $31.1\ \mu\text{m}$, respectively, which significantly exceeds the roughness of pristine pBZn. In stark contrast, the surface of ZnTCF@Sn is relatively flat and compact, maintaining low surface roughness $(S_{\text{q}}$: $0.864\ \mu\text{m}$, $S_{\text{a}}$: $0.684\ \mu\text{m}$, and $S_{\text{z}}$: $7.04\ \mu\text{m}$) without visible Zn chunks. Therefore, the novel ZnTCF@Sn architecture promotes uniform Zn deposition by providing numerous zincophilic nucleation sites and inducing effective dispersion of current and $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ flux. $^{[7b]}$

### 2.3. Exploration of Zn Deposition Mechanism and Anticorrosive Properties of ZnTCF@Sn
For an in-depth study of the Zn deposition behavior, the surface morphology evolution of pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn during Zn deposition was examined by gradually increasing the deposition capacity at a current density of $2\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$. For pBZn (Figure 3a), during the nascent stage (step 1, $0.1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$), numerous irregular hexagonal Zn sheets were observed, which can further provide "hot spots" leading to inhomogenous Zn deposition and the formation of Zn protrusions and dendrites. $^{[44]}$ The morphology of the Zn deposits at the nascent stage has a significant impact on the later Zn deposition behavior. $^{[45]}$ In step 2 $(1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2})$, the initial hexagonal Zn sheets turned into clusters of large and stacked hexagonal Zn sheets, and in successive Zn deposition up to step 4 $(5\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2})$, moss-like Zn agglomerates were observed, emphasizing the inhomogenous Zn deposition behavior of pBZn. The low-magnification SEM images of pBZn during the overall Zn deposition (Figure S21a, Supporting Information) clearly show the nonuniform Zn deposits, including protrusions. Such irregular Zn deposition behavior can cause considerable volume change, acceleration of side reactions (HER and Zn corrosion), and the formation of "dead Zn," resulting in higher Zn deposition/dissolution overpotentials and even internal short circuits during repetitive cycles. $^{[8,46]}$ In addition, as exhibited in Figure S22 (Supporting Information), whereas pBZn forms large moss-like Zn agglomerates after Zn deposition $(3\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2})$, ZnTCF shows small Zn fragments and agglomerates with relatively uniform distribution of Zn deposits. Unlike pBZn and ZnTCF, pBZn@Sn forms locally dense and compact Zn deposits without porous Zn agglomerates. In contrast, as shown in Figure 3b and Figure S21b (Supporting Information), at step 1 $(0.1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2})$, ZnTCF@Sn exhibits uniform Zn deposition centered on the spikes of ZnTCF@Sn with a clear distinction of Zn and Sn in the EDS elemental mapping images (Figure S23, Supporting Information). This result was attributed to the numerous nucleation sites, improved surface energy, and high Zn affinity of ZnTCF@Sn, which suppressed the agglomeration of Zn deposits. $^{[11a,19]}$ In step 2 $(1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2})$, the Zn deposits filled the inner cavities between the spikes and covered the ZnTCF@Sn surface with flat Zn sheets. Even after a large amount of deposition (Step 4, $5\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$), the ZnTCF@Sn surface remained dense and smooth without remarkable Zn clusters and protrusions. Digital images of pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn before and after Zn deposition (Figure S24, Supporting Information) also show macroscopic surface morphology changes. The ZnTCF@Sn surface changed from dark gray to white, with no obvious traces of locally accumulated Zn deposits owing to homogenous Zn deposition, whereas pBZn exhibited nonuniform Zn deposits entangled with the separator. The Zn deposition behavior of ZnTCF@Sn is highly desirable for enhancing the stability of ZMA while effectively ameliorating the abovementioned issues of pBZn during repetitive Zn deposition/dissolution. ### 2.4. Electrochemical Performances of Asymmetric and Symmetric Cells Applied with ZnTCF@Sn

The electrochemical dynamics and performance of Zn deposition/dissolution were evaluated using various electrochemical analyses to investigate the effectiveness of ZnTCF@Sn. The CV test results of symmetric cells at a scan rate of $1\ \text{mV}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ (Figure 4a and Figure S28, Supporting Information) show that the CV curve of pBZn at the first cycle was close to a straight line, with a limited maximum current density ($I_{\text{max}}$, $0.27\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$) and a peak area with a gentle slope of $5.3\ \text{mA}\ \text{V}^{-1}$. $I_{\text{max}}$ ($5.07\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$) for ZnTCF@Sn was larger by more than 18.7 times than that of pBZn, simultaneously showing a larger peak area and a steeper slope of $131.88\ \text{mA}\ \text{V}^{-1}$. This reflects the enhanced electrochemical redox kinetics for Zn deposition and the elevated concentration of $Zn^{2+}$ on the ZnTCF@Sn surface owing to the abundant zincophilic nucleation sites, which lead to uniform Zn deposition with a fast $Zn^{2+}$ transfer rate. $^{[35,54]}$ While the $I_{\text{max}}$ of the pBZn-CV curves decreased continuously during the repetitive CV tests, the shape of the CV curves of ZnTCF@Sn remained identical under the same conditions, indicating the high reversibility of Zn deposition/dissolution of the ZnTCF@Sn electrode (Figure S28, Supporting Information). To clarify this superior reversibility of ZnTCF@Sn, the CE of the asymmetric cells (pBZn/Cu and ZnTCF@Sn/Cu cells) was quantified at a current density of $3\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ and a capacity of $1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure S29, Supporting Information). After ≈550 cycles, the CE of the pBZn/Cu cell showed a poor cycle lifespan and messy distribution, which is attributed to the gradual formation of "dead Zn" and short circuits resulting from intense side reactions and dendrite growth, $^{[20]}$ whereas the CE of the ZnTCF@Sn cell stays stable for 1000 cycles with average CE of 99.88%. Besides, even under harsh operating conditions (current density: $10\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ and capacity: $1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$) (Figure 4b), the ZnTCF@Sn cell achieved an ultra-stable CE for 2000 cycles with a high average CE of ≈99.81% and steadily maintained a lower voltage hysteresis than that of pBZn and ZnTCF without significant variation for all cycles (Figure S30a,b, Supporting Information). In sharp contrast, the CE of the pBZn/Cu and ZnTCF/Cu cell fluctuated violently after ≈500 and 600 cycles, respectively, resulting in quick cell failure, and the corresponding voltage curves (Figure S30a,b, Supporting Information) showed the progressively increasing voltage hysteresis starting from the ≈300th and 500th cycle, respectively. The CE tests of the ZnTCF@Sn cells indicated that ZnTCF@Sn effectively enhanced the reversibility of Zn utilization and lowered the potential barrier for Zn deposition/dissolution, enabling stable cycling even at a high current density. $^{[55]}$

Figure 4c displays the rate performance of the pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn symmetric cells at a fixed capacity of $1\ \text{mAh}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ and increasing current densities from 1.0 to $20\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$. All the voltage-time curves for the rate performance clearly present that the ZnTCF@Sn symmetric cell shows much lower overpotentials and more even Zn deposition/dissolution voltage curves than the pBZn symmetric cell. In particular, when the current density increased from $10\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ to an extremely high value of $20\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$, the voltage-time curves of the pBZn cell underwent violent fluctuations with a larger overpotential difference between the pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn cells. The excellent rate capability of the ZnTCF@Sn cell indicates that ZnTCF@Sn can satisfy the operating requirements for various power applications and can be stably operated even under harsh current conditions, owing to the uniform distribution of the current/$Zn^{2+}$ flux and superior Zn deposition kinetics of ZnTCF@Sn. $$

As shown in Figure 4d, the ZnTCF@Sn delivered an $i_0$ of $5.33\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$, which was more than 40% higher than that of pBZn ($3.78\ \text{mA}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$), directly suggesting the improved Zn deposition kinetics of the ZnTCF@Sn.

Moreover, after 500 cycles, the polarization difference $(\approx 79\ \mathrm{mV})$ between the pBZn and ZnTCF@Sn cells (Figure S43, Supporting Information) was significantly higher than that at a relatively low current rate of $0.2\ \mathrm{A\ g^{-1}}$ $(\approx 22\ \mathrm{mV})$ (Figure S39, Supporting Information). The superior cyclability of the ZnTCF@Sn full-cell originates from the enhanced electrochemical kinetics as well as the effective ability to suppress dendrite growth and side reactions of the ZnTCF@Sn electrode. Besides, the SEM image of the ZnTCF@Sn electrode after 1000 cycles (Figure 5g) reveals a dense surface without striking Zn protrusions, whereas the cycled pBZn electrode shows a loose surface morphology entangled with the separator as the Zn dendrites/protrusions and by-products violently spread out toward the separator's porous structure. As listed in Table S11 (Supporting Information), the $\mathrm{ZnTCF@Sn/MnO_{2}}$ cell performance demonstrates competitiveness compared with previously reported surface-modified ZMAs. Therefore, it is evident that the capacity and durability of the $\mathrm{MnO}_{2}$ full-cell can be remarkably improved by employing stable ZnTCF@Sn.

Most studies on ARZMBs generally utilize thick ZMAs with excessive capacity (thickness: $\geq 100\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, capacity: $\geq 58.55\ \mathrm{mAh\ cm^{-2}}$) and low $\mathrm{MnO}_{2}$ loading cathodes $(\leq 2\ \mathrm{mg\ cm^{-2}})$. Such cells are far from being used in practical applications owing to their high negative-to-positive electrode capacity (N/P ratio), which limits the energy density of ARZMBs. Thus, to implement ARZMBs for practical use, we conducted additional GCD cycling tests under more realistic operating conditions (N/P ratio of $\approx 10.88$ and a current density of $1.5\ \mathrm{A\ g^{-1}}$) applied to thin ZnTCF@Sn and thin pBZn (t-ZnTCF@Sn and t-pBZn, respectively) with a low capacity $(\approx 10.0\ \mathrm{mAh\ cm^{-2}}$, Figure S44, Supporting Information) as anodes and a high $\mathrm{MnO}_{2}$ loading cathode $(\approx 3.0\ \mathrm{mg\ cm^{-2}})$ (Figure 5h). After the initial rapid activation process during two and three cycles for t-pBZn and t-ZnTCF@Sn full-cells, respectively, the t-pBZn full-cell showed an initial discharge capacity of $200.1\ \mathrm{mAh\ g^{-1}}$, which decreased rapidly to $54.3\ \mathrm{mAh\ g^{-1}}$ after 1000 cycles, corresponding to only $27\%$ of the initial capacity, with a capacity attenuation rate of $0.0729\%$ per cycle. In contrast, the t-ZnTCF@Sn full-cell achieved an $89\%$ higher discharge capacity $(102.7\ \mathrm{mAh\ g^{-1}})$ with a lower capacity attenuation rate of $0.0543\%$ per cycle than that of the t-pBZn full-cell after 1000 cycles, which shows clear evidence of the improved reversibility and electrochemical kinetics of the ZnTCF@Sn electrode even under harsh conditions. As proof of practical application, the two $\mathrm{ZnTCF@Sn/MnO_{2}}$ coin-cells with low N/P ratio could comfortably light several light-emitting diodes.

## 3. Conclusion
We have demonstrated that preferentially exposed Zn crystal facets significantly affect the surface morphology, crystallographic orientation, and uniformity of the Sn-based EUL in the CRR between $\mathrm{Sn}^{4+}$ and Zn. Utilizing the difference in the chemical reactivities of the Zn crystal facets, a multifunctional Sn-based interfacial layer with a unique architecture (ZnTCF@Sn) was developed using a novel two-step CRR process. ZnTCF@Sn endows ZMA with significant advantages: 1) From a crystallographic perspective, DFT calculations revealed that a similar intensity ratio between $\mathrm{Sn}_{(200)}$ and $\mathrm{Sn}_{(101)}$ in ZnTCF@Sn simultaneously provides high Zn binding and surface energy. Therefore, ZnTCF@Sn effectively reduced the Zn deposition barrier (including NOP and GOP) and contact angle with the aqueous electrolyte to improve the Zn deposition kinetics and wettability; 2) from a morphological perspective, COMSOL simulations demonstrated that the zincophilic stalagmite-like ZnTCF@Sn surface ensures a uniform distribution of the current/$\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ flux without $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ concentrated spots, which facilitates the parallel Zn deposition along the stable $\mathrm{Zn}_{(002)}$ facet with suppression of Zn dendrite growth and protrusions; 3) from a compositional perspective, the uniform and inherently anticorrosive (low HER-inducing) Sn coverage on the ZnTCF@Sn surface inhibited the Zn-devouring side reactions, resulting in the improved reversibility and chemical stability. The ZnTCF@Sn symmetric cell exhibited a very low voltage polarization of $\approx 19\ \mathrm{mV}$ at $2\ \mathrm{mA\ cm^{-2}}$ and an ultralong lifespan of over $1200\ \mathrm{h}$, even at an extremely high current density $(20\ \mathrm{mA\ cm^{-2}})$, achieving an extraordinary cumulative capacity of $24\ \mathrm{Ah\ cm^{-2}}$. Moreover, the $\mathrm{ZnTCF@Sn/MnO_{2}}$ full-cell displayed much better cyclability over 1000 cycles than the $\mathrm{pBZn/MnO_{2}}$ full-cell under practical operating conditions

(N/P ratio: $\approx$10.88 and current density: $1.5\ \text{A}\ \text{g}^{-1}$). This work elucidates the importance of preferentially exposed Zn crystal facets as reductants in the CRR process, which has the potential to be applied to stable ZMA design based on various CRRs, and provides a straightforward yet effective approach for solving the chronic problems (dendritic growth and side reactions) of ZMAs, bringing us closer to the realization of a practical ARZMB.
