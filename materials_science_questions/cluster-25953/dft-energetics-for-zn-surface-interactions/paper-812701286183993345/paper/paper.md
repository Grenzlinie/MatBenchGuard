# Assessing the Role of Metal Identity on CO₂ Adsorption in MOFs Containing M–OH Functional Groups

Caitlin E. Bien, Qiao Liu, and Casey R. Wade*

Department of Chemistry and Biochemistry, The Ohio State University, 100 West 18th Ave., Columbus, OH 43210, United States

Supporting Information Placeholder

ABSTRACT: Heterobimetallic analogues of CFA-1 $[Zn_{1+z}M_{4-z}X_4(bibta)_3$, bibta²⁻ = 5,5'-bibenzotriazolate, M = Co (z = 0), Ni (z = 1), Cu (z = 2.3), X = Cl⁻, Br⁻, $CH_3CO_2^-]$ have been prepared via postsynthetic cation exchange. Subsequent postsynthetic $X^-/HCO_3^-$ ligand exchange followed by thermal activation generates nucleophilic M–OH groups at the Kuratowski-type metal nodes of the heterobimetallic MOFs. While the Cu-exchanged MOF suffered from degradation as a result of the postsynthetic modifications, the Co and Ni analogues (**Co-OH** and **Ni-OH**) proved to be stable to activation, and room temperature isotherm measurements show steep CO₂ uptake at pressures compatible with direct air capture and other trace CO₂ removal applications. **Ni-OH** exhibits a greater low-pressure CO₂ capacity and higher isosteric heat of adsorption than **Co-OH** and the all-Zn MOF, **Zn-OH**. In situ diffuse reflectance IR spectroscopy indicate that **Co-OH** and **Ni-OH** adsorb CO₂ via a $M\text{–}OH \rightarrow M\text{–}O_2COH$ chemisorption mechanism aided by inter-cluster hydrogen bonding interactions. However, CO₂ adsorption in **Ni-OH** gives rise to spectroscopic features that are not observed for **Co-OH** and **Zn-OH** and can be attributed to Ni-bicarbonate groups that do not engage in inter-cluster hydrogen bonding. DFT calculations performed on model clusters support the experimentally observed trend in CO₂ affinity.

## INTRODUCTION

Rising levels of anthropogenic CO₂ and the related environmental impacts have prompted increased interest in new technologies for CO₂ capture and conversion.¹,² Direct air capture has been proposed as a means of dispersing mitigation efforts and achieving a net reduction in CO₂ levels, but a number of scientific challenges remain to be addressed before this approach becomes economically and technologically feasible.³⁻⁸ Among these challenges is the development of selective and efficient adsorbent systems capable of extracting CO₂ from ambient air. Here, the underlying challenge lies in separating relatively dilute atmospheric CO₂ (~400 ppm) from reactive gases such as O₂ and H₂O that are present in much higher concentrations. Liquid amine scrubbing and causticization are well-developed technologies for direct air capture of CO₂ but are energy-intensive and may not prove to be viable for large scale implementation.⁹,¹⁰ Trace CO₂ removal from confined environments such as space vehicles or subaquatic vessels poses challenges similar to direct air capture, but is implemented on a much smaller scale and with less stringent CO₂ concentration requirements. With regard to the latter, NASA has determined that CO₂ levels in space vehicles should be maintained below a partial pressure of 2 Torr (~2630 ppm) to avoid acute toxic effects.¹¹ Historically, zeolite-based adsorbent systems have been employed for CO₂ removal in NASA space vehicles, but these materials have faced issues related to performance and reliability, prompting the pursuit of improved CO₂ adsorbent technologies.

Porous materials including zeolites, silica, organic polymers, and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have been widely studied as for trace CO₂ capture.⁴,¹²⁻¹⁷ In particular, amine-functionalized porous solids that emulate liquid amine scrubbing processes, but with lower regeneration energy requirements, have shown considerable promise as next generation adsorbents.¹⁸⁻²³ Hybrid ultramicroporous materials (HUMs) that capture trace CO₂ via strong physisorptive interactions and tailored pore sizes have also recently emerged.²⁴,²⁵

Figure 1. Postsynthetic ligand exchange of **Zn-OAc** to generate **Zn-OH** and reversible CO₂ binding.

We have an ongoing interest in the design of MOFs containing nucleophilic transition-metal hydroxide functional groups (M–OH) for trace CO₂ capture.²⁶ This pursuit has been inspired by the efficient CO₂ fixation mechanism of α-carbonic anhydrases, which mediate the interconversion of CO₂ and $HCO_3^-$ via a pseudo-tetrahedral Zn-OH active site. Although α-carbonic anhydrases and functional model complexes have been well-studied, the underlying mechanistic framework (i.e. $M\text{–}OH \rightarrow M\text{–}O_2COH$ conversion) has only recently been applied for gas-solid phase adsorption in MOFs.²⁷,²⁸ In this context, we have reported the use of a mild postsynthetic ligand exchange (PSLE) procedure to generate Zn–OH groups at the Kuratowski-type metal nodes of

CFA-1 (Zn-OAc Figure 1).$^{26}$ The resulting material, Zn-OH, shows excellent performance for adsorption of trace CO₂ facilitated by the combination of a Zn-OH → Zn-O₂COH chemisorption process and inter-cluster hydrogen bonding interactions.

Postsynthetic metal and linker exchange strategies have emerged as powerful tools for obtaining MOFs with tailored compositions and enhanced performance in heterogeneous catalysis and gas storage or separation.$^{29-36}$ The Kuratowski-type secondary building units (SBUs) in Zn-OAc and related benzotriazolate MOFs are known to be amenable to cation exchange for other first row transition metal ions,$^{37-40}$ prompting us to investigate the role of metal identity on CO₂ adsorption in metal-exchanged congeners of Zn-OH. Herein we report the synthesis of heterobimetallic analogues of Zn-OAc containing Co, Ni, or Cu. The heterobimetallic MOFs show moderate to excellent performance for trace CO₂ capture after postsynthetic ligand exchange and activation to generate nucleophilic M-OH groups. Notably, the Ni-exchanged analogue outperforms the parent Zn MOF and in situ diffuse reflectance IR experiments and DFT calculations have been used to gain insight into the effect of metal identity on the CO₂ adsorption mechanism.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Scheme 1. Synthesis of heterobimetallic MOFs via postsynthetic modification

Postsynthetic Metal and Ligand Exchange. CFA-1 (Zn₅OAc₄(bibta)₃), bibta²⁻ = 5,5'-bibenzotriazolate OAc⁻ = CH₃COO⁻, Zn-OAc) was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure,$^{26}$ and the conditions used for postsynthetic metal exchange were adapted from procedures reported for the preparation of heterobimetallic analogues of CFA-1 and MFU-4l (Scheme 1).$^{37-41}$ Co-Cl and Ni-Cl were obtained by heating suspensions of Zn-OAc in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solutions of CoCl₂ and NiCl₂ at reflux and 80 °C, respectively for 16 h. The Cu-exchanged MOFs Cu-X were prepared at lower temperature (60 °C) using N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMA) solutions of CuX₂ (X = Cl or Br) to avoid the reduction of Cu²⁺.$^{38}$ The solids were collected by filtration and washed with DMF until the bands corresponding to M²⁺ species were no longer observed in the UV-Vis absorption spectra of the supernatant solutions. Co-Cl and Ni-Cl were subsequently washed with MeOH while Cu-Br and Cu-Cl were found to be less stable to alcoholic solvents and were instead washed with acetonitrile (MeCN). The PXRD patterns of the metal-exchanged MOFs closely match that of Zn-OAc, indicating no major structural changes (Figure S2). ¹H NMR spectra measured for acid-digested samples of Cu-X, Ni-Cl, and Co-Cl showed broad resonances consistent with the protonated H₂bibta linkers. Acetic acid (AcOH) was not observed in the spectra of Cu-X and Ni-Cl, indicating that the acetate ligands are replaced by halides during metal exchange (Figure S3-S5). However, the spectrum of Co-Cl displayed a resonance corresponding to AcOH at ~1.7 ppm, and integration showed that it was present in a ~0.57:1 ratio with respect to the with the H₂bibta linkers (Figure S6). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) in concert with ¹H NMR data support empirical formulas of Cu₁.₇Zn₃.₃Br₄(bibta)₃, Ni₃Zn₂Cl₄(bibta)₃ and Co₄ZnCl(OAc)₃(bibta)₃ for Cu-Br, Ni-Cl, and Co-Cl, respectively. Notably, the extent of Ni²⁺ incorporation in Ni-Cl (~3 out of 4 peripheral SBU sites) is greater than previous literature reports, which we attribute to the use of more forceful metal exchange conditions.³⁷ The cation-exchanged MOFs were desolvated by heating at 100 °C under vacuum (10⁻⁴ Torr) for 16 h. Subsequent N₂ adsorption isotherms (77 K) gave calculated Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas of 1911, 1912, 1966, and 2215 m² g⁻¹ for Cu-Br, Cu-Cl, Ni-Cl and Co-Cl, respectively (Figure S7). These high surface areas are consistent with that measured for Zn-OAc (2075 m² g⁻¹), supporting cation exchange at the SBUs rather than encapsulation of metal salts in the MOF pores.

Cu-Cl, Cu-Br, Ni-Cl, and Co-Cl were treated with aqueous NaHCO₃ solutions (1.0 M, 3×30 min) to facilitate X⁻/HCO₃⁻ (X = OAc, Br, Cl) ligand exchange, and subsequent washing with H₂O and organic solvent yielded Cu-HCO₃(X), Ni-HCO₃ and Co-HCO₃. Co-Cl proved to be sensitive to the aqueous ligand exchange conditions, a behavior that is consistent with the poor water stability recently reported for Co-exchanged MFU-4l.⁴² Notably, isolation of Co-HCO₃ by centrifugation gave a much less crystalline product compared to that obtained by filtration (Figure S8). Lower yields were also observed in the latter case, suggesting that filtration facilitates the separation of degraded or amorphous material. Consequently, all samples of Co-HCO₃ used for further experiments were isolated by filtration. Cu-Cl, Cu-Br and Ni-Cl were found to be stable toward the aqueous ligand exchange and washing procedures. However, while Co-HCO₃ and Ni-HCO₃ could be solvent-exchanged with anhydrous THF to remove excess water, Cu-HCO₃ experienced a significant loss of crystallinity when THF or other common solvents such as acetone, methanol, or CH₂Cl₂ were used (Figure S9). Ultimately, the MOF was found to be stable to washing with anhydrous MeCN. The PXRD patterns of Cu-HCO₃(X), Ni-HCO₃ and Co-HCO₃ are similar to those of the parent MOFs, indicating that the framework structures remain largely unchanged under the optimized postsynthetic ligand exchange conditions (Figure S10). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and ¹H NMR analysis of acid-digested samples confirmed nearly complete exchange of the halide and acetate ligands from the MOFs (Figures S6, S11-S14).

Gas Adsorption Measurements. Ni-HCO₃ and Co-HCO₃ were activated by heating at 100 °C under vacuum (10⁻⁴ bar) to generate Ni-OH and Co-OH. Subsequent N₂ adsorption isotherm measurements (77 K) gave BET surface areas of 2285 and 1930 m² g⁻¹, respectively, which are commensurate with the parent MOFs (Figure S15). CO₂ adsorption isotherms measured at 300 K (Figure 2) show steep low-pressure uptake indicative of a strong chemisorptive mechanism akin to that of Zn-OH. The CO₂ capacities of Zn-OH and Ni-OH at 1 bar are nearly identical, but Ni-OH displays steeper uptake and slightly higher capacity at pressures below 10 mbar. While Co-OH exhibits a similar CO₂ adsorption profile, its capacity is lower over the entire pressure region. This difference can be attributed, in part, to a lower isosteric heat of adsorption (*vida infra*), but is likely exacerbated by framework degradation during the aqueous ligand exchange.

Notably, the CO₂ capacity of **Ni-OH** at 0.4 mbar (2.7 mmol g⁻¹) exceeds that of MAF-27ox (~1.1 mmol g⁻¹), SIFSIX-3-Cu (1.24 mmol g⁻¹) and NbOFFIVE-1-Ni (1.3 mmol g⁻¹) at similar temperatures.²⁴,²⁷,⁴³ Its capacity also compares well to the amine-functionalized MOF Mg₂(dobpdc)-en (2.83 mmol g⁻¹) but is lower than that observed for Mg₂(dobpdc)-N₂H₄ (3.89 mmol g⁻¹).⁴⁴,⁴⁵

Samples of **Cu-OH(X)** prepared by activation of **Cu-HCO₃(Cl)** and **Cu-HCO₃(Br)** showed no appreciable N₂ uptake at 77K. The corresponding CO₂ isotherms exhibit some low-pressure adsorption (Figure S16), but are clearly not commensurate with the Ni and Co congeners. Indeed, PXRD analysis of **Cu-OH(Cl)** and **Cu-OH(Br)** revealed complete loss of framework crystallinity after activation (Figure S17). Although we have not clearly identified the cause of framework instability of **Cu-OH**, ¹H NMR and diffuse reflectance IR analysis reveal the presence of a significant amount of MeCN (~1 molecule per metal node) after activating samples *in vacuo* at 100 °C (Figure S18 and S19). This observation leads us to suspect that the removal of strongly bound solvent molecules destabilizes the Cu/Zn SBUs.

Figure 2. CO₂ isotherms measured for **Zn-OH**, **Ni-OH**, and **Co-OH** at 300 K.

Variable temperature CO₂ isotherms have been used to calculate the isosteric heat of adsorption (Qₛₜ) as a function of CO₂ loading for **Ni-OH** and **Co-OH** (Figure S20). The data calculated for **Ni-OH** show an unusual increase in Qₛₜ from zero coverage up to 2.5 mmol CO₂ g⁻¹ loading, which correlates with the inflection point in the isotherms. This profile is similar to that previously observed for **Zn-OH** and may be attributed to the steep low-pressure CO₂ uptake causing equilibration and/or pressure measurement issues during the isotherm measurements (see Supporting Information for details). Qₛₜ reaches a maximum value of 84 kJ mol⁻¹, which is slightly greater than the value observed for **Zn-OH** (71 kJ mol⁻¹ at 2.1 mmol g⁻¹) and correlates well with the steeper CO₂ adsorption observed in the single component isotherms. **Co-OH** exhibits a more typical Qₛₜ profile, plateauing at ~68 kJ mol⁻¹ up to a loading of ~2.0 mmol g⁻¹.

Thermal swing adsorption-desorption (TSAD) measurements have been carried out to compare the regeneration temperature of **Ni-CO₂** to **Zn-CO₂** and evaluate stability to thermal cycling. A ~10 % weight loss was observed when CO₂-loaded samples **Ni-CO₂** and **Zn-CO₂** were heated to 100 °C under flowing N₂ (Figure S31). Upon ramping to 150 °C, **Zn-CO₂** shows only a small weight change attributable to buoyancy effects while **Ni-CO₂** continues to desorb 3-4 wt. % CO₂. This data confirms that **Ni-CO₂** requires higher temperatures for regeneration than **Zn-CO₂**, in line with its greater isosteric heat of CO₂ adsorption. TSAD cycling of **Ni-OH** with 5% CO₂ shows no significant loss in capacity over 5 cycles using a regeneration temperature of 150 °C (Figure S32). The MOF exhibits a maximum CO₂ capacity of 9 wt % (2.0 mmol g⁻¹) under these conditions, which is very similar to the capacity observed for **Zn-OH** under similar conditions,²⁶ but lower than expected from the single component isotherm (12.8 wt %, 2.9 mmol g⁻¹).

DRIFTS Studies. Variable temperature diffuse reflectance
Fourier transform IR spectra (VT-DRIFTS) were measured for **Ni-
CO₂** and **Co-CO₂** to interrogate the mechanism of CO₂ binding in
comparison to the Zn analogue. The symmetry of the CFA-1
framework results in crystallographically distinct, peripheral metal
sites at the Kuratowski-type nodes (M_A and M_E, Scheme 2).³⁹ In
our previous investigation of **Zn-OH**, the short Zn···Zn distance
between Zn_E sites of adjacent clusters and the appearance of a low
energy O-H stretching band (2657 cm⁻¹) upon CO₂ loading
signaled the formation of inter-cluster hydrogen bonding
interactions between Zn_E-O₂COH groups (E₂).²⁶ This hypothesis
was supported by periodic DFT calculations which also indicated
that adsorption of a single CO₂ in the Zn_E···Zn_E pocket (E₁) would
occur with a similar binding energy (E₁ = 71.4 kJ mol⁻¹, E₂ = 68.0
kJ mol⁻¹) and with stabilizing hydrogen bonding interactions.
Conversely, the Zn_A sites are well-separated from neighboring
clusters, precluding the formation of inter-cluster hydrogen bonds
and resulting in a significantly lower calculated CO₂ binding
energy (37.5 kJ mol⁻¹). VT-DRIFT spectra of **Zn-CO₂** showed no
clear indication of the formation of E1 as an intermediate species
or CO₂ binding at the structurally distinct Zn_A site. At room
temperature, **Co-CO₂** exhibits IR bands attributable to Co-O₂COH
groups at 1328, 1505, 1573 and 2634 cm⁻¹ (Figure 4). These
features are strikingly similar to those observed for **Zn-CO₂** (Table
S1).²⁶ Notably, the low energy O-H stretch observed at 2634 cm⁻¹
supports the presence of inter-cluster hydrogen bonding
interactions akin to those identified in **Zn-CO₂**. Upon heating
under a flow of dry N₂, the Co-O₂COH bands gradually disappear
concomitant with the appearance of an O-H stretching band at
3675 cm⁻¹. A transient O-H stretch that grows in and then
disappears as the sample is heated is also observed at 3659 cm⁻¹.
This feature can be explained by formation of intermediate species
E1 arising from the loss of one CO₂ molecule from the inter-cluster
pocket. The VT-DRIFTS data for **Ni-CO₂** are considerably more
complex than those observed for **Zn-CO₂** and indicate
the presence of at least two distinct metal bicarbonate species
(Figure 5). Bands appearing around 1340, 1503, 1582, and 2656
cm⁻¹ closely match those assigned to hydrogen-bonded M_E-
O₂COH groups in **Co-CO₂** and **Zn-CO₂**. However, new symmetric
and asymmetric CO₂ stretches present at 1452, 1623, and a large
O-H stretch at 3616 cm⁻¹ in the room temperature spectrum of **Ni-
HCO₃** are not observed for **Co-CO₂** and **Zn-CO₂**. The IR spectrum
of the ¹³C isotopologue, **Ni-H¹³CO₃**, confirms that the new bands
are associated with adsorbed CO₂ (Figure S37).

The evolution of the distinct M-O₂COH species can be seen from difference spectra calculated at 100 °C and 150 °C (Figure 5b, 5c) and a plot of normalized peak intensity versus temperature (Figure 5d). The latter clearly shows that the asymmetric $v(\text{CO}_2)$ and $v(\text{O-H})$ bands corresponding to the new species at 1623 and 3616 cm⁻¹ evolve more quickly upon heating than those assigned to the M_E-O₂COH groups at 1503 and 1582 cm⁻¹. Moreover, the new bands closely match those recently reported for Zn-O₂COH (1660, 3626 cm⁻¹) and Co-O₂COH (1636 cm⁻¹) groups in MFU-4l, neither of which is capable of engaging in inter-cluster hydrogen bonding.²⁸,⁴⁸ Taken together, these observations prompt us to assign the new species as non-hydrogen bonded Ni–O₂COH groups at the isolated M_A sites of the metal notes. Given that similar IR bands are not observed for Zn-CO₂ or Co-CO₂, the isolated Ni–OH groups appear to have a higher CO₂ binding energy that accounts for the increased low-pressure adsorption and heat of adsorption observed for Ni-OH.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations. DFT calculation have been carried out on model Kuratowski clusters to gain insight into the differences in CO₂ adsorption among Zn-OH, Ni-OH, and Co-OH. The model clusters contain truncated benzotriazolate ligands with starting geometries based on the reported crystal structure of CFA-1.³⁹ In all cases, Zn was placed at the central octahedral metal site, in line with experimental results indicating that this site does not undergo cation exchange.³⁸,³⁹ Complete exchange of the peripheral metal sites in Co-OH prompted the use of a Co₄Zn model cluster. However, the experimental Ni:Zn ratio of 3:2 for Ni-OH is more ambiguous since bulk characterization methods do not reveal the distribution of compositions at individual metal nodes. Consequently, heterobimetallic clusters at the extrema of possible compositions, NiZn₄ and Ni₄Zn, have been evaluated. Geometry optimizations and frequency calculations were carried out for hydroxide-functionalized clusters (MₓZnᵧ-OH) and clusters with bicarbonate ligands coordinated at one peripheral metal site (MₓZnᵧ-HCO₃). All DFT calculations were performed at the B3PW91 level using a mixed basis set of LANL2DZ with core pseudopotentials for Co, Ni, and Zn, 6-311(g,d) for H, C, and 6-311+(g,d) for N and O. All calculations with the heterobimetallic Ni and Co clusters were performed at the highest electronic spin states consistent with the weak field benzotriazolate ligands and high spin states observed for related homogeneous clusters.⁴⁹⁻⁵¹

![](./images/812701286183993345_9.jpg)

Figure 6. DFT optimized structures of MₓZnᵧ-HCO₃ model clusters with M-O bond lengths and calculated CO₂ binding energies given in parentheses.

Initial structure optimizations of two possible HCO₃⁻ linkage isomers, Zn₅-HCO₃(proximal) and Zn₅-HCO₃(distal), established the latter as the more thermodynamically stable structure (Figure

S38). This finding is consistent with the bicarbonate binding mode observed for several structurally characterized molecular complexes, prompting use of the distal isomers starting points for optimization of the remaining clusters. $^{52-56}$ The $HCO_3^-$ ligand adopts asymmetric chelate modes in the optimized $Co_4Zn-HCO_3$ and $Zn_5-HCO_3$ clusters, but is nearly symmetric in $NiZn_4-HCO_3$ and $Ni_4Zn-HCO_3$. These differences are in line with molecular bicarbonate complexes and reflect the propensity of Ni to adopt higher coordination numbers. $^{49,50,52,57}$ The first $CO_2$ binding energies calculated for each cluster show a clear trend ($Ni_4Zn > NiZn_4 > Zn_5 > Co_4Zn$) that correlates with the "bidenticity" of the $HCO_3^-$ ligands and agrees with the experimental isotherm data (Figure 6). $^{57}$ The larger $CO_2$ binding energy calculated for $Ni_4Zn$ versus $NiZn_4$ also indicates that the overall cluster composition has a significant influence. Although the calculated structures and binding energies do not take inter-cluster hydrogen bonding interactions into account, they should be expected to reflect the relative strength of $CO_2$ binding at the isolated $M_A$ sites. Consequently, the observed trend points to stronger $CO_2$ binding at the $Ni_A$ site of $Ni-OH$ and supports observation of the corresponding species in the DRIFT spectra.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, postsynthetic metal and ligand exchange have been used generate a series of heterobimetallic analogues of CFA-1 containing nucleophilic transition metal hydroxide functional groups at the Kuratowski-type metal nodes. While the $Cu-$ exchanged MOFs proved to be unstable to activation, $Co-OH$ and $Ni-OH$ showed low pressure $CO_2$ adsorption capacities similar to the parent MOF, $Zn-OH$. $Ni-OH$ outperforms $Zn-OH$ at pressures below 10 mbar in single component $CO_2$ isotherms, but column breakthrough measurements indicate that $Ni-OH$ is more susceptible than $Zn-OH$ to decreased performance in the presence of water. DRIFTS experiments support a $OH^-/HCO_3^-$ chemisorption mechanism for $Co-OH$ and $Ni-OH$ that is accompanied by the formation of inter-cluster hydrogen bonds similar to those previously identified for $Zn-OH$. In addition, $Ni-$ $OH$ gives rise to a new set of IR bands attributed to the formation of $Ni-O_2COH$ species that do not engage in inter-cluster hydrogen bonding. Analogous species are not observed for $Co-OH$ and $Zn-$ $OH$, suggesting that the Ni/Zn Kuratowski-type nodes have an intrinsically higher $CO_2$ affinity. This hypothesis is supported by DFT calculations performed on model clusters which reveal the first $CO_2$ binding energy follows the trend $Ni_4Zn > NiZn_4 > Zn_5 > Co_4Zn$. Overall, these findings show that postsynthetic cation exchange can be used to modulate $CO_2$ adsorption in MOFs containing nucleophilic $M-OH$ groups with changes in metal identity and cluster composition offering control over $CO_2$ capacity, heat of adsorption, and regeneration temperature. While some obstacles related to stability and water compatibility remain to be addressed, this class of materials clearly holds promise for trace $CO_2$ capture applications.

EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

General Considerations. $Zn(OAc)_2\cdot2H_2O$ (Alfa Aesar, 97+ %), $NiCl_2\cdot6H_2O$ (Alfa Aesar, 99.3 %), $CuCl_2\cdot2H_2O$ (Fisher Scientific), $CuBr_2$ (Baker Chemical), $CoCl_2$ (Fisher Scientific, 99.7 %), $NaHCO_3$ (Fisher Scientific, 99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, Fisher Scientific), dimethylacetamide (Alfa Aesar), ethanol (Decon Laboratories), and glacial acetic acid (Fisher Scientific) were used as received. $H_2$bibta was synthesized according a reported procedure. $^{26}$ Tetrahydrofuran (Fisher Scientific) and acetonitrile (Fisher) were degassed by sparging with ultra-high purity argon and dried via passage through columns of drying agents using a solvent purification system from Pure Process Technologies. All other solvents and reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used as received. Routine powder X-ray diffraction patterns for phase identification were collected using a Rigaku Miniflex 600 diffractometer with Nickel-filtered $Cu-K\alpha$ radiation ($\lambda = 1.5418$ Å). Elemental microanalyses were performed by Robertson Microlit, Ledgewood, NJ. Solution-state NMR spectra were measured using a Bruker DPX 400 MHz spectrometer. For $^1$H NMR spectra, the solvent resonance was referenced as an internal standard. Solvent-suppressed $^1$H NMR spectra were collected using $180^\circ$ water selective excitation sculpting with default parameters and pulse shapes. $^{58}$ Briefly, spectra were collected using selective pulses of 1 ms with the transmitter frequency set to the center of the solvent resonance. The recycle delay between scans was 2 s, 16K points were collected, and the acquisition time was 0.7 s. Samples were digested for solution state $^1$H NMR analysis by suspending 15-20 mg of solid in $CF_3CO_2H$ (0.5 mL) and gently heating the suspensions until all of the solids dissolved. DMSO-d$_6$ (0.1 mL) was then added to the solutions to provide a lock signal for shimming. High resolution XPS analysis was performed at the Ohio State Surface Analysis lab using a Kratos Axis Ultra X-ray photoelectron spectrometer with a monochromatic Al K$\alpha$ X-ray source. The binding energy of all photoelectron spectra were referenced to adventitious carbon at 284.5 eV.

Synthesis of Co-Cl. Anhydrous $CoCl_2$ (2.02 g, 16 mmol) was added as a solid to a suspension of $Zn-OAc$ (0.97 g, 0.77 mmol) in DMF (200 mL) under a $N_2$ atmosphere and heated to reflux with stirring. After 16 h, the suspension was cooled and the solid was collected via filtration. The excess metal salts were removed by repeatedly soaking the solid in fresh DMF (50 mL) until the absorption bands corresponding to $Co^{2+}$ were no longer observed in the UV-Vis spectrum of the supernatant. The solid was subsequently washed with MeOH (3 × 50 mL) to afford $Co-Cl$. Co:Zn molar ratio determined by ICP-OES: 4.61:1.

Synthesis of Ni-Cl. $Zn-OAc$ (0.50 g, 0.40 mmol) was added to a solution of $NiCl_2\cdot6H_2O$ (4.7 g, 20 mmol) in DMF (100 mL) under a $N_2$ atmosphere and heated to 80 °C with stirring. After 16 h, the suspension was cooled and the solid was collected via filtration. The excess metal salts were removed by repeatedly soaking the solid in fresh DMF (50 mL) until the absorption bands corresponding to $Ni^{2+}$ were no longer observed in the UV-Vis spectrum of the supernatant. The solid was subsequently washed with MeOH (3 × 50 mL) to afford $Ni-Cl$. Ni:Zn molar ratio determined by ICP-OES: 1.5:1.

Synthesis of Cu-X (X = Br or Cl). $Zn-OAc$ (0.50 g, 0.40 mmol) was added to a solution of $CuCl_2$ or $CuBr_2$ (20 mmol) in DMA (100 mL) under a $N_2$ atmosphere and heated to 60 °C with stirring. After 16 h, the suspension was cooled and the solid was collected via filtration. The excess metal salts were removed by repeatedly soaking the solid in fresh DMF (50 mL) until the absorption bands corresponding to $Cu^{2+}$ were no longer observed in the UV-Vis spectrum of the supernatant. The solid was subsequently washed with MeCN (3 × 50 mL) to afford $Cu-X$. Cu:Zn molar ratio determined by ICP-OES: 0.54:1.

Synthesis of Co-OH, Ni-OH and Cu-OH(X). $Co-Cl$, $Cu-X$ or $Ni-Cl$ (0.25 g) were soaked in aqueous solutions of 1.0 M $NaHCO_3$ (6.3 mL). After 30 min, the solids were collected by filtration or centrifugation and the soaking procedure was repeated a total of three times. The solids were subsequently washed with water (2 × 20 mL), and organic solvent. $Co-HCO_3$ and $Ni-HCO_3$ were washed with EtOH (2 × 20 mL) and anhydrous THF (2 × 20 mL) while $Cu-HCO_3(X)$ samples were washed with MeCN (2 × 20 mL). $Co-OH$, $Cu-OH(X)$, and $Ni-OH$ were obtained upon heating the solid samples in vacuo at 100 °C for 12-48 h.

Gas Adsorption Measurements. Single component, gas adsorption isotherms were measured using a Micromeritics 3Flex Surface Characterization Analyzer. All measurements were performed using ultra high purity grade gases (99.999 %)

purchased from Praxair (N₂: NI 5.0UH-K; CO₂: CD 5.0LS-K; O₂: OX 5.0RS-K; He: HE 5.0UH-K). Prior to analysis, samples (100-200 mg) were transferred to oven-dried and tared sample tubes equipped with TranSeals™ (Micrometrics) and heated to 100 °C (1 °C min⁻¹) under vacuum until the outgas rate was less than 0.0033 mbar min⁻¹. Surface areas were calculated from N₂ adsorption isotherms (77 K) by fitting the isotherm data to the BET equation with the appropriate pressure range (0.0001 ≤ P/P₀ ≤ 0.1) determined by the consistency criteria of Rouquerol.⁵⁹,⁶⁰ A Micrometrics thermocouple-controlled heating mantle was used to maintain the sample temperature for isotherms measured between 300 and 373 K.

Thermogravimetric Analysis Measurements. TGA and Thermal swing adsorption (TSA) data were recorded using a TA Instruments Q50. TGA data was collected under a flow of N₂ gas (50 mL min⁻¹). For TSA experiments, a sample of Ni-CO₂ (10 mg) was quickly transferred to a 100 μL Pt sample pan, loaded into the balance/furnace under a flow of N₂ (50 mL min⁻¹), and heated to 150 °C for 30 min. The adsorption steps were carried by cooling the sample to 27 °C, switching the sample flow gas (50 mL min⁻¹) to 5% CO₂ in N₂ and maintaining the sample temperature at 27 °C for 35 min. The desorption steps were performed by switching the sample flow gas to N₂, heating to 150 °C at a rate of 5 °C min⁻¹, and maintaining the sample temperature at 150 °C for 30 min.

DRIFTS and ATR-IR Measurements. DRIFTS spectra were recorded on a Bruker Tensor spectrometer equipped with a liquid N₂-cooled MCT detector and Praying Mantis diffuse reflectance accessory (Harrick Scientific). Background spectra were collected with anhydrous KBr. Samples were diluted with anhydrous KBr and loaded into the sample cup of a high temperature reaction chamber fitted with ZnSe windows. The chamber was sealed and placed under a flow of dry N₂ gas. DRIFTS spectra were measured periodically while heating the samples from 25 °C to 150 °C.

Computation Details. All calculations were performed using Gaussian16 (version: g16a03). The isomers Zn₅-HCO₃(distal) and Zn₅-HCO₃(proximal) (Figure S38) were optimized using a combination of Becke's gradient-corrected exchange functional and Perdew's 1986 electron correlation functional (BP86).⁶¹ A mixed basis set was employed, using the LANL2DZ basis set with effective core potentials for Zn and Gaussian's internal 6-311G(d) for C and H, and 6-311+G(d) for heteroatoms (N and O).

Structure optimization and frequency calculations on MₓZny-OH and MₓZny-HCO₃ model clusters were carried out using B3PW91 (Becke's 3-parameter functional⁶² and Perdew-Wang 91 hybrid)⁶³ and the following mixed basis set: LANL2DZ with effective core potentials for Zn, Co and Ni and Gaussian's internal 6-311(g,d) for C and H, and 6-311+(g,d) for heteroatoms (N and O). The geometry of the model clusters were optimized to a minimum starting from the crystallographically-determined structure of the Zn₅ cluster. Analytical frequency calculations were performed to confirm that no imaginary frequencies were present. The CO₂ binding enthalpy for each model cluster was determined according to:

$$
\text{E[CO}_2\text{ binding]} = \text{E[M}_x\text{Zn}_y\text{-HCO}_3\text{]} - \text{E[M}_x\text{Zn}_y\text{-OH]} - \text{E[CO}_2\text{]}
$$

where E is the thermally-corrected electronic energy obtained from frequency calculations performed on the optimized structures.
