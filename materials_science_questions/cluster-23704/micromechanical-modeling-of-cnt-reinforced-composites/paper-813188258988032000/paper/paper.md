# Strain Localisation in iPP/MWCNT Nanocomposites Using Digital Image Correlation

E. Ivanov*, P. Ienny†, R. Leger†, A. S. Caro-Bretelle† and R. Kotsilkova*

*Institute of Mechanics, Bulgarian Academy of Sciences, Acad. G. Bonchev St., Block 1, 1113, Sofia, Bulgaria
†Centre des Matériaux des Mines d'Alès, Ecole des Mines d'Alès, 6 avenue de Clavières, F-30319, Alès Cedex, France

ABSTRACT: Polypropylene/multiwall carbon nanotube nanocomposites with different rates of weight incorporation (0–1%) were prepared by melt compounding and cast extrusion. The effect of maleic anhydride (5 wt%) grafted on polypropylene is studied through mechanical tests at different scales and morphological observations. In particular, the micromechanism of deformation was investigated through instrumented tensile experiments (at a macro and micro scales) using of a non-contact method known as digital image correlation. The objective of this paper is first to characterise global behaviour (Young modulus, tensile strength, and ultimate properties) and second to go further in local analysis. In particular, optical instrumentation enables estimation of strain profile distribution onto the sample in a constricted area. Statistical parameters extracted from these local profiles are promising tools to enhance mechanical properties in link with microstructural composition. Tensile tests confirm composite reinforcement at a low level of nanocomposite incorporation, and local analysis enables quantitative measurements of adding maleic anhydride in formulations. The results reveal that addition of maleic anhydride delays strain localisation in the necked area.

KEY WORDS: digital image correlation, micromechanism of deformation, multiwall carbon nanotubes, polymer nanocomposites, polypropylene, striction, ultimate properties

## Introduction

Polymer nanocomposites filled up with carbon nanotubes (CNTs) gained intensive interest thanks to their unique and valuable mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties as compared with virgin polymers [1–10]. One of the CNTs' advantages as a reinforcement filler is their large surface area that can induce a better adhesion with the polymer matrix, which is an important factor for an effective enhancement of the composite properties.

However, in order to take advantage of the unique properties of CNTs, the main challenge is to improve the dispersion and distribution of CNTs within the polymer matrix, as CNTs have a tendency to form agglomerates. This is due to the strong intermolecular Van der Waals interactions among the CNTs in combination with their high surface area and high aspect ratio. In the polymer composite, these agglomerates decrease the surface area, disturb the formation of the polymer network, and are responsible for low mechanical properties. That is why the main task of processing is to dissolve such agglomerates as much as possible. Therefore, uniform dispersion of the CNT is required to realise the potentiality of the nanotubes as reinforcing fillers [11–17].

The methods for preparation of polymer nanocomposites filled with CNTs are *in situ* polymerisation, solution mixing, and melt blending. An extrusion process is frequently used due to its industrial importance and ability to disperse CNTs in the matrix. Among all polymer matrices, polypropylene (PP) is one of the widely used thermoplastic polymers for industries like automotive and food packaging for its low cost and well-balanced mechanical and physical properties as well. Other authors recently reported their findings on polymer chains grafted onto CNTs showing that they allow reduction of the aggregation of nanotubes and improvement of the homogeneous dispersion in the matrix, which finally leads to the increase of the mechanical properties due to load transfer between matrix and nanotubes [18–21].

In the literature, there are many studies on the macromechanical properties of the polymer nanocomposites filled with nanoparticles, but there are very limited information about micromechanical and nanomechanical properties and the micromechanism of deformation [22]. At nanoscale level, the mechanics of the materials is different, and interfacial interactions play an important role. One of the non-contact measurement techniques in the experimental mechanics is the digital image correlation (DIC). The DIC method has been widely accepted and used as a powerful and flexible tool for the surface deformation measurement in the field of experimental solid mechanics. It directly provides full-field displacements and strains by comparing the digital images of the specimen surface in the undeformed (chosen as the reference state) and deformed states. In principle, DIC is an optical metrology based on digital image processing and numerical computing [23–25].

The main objective of the present work is to investigate the effect of multiwall CNT (MWCNT) concentration at 5 wt% of maleic anhydride (MA) in isotactic PP (iPP) on the micromechanism of deformation of iPP/MWCNT nanocomposites based on tensile experiments and DIC. It is well known that the grafted MA groups will form chemical or hydrogen bonding with the CNTs, improving the interfacial adhesion between CNTs and the iPP matrix. This compatibiliser therefore acts as a coupling agent to enhance homogeneous dispersion of the percolated network structure of CNTs in the PP matrix [26].

## Experimental Study
### Materials used and fabrication of nanocomposites

A masterbatch of 20wt% MWCNT in PP was obtained in pellet form from Hyperion Catalysis International (Cambridge, MA). MWCNTs are commercially manufactured from high-purity, low-molecular-weight hydrocarbons in a continuous, gas-phase, catalysed reaction. A typical outside diameter of the tubes ranges from 10 to 15 nm, the lengths are between 1 and 10 µm, and their density is approximately $1.75\,\text{g}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$. The masterbatch is produced by initially dispersing intertwined agglomerates of nanotubes into the polymer.

The iPP homopolymer “Buplen” 6231 with a density of $\rho=0.901\,\text{g}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ and a melt flow index (230/2.16) of 12.2 g/10 min, supplied by Lukoil Neftochim Co. (Sofia, Bulgaria), was used as the matrix polymer. MA grafted PP Licomont AR 504, a product of Clariant GmbH (Frankfurt, Germany), was used as a chemical compatibiliser for improving the interaction between the polymer matrix and MWCNTs and the wetting of MWCNT particles as well.

Nanocomposites were produced by direct melt compounding in a Brabender DSE 35/17D twin screw extruder (Brabender GmbH and Co., Duisburg, Germany) according to a two-step process. First, the masterbatch was diluted to different CNT concentrations in the range of 0.05–1 wt% CNTs with neat iPP at a melt temperature of $200^\circ\text{C}$ and a screw speed of 30 r.p.m. The extruded composites were cooled and pelletised. In order to improve the CNT dispersion, these compositions were extruded a second time. MA grafted PP (5% wt) was added into the nanocomposites during a second stage and pelletised. The compound is then calendered as sheets of various thicknesses (0.4–1 mm) at a melt temperature of $230^\circ\text{C}$ and a screw speed of 30 r.p.m.

## Characterisation of composites
### Microstructure

An optical microscope has been used to investigate the quality of the MWCNT distribution and dispersion in the matrix. Strips of 1 mm thick have been cryo-fractured and observed. First of all, neat and maleic iPP are characterised. Optical micrographs (Figure 1) reveal large spherulites with a diameter of $100\,\mu\text{m}$ and small spherulites of about $50\,\mu\text{m}$ for the neat iPP (Figure 1a); the addition of MA produces a non-homogeneous structure ranging from large spherulites of $200\,\mu\text{m}$ to a mixture of large and small spherulites from 10 to $100\,\mu\text{m}$ (Figure 1b–d). The agglomeration of nanotubes in the nanocomposites has been also studied through this optical device (Figure 2). CNT aggregates are observed as dark spots with a broad particle size distribution (smaller to bigger as the CNT concentration increases [27]). At these levels of incorporation, aggregates are well dispersed. After 0.5 wt% CNTs, we observe a non-homogeneous structure with smaller non-dispersed aggregates for the formulation with MA (Figure 3). At a higher level of CNT incorporation (Figure 4), micrographs reveal a non-homogeneous structure with non-dispersed aggregates of the masterbatch (smaller for the formulation with MA).

Standard tensile test instrumented by digital image correlation
Tensile properties (uniaxial tensile test) were determined by using a ZWICK TH010 (Zwick GmbH & Co. KG (Germany)) universal testing machine equipped with a high-resolution charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Redlake Megaplus II (E-Vision System Inc. (Canada))) with a telecentric lens of a focal length of 55 mm adapted for DIC and a load cell of 0.5 kN. A wedge grip was used for clamping the samples.

The camera is fixed on a Newport Motion Controller Model ESP301 (Newport Corporation (United States)). Such equipment is very useful in case of large strains in order to follow the region of interest (ROI) at the centre of the sample and remove the rigid body motion. The optical axis of the camera is perfectly perpendicular to the surface of the specimen. Acquisition of images (1920×1080 pixels, 256 grey levels) is performed by LabVIEW® software (National Instruments (Nanterre)), which allows the simultaneous acquisition of images and data provided by the testing machine (such as load and crosshead displacement). According to the test speed of $10\,\text{mm}\,\text{min}^{-1}$, two images were recorded every second. The scale factor or magnification (k), which depends on the focal lens and the distance between the camera and object, was fixed to $32\,\mu\text{m}$ per pixel. This scale factor constitutes a compromise between a necessary spatial resolution for the local strain measurements and the limitation of the perspective errors associated to the out-of-plane displacement [28].

As far as local analysis is concerned, expected samples were machined with a curved profile so that the neck initiation takes place within the observation field of the camera (Figure 5a). This observed zone corresponds to the minimal section (4 mm) of the specimen. A radius curvature of 42.5 mm is used to keep the tensile load uniaxial and limit heterogeneities. The specimen thickness is between 0.4 and 1 mm.

A speckle pattern was applied before the tensile test. It consists, for the macroscopic samples in a first thin layer, of white paint and a sputtered layer of blue paint on the surface of samples. This creates the artificial image signature. The tensile tests were carried out until the samples break. The tensile direction is defined by $\mathbf{x}$, and $\mathbf{y}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{x}$ (Figure 5).

Time, axial load, and axial displacement were recorded by the tensile device software. The nominal stress (the first Piola-Kirchhoff tensor) is defined by
$$
P=F / S_{0}=F / l_{y} l_{z}, \tag{1}
$$
where $F$ is the axial recorded load and $l_{y}$ and $l_{z}$ are the initial useful section dimensions in the $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{z}$ directions, respectively. Nevertheless, this definition neglects the cross-sectional area reduction occurring during the loading and the necking. Considering this variation, the true axial stress can be defined by
$$
\sigma_{\text {true }}=F / S=F / l_{y}^{\prime} l_{z}^{\prime} \tag{2}
$$
where $l_{y}^{\prime}$ and $l_{z}^{\prime}$ are the current section dimensions in the $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{z}$ directions, respectively.

The image processing after the test is based on the DIC computation. A two-dimensional digital extensometer is defined in the ROI (Figure 5). The gauge length has to be small enough to accurately describe the strain gradients in the $\mathbf{x}$ direction and large enough to evaluate the mean strain in the $\mathbf{y}$ direction. Each point of the virtual mesh corresponds to the centre of a pattern. This pattern, defined by its correlation size $(C s=30$ pixels $=0.96 \mathrm{~mm})$, is the representative area of the material point that is tracked (Figure 5). The distance between mesh points is given by the grid step parameter $(G s=3$ pixels $=0.096 \mathrm{~mm})$. A deformation calculation is obtained by the first-order derivation of the displacement field around the considered point. The dimension of the area where the in-plane gradient tensor is evaluated is fixed by the number of step parameters ( $N s=4$ in the $\mathbf{x}$ direction and $N s=20$ in the $\mathbf{y}$ direction), which qualifies the number of points to take into account in the horizontal and vertical directions. The bidimensional gauge dimensions ( $G L x$ and $G L y$ ) expressed in pixels and millimetres, used for different calculations, are as follows: $G L x=24$ pixels $=0.77 \mathrm{~mm}$ and $G L y=120$ pixels $=4 \mathrm{~mm}$. It should be noted that in the $x$ direction, the gauge length estimated by the calculation is smaller than the correlation size $(C s)$. Also, the effective gauge length should be slightly higher than the one estimated. Otherwise, the strain gauge is chosen far from the sample boundaries to avoid edge effects $(4 \mathrm{~mm}<6 \mathrm{~mm})$. These correlation parameters have been optimised to establish a reliable mechanical response of the material in the central zone where the necking takes place. It may be noted that in order to ensure a good local analysis, the longitudinal gauge length is chosen around $1 \mathrm{~mm}$.

The DIC processing leads to an incremental displacement file. In-plane displacement fields $\boldsymbol{u}(x, y)=\left(u_{x}(x, y), u_{y}(x, y)\right)$ of a point $M(x, y)$ were evaluated for each point of a virtual mesh in the ROI of the sample (Figure 5), which included the strain localisation area. The calculation of the displacement gradient around a considered point allows the calculation of the two-dimensional displacement gradient tensor $\mathbf{F}$ (Equation (3)), and thus, the in-plane Green Lagrange strain tensor $\mathbf{E}$ is known for all points defining the ROI of Figure 5 (Equation (4)):
$$
\mathbf{F}(x, y)=\mathbf{1}+\nabla \boldsymbol{u}(x, y), \quad \forall M(x, y) \in \text { ROI } \tag{3}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{E}(x, y) & =\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{F}(x, y)^{\mathrm{T}} \otimes \mathbf{F}(x, y)-\mathbf{1}\right) \\
& =E_{x}(x, y) \mathbf{x} \otimes \mathbf{x}+E_{y}(x, y) \mathbf{y} \otimes \mathbf{y} \\
& +E_{x y}(x, y) \mathbf{x} \otimes \mathbf{y}, \forall M(x, y) \in \text { ROI }
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
$$

It is therefore possible to define in-plane strain components of each point defining the ROI domain. Specific points $M_{1}, M_{2}, M_{3}$, and $M_{4}$ are located in the centre of the ROI zone and will be useful for further investigations (Figure 5). The strain cartography of the ROI domain leads to a definition of the point where the

maximum strain is reached. This point will be denoted $M_{5}$ and does not necessarily correspond to the point at the minimal section $(M_{1})$.

It is useful to define the in-plane stretch ratios in the $\mathbf{x}$ or $\mathbf{y}$ direction,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{x}(x, y) & =\frac{l_{x}^{\prime}(x, y)}{l_{x}(x, y)}=\sqrt{2 \mathbf{E}_{x}(x, y)+1}, \lambda_{y}=\frac{l_{y}^{\prime}(x, y)}{l_{y}(x, y)} \\
& =\sqrt{2 \mathbf{E}_{y}(x, y)+1}
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

where $l_{x}(x, y), l_{y}(x, y)\left(l_{x}^{\prime}(x, y), l_{y}^{\prime}(x, y)\right)$ are the initial (current) section dimensions of the square pattern in the $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ directions around $M(x, y)$.

The out-of-plane stretch $\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}(x, y)$ is deduced from the in-plane principal stretch ratios in the longitudinal and transverse directions thanks to a transverse isotropy assumption [22]. This last hypothesis has been validated by some authors for a similar material and in the same range of solicitation [29]. Under this assumption, $S=\lambda_{y}(x, y) \lambda_{z}(x, y) S_{0}(x, y)=\lambda_{y}^{2}(x, y) S_{0}(x, y)$, and Equation (2) can be rewritten as

$$
\sigma_{\text {true }}(x, y)=F / S_{0}(x, y) \lambda_{y}^{2}(x, y).
\tag{6}
$$

The axial true strain $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{X}}$ and nominal strain $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}}$ are defined by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{X}}(x, y) & =\int_{l_{x}}^{l_{x}^{\prime}} \frac{\mathrm{d} l(x, y)}{l(x, y)}=\ln \left(\frac{l_{x}^{\prime}(x, y)}{l_{x}(x, y)}\right) \\
& =\ln \left(\lambda_{x}(x, y)\right), \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}}(x, y)=\frac{l_{x}^{\prime}(x, y)-l_{x}(x, y)}{l_{x}(x, y)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

## Results and Discussion
### Mechanical properties—global analysis from DIC

The elastic properties of the samples were first determined from the knowledge of both the axial load and true local strain value measured by DIC analysis. For a low level of load, the reversible strain is analysed all along the longitudinal direction. The Young modulus is therefore computed from Equations (6) and (7):

$$
E=\frac{\sigma_{\text {true }}(x, y)}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{X}}(x, y)}, \quad \forall M(x, y) \in \mathrm{ROI}.
\tag{8}
$$

To assess the reliability of Young modulus identification, four evaluations have been made depending on their positions along the longitudinal axis ($\mathbf{x}$ direction) (points $M_{1}$–$M_{4}$ in Figure 5). For a given time, these points are at the same level of axial load but differ from the values of their current stretches and true stresses. $\lambda_{\mathbf{x}}$ is maximum at point $M_{1}$, associated to the minimal section, and decreases until point $M_{4}$. In fact, the behaviour of these points is just shifted in time. As far as we are in the elastic range of strain (between 0.25 and 0.5% of axial strain), the Young modulus $(E)$ is almost the same for all the studied points: $1540 \pm 50 \mathrm{MPa}$ (see Figure 6 for the composite MWCNT (0.1 wt% CNTs without MA)). This analysis was repeated three times for all the formulations to ensure repeatability. All results are reported in Figure 7.

The incorporation of nanocharges even at 0.05 wt% CNTs induces a rise of the Young modulus until 0.5 wt% CNTs with and without MA. At 1 wt% CNTs, the modulus almost reaches the initial value of pure iPP. The initial reinforcement is probably due to good spatial repartition of MWCNTs in the matrix (Section 2.2.1). The effects of CNTs on the crystallisation behaviour of PP have been studied by some authors [30–32]. The CNTs served as nucleating agents to enhance crystallisation, and the crystallites strengthen the composite [33, 34]. Micromechanical models can be a good tool to understand this mechanical behaviour. As observed in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) investigations, MWCNTs are individual and randomly distributed under 0.1 wt% CNTs and agglomerate after that level. Nevertheless, these agglomerates seem to be well distributed in the matrix. A Mori Tanaka micromechanical model [35] is used to predict the Young modulus of reinforced iPP in both cases. For low-level incorporation, the effective elastic constants of the composite can be expressed as

$$
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}+c_{f}\left\langle\left[\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}-\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\right) \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(\left(1-c_{f}\right) \boldsymbol{I}+c_{f} \boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{e}}\right)\right]^{-1}\right\rangle, \quad (9)
$$

where $\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}$ and $\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}$ are respectively the matrix and fibre stiffness tensor, $c_{f}$ is the volume fraction of the fibre, $\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{e}}=\left(1+\boldsymbol{S} \boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}-\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\right)\right)^{-1}$ where $\boldsymbol{S}$ is the classical Eshelby tensor (for fibrous reinforcement) and $< . >$ is a weighted orientation averaging operator added to consider the arbitrary fibre orientation [36].

To implement this model, a Matlab® [37] software was used, and appropriate elastic properties of matrix and fibres have been fixed. In [38], a longitudinal Young modulus (denoted $E_{\mathrm{L}}$) of 600 GPa has been chosen for the MWCNT fibre as a result of a literature review. As the nanotubes are transverse isotropic, four more parameters are necessary to evaluate $\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}$: the transverse Young modulus (equal to 6 GPa), the two Poisson ratio (0.125 and 0.3) and the shearing modulus (2.5 GPa). The matrix is also supposed isotropic (a Young modulus of 1.017 GPa and a Poisson ratio of 0.3). The rule-of-thumb conversion factor of 2:1 is used to convert the weight fraction of fibres to volume fraction. In this model, a strong assumption of perfect bonding and loading transfer is questionable [39]; for this reason, some authors reduce the effective occupied nanotube volume by 25% of the initial volume to match experimental data [38]. $c_{f}$ will be therefore replaced by $\alpha^{*} c_{f}$ in the micromechanical implementation, where $\alpha$ is a constant to be determined. The first results indicate that for $\alpha=0.26$, the Mori Tanaka model well reproduced experimental data for the specimen without MA (Table 1). At 0.5% CNTs of weight incorporation, the prediction overestimates the experiments. In fact, at this level of incorporation, the fibre agglomerates, and the model is not applicable (Section 2).

The addition of MA leads to a new identification of parameter $\alpha$ (Table 1). The smaller effective volume fraction is in accordance with the compatibilisation effect of MA: MA does not improve the dispersion as nanotubes are already well dispersed by the process but surrounds the CNTs by a phase softer than the matrix. Therefore, the loading transfer decreases, which leads to the following smaller equivalent volume of nanotubes.

For a higher level of weight fraction, Equation (9) is revisited to consider the agglomeration of fibres. It becomes

$$
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}+\hat{c}_{f}\left[\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\rangle-\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\right) \hat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\boldsymbol{e}}\left(\left(1-c_{f}\right) \boldsymbol{I}+\hat{c}_{f} \hat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\boldsymbol{e}}\right)\right]^{-1}, \quad (10)
$$

where $\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{\boldsymbol{e}}=\left(1+\boldsymbol{S} \boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{-1}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\rangle-\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\right)\right)^{-1}$ and $\hat{c}_{f}$ is the volume fraction of agglomerates. In this equation, $\left\langle\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\rangle$ is the stiffness matrix of agglomerates in which fibres are supposed to be arbitrarily oriented. Thus, the matrix embedded in the agglomerate is neglected. This matrix is therefore isotropic. The same assumption for the effective fibre volume is made and $\hat{c_{f}}$ is replaced by $\alpha^{*} \widehat{c_{f}}$ in Equation (10). We suppose $\alpha=0.21$ and $\alpha=0.26$ for the formulations with and without MA, respectively. Results are summarised in Table 2. The equivalent weight fraction of agglomerates is higher for samples without MA. This result is in accordance with the SEM observation: adding MA decreases the volume fraction of agglomerates. Ultimate properties at the broken section of the sample, i. e. the strain at break and the tensile strength of point $M_5$ (Figure 5), are presented in Figure 8 for all composites. These two parameters are deduced from the local stress-strain curves obtained by DIC and concern the axial true strain and the true maximum stress, respectively. From this point of view, no distinction can be made between composites with or without MA adding.

## Mechanical properties—local analysis

### Strain profile distribution
This approach involves the analysis of the onset and growth of a necking phenomenon in the composite. The idea developed here is to understand how the incorporation and the dispersion of nanofillers affect the phenomenology of rupture of the composite. A first application of this methodology allowed understanding of how the incorporation of silica within a polycarbonate could lead in a more homogeneous damage [22]. Strain profile distributions have been established for different states of stress for iPP and its composites along the longitudinal axis (x direction) of the tensile bar and through the necking area (ROI, Figure 5). A typical result is presented in Figure 9 for the composite iPP/0.05 wt% CNTs.

These strain profiles have been used to characterise the degree of flatness of each distribution. The shape of the strain profile depends on the formulation: the profile of iPP/MWCNT composites is higher and narrower than that of the one with MA. To enhance local behaviour, we introduce a statistical coefficient called the flatness coefficient, which differentiates a sharp peak from a flat top in a non-Gaussian curve,

$$
C_{f}=\frac{\Delta \mathrm{i} / h_{\max }}{\left(\Delta \mathrm{i} / h_{\max }\right)_{25 \mathrm{MPa}}}, \tag{11}
$$

where $\Delta i$ is the peak width at half-height, $h_{\max }$ is the total height (Figure 9) and $(.)_{25 \mathrm{MPa}}$ is the value parameter for $\sigma_{N}$ $(M_{1})=25$ MPa. At this level of stress, all the tested specimens follow a homogeneous behaviour far away from the striction phenomenon. This flatness coefficient traduces the ability of the necking zone to spread over the tensile bar or to be localised in the machined minimal section. An

important flatness coefficient indicates a large strain peak, meaning a high dissipative energy by plasticity before fracture [22]. This statistical coefficient is plotted (Figure 10) as a function of axial strain for the point that presents the maximum strain (point $M_5$(1.8 mm, 0 mm) in Figure 9). For each formulation, three samples have been tested. Presented results are an average of these three values, which present a very narrow dispersion regardless of the considered property (standard deviation is under 1% of the mechanical property).

The general evolution is the same for all the tested samples. The flatness coefficient shows a first drop corresponding to the onset of the necking associated to the growth of a strain peak, and then, the coefficient stabilises during the neck propagation.

Under 0.5 wt% of CNT, the flatness coefficient is always higher when MA is added (Figure 10). MA PP has a low surface energy and is expected to give a good compatibility between the CNT and the polymer by formation of stronger linkages in the interfaces and reducing the CNT surface tension. In particular, hydrogen bonding between MA grafted PP and CNTs can be observed using infrared spectroscopy [40]. This better load transfer is in accordance with a better strain homogenization observed for all samples under 0.5 wt% of CNT. The effect on the flatness coefficient of MA is reversed beyond 0.5% of CNTs as illustrated for samples with 0.5 wt% CNT (Figure 10c) and 1 wt% CNT (Figure 10d). The reason is the particle agglomeration: they are responsible for a great heterogeneity of the strain profile, introducing sometimes a multipeak strain curve. It is also important to note that the onset of necking does not occur necessarily in the minimal section when aggregates are present in the materials. To illustrate these points, strain profiles are plotted for all formulations at the same true stress equal to 36 MPa at the broken point (Figure 11). As shown in Figure 11a for non-maleic samples, the axial strain profile remains perfectly Gaussian under 0.5 wt% CNT and becomes heterogeneous for a higher level of nanofiller incorporations (see samples at 0.5 and 1 wt% CNTs in Figure 11b). The same kind of evolution is observed for the sample with MA (Figure 11c and d). In Figure 11b, each local peak should therefore correspond to an aggregate: in these cases, with an incorporation rate higher than 0.5 wt% CNT, characterization of a local phenomenon via the statistical parameter defined by (11) cannot be a good indicator of the observed phenomenology. As not expected, it is observed that the level of stabilisation of the flatness coefficient is higher for higher rates of CNTs (Figure 11). If this phenomenon is related to the preferential onset of fracture at the aggregate positions, it would have been expected that the presence of aggregates in composite prevents the necking from propagating. A reverse tendency is observed because several peaks appear from aggregate positions. So other statistical methods should then be established.

The presence of fillers in the PP matrix increases the flatness coefficient value and so helps the spreading out of the necking area (Figure 12). On the other hand, for the same flatness coefficient value, which defines the shape of the peak, the maximum strain is greater for the composites containing MA. These results confirm the better properties for these last formulations due to a greater accommodation with plasticity.

On the other hand, although weaker mechanical properties are obtained with the addition of MA (with the exception of the pure iPP), the use of MA as a compatibiliser homogenise the mechanical response of nanocomposites that remain the same until the threshold stress of 40 MPa, regardless of the incorporation fraction of CNTs.

## Conclusions

This work investigated the effects of incorporation of CNTs in the PP matrix (PP) on elastic properties and on the onset and growth of the necking phenomenon in these composites.

As a first result, image correlation is diverted from its primary use and leads to the determination of some macroscopic properties such as Young modulus, tensile strength, and ultimate strain. This true strain value is measured locally by DIC analysis in the necking area. Between 0.05 and 0.5 wt% CNTs, most of these properties (except the ultimate strain) are improved with the amount of CNTs. Nevertheless, the major expected effect of iPP grafted MA (a better dispersion of nanotubes and an improvement in the load transfer between filler and matrix) is not interesting from a macroscopic point of view in our study: MWCNTs are already well dispersed in the iPP matrix as highlighted by the results obtained from strengthening these materials. The mechanical characterisation has shown that both Young modulus and tensile strength of the nanocomposites increase by the addition of nanotubes until 0.5 wt% CNTs. After that, properties decrease with the addition of MWCNTs due to the presence of aggregates, found even when iPP grafted MA is added.

The analysis of the onset and growth of a necking phenomenon in the composite is studied from strain profile distributions. Via a flatness coefficient, this approach provides a means of analysing the plastic dissipative potential before fracture. The local analysis highlights particular behaviours produced by iPP-g-MA. The enhancement of compatibility between CNT and polymer produces a strain homogenisation: striction in the central area of the sample is either the source of local breaking (neat iPP) or the origin of spreading through the sample (iPP-g-MA). Furthermore, these results reveal the benefits of anhydride maleic with regard to dissipative energy phenomena.

Finally, this integrated local approach may be followed to introduce an energy analysis of the fracture of these heterogeneous materials by using a numerical tool such as finite element modelling. Also, instrumented tests in SEM should validate the phenomenology of the deformation observed.
