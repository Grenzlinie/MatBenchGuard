# Near neighbor chemical bonding effects on Si atom native bonding defects in silicon nitride and silicon dioxide insulators

G. Lucovsky and S. Y. Lin
Department of Physics, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, North Carolina 27695-8202

(Received 29 January 1985; accepted 28 February 1985)

There has been considerable interest in the use of deposited thin films of $Si_{3}N_{4}$ and $SiO_{2}$ as gate insulators and/or passivation layers for compound semiconductors. This is contingent on a low temperature deposition process which: (a) produces insulators which are effectively free of native bonding defects and impurity atoms, and at the same time (b) minimizes chemical degradation, and the associated generation of interfacial defect states at the compound semiconductor surfaces. Recently, the group at North Carolina State University and The Research Triangle Institute has developed a low temperature, remote plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (CVD) process for depositing trilayer gate insulators consisting of $SiO_{2}-Si_{3}N_{4}-SiO_{2}$ layers onto $p$-type (In,Ga)As to produce high gain, stable, $n$-channel conducting field-effect transistor (FET) devices. The processing chemistry for the deposition of these $Si_{3}N_{4}$ and $SiO_{2}$ layers prevents the incorporation of significant amounts of chemical impurities in the form of oxygen atoms in the $Si_{3}N_{4}$, and hydrogen and nitrogen atoms in the $SiO_{2}$. This paper addresses the question of native bonding defects in $Si_{3}N_{4}$ and $SiO_{2}$, and considers the effects of near neighbor impurity atoms on the gap state energies of two types of defects, Si dangling bonds and Si-Si bonds.

## I. INTRODUCTION

There has been a long history of interest in the use of deposited thin films of silicon nitride and silicon dioxide as gate insulators and/or passivation layers for compound semiconductor devices. Deposition of films by CVD requires temperatures in excess of $700\ ^{\circ}C,^{1}$ which is well above the temperature at which most compound semiconductor surfaces of interest, e.g., GaAs, InP, etc., show chemical degradation due to loss of the more volatile constituent atom (As, P, etc.) This has prompted the study of low temperature deposition processes which include both plasma and photon-assisted CVD. $^{1-3}$ Recently the group at North Carolina State University and The Research Triangle Institute has developed a low temperature $(200-300\ ^{\circ}C)$ remote plasma-enhanced CVD (RPECVD) deposition process that has yielded stable gate insulators for (In,Ga)As FET devices. $^{4,5}$ The dielectric they have used is a structure consisting of a trilayer $SiO_{2}-Si_{3}N_{4}-SiO_{2}$ sandwich. The process chemistry for the deposition of the $Si_{3}N_{4}$ layer prevents the incorporation of significant amounts of oxygen atom impurities, whereas most other PECVD techniques incorporate significant amounts of bonded oxygen (generally of the order of several atomic percent). In a similar way conventional PECVD of $SiO_{2}$ using $N_{2}O$ as the source of oxygen yields oxide films with nitrogen impurities present. The process described in Refs. 4 and 5 uses $O_{2}$ as the oxygen source, and thus eliminates nitrogen impurities in the $SiO_{2}$ layers. The use of $O_{2}$ is not possible in capacitively coupled PECVD systems where $SiH_{4}$ and the oxygen bearing molecule are excited in a common plasma region. $^{4,5}$

We have previously addressed the question of bonding defects in $a$-Si: H alloys due to oxygen, nitrogen or carbon atoms using a tight-binding theory, $^{6,7}$ and have found that these atoms by themselves do not produce states in the gap, but that they can modify the energies of dangling bond defects on neighboring Si atoms. This has prompted us to make a similar theoretical study of defects states in $Si_{3}N_{4}$ and $SiO_{2}$, and in particular to determine the effects of near neighbor impurity atoms on intrinsic defects such as Si atom dangling bonds, and Si-Si bonds. These two types of intrinsic bonding defects in $Si_{3}N_{4}$ were shown by Robertson and Powell (hereafter RP) $^{8}$ to produce states either within the forbidden gap (the Si-Si bond), or in the vicinity of the conduction band edge (the Si neutral dangling bond). Parallel results had previously been reported by Laughlin, Joannopoulos, and Chadi (hereafter LJC) $^{9}$ for the same two types of Si atom bonding defects in $SiO_{2}$. Our calculations represent an extension of the RP and LJC models to include the effects of near neighbor impurities on the energies of these defect states. Section II of this paper describes the techniques used in our model calculation. This includes a description of the cluster Bethe Lattice, which serves as a structural model, and the choice of tight-binding parameters. Section III presents our calculations. We include a calculation for defect-free clusters of $SiO_{2}$ and $Si_{3}N_{4}$. We then extend this structural model to include: (a) two types of intrinsic bonding defects, Si atom dangling bonds and Si-Si bonds in otherwise perfect $Si_{3}N_{4}$ and $SiO_{2}$ hosts, (b) oxygen impurity atoms in the perfect $Si_{3}N_{4}$ network (hereafter we designate the alloyed compound as $Si_{3}N_{4}:O$), and oxygen impurity atoms as nearest neighbors to the two types of intrinsic bonding defects identified above, but also in $Si_{3}N_{4}:O$, and (c) similar defect bonding configurations involving nitrogen atoms in $SiO_{2}$ and $SiO_{2}:N$. Section IV discusses the results of our calculations in the context of what we have previously reported for the Si atom dangling bond defects in $a$-Si. $^{6,7}$ It also includes a general discussion of the effects of near neighbor impurity atoms on other properties including the energies of Si atoms $2p$ core states, $^{10}$ and on Si-H bond stretching vibrations. $^{11}$ These comparisons demonstrate the usefulness of the atomic electronegativity as a scaling variable for local properties at alloy or impurity atoms in amorphous host materials.

### III. RESULTS OF DEFECT STATE CALCULATIONS

We first present calculations of the LDOS functions for oxygen impurity atoms in Si₃N₄, and nitrogen impurity atoms in SiO₂. These are shown, respectively, in Figs. 4 and 5 for situations in which all valence bonding requirements are satisfied by the Si, O, and N atoms. The most important manifestations of oxygen impurity atoms in Si₃N₄: O subject to this restriction, are (a) changes in the character of the valence band states of the Si and N atoms that are, respectively, nearest and next nearest neighbors to the O impurity, and (b) a band of nonbonding oxygen $2p$ states just below the top of the valence band edge. The band of nonbonding oxygen $2p$ states, and an associated band of modified nitrogen nonbonding $2p$ states, are then the only bands of new states that are potentially active in electronic transport and trapping phenomena. Specifically, these states can be important in hole transport by providing localized states for trapping, and/or a band of states for a hopping conduction mechanism. The exact role these state play will depend on the number of such states present, that is the oxygen impurity atom concentration. The character of the valence band states for a nitrogen impurity in SiO₂: N is qualitatively similar. However in this case the nitrogen atom nonbonding $2p$ band lies above the highest nonbonding oxygen $2p$ states of the SiO₂ host, and in this context may be even more active than the corresponding oxygen $2p$ states in Si₃N₄: O, either as trapping centers, or as a band of states for hole conduction.

### IV. CHEMICAL EFFECTS IN DEFECT STATE ENERGIES

In two previous publications (Refs. 6 and 7) we demonstrated that the energies of Si atom dangling bond states in the gap of $a$-Si displayed shifts in their energies that scaled with the electronegativities of impurity or alloy atoms bonded to the Si atom with the dangling bond. The origin of the scaling was shown to be related to the increased bond strength between the Si atom and its more electronegative neighbor (relative to a silicon atom for which the more electronegative atom was substituted for). Since the dangling bond state has an anti-bonding character, increasing the bonding strength to this Si atom will naturally shift the dangling bond state to higher energies within the pseudogap of the $a$-Si host. In the case of the $a$-Si based materials all of the dangling bond states in question were well within the pseudogap region, lying at least 0.3 eV below the bottom of the conduction band. In both Si₃N₄ and SiO₂, our calculations, as well as those of RP in Ref. 8 and LJC in Ref. 9 yield Si atom dangling bond states are near the conduction band edge. However, in spite of the closeness to the conduction band edge, these dangling bond defects states still display the same trends as we previously reported in the $a$-Si alloys. Specifically the substitution of a more electronegative atom (oxygen) for a nitrogen atom in Si₃N₄ shifts the dangling bond state up in energy, while, the substitution of a less electronegative atom, nitrogen for oxygen in SiO₂ shifts the dangling bond defect state down in energy.

The Si-Si defect states are near mid-gap in both Si₃N₄ and SiO₂ and the shifts in the positions of these states with the respective oxygen and nitrogen atom substitutions parallel what we have found for the dangling bond states (see Table II). The Si-Si states have a bonding, rather than an anti-bonding character as do the dangling bond states, so that the result we have found does not simply derive from the same physics that we discussed above.

We have also found that nearest neighbor hydrogen atoms substituted for nitrogen in Si₃N₄ or oxygen in SiO₂ always shift defect states downward in energy. This is expected for the dangling bond defects, since it parallels what we have previously reported for $a$-Si alloys. The behavior for the Si-Si bonding group is consistent with the other substitutions that we have discussed above, oxygen for nitrogen in Si₃N₄

and nitrogen for oxygen in $SiO_{2}$, and reinforces the trends observed, but does not add insight in the physics that is operative for the chemically induced shifts of the Si-Si bonding state.

## V. DISCUSSION

There are two aspects of this work that we will now discuss. The first relates to the relevance of this study to oxide and nitride films for gate insulators and/or passivation layers for semiconductors. The second relates to the scaling of local bonding properties with relative electronegativities. We have shown that oxygen atoms in $Si_{3}N_{4}$, and nitrogen atoms in $SiO_{2}$ can produce qualitatively similar effects on states near, or in the forbidden gap. Specifically, isolated impurity atoms will introduce localized nonbonding states near the top of the valence band which may play a role in hole transport and trapping phenomena. In this context, the nitrogen atom defects in $SiO_{2}$ introduce states that are higher in energy than the host oxygen atom nonbonding $p$ states, and these nitrogen defect states are then expected to be active in hole trapping and transport phenomena. The oxygen atom defects in $Si_{3}N_{4}:O$ produce a band of states between the host nitrogen bonding and nonbonding $p$ states, and as such these states are not expected to play nearly as important a role as the nitrogen states in $SiO_{2}:N$. We have also shown that the two types of Si atom native bonding defects, Si atom dangling bonds and Si-Si bonds, are also sensitive to changes in the local bonding chemistry of the Si atom or atoms involved the defect local atomic bonding configuration in $SiO_{2}:N$ and $Si_{3}N_{4}:O$. In addition to the substitutions discussed above, we have found that dangling bonds and Si-Si bonds with near neighbor SiH groups are also shifted deeper into the gap, and therefore can be active as deep trapping centers.

Most plasma-enhanced CVD processes have employed capacitive excitation of the reactive gases, and have provided a substrate for film deposition within the plasma glow region. $^{1}$ This in turn has restricted the molecular species which can be used in the PECVD process: e.g., for the deposition of $SiO_{2}$ this has favored the use of $N_{2}O$ as the oxygen atom source, and has restricted the use of pure $O_{2}$. These restrictions, and other aspects of plasma phase chemistry have lead to the incorporation of nitrogen and hydrogen (as SiH and OH) impurities in $SiO_{2}$ and oxygen and hydrogen (as SiH and NH) impurities in $Si_{3}N_{4}$. It is quite possible that the impurity induced defect states that we have identified in this study play a role in these materials and are in part responsible for some of the problems encountered in trying to use these materials as gate insulators for III-V semiconductor FET's. $^{12}$ We believe that the relatively high densities of Si-H bonds in these materials, coupled with the native Si atom defects, may in effect be the chemical bonding defects responsible for the poor behavior of these conventional PECVD films as gate insulators. In contrast we have developed a deposition technique that effectively precludes the incorporation of oxygen and hydrogen (as SiH) in $Si_{3}N_{4}$, and nitrogen and hydrogen (as SiH) in $SiO_{2}$ and have been successful in using these insulators in a trilayer structure for (In,Ga)As FET's. $^{4,5}$ These insulators do not contain measurable densities of SiH bonds, as do those produced by the capacitive reactor techniques. We believe that these differences in impurity atom content, as well as the use of a trilayer structure are both important aspects of the success we have achieved in developing stable gate insulators for the (In, Ga)As FET. $^{4,5}$

In previous studies of the frequencies of Si-H vibrations in $a$-Si alloys, $^{11}$ and of the energies of the Si $2p$ core states, $^{10}$ we have shown that scaling of these properties with relative electronegativities was a useful concept. In previous papers we had extended these ideas to the energies of dangling bond states in $a$-Si alloys with various impurity atoms, $^{6,7}$ and in this paper, we have shown that similar considerations apply to Si atom dangling bond defect states, and Si-Si bond defect states in $SiO_{2}$ and in $Si_{3}N_{4}$. The trends in the dangling bond energies derive from the same type of physics as discussed in Refs. 6 and 7, whereas we cannot offer a simple physical explanation for the trends in the Si-Si bond states. Finally, this paper represents the first attempt to quantify effects on electronic states associated with chemical impurities and native bonding defects in deposited $Si_{3}N_{4}$ and $SiO_{2}$ films. As such it answers some questions about these defects, but at the same time raises other questions that we are currently studying.

Note added in proof: we have made additional tight-binding calculations for SiH and SiOH bonding groups in $SiO_{2}$, and SiH and SiHN bonding groups in $Si_{3}N_{4}$. We find that in both instances the SiH groups produce a localized state about 1 to 2 eV above the top of the valence band edge. As noted earlier in this paper, the proximity of SiH groups to Si atom dangling bonds and Si-Si bonds serves to shift both of these native bonding states deeper into the gap (see Table II). At this point we conjecture that local bonding configurations in $SiO_{2}$ that involve either a Si atom dangling bond or Si-Si bond, and a nearest neighbor SiH group may be one of the more important defect clusters in thermally grown $SiO_{2}$ layers on crystalline Si, contributing to the so-called $Q_{SS}$ defect states. We have found that SiOH groups in $SiO_{2}$, and SiNH groups in $Si_{3}N_{4}$ do not produce localized states within the respective forbidden energy gaps. However, as in the case of the other groups studied in this paper, they shift the energies of Si atom dangling bonds or Si-Si bonds if they are nearest neighbors to either of these defect groups.
