## ABSTRACT

The defect properties of multilayer two-dimentional (2D) Ruddlesden-Popper Lead Iodine perovskite including the transition energy levels, the formation energy, and the self-compensation of defects have been investigated by utilizing the density functional theory (DFT). The achieved results indicate that the transition energy levels of certain dominant defects (Iᵢ and Vᵢ) on the surface are deeper than those of the same type of defects inside of multilayer 2D perovskite, which may reduce the carrier concentration resulting in the decrease of charge mobility. This research provides a very meaningful reference for understanding the properties of 2D perovskite and thus improving its device performance.

## 1. Introduction

Since an early report of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on lead halide perovskite materials in 2009 [1], perovskites have attracted much attention due to their excellent optoelectrical merits [2–9], including desirable band gap [5], high absorption coefficient [6], low exciton binding energy [7], ultralong charge carrier diffusion length [8], and unusual defect tolerance [9]. In general, two-dimentional (2D) Ruddlesden-Popper perovskite is more stable than three-dimentional (3D) perovskite [10–12], which has aroused wide concern among researchers. And great efforts have been made so far on the investigation of band structure and electronic properties of 2D-layered organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite [13–16]. Noteworthily, the stability of 2D Ruddlesden-Popper perovskite and the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of corresponding PSCs are closely related to the number of perovskite layers (n). The smaller number of 2D perovskite layers, the more stable it is, but the PCE of corresponding PSC devices is lower [17–24]. One reason is the poor optical property of low-layer 2D perovskite, including large bandgap [19], narrow absorption range, large exciton binding energy [11], and difficult exciton dissociation [23]. Another reason comes from the electrical property, i.e., the macroscopic conductance of low-layer 2D perovskite is low on account of the specific orientation of grain growth and the anisotropic charge carrier transport [25–28]. Besides, the intrinsic defect property plays an important role in the macroscopic conductance of low-layer 2D perovskite. The shallow defects can provide free holes or electrons and thus increase the carrier concentration of perovskite, but the deep defects perform as a recombination center that can cause carrier recombination [29,30].

Noteworthily, the defect properties of semiconductors including perovskite materials can be studied by density functional theory (DFT), which can give a reasonable explanation of experimental data [31]. For example, the defect calculation result of 3D CH₃NH₃PbI₃ indicates that deep defects and nonradiative recombination centers are hard to be formed [32]. The defect level of vacancy Vᵢ in CsSnI₃ is deeper than CH₃NH₃PbI₃ and the deeper defect level makes the emission spectrum red shift [33]. The defect of Vᵢ in CsGeI₃ can form a Ge-Ge dimer acting as a recombination center, which can induce the free charge carrier to recombine in CsGeI₃ PSCs [34]. For 2D perovskite, researchers have studied the defect properties of single-layer 2D perovskite (CH₃NH₃)₂Pb(SCN)₂I₂ via DFT [35], and the case that the defect level of vacancy V_SCN is deeper than Vᵢ has been discovered. Despite these achievements, there are few theoretical studies on the defects property of multi-layer 2D perovskite (n = 2, 3), and relevant exploration is urgently needed for the advancement of 2D PSCs.

## 2. Computational methods

First-principles DFT calculations were performed by using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) in this work [36]. The projector-augmented wave (PAW) potentials [37] were used to describe the electron-ion interactions and the electron exchange-correction function was described by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) parametrized by Perdew – Burke – Ernzerhof (PBE) [38]. The cutoff energy was set to 500 eV. The initial crystal structures of perovskite were taken from literature [39], and the distances between the two Pb-I frameworks are 13.79 Å, 19.67 Å and 25.98 Å for single layer (n = 1), double layer (n = 2) and triple layer (n = 3) 2D perovksites, respectively. The atomic positions were fully optimized until the forces on the atoms are below 0.05 eV/Å. The convergence threshold for self-consistent-field (SCF) iteration was set at 10<sup>−6</sup> eV. A 4 × 4 × 1 Monkhorst – Pack k-grid was employed for the geometry relaxation and SCF simulations of 2D perovskite, and a 6 × 6 × 6 Monkhorst – Pack k-grid was used for the cubic CH<sub>3</sub>NH<sub>3</sub>PbI<sub>3</sub>. The density of states (DOS) and partial DOS (PDOS) for 2D/3D perovskite were calculated by the Heyd – Scuseria – Ernzerhof (HSE06, 45% HF) hybrid functional, in which the effect of spin-orbital coupling (SOC) was also taken into consideration.

For defect calculations, we first built a supercell without defect as shown in Fig. 1a (we have repeated experiments in the literature [32], and the supercell was 3 × 3 × 3 in the literature). Then, we made a defect based on the supercell, and the structures were optimized again. During this step, the lattice constants were fixed as the supercell, because the actual defect concentration is much lower than that in our calculation, and the change of lattice constants caused by the defect is limited. For MAPbI<sub>3</sub> (CH<sub>3</sub>NH<sub>3</sub>PbI<sub>3</sub>), there are 12 possible point defects, including vacancies (V<sub>MA</sub>, V<sub>Pb</sub> and V<sub>I</sub>), interstitial (MA<sub>i</sub>, Pb<sub>i</sub>, and I<sub>i</sub>), cation substitutions (MA<sub>Pb</sub> and Pb<sub>MA</sub>) and antisite substitutions (MA<sub>I</sub>, Pb<sub>I</sub>, I<sub>MA</sub> and I<sub>Pb</sub>). Vacancies refer to certain atom missing in the supercell. The interstitial defect refers to certain atom filling in the position of the interstitial space, and the interstitial space of MAPbI<sub>3</sub> is the octahedral space at the face-center position as shown in Fig. 1b. The cationic substitution defect means that a kind of cation is substituted by another kind of cation. The antisite substitution defect refers to the substitution of one kind of ion by the opposite charged ion. The structures of all 12 possible point defects of MAPbI<sub>3</sub> are shown in Fig. 1c to 1n.

In this research, we find that a large supercell size of 3 × 3 × 1 for 2D perovskite (we built a bigger 4 × 4 × 4 supercell for 3D perovskite, and the reasons are discussed later) is required to obtain reliable results. In the supercell calculation, we use GGA/PBE without SOC at the Gama point.

### 3.2. The transition energy levels

As for the semiconductor materials used in solar cells, defects play an important role in determining the carrier transport, the carrier concentration, and the carrier recombination. The so-called intrinsic point defect refers to the type of defect caused by the local disorder of its own atomic arrangement, without other impurity ions. For perovskite containing octahedral structural units, there are 12 possible point defects, including vacancies ($V_M$, $V_{Pb}$, and $V_I$), interstitial ($M_i$, $Pb_i$, and $I_i$), cation substitutions ($M_{Pb}$ and $Pb_M$), and antisite substitutions ($M_I$, $Pb_I$, $I_M$, and $I_{Pb}$). Vacancies refer to defects caused by the absence of atoms at a certain position in the perovskite crystal lattice, such as the Pb vacancy expressed as $V_{Pb}$. The interstitial defect refers to a defect caused by the misplaced atom filling in the position of the interstitial space between the atoms. In the crystal lattice of perovskite, there is a relatively large octahedral space formed by two M atoms and four I atoms, which may cause interstitial defects, such as Pb interstitial defects denoted as $Pb_i$. A cationic substitution defect means that a different cation is substituted, for example, Pb replaces M and occupies the position of the original M, and it is represented by $Pb_M$. The antisite substitution defect refers to the substitution of the oppositely charged ion, for example, Pb substitutes I and the position of the original I is occupied by Pb, as represented by $Pb_I$.

The properties of point defects can be obtained by using the developed defect calculation model[31]. The defect system is modeled by putting a defect at the center of a supercell. For charged defect, a background charge, either positive charge or negative charge, is added to keep the global charge neutrality. The methodology was performed similar to that reported in the literature which is on the defect properties of two- dimensional perovskite with a DFT study[35].

The transition energy level is the energy level where the gains and losses of electron. Defects with the transition energy level close to VBM are called acceptors, defects with the transition energy level close to CBM are called donors, and defects with the transition energy level close to the middle of band gap are recombination center. The donor donates an electron into the conduction band and form a free electron, while the acceptor captures an electron from the valence band and leave a free hole in valence band. To estimate the transition energy levels and for- mation energy of defects, the total energy $E(\alpha, q)$ of a supercell con taining the defect $\alpha$ with charge q has been calculated. We also calculate the total energy of 2D and 3D perovskite without a defect (host), as well as the total energies of Pb, I, and M at their stable phases. From these quantities, the defect formation energy $\Delta H(\alpha, q)$ as a function of the Fermi energy $E_{F}$ and the atomic chemical potentials $\mu_{i}$ can be described as:

$$
\Delta H(\alpha, q)=\Delta E(\alpha, q)+n_{P b} \mu_{P b}+n_{I} \mu_{I}+n_{M} \mu_{M}+q E_{F} \tag{1}
$$

$$
\mathrm{where} \Delta E(\alpha, q)=E(\alpha, q)-E(\text { host })+n_{P b} E(P b)+n_{I} E(I)+n_{M} E(M)+q \varepsilon_{V B M}(\text { host })
$$. $\varepsilon_{VBM}$(host) refers to the VBM of the host. E(Pb), E(I), and E(M) are the energy of constituent Pb, I, and M, and n is the number of elements. The transition energy level $\varepsilon_{\alpha}(q / q')$ of defect $\alpha$ is the Fermi energy $E_{F}$ at which the formation energy $\Delta H(\alpha, q)$ with charge q is equal to the formation energy $\Delta H(\alpha, q')$ with charge q', and $\varepsilon_{\alpha}(q / q')$ can be described as:

$$
\varepsilon_{\alpha}\left(q / q^{\prime}\right)=\left[\Delta E(\alpha, q)-\Delta E\left(\alpha, q^{\prime}\right)\right] /\left(q^{\prime}-q\right) \tag{2}
$$

The calculated transition energies of these intrinsic defects are shown in Fig. 3. The donor-like defects are $M_{i}, Pb_{M}, V_{I}, Pb_{I}, M_{I}$ , and $Pb_{I}$ , and the acceptor-like defects are $I_{i}, M_{Pb}, V_{M}, I_{M}$ , and $I_{Pb}$ . For 3D perovskite, the calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $M_{i}, Pb_{M}$ , $V_{I}$ , and $M_{I}$ are at $-0.022 eV, 0.015 eV, 0.025 eV$ , and $0.004 eV$ below the CBM, which are very shallow donor level and are able to produce high electron density at room temperature. The calculated (0/1-) transition energy levels of $I_{i}, M_{Pb}, V_{M}$ , and $V_{Pb}$ are at $0.013 eV,-0.011 eV,-0.040$  $eV$ , and $-0.076 eV$ above the VBM, which are very shallow acceptor level and can produce high hole density at room temperature. There arefour types of defects, $Pb_{i}, Pb_{I}, I_{M}$ , and $I_{Pb}$ , producing deep levels $(0.637$  $eV, 0.736 eV, 0.538 eV$ , and $0.476 eV$ ) in the band gap. The defects with deep levels are typically nonradiative recombination centers, which can directly affect the performance of solar cell. And the calculated (0/1-) transition energy levels of $I_{M, LA}$ and $I_{M, LC}$ are $-0.007 eV$ and $0.007 eV$ above the VBM. Besides, the calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $Pb_{M, LA}$ and $Pb_{M, LC}$ are $0.028 eV$ and $-0.012 eV$ below the CBM. The calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $Pb_{I, LA}, Pb_{I, LB}$ , and $Pb_{I, LC}$ are $0.140 eV, 0.113$  $eV$ , and $0.131 eV$ below the CBM. For other defects, $V_{Pb}$ and $M_{Pb}$ are shallow defects, but $I_{Pb}$ presents as deep defect.

For 2DL3, there are four types of atomic layers (LA, LB, LC, and LD), and all defects have multi-position. The calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $V_{I, LA}, V_{I, LB}, V_{I, LC}$ , and $V_{I, LD}$ are $-0.003 eV, 0.126 eV$ , $-0.012 eV$ , and $0.031 eV$ below the CBM. The calculated $(0 / 1-)$ tran sition energy levels of $I_{i, LA}, I_{i, LB}, I_{i, LC}$ , and $I_{i, LD}$ are $0.127 eV, 0.009 eV$ , $-0.013 eV$ , and $0.022 eV$ above the VBM. The calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $Pb_{i, LA}, Pb_{i, LB}, Pb_{i, LC}$ , and $Pb_{i, LD}$ are $0.268 eV$ , $0.123 eV, 0.048 eV$ , and $0.037 eV$ below the CBM. And the calculated $(0 /$ 1-) transition energy levels of $I_{Pb, LB}$ and $I_{Pb, LD}$ are $0.051 eV$ and $-0.150$  $eV$ above the VBM. Defects as $V_{M}, V_{Pb}, M_{Pb}, I_{M}$ , and $Pb_{M}$ show a little difference of energy levels between different positions. The calculated $(0 / 1-)$ transition energy levels of $V_{M, LA}$ and $V_{M, LC}$ are $-0.028 eV$ and $-0.007 eV$ above the VBM. As well, the calculated $(0 / 1-)$ transition energy levels of $V_{Pb, LB}$ and $V_{Pb, LD}$ are $-0.076 eV$ and $-0.089 eV$ above the VBM. The calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $M_{i, LA}, M_{i, LB}$ , $M_{i, LC}$ , and $M_{i, LD}$ are $-0.039 eV,-0.038 eV,-0.027 eV$ , and $-0.037 eV$ below the CBM. The calculated $(0 / 1-)$ transition energy levels of $M_{Pb, LB}$  and $M_{Pb, LD}$ are $-0.023 eV$ and $-0.011 eV$ above the VBM. The calcu lated $(0 / 1-)$ transition energy levels of $I_{M, LA}$ and $I_{M, LC}$ are $0.038 eV$ and $0.067 eV$ above the VBM. And the calculated $(0 / 1+)$ transition energy levels of $Pb_{M, LA}$ and $Pb_{M, LC}$ are $0.020 eV$ and $-0.016 eV$ below the CBM.

In a word, for 2D perovskite, the transition level of certain shallow defects on LA or LB (such as $V_{I, LB}$ and $I_{i, LA}$ ) is deeper than that of the same type of defects on LC or LD. At the same time, shallow defects in 2D perovskite are deeper than that of the same type of defects in 3D perovskite. Because 2D perovskite exhibits quantum-confinement effect in a direction perpendicular to Pb-I framework that makes the CBM moves up and the VBM moves down. This situation is similar to the defect property of semiconductor quantum dots and bulk semi- conductors [45]. The transition level of shallow defects become deeper may cause the reduction of carrier concentration and affect the charge transport ability of material. In the experiment, the deeper defect level of 2D perovskite is also discovered by the transient absorption mea- surement [46].

### 3.3. The formation energy

Hence, in order to understand whether the defects mentioned above are easy to form or not and the self-compensation properties of defects, we have calculated the formation energies with different chemical potentials of the constituent elements. According to the thermodynamic equilibrium growth conditions, the existence of 2D and 3D perovskite should satisfy

$$2\mu_{M}+\mu_{Pb}+4\mu_{I}=\Delta H(M_{2}PbI_{4})=-8.16eV \tag{3}$$

$$3\mu_{M}+2\mu_{Pb}+7\mu_{I}=\Delta H(M_{3}Pb_{2}I_{7})=-13.26eV \tag{4}$$

$$4\mu_{M}+3\mu_{Pb}+10\mu_{I}=\Delta H(M_{4}Pb_{3}I_{10})=-18.51eV \tag{5}$$

$$\mu_{M}+\mu_{Pb}+3\mu_{I}=\Delta H(MPbI_{3})=-5.09eV \tag{6}$$

Where $\mu_{i}$ is the chemical potential of the constituent element in its stable phase and $\Delta H(M_{2}PbI_{4})$, $\Delta H(M_{3}Pb_{2}I_{7})$, $\Delta H(M_{4}Pb_{3}I_{10})$, and $\Delta H$ (MPbI₃) are the formation enthalpy of M₂PbI₄, M₃Pb₂I₇, M₄Pb₃I₁₀, and MPbI₃. In order to avoid producing the possible secondary phases of PbI₂ and MI, the chemical potential should be restrained as:

$$\mu_{M}+\mu_{I}<\Delta H(MI)=-3.09eV \tag{7}$$

$$\mu_{Pb}+2\mu_{I}<\Delta H(PbI_{2})=-1.97eV \tag{8}$$

M₂PbI₄ are stable in the area, in which the chemical potentials of Pb and I satisfy Eq. (3), (7), and (8). M₃Pb₂I₇ are stable in the area, in which the chemical potentials of Pb and I satisfy Eq. (4), (7), and (8). And M₄Pb₃I₁₀ are stable in the area, in which the chemical potentials of Pb and I satisfy Eq. (5), (7), and (8). Besides, MPbI₃ are stable in the area, in which the chemical potentials of Pb and I satisfy Eq. (6), (7), and (8).

The electrical conductivity of semiconductors is determined by the formations and compensations of charged defects, which depends on both the Fermi level position and the chemical potential. Therefore, the formation energies of defects are calculated as a function of Fermi level position at three representative chemical potential points, A (I-rich/Pb-poor), B (moderate), and C (I-poor/Pb-rich). The phase diagrams of 2D1L, 2D2L, 2D3L, and 3D perovskites are shown in Fig. 4.

For 2D1L perovskite, at chemical potential point A, the Fermi level is pinned at 0.18 eV above the VBM that is determined by the formation of Vₘ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ as shown in Fig. 5a, which indicates that 2D1L perovskite grown under this condition should exhibit p-type conductivity. At chemical potential point B, the Fermi level is pinned at 0.67 eV above the VBM by Vᵢ and Vₘ. In this case, the 2D1L perovskite should still exhibit a moderate p-type conductivity. At chemical potential point C, 2D1L perovskite should be either intrinsic or slightly n-type, as the Fermi level is pinned at 1.14 eV above the VBM by Vᵢ and Vₘ.

For 2D2L perovskite, at chemical potential A, the Fermi level of the semiconductor is 0.06 eV higher than VBM, which is determined by two vacancy defects Vₘ, ʟᴀ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ as shown in Fig. 5b, indicating the 2D2L perovskite grown under this condition exhibit p-type conductivity. At chemical potential B, the Fermi level is also determined by vacancy defects Vₘ, ʟᴀ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ, which is 0.57 eV higher than VBM. At chemical potential C, 2D2L perovskite exhibits a slight n-type conductivity because the Fermi level is 1.05 eV higher than VBM.

For 2D3L perovskite, in the growth environment of chemical potential point A, the position of the Fermi level of the semiconductor is 0.01 eV higher than VBM as shown in Fig. 5c, which is determined by the vacancy defects of Vₘ, ʟᴀ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ, indicating the 2D3L perovskite is a p-type semiconductor under this growth condition. At chemical potential point B, the Fermi level is also determined by the vacancy defects of Vₘ, ʟᴀ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ, which is 0.51 eV higher than VBM. In this case, the 2D3L perovskite performs as a medium p-type semiconductor. At chemical potential point C, the 2D3L perovskite exhibits a slight n-type conductivity because the Fermi level is 0.99 eV higher than VBM that is determined by the same vacancy defects (Vₘ, ʟᴀ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ) mentioned above.

For 3D perovskite, in the growth environment of chemical potential point A, the Fermi level of the semiconductor penetrates into VBs as shown in Fig. 5d, which is determined by the formation of Vₚᵦ, indicating that 3D perovskite formed under this growth condition is a p-type semiconductor. At chemical potential point B, the Fermi level is determined by the formation of Vₚᵦ and Vᵢ, with a position of 0.39 eV above the VBM. In this situation, 3D perovskite exhibits a moderate p-type conductivity. In the growth environment of chemical potential C, the 3D perovskite should be an intrinsic or slightly n-type semiconductor because the Fermi level is 0.87 eV above the VBM, determined by Vₘ, ʟᴀ and Vᵢ, ʟᴄ.

In general, the results showed that the formation energies of vacancy defects in 2D and 3D perovskites are relatively low because the VBM of perovskite is an antibonding orbit, and whether the perovskite is an n-type or a p-type semiconductor is determined by the vacancy defects. We can transform the n-/p-type property of perovskite by regulating the growth environment of perovskite and the relative concentrations of intrinsic defects. It is noted that the four point defects that produce deep levels—Pbᵢ, Pbᵢ, Iₘ, and Iₚᵦ—have rather high formation energies for 2D and 3D perovskites, which means these defects are hard to be formed. Vᵢ and Iᵢ have relatively low formation energies, which means the relatively high concentration of defects.

### 4. Conclusions

In summary, the defect properties, such as the orbital composition, the defect transition energy levels, the defect formation energy, the defect tolerance, and defect self-compensation of multilayer 2D Ruddlesden-Popper Lead Iodine perovskite have been investigated via DFT. Like 3D perovskite, 2D perovskite cannot form deep defects with lower formation energy, due to the similarity of electronic structure between 2D and 3D perovskites. Besides, the transition energy levels of certain defects on the surface of multilayer 2D perovskite were found to be deeper than those of the same type of defects inside, containing easy-to-form vacancy defect Vᵢ and interstitial defect Iᵢ. The deeper transition energy levels of dominant shallow defects may reduce the carrier concentration, which is largely responsible for the dissatisfactory charge transport ability of multilayer 2D perovskite. The findings play a positive role in stimulating the advancement of 2D perovskite solar cells.
