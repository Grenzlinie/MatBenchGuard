# Deep level defect luminescence in cadmium selenide nano-crystals films

V. Babentsov $^{1}$ , J. Riegler, J. Schneider, O. Ehlert, T. Nann, M. Fiederle*

Freiburg Materials Research Center (FMF), Stefan-Meier-Strasse 21, D-79104 Freiburg, Germany

Received 13 January 2005; accepted 31 March 2005
Available online 10 May 2005
Communicated by M. Schieber

## Abstract

Undoped CdSe monocrystals and CdSe nano-crystals films have been studied at various temperatures by continuous wave (cw) photoluminescence. We report on a characteristic deep level emission, which is consistently observed in the wurtzite bulk- and nanocrystalline forms of CdSe. Two broad luminescence bands, which are separated from the excitonic emission by 0.5 and 0.7eV occur in CdSe, prepared by quite different techniques. These bands experience, similar to the excitonic emission, a spectral shift to high energy enforced by the quantum confinement in nano-CdSe. The defects responsible for this luminescence are probably two different $V_{Cd}-V_{Se}$ divacancies: one is oriented along the hexagonal c-axis, the other is oriented along the basal Cd-Se bond directions.

## 1. Introduction

$II_{B}-VI$ semiconductors are well-known lumines cent materials (phosphors) and nanocrystals (NCs) represent a new generation of phosphors, bearing considerable potential for novel optoelectronic devices and light emitters. In NC phosphors, colour tuning can be enforced by quantum confinement, since the exciton radius exceeds the particle size. In particular, cadmium selenide, CdSe, $E_{g}(300K)=1.74eV\approx712nm$, is well sui ted for this purpose. In a nanocrystalline (nc) CdSe phosphor the excitonic luminescence $(E_{EX})$ can be tuned from the deep red into the visible spectral range according to Ref. [1]:

$$h\nu=E_{\mathrm{g}}+\Delta E\left(\frac{1}{R^{2}}\right).\tag{1}$$

An important issue of luminescent semiconductor materials is that of non-radiative and radiative recombination enforced by the presence of deep level defects. Extensive research in this field has been performed over almost half a century and a very detailed understanding of deep levels defects has now been reached for most bulk and thin layer semiconductors. However, for $\mathrm{II_B-VI}$ and $\mathrm{III-V}$ nanocrystals, such information is, up to now, very sparse.

In this paper we report on a characteristic deep-level luminescence, which is consistently observed in nano- and bulk CdSe crystals.

## 2. Experiment
The luminescence spectra were recorded on a grating spectrometer SpectraPro-750 (Action Research Corp.) in combination with a cooled Ge-detector (North Coast EO-817) which is well suited for registration of the deep level emission. They are not corrected for the characteristics of detector whose sensitivity falls abruptly beyond the 0.8–1.8 eV region. Measurements were performed in a variable temperature (80–300 K) cryostat. For PL excitation we used the 488 nm (2.539 eV) line of an $\mathrm{Ar^+}$ laser (0.1–100 mW) which was focused within a spot of $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ diameter.

Spherical CdSe nanocrystals (cores) were prepared according to a previously published procedure [2]. The cores were subsequently passivated with a ZnS shell of approximately 2 nm thickness. The preparation method for the shell was published elsewhere as well [3]. The samples used in this study (nc-CdSe1-4) had core diameters of, $R=3.2$; 2.0; 1.9 and 1.7 nm. Since the nanocrystals were prepared in trioctylphosphineoxide (TOPO), the particles were ligated with these molecules. Subsequently, thin films of NCs were prepared by drop casting chloroformic dispersions onto glass slides. The bulk CdSe crystals investigated were grown from the vapor phase.

## 3. Experimental results
Fig. 1 shows an example of the 80 K luminescence spectrum of the nc-CdSe sample, $R=1.9\,\mathrm{nm}$, contrasted to that of the bulk CdSe crystal.

The high energy peak in the spectrum of the nanocrystalline sample occurs at $E_{\mathrm{EX}}=2.12\,\mathrm{eV}\approx585\,\mathrm{nm}$, its full width at half maximum (FWHM) amounts to $0.16\,\mathrm{eV}\approx47\,\mathrm{nm}$. This (inhomogeneous) width is assumed to arise from the radial size distribution $\Delta R/R$ of the CdSe nanospheres.

At 80 K the bandgap of CdSe is 1.818 eV and the sharp peak at this energy, prominent in the bulk CdSe spectrum, is assigned to excitonic emission. The weak peak at 1.73 eV can be ascribed to donor acceptor (D–A) recombination, which will not be further discussed.

![](./images/812277891822780418_4.jpg)

Fig. 1. Luminescence spectrum, at 80 K, of nanocrystalline CdSe, $R=1.9\,\mathrm{nm}$, (bottom) as contrasted to that of bulk CdSe (top). Note that the intensity of the deep level defect ($E1$, $E2$) luminescence in the bulk CdSe crystal is expanded by a factor 20.

Of importance is the appearance of two broad deep level emission, labelled $E1$ and $E2$, see Fig. 1. They can already be detected in bulk CdSe, but their intensity is substantially enhanced in the nanocrystalline sample. This is a strong indication that the deep levels responsible arise not from impurity, but from intrinsic lattice defects, not unexpected, in view of the much greater surface to volume ratio in NCs.

As compared to bulk CdSe, the excitonic emission of the nc-CdSe sample is shifted to higher energy by 0.30 eV, as a result of the quantum confinement, see Fig. 1. Remarkably, this is also true for both deep level emissions $E1$ and $E2$. The deep level luminescence bands at 80 K have been decomposed into two Gaussian bands, $E1$ and $E2$ (see Fig. 1), and numerical results for bulk and nc-CdSe samples are presented in Table 1.

All of the data so far presented were obtained at 80 K. We did also investigate the deep level emission at various temperatures. In Fig. 2 are shown representative spectra of films prepared from NCs of various sizes, namely, $R=3.2$; 1.9 and 1.7 nm, recorded at 295 K. Temperature dependence of the emission for one nc-CdSe sample, $R=1.9$, is illustrated in Fig. 3. It is seen that above 150 K the deep level emission is thermally strongly quenched and the excitonic luminescence dominates.

From this we obtain $S=18$. This value is much larger that that observed for excitonic transitions in CdSe, nanocrystals, where $S<1$ is typical [1].

The microscopic structure of the defect centers responsible for the deep luminescence cannot yet be definitely established from the spectroscopic data presented, although cation-vacancy related defects can be assumed to be prime candidates.

We should mention that the occurrence of deep defect induced luminescence in nanocrystalline CdSe, prepared by quite different techniques as chemical deposition [5] or molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) [6] has been repeatedly reported in the recent literature. The same luminescence was also observed in three-dimensional close-packed solids of CdSe quantum dots [7]. This demonstrates that the defect centre responsible is very common to nc-CdSe.

Numerous investigations were focused on the fast relaxation of excitonic emission in CdSe NCs and used photomultiplier or Si detectors which are not sensitive in the IR spectral region; in these cases more deep level luminescence $E2$ was overlooked. In other cases, zinc blende CdSe nanocrystals were studied, which demonstrate only one $E1$ emission band in their spectra. Because of technical problems and obscuration by emission from the surface states (see Fig. 2), the deep level luminescence in NCs CdSe for a long time was observed as structureless emission and was related to surface defects [8].

In this work it is demonstrated for the first time that in a favourable experimental conditions the structure of a deep level luminescence can be clearly seen. These conditions are: relatively good passivated NCs (see Fig. 2), drop casting of NCs films on glass followed by evacuation of the chamber, PL measurements at low temperatures (see Fig. 3) and cw laser excitation.

In Fig. 4 we plotted the energy of luminescence peaks $E1$ and $E2$ against the corresponding excitonic energy $E_{\text{EX}}$. The data indicate a linear dependence, according to

$$
E1 = E_{\text{EX}} - (0.50-0.51)\,\text{eV}, \tag{3a}
$$

$$
E2 = E_{\text{EX}} - (0.71-0.74)\,\text{eV}. \tag{3b}
$$

Such a behaviour is expected if the $E1$ and $E2$ emissions are caused by recombination from a shallow electron level with a hole trapped at a deep acceptor state, presumably a cadmium vacancy, $V_{\text{Cd}}$, or a $V_{\text{Cd}}$- associated defect.

### 4.2. Divacancy model

We should also point out that in bulk CdSe the occurrence of deep level luminescence at 1.3 and 1.0 eV is quite frequent and has been observed already four decades ago [9]. Up to now, no firm model for the microscopic structure of the ubiquitous luminescence center could be established. However, it was suspected already in 1967 that the 1.3 and 1.0 eV defect luminescence in bulk CdSe might arise from $V_{\text{Cd}}$–$V_{\text{Se}}$ divacancy centers [9].

In fact, such assignment is strongly corroborated by the experimental results reported in this work. First of all, the divacancy model explains the omnipresence of the E1 and E2 luminescence in bulk and nanocrystalline CdSe. Isolated monovacancies, either $V_{\text{Cd}}$ or $V_{\text{Se}}$, will be present during the crystal growth process. They are mobile at elevated temperatures and, consequently, have a strong tendency to associate, either with foreign impurities or by forming nearest neighbor cation–anion divacancies, $V_{\text{Cd}}$–$V_{\text{Se}}$, which have a much higher activation energy for diffusion, as compared with the monovacancies. Consequently, cation–anion divacancies should be a dominant stoichiometric lattice defect in as-grown CdSe, and other II$_{\text{B}}$–VI compounds.

The divacancy model also explains readily the occurrence of two deep level emissions E1 and E2, as reported in this paper, since in the wurtzite lattice of CdSe there are also two different types of $V_{\text{Cd}}$–$V_{\text{Se}}$ associates: one type is oriented along the hexagonal $c$-axis, the other type is oriented along the basal Cd–Se bond directions. We will show below that these two types of divacancies become energetically different, after trapping an electron–hole pair.

### 4.3. Electronic structure

The electronic structure of the $V_{\text{Cd}}$–$V_{\text{Se}}$ divacancy center in CdSe could be scaled from that of the isolated monovacancies $V_{\text{Cd}}$ and $V_{\text{Se}}$, if such information on monovacancies, as usually obtained from magnetic resonance techniques (ESR, ODMR) would exist. However, we found no relevant work in the literature; apparently no intrinsic point defect in bulk CdSe has ever been identified by ESR or ODMR [10].

We will therefore attempt to predict some features of the $V_{\text{Cd}}$–$V_{\text{Se}}$ divacancy in CdSe from that of the cation monovacancy $\text{V}_{\text{Zn}}\text{Se}_{4}$ in ZnSe and from that of the anion monovacancy $\text{V}_{\text{Se}}\text{Zn}_{4}$ in ZnSe which have been investigated in detail.

In cubic ZnSe, the isolated cation vacancy $\text{V}_{\text{Zn}}\text{Se}_{4}$ as created by 1.5 MeV electron irradiation, was found to undergo a trigonal static Jahn–Teller distortion after trapping a hole, which is then strongly localized at only one of the four selenium ligands [11]. This is indicative of strong electron–phonon coupling, as observed for the deep acceptor involved in the E1, 2 luminescence in CdSe.

In contrast, the anion vacancy $\text{V}_{\text{Se}}\text{Zn}_{4}$ in ZnSe is expected to preserve full cubic symmetry after trapping an electron, as typical for an $\text{F}^{+}$-center,

where the electron is spread equally over the four zinc ligands around the anion vacancy [12]. This is also true for the $F^{+}$-center $V_{S}Zn_{4}$ in ZnS [13].

From these data, a tentative model for the electronic structure of the $V_{Cd}-V_{Se}$ divacancy center in CdSe can be constructed. We assume that the hole is strongly localized at only *one* of the three Se-ligands around $V_{Cd}$, as a result of the Jahn–Teller distortion. On the other hand, the electron has two possibilities of being trapped at a Cd-ligand around $V_{Se}$, as illustrated in Fig. 5. These configurations are energetically nonequivalent as a result of the different electron–hole (e–h) Coulomb attraction $U$. For the E1 configuration in Fig. 5 the e–h distance is $d = 1.667$ b, where $b =$ 0.263 nm is the mean Cd–Se bond length. For the e–h Coulomb energy we then obtain

$$
U(E1) = e^{2}/d = 0.600\ e^{2}/b = 3.281\ \text{eV}. \tag{4a}
$$

Correspondently, for the *E2* configuration in Fig. 4 the e–h distances are $d = 1.915$ b; thus

$$
U(E2) = 0.522\ e^{2}/b = 2.854\ \text{eV}. \tag{4b}
$$

![](./images/812277891822780418_8.jpg)

Fig. 5. Axial (*E1*) and basal (*E2*) divacancy $V_{Cd}-V_{Se}$ centers in hexagonal CdSe, as trapping sites for electron–hole pairs. The hole (+e) is localized at only one Se-ligand around $V_{Cd}$. For the electron (–e) either one (*E1*) or two (*E2*) trapping sites around $V_{Se}$ are seen to exist. The *E1* and *E2* configurations have different Coulomb energies, as explained in the text.

The above results assumes full tetrahedral (sp³) symmetry around a lattice point. As a result of the trigonal Jahn–Teller distortion, sp²-hybridisation of the trapped hole orbital may be more realistic. In this case we have $d = 1.805$ and 2.108 b for the configurations *E1* and *E2*, respectively, where $b =$ 0.263 nm is the mean Cd–Se bond length. Thus, in this case,

$$
U(E1) = 0.554\ e^{2}/b = 3.029\ \text{eV}, \tag{5a}
$$

$$
U(E2) = 0.474\ e^{2}/b = 2.592\ \text{eV}. \tag{5b}
$$

After electron–hole annihilation within the divacancy center a photon is created according to $hv = U(E1,E2)$. This determines the energetical position of the zero-phonon line. However, because of strong electron–photon coupling, as manifested by the large Huang–Rhys factor $S = 18$, it has practically zero intensity, $e^{-S}$. The maximum of the luminescence $hv(E1,E2)$ is then Stokes-shifted from the position of the zero-phonon line to lower energy by $Sh\omega_{\text{LO}} = 0.468$ eV. Thus, from Eqs. (4) we predict

$$
hv(E1) = U(E1) - Sh\omega_{\text{LO}} = 2.561\ \text{eV}, \tag{6a}
$$

$$
hv(E2) = U(E2) - Sh\omega_{\text{LO}} = 2.124\ \text{eV} \tag{6b}
$$

and
$$
hv(E1) - hv(E2) = U(E1) - U(E2) = 0.437\ \text{eV}.
$$

Experimentally, we found for the bulk CdSe crystal $hv(E1) = 1.30$ eV, $hv(E2) = 1.05$ eV, and $hv(E1) - hv(E2) = 0.25$ eV. These values are smaller than the theoretical prediction by factor of about 2. This discrepancy can be removed, if some dielectric screening of the (e–h) Coulomb attraction is taken into account, corresponding to an effective dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} \approx 2$, naturally much smaller than that of bulk CdSe, where $\varepsilon_{0} = 9.7$. In nanocrystal, the electronic part $\varepsilon_{\infty}$ of the static dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{0}$ will be lowered, as a result of the quantum confinement. By a

"semiempirical pseudopotential method" Wang and Zunger [14] have calculated both exciton energy and dielectric constant of CdSe quantum dots as a function of particle size, in particular also for a $Cd_{534}Se_{527}$ cluster, $R=1.923$ nm. For the exciton energy they obtained $E_{EX}=2.1$ eV, in agreement with our experimental value observed for $R=1.9$ nm CdSe nanospheres, see Table 1. Furthermore, the electronic part of the dielectric constant, $\varepsilon_{\infty}$, is lowered, as expected. For the $R=1.9$ nm quantum dot Wang and Zunger [14] predict $\varepsilon_{\infty}($ dot $)=5.5$, distinctively smaller than $\varepsilon_{\infty}($ bulk $)=6.2$. The ionic part of the dielectric constant, $\Delta \varepsilon=3.5$, can be taken to be the same for the quantum dot and for bulk crystal. Thus, $\varepsilon_{0}($ dot $)=\varepsilon_{\infty}($ dot $)+\Delta \varepsilon=9.0$ is predicted for our $R=1.9$ nm nanospheres, only slightly smaller than the bulk value of $\varepsilon_{0}=9.7$. However, it must be realized that the use of the dielectric constant will overestimate the dielectric shielding in our case. The same effect, i.e. a reduction of the Coulomb energies $U(E 1)$ and $U(E 2)$, see Eq. (5), can also result from a delocalisation of the trapped electron into more distant lattice sites around the $V_{Cd}-V_{Se}$ divacancy. This delocalisation, characteristic for a shallow electronic trap, is also evidenced by the quantum confinement shift of the $E 1$ and $E 2$ luminescence.

The success of our simple point charge model in predicting the energies is surprising and might, admittedly, be fortuitous. Therefore, further dis- cussion of this topic should await the results of a rigorous ab initio total-energy calculation, as performed for anion and cation vacancies in cubic IIB-VI semiconductors by Illgner and Overhof [12].

## 5. Conclusion

In summary, a characteristic deep level lumines- cence was consistently observed in nanocrystalline and bulk CdSe crystals. It consists of two Gaussian bands, $E 1$ and $E 2$, and demonstrate a high energy spectral shift, as enforced by the quantum confinement.

A divacancy in the wurtzite lattice of CdSe, $V_{Cd}-V_{Se}$, is tentatively proposed as the defect responsible for these luminescence bands. There are two different types of $V_{Cd}-V_{Se}$ associates: one type is oriented along the hexagonal $c$-axes, the other type is oriented along the basal Cd-Se bond directions.
