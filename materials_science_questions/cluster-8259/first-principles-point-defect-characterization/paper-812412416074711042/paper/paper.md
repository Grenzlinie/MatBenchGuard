The Schottky barriers formed by deposition of transition metals on Si surfaces are explained in terms of Fermi-level pinning by Si dangling bonds that are sheltered by vacancies at Si/transition-metal-silicide interfaces.

The deposition of a transition metal on a Si surface causes a chemical reaction that produces a metal-silicide layer, an abrupt silicon/silicide interface, and a Schottky barrier whose height, 0.55 to 0.87 eV, depends remarkably little on the transition metal [1]. Here we show that such barriers can be understood in terms of Fermi-level pinning [2] by Si dangling bonds that are "sheltered" [3] by vacancies at the Si/silicide interface. This explanation, which differs substantially from previous theories of Si Schottky barrier formation [1,4,5], unifies the understanding of Si/transition-metal Schottky barrier heights with the generally accepted model of Fermi-level pinning by native defects [6-13] at (110) interfaces between III-V semiconductors and (i) non-reactive metals [6,11,14], (ii) reactive metals [9,13], (iii) oxides [15], and (iv) other semiconductors [7].

A successful theory of Si/transition-metal Schottky barriers must provide natural explanations for the following facts: (i) The barrier heights for n-type Si, $\phi_{B,n}$, are approximately the same, 0.55 to 0.87 eV, for different transition metals [4]. (ii) There are, however, chemical trends in the variations of $\phi_{B,n}$: For the near-noble metals, we have $\phi_{B,n}(Pt)>\phi_{B,n}(Pd)>\phi_{B,n}(Ni)$ [16]. (iii) Barriers are observed to form before the completion of a metallic silicide, or even a full monolayer [1]. (iv) The barrier heights are nearly independent of silicide crystal structure. (v) Differences in silicide stoichiometry do not have a major effect on the barrier heights either: There are relatively tiny (~0.03 eV) variations among the various silicides for a given transition metal -- e.g., $Ni_2Si$, NiSi, $NiSi_2$ [16,17]. (vi) The barrier heights for n- and p-type Si very nearly add up to the band gap energy $E_g$ [18,19]:

$$
\phi_{B,n} + \phi_{B,p} \simeq E_g = 1.1 \text{ eV.} \tag{1}
$$

Furthermore a successful theory must establish a link between the Schottky barriers observed for Si at transition-metal-silicide interfaces with those observed for III-V semiconductors at non-reactive and reactive metal interfaces.

Before proposing our microscopic theory, we recall the basic idea of Fermi-level pinning [2], illustrated in Fig. 1 for the case of heavily doped n-type Si interfaced with a metallic silicide [20]. At the interface, one-electron "surface states" of some kind are assumed to exist [2]; the relevant states of the neutral surface are those that define the Fermi energy for electrons: the lowest-energy unoccupied or only partially occupied states with energies within the band gap. In principle, these Bardeen "surface states" may be either intrinsic to the interface (or free Si surface) or they may be associated with defects. The Si surface Fermi energy $E_{F}^{s}$ is "Fermi-level pinned" near the energy of the "surface states," and the barrier height is the energy of the surface conduction band edge relative to the pinning "surface states." For p-type Si, the theory is similar, with the surface Fermi level for holes being the highest electron-occupied or partially occupied state within the band gap.

A favorite candidate for the "surface states" is intrinsic interface states associated with the Si-silicide bonding [1,17]. There are difficulties with this interpretation, however: The energies of Si/transition-metal-silicide interface states depend on the transition metal, and so the Schottky barrier height can be expected to vary by ≈1 eV, depending on the transition metal, the silicide's crystal structure, and the stoichiometry -- contrary to the observations. Furthermore, the observation that the barrier heights for n-type and p-type Si add up to the band gap [18,19], Eq. (1), indicates that the surface Fermi levels for electrons and holes must be at the same energy -- that is, the surface state band gap must be zero (metallic) or nearly zero. This is contrary to experiments for the free Si surface [21], but it is not presently known if the transition-metal-silicide interface bands are all metallic or not.

It is important to realize that by changing the transition metal atom at a given site, one changes the local crystal potential by an amount of order 1 eV. Thus the many data demonstrating that the Si/transition-metal-silicide Schottky barrier heights are insensitive on a 1 eV scale to the transition metal, or to the structure or stoichiometry of the silicide, indicate that the primary physics determining the Schottky barrier height is associated with Si and not with the transition metal or the silicide. The studies [1] showing barrier formation at sub-monolayer silicide coverage (before silicide interface states can be fully formed) confirm this. Thus the data alone force us to consider a mechanism of Schottky barrier formation that is almost independent of the (silicide) metal -- a situation analogous to what has been found in the III-V semiconductors, where Fermi-level pinning by native defects is considered responsible for Schottky barrier formation [6-13].

We are therefore compelled to examine the possibility that native defects produce the "surface states" that pin the surface Fermi levels and form the Schottky barriers at Si/transition-metal-silicide interfaces. The native defects responsible for the Schottky barriers between GaAs or InP and non-reactive metals are thought to be antisite defects [11-13] -- which do not occur in homopolar Si. However InP/reactive-metal interfaces have Schottky barriers determined by In dangling bonds (or P vacancies) [13]; this suggests that Si dangling bonds may play a dominant role in Fermi-level pinning at Si/transition-metal-silicide interfaces. For the reactive systems that are under consideration here (transition-metals "eating" their way into Si), as for reactive metals on InP [9,13], a reasonable concentration of vacancies or dangling bonds near the interface seems quite likely.

In studying III-V Schottky barriers we concluded that the native defects responsible for Fermi-level pinning were "sheltered" or encapsulated by the interface [3]. We incorporate this thinking into the present work, by proposing that Si/transition-metal-silicide Schottky barrier heights are determined by Si dangling bonds that pin the Fermi level. These bonds dangle into interface vacancies, which shelter them from the adjacent metal; and they are encapsulated by interfacial transition-metal atoms so that the pinning level is almost independent of either the transition metal or the silicide stoichiometry.

For concreteness we consider here a particular example of a sheltered Si dangling bond -- the case of a missing Si bridge atom at the $Si/NiSi_{2}$ interface (Fig. 2). In this case the missing-bridge vacancy is tetrahedrally coordinated with three transition-metal atoms from the silicide and one Si atom from the Si. The Si dangling bond of this last atom dangles into the vacancy left by the missing bridge. If the vacancy in Fig. 2 were replaced by a Si atom, one would have the geometry and bonding for the $Si/NiSi_{2}$ interface determined by Cherns et al. [22]. A careful reading of the present paper will reveal that changing the transition-metal atoms, their number, or their positions will not greatly alter the pinning energy of the Si dangling bond.

Before turning to a more elaborate calculation, we first consider a very simple model calculation of the Si dangling bond defect. This model is justified by the results of the more-complete calculation described below, and clearly reveals the essential physics of the problem. In the model, only four atoms are explicitly considered -- those surrounding the Si vacancy -- and only one hybrid orbital per atom -- the $sp^{3}$ hybrid orbital oriented toward the vacancy [23].

At first we take all four atoms to be Si, simulating the bulk Si vacancy, and we construct the $A_{1}$ and $T_{2}$ states:

$$|A_{1}\rangle=(|1\rangle+|2\rangle+|3\rangle+|4\rangle)/2,$$

$$|T_{2},x\rangle=(|1\rangle-|2\rangle-|3\rangle+|4\rangle)/2,$$

$$|T_{2},y\rangle=(|1\rangle+|2\rangle-|3\rangle-|4\rangle)/2,$$

and

$$|T_2,z\rangle = (|1\rangle - |2\rangle + |3\rangle - |4\rangle)/2,$$

where $|i\rangle$ is the hybrid orbital centered on the i-th atom. In this model the $A_1$ (s-like) and $T_2$ (p-like) bulk vacancy energies with respect to the valence band maximum are

$$E(T_2,V=0) = \varepsilon_{Si} - \beta,$$

and

$$E(A_1,V=0) = \varepsilon_{Si} + 3\beta.$$

Here $\varepsilon_{Si}=\langle i|H|i\rangle$ is the Si hybrid energy. (In terms of atomic orbital energies in the solid [24,25] we have $\varepsilon=(E_g+3E_p)/4$.) The interaction between any two orbitals is $\beta=\langle i|H|j\rangle$. A numerical estimate of $\varepsilon_{Si}$ is 0.24 eV, calculated directly from atomic orbital energies in the solid; when the $A_1$ and $T_2$ vacancy levels are fit to theoretical calculations, $\varepsilon_{Si}$ lies between 0.12 and 0.42 eV, and $\beta$ lies between -0.33 and -0.45 eV [25-29]. (Our zero of energy is the valence band maximum.) The central physical point contained in this model is that the bulk Si vacancy produces a $T_2$ level in the gap (occupied with two electrons) and a doubly occupied $A_1$ level resonant with the valence band.

Secondly, we change atoms 2, 3, and 4 of Fig. 2 from Si to Ni continuously, by increasing the potential $V=\varepsilon_{Ni}-\varepsilon_{Si}$ from zero to the large positive value appropriate to Ni (V=5.15, 5.11, and 4.84 eV for Ni, Pd, and Pt [30]). This extended defect [31] simulates the environment of a Si dangling bond sheltered by a vacancy at the interface with the transition-metal silicide. The $a_1$-symmetric (σ-bonded) levels are the interesting defect states. (The other states lie high in the conduction band for large V.) Their energy levels are

$$
\begin{align*}
E_{\pm} &= \varepsilon_{Si} + \beta + (V/2) \\
&\quad \pm \left[4\beta^2+\beta V+(V^2/4)\right]^{1/2}.
\end{align*}
$$

For large positive V, these approach $V+\varepsilon_{Si}+2\beta$ and $\varepsilon_{Si}$, respectively. The level that approaches $\varepsilon_{Si}$ corresponds to the Si dangling bond deep level in the band gap.

The main point illustrated by this model is that the transition-metal atoms are very electropositive with respect to Si and drive the $T_2$ bulk Si vacancy level $(E_+)$ far up into the conduction band from the gap. At the same time they drive the $A_1$ bulk Si vacancy level $(E_-)$ up out of the valence band into the gap, where it is "deep-level pinned" [25] (not to be confused with Fermi-level pinning) to the Si hybrid dangling bond energy $\varepsilon_{Si}$. This occurs because of an approximate interlacing theorem [31]: The strong repulsive potential of the transition metals cannot move the lower $A_1$ level beyond the Si dangling bond level. Thus the wavefunction of the Si dangling bond $A_1$-derived resonance level is excluded from the region occupied by the transition-metal atoms, and this effect causes the energy of the $A_1$ level to move up into the fundamental band gap and to approach, but not cross, the dangling hybrid energy $\varepsilon_{Si}$. Hence the role of the transition metal is to supply a strong electron-repulsive potential in the vicinity adjacent to the vacancy, and to drive the Si dangling bond level up out of the valence band. Clearly, changes of stoichiometry, crystal structure, or transition metal will have little effect on the primary physics: the resulting deep level is a Si dangling bond state derived from the $A_1$ valence band resonance associated with the dangling bonds in bulk Si; its energy lies just below the dangling Si hybrid energy $\varepsilon_{Si}$, and is deep-level pinned to it.

There is a small effect on the Schottky barrier height due to differences in transition metals, because the defect potential is not infinite and the Si dangling bond does sample the transition-metal atoms a bit. For the n-Si Schottky barrier we predict $\phi_{B,n}(Pt)>\phi_{B,n}(Pd)>\phi_{B,n}(Ni)$ -- the observed chemical trend. Moreover, similar small perturbations of barrier heights due to changes of crystal structure or stoichiometry are to be expected.

What role do the d-states of the transition-metal play? These states lie well below the Si band gap, and merely serve to repel the Si dangling bond levels upward further in energy; they do not significantly alter the qualitative physics: The $A_1$-derived level is still approximately deep-level pinned to the hybrid energy $\varepsilon_{Si}$. The d-states are important in determining the conduction band states of the metallic transition-metal silicide -- however, in a Fermi-level pinning model of Schottky barrier formation, the metal does not play a significant role. In determining the charge state of the Fermi-level pinning defect level, one must take the d-electrons into account. Electron-counting [32] shows that the dangling bond level in the gap, when the defect is neutral, is occupied by a single electron. (Electron spin resonance should be able to detect such neutral defects if they occur in sufficient concentrations after Schottky barrier formation.) Therefore, for small charge-state splittings, the same energy level accounts for Fermi-level pinning in n-Si and p-Si, and Eq. (1) is obeyed. Thus the main experimental facts concerning the Schottky barriers between Si and transition-metal silicides are explained by this simple model.

The physics of the simple model has been abstracted from more detailed model calculations (Fig. 3), which treat an embedded complex of three Ni atoms and a vacancy in Si. The local environment of the Si dangling bond in this model is the same as at a $Si/NiSi_2$ interface. (Recall that experiments indicate that only the local atomic bonding is relevant to Schottky barrier formation.) The defect perturbation in this more detailed model spans thirteen atoms, and the host Hamiltonian is taken to be one specifically tailored to treat Si vacancies [26]. Details of the calculation will be presented in subsequent work. The resulting vacancy energies, as functions of V [33], are displayed in Fig. 3. The level derived from the Si $A_1$ vacancy level exhibits deep-level pinning to the 0.40 eV dangling bond level with increasing transition-metal defect potential V, as described qualitatively in the simple model. We have also performed calculations using matrix elements extracted from $NiSi_2$ band structures [34] (to be reported later) which included the Ni d states and found an inconsequential effect on the Si dangling bond state, less than 0.08 eV [35]. We conclude by mentioning an experiment that can be used to test the present theory [36]: If great care is taken to saturate the Si/silicide interface with hydrogen, the dangling Si bonds producing Fermi-level pinning should be tied up by the H atoms, and their levels should be moved out of the fundamental band gap; thus the normal Schottky barrier should be suppressed. One should bear in mind, of course, that only a few residual defects are required to achieve Fermi-level pinning.
