Exposure of heavily B-doped $(2×10^{19}-4×10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-3})$ diamond to a deuterium plasma results in the formation of passive B-D complexes which are bound with a dissociation barrier of 2.5 eV. In heavily doped material there is evidence that deeper deuterium traps exist. Using first-principles methods we show that nearest-neighbor boron pairs are bound and can efficiently trap deuterium. The calculated binding energy for the B-D pair is in good agreement with experimentally obtained values. The comparison of the measured deuterium and boron concentrations with the model suggest that boron dimers are deeper deuterium traps, but complementary infrared or Raman measurements are needed to draw absolute conclusions regarding the formation of $\text{B}_2\text{D}$ or $\text{B}_2\text{D}_2$ complexes.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Diamond as a material has been associated with a diverse range of applications ranging from mechanical roles (e.g., drill bits and wear resistant coatings) to detectors, heat sinks, and electronics. For electronic device applications there has been considerable effort in the growth of $p$- and $n$-type material, with limited success with $n$-type material which is particularly difficult to realize.

It has been shown that, in deuterium plasma treatment of boron doped diamond, deuterium atoms passivate boron acceptors, probably as a B-D complex. $^{1-6}$ An estimated value of 2 eV for the dissociation energy of these complexes has been previously deduced from the onset of dissociation of the (B,D) pairs in diamond. $^{4}$ This onset is seen through the modification of the deuterium diffusion profile characteristics as the diffusion temperature increases. Below $550\,^\circ\text{C}$, these profiles demonstrate a deuterium concentration equals to that of boron implying the formation of a B-D pair. At $600\,^\circ\text{C}$ and above, experiment shows that the deuterium diffusion profiles approach a complementary error function with the deuterium concentration well below that of boron indicating a weaker interaction between deuterium and boron as the temperature increases.

For such investigations, boron-doped $p$-type diamond epilayers were used with boron doping above $1×10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$. Strongly $p$-type diamond can be produced since concentrations as high as $8×10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ have been achieved which correspond to about 0.5 at. \%. $^{7}$ Such high concentrations make it probable that some boron will (at least accidentally) form aggregates of two or more. Assuming random statistics, the number of nearest neighbor boron pairs is given by: $^{8}$

$$
\frac{2[\text{B}]^{2}}{[\text{C}]},
$$

where $[\text{B}]$ is the total boron concentration and $[\text{C}]$ is the carbon atom site density, which can be taken to be constant $(\sim 1.76×10^{23}\ \text{cm}^{-3})$. For example, statistically one then expects of the order of $2×10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ of the boron atoms to be present in pairs for $[\text{B}]=1×10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ and $1.5×10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ of the boron to be present in pairs for $[\text{B}]=8×10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$. Such concentrations should be above the detection limit for many experimental techniques.

To our knowledge, there is no evidence of boron aggregation in diamond. However, it is well known that boron forms clusters of two or more impurities in silicon—especially neutron irradiated material. $^{9}$ In particular, substitutional boron pairs have been tentatively assigned to three infrared absorption peaks at 553, 560, and $570\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ labeled $P_{1}$, $P_{2}$, and $P_{3}$ respectively, $^{10}$ each peak corresponding to one of the three symmetric isotopic combinations of $^{10}\text{B}$ and $^{11}\text{B}$. However, recent $ab$ initio theory suggests that nearest-neighbor B pairs are not stable, with the total energy of the substitutional pair decreasing as the boron separation increases. $^{11}$ It is likely that these metastable pairs are formed by vacancies being trapped by boron interstitial dimers. $^{12}$

In this study, we report on a more accurate determination of the dissociation energy of the B-H complex from an analysis of the deuterium diffusion profiles. We study the diffusion of deuterium in strongly $p$-type boron-doped diamond where we find the existence of deep traps in addition to single boron traps. Then, we present the results of first-principles calculations of the structure, energetics and characteristics of various boron-hydrogen complexes in diamond taking also into account the possible existence of nearest-

## II. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

A series of homoepitaxial boron-doped diamond films were grown on (100) type-Ib synthetic diamond substrates by microwave plasma assisted chemical vapor deposition at $T=820{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ with $4 \%$ methane in hydrogen. The total pressure was 30 torr. The boron doping was achieved by adding diborane into the gas phase. For the present study, the structures consisted of a $0.5 \mu \mathrm{m}$ undoped diamond layer and a 1.5-2 $\mu \mathrm{m}$ thick diamond epilayer doped with boron in the concentration range $2 \times 10^{19}-4 \times 10^{20} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$.

On these samples, we first diffused deuterium at $410{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for a duration short enough to give a diffusion depth of typically 40-50 nm. At this low diffusion temperature deuterons are strongly trapped by ionized boron acceptors and do not dissociate. As a consequence the deuterium concentration matches very well the boron concentration. Using this preloaded deuterium as an internal source of deuterium, we have performed thermal annealing experiments under high vacuum in order to diffuse the deuterium deeper into the bulk. The temperatures we have investigated were in the range $550{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ [which corresponds to the onset of dissociation of $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ pairs] to $710{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ (which is just below the $730{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ onset of deuterium out-diffusion for diamond $^{4}$ ). As a consequence, we stay in a diffusion regime where the deuterium dose remains constant during diffusion.

Figure 1 presents the preloaded deuterium profiles and the deuterium diffusion profiles after increasing time of thermal annealing at $711{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for diamond epilayers doped with 1.3 $\times 10^{20} \mathrm{~B}-\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$. Beyond some accumulation of deuterium in the near surface region, deuterium clearly diffuses deeper in the samples. The accumulation region is attributed to deuterium trapped on defects resulting from the preloading deuterium plasma step.

Zundel and Weber have performed a careful analysis of the hydrogen diffusion regimes in crystalline semiconductors taking into account that a part of hydrogen is trapped by ionized dopants during its diffusion. $^{13}$ As a consequence, hydrogen are present as free species $[\mathrm{H}]$ and as trapped species $[\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H}]$. The total hydrogen concentration $\left[\mathrm{H}_{T}\right]$ is the sum of $[\mathrm{H}]$ and $[\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H}]$. Under local equilibrium conditions and under the assumption that $[\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H}] \ll\left[\mathrm{N}_{A}\right]$, the diffusion equation of total hydrogen can be written: $^{13}$

$$
\frac{\partial\left[\mathrm{H}_{T}\right]}{\partial t}=D_{\text {eff }} \frac{\partial^{2}\left[\mathrm{H}_{T}\right]}{\partial x^{2}},\quad (1)
$$

where the effective diffusion coefficient of hydrogen is given by

$$
D_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{D_{i}}{1+\frac{4 \pi R D_{i} N_{A}}{\nu}},\quad (2)
$$

where $D_{i}$ is the intrinsic diffusion coefficient of $\mathrm{H}^{+}, R$ the capture radius, $N_{A}$ the boron concentration, and $\nu$ the dissociation frequency of $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ complexes.

At very high temperatures, i.e., in the case where $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ interactions are negligible, $D_{\text {eff }}$ is equal to $D_{i}$. However, at lower temperatures, $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ interactions occur during the hydrogen diffusion and a regime exists where its diffusion is entirely controlled by the trapping-detrapping mechanism. In other words, the transit time of a free proton between two next-nearest-neighbor traps is negligible compared with the time the hydrogen remains trapped on the boron acceptor. This regime corresponds to the situation where $4 \pi R D_{i} N_{A} \nu^{-1} \gg 1$. In this case, the effective diffusion coefficient is independent of the intrinsic diffusion coefficient $D_{i}$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\nu}{4 \pi R N_{A}},\quad (3)
$$

with

$$
\nu=\nu_{0} \exp \left(-\frac{E_{\text {diss }}}{k_{B} T}\right),\quad (4)
$$

where $\nu_{0}$ is the attempt frequency and $E_{\text {diss }}$ the dissociation energy of $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ complexes.

In our diffusion experiments, the initial condition is a quasi-$\delta$ function of deuterium present in the near surface region. For annealing temperatures below $730{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, the deuterium dose remains constant since no out-diffusion occurs. Under annealing at temperatures above $550{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ which is the onset of $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ dissociation, $(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H})$ pairs in this region will dissociate and hydrogen will diffuse deeper in the bulk so that the condition $[\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{H}] \ll[\mathrm{B}]$ will be rapidly fulfilled. For a given deuterium dose M present in the quasi-$\delta$ function, the solution of Eq. (1) is given by the Gaussian function

$$
\left[\mathrm{H}_{T}\right](x, t)=\frac{M}{\sqrt{\pi D_{\mathrm{eff}} t}} \exp \left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4 D_{\mathrm{eff}} t}\right),\quad (5)
$$

Reporting these values in Eqs. (3) and (4), one can calculate the dissociation energy of the (B,H) complexes if we know the capture radius $R$.

For low doped semiconductors, the capture radius is usually calculated from the condition that the average thermal energy $k_{B}T$ equals the attractive potential energy $V(r)$ between the protons and the ionized acceptors

$$
k_{B}T=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}R}.\qquad(6)
$$

In the present case, protons diffuse in a boron-doped diamond where the boron concentration is well above the total hydrogen concentration. Moreover, at the considered diffusion temperatures, a significant fraction of the boron acceptors is ionized giving rise to a large concentration of free holes. In these conditions, the simple Coulomb potential must be replaced by a screened Coulomb potential and R is the solution of the equation $^{15}$

$$
k_{B}T=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}R}\exp\left(-\frac{R}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}k_{B}T/q^{2}p}}\right),\qquad(7)
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the dielectric constant of diamond ($\varepsilon=5.7$) and $p$ the free hole concentration at the diffusion temperature $T$.

For $[\text{B}]=1\times10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, the thermal ionization energy of boron acceptors is rather low so that we can consider that $p\approx N_{A}$ at $711\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. In this case, we deduce a value of $7\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ for $R$ from expression (7). For $[\text{B}]=2\times10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, the ionization energy of boron is about $0.28\ \text{ev}.^{16}$ For a non compensated sample, the free hole concentration at $563\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ is $\approx2$ $\times10^{18}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$. In this case, $R=20\ \mathring{\text{A}}$.

## III. THEORETICAL CALCULATIONS

### A. Method

All calculations were carried out using the local-densityfunctional technique as implemented in AIMPRO (ab initio modeling program) (Refs. 24,25). To model the various defects, cubic supercells of side length 2 or 3 lattice constants, containing 64-216 host atoms have been used. All calculations, including the defect cells, have been performed using the Monkhorst-Pack $^{26}$ scheme for sampling the Brillouin zone with a mesh of $2 \times 2 \times 2$ special $k$ points.

Norm-conserving pseudopotentials $^{27,28}$ enabled core electrons to be eliminated, except for $\mathrm{H}$ atoms where the bare Coulomb potential is used. The wave-function basis consists of independent sets of $s, p$, and $d$ Gaussian orbitals with four different exponents, sited at each $\mathrm{C}$, or $\mathrm{B}$ site, and the $\mathrm{H}$ atoms are treated using four independent sets of $s$ and $p$ Gaussians. The charge density is Fourier transformed using plane waves with a cutoff of $300 \mathrm{Ry}$. The lattice constant and bulk modulus of diamond using these bases are within $\sim 1-5 \%$, respectively of the experimental values, while the direct and indirect band gaps are close to previously published plane-wave local-density approximation values $^{29}(5.68$ and $4.18 \mathrm{eV})$.

Several important physical quantities can be obtained directly from these calculations. The formation energy $E^{f}(\mathrm{X}, \mathrm{q})$ of a defect $X$ in charge state $q$ made up from atoms with chemical potentials $\mu_{i}$ is given by $^{30}$

$$
E^{f}(X, q)=E(X, q)-\sum \mu_{i}+q\left(E_{v}+\mu_{e}\right)+\chi(q). \quad(8)
$$

Here $E(X)$ is the total energy of the supercell containing the defect, and the sum is over all atoms in the supercell. $E_{v}$ and $\mu_{e}$ are the valence-band top and electron chemical potentials, respectively. Finally, $\chi(q)$ is a term correcting for the electrostatic and multipole interactions due to the periodic nature of the calculation. $^{31}$ Typically one ignores the multipole terms and simply adds the Madelung energy of a lattice of point charges given by

$$
\chi(q)=\frac{q^{2} \alpha}{L \varepsilon},
$$

where $\alpha$ is the Madelung constant for the cubic lattice (1.4618), $L$ is the length scale of the lattice ( $2 a_{0}$ for the 64 atom cell), and $\varepsilon$ is the static dielectric constant (5.7 for diamond). For a $q=1$ this yields a correction of $\sim 0.5 \mathrm{eV}$, but this represents the energy for an array of point charges, and as such must be viewed as a rather crude correction. The acceptor levels for defects can be obtained by evaluating the value of $\mu_{e}$ for which $E^{f}(X, q)=E^{f}(X, q-1)$. The value taken for $E_{v}$ can be taken from the Kohn-Sham band structure, or evaluated from the ionization energy of a bulk lattice cell $^{32}$

$$
E_{v}=E(\text { bulk }, 0)-E(\text { bulk }, 1) .
$$

When using the band structure, the valence-band width (VBW) is taken from the neutral pure diamond cell. Then, for a given defect the band structure is evaluated at $\Gamma$ and the defect valence-band minimum evaluated $\left(E_{\text {min }}^{d}\right)$. Then

$$
E_{v}=E_{\min }^{d}+\text { VBW. }
$$

Binding energies can be evaluated from the difference in formation energies so that the binding energy of complex $A$ made up from defects $B$ and $C$ can be written as

$$
E^{b}(A)=E^{f}(B)+E^{f}(C)-E^{f}(A),
$$

which will not depend on the chemical potentials of the impurity species, but only on the supercell total energies and the carbon chemical potential. This method effectively estimates the energy required to separate the constituents to infinity, and one therefore has to take care in choosing the correct equilibrium forms (e.g., charge states) of $B$ and $C$. Where charged defects are involved, the inadequacies of the periodic approach lead to additional uncertainties in the values of $E^{b}$ that are hard to quantify, but are probably of the order of a few tenths of an electron volt. An alternative method for calculating the binding energy compares the total energies of the complex and their separated forms within a supercell. This has the advantage for neutral complexes that no total energies of charged cells are required, but typically this method results in defects that are relatively close together due to the limitations in practical cell sizes. Both methods are used in this study.

### B. Theoretical results
We have considered structures made up either one or two B atoms and up to two hydrogen atoms. We shall refer to these structures using the notation $B_nH_m$, where $n\geqslant m$, $n$ being the number of B atoms and $m$ the number of hydrogen atoms.

#### 1. Structure and binding energies of boron and B-H complexes
The boron acceptor was relaxed in the neutral and negative charge states. As found previously, $^{34}$ in the neutral charge state the defect undergoes a distortion from tetrahedral to trigonal $(C_{3v})$ symmetry. Three of the B-C neighbors are located $1.58$ Å, and the unique C neighbor is located at $1.55$ Å. In the negative charge state the B atom is located on-site with four equivalent C neighbors at $1.56$ Å. The small differences in structure between charge states is consistent with the delocalized wave function expected for this effective mass acceptor. There is evidence for an off-site distortion from Raman and Zeeman data. $^{35}$

The addition of a H atom to substitutional B yields a strongly distorted bond-centered structure for the H, with a $\angle$ BHC much smaller than $180^\circ$, being just $87^\circ$ in the current calculations, with the B-H separation being $1.18$ Å in both 64 and 216 atom supercells. The structure is shown schematically in Fig. 5(b). The bonding nature of this defect has been discussed in detail elsewhere. $^{34}$

The binding energy of the H atom to the boron acceptor can be evaluated using Eq. (12), which is then
$$
E^b(\text{BH})=E^f(\text{B}_s)+E^f(\text{H})-E^f(\text{BH}).
$$

Here, from Eq. (8) the formation energies of $B_1H_1$, $B_s$ and $H_{BC}$ in the 64 atom cells are given by
$$
E^f(\text{BH})=E(\text{BH})-\mu_{\text{B}}-\mu_{\text{H}}-63\mu_{\text{C}},\tag{13}
$$
$$
E^f(\text{B}_s)=E(\text{B}_s)-\mu_{\text{B}}-63\mu_{\text{C}},\tag{14}
$$
and
$$
E^f(\text{H})=E(\text{H})-\mu_{\text{H}}-64\mu_{\text{C}}.\tag{15}
$$

All atomic chemical potentials cancel except for $\mu_{\text{C}}$, which is taken as the energy per atom of bulk diamond. This yields a binding energy of 4.0 eV. As mentioned in Sec. III A, to model the physically correct reaction, the binding energy is evaluated using the charged constituents, viz. $({\text{B}-\text{H}})^0\rightarrow\text{H}^++\text{B}^-$. This yields a lower binding energy of 2.7 eV, of which around 1.1 eV arises from the Madelung terms [Eq. (9)]. The fact that $E^b$ is lower for the charged species is a reflection of the relative positions of the H donor level and the B acceptor level. Repeating the same calculations using a 216 atom cell yields binding energies of 3.9 and 2.7 eV for neutral and charged species, respectively, i.e., we conclude that the binding energies evaluated in this way are converged with respect to cell size. For the larger cell only around 0.7 ev arises from the Madelung correction. We have also investigated the binding energy by moving the H atom to bond centers separated from the B site: for the case where the H and B sites are separated by the maximum distance possible in a 64 atom cell the total energy is around 1.9 ev above that of the B-H complex, whereas for a 216 atom cell with a maximum separation the energy difference is 2.2 eV.

It should be noted that the Madelung correction [Eq. (9)] is an upperbound to the electrostatic interaction of charged cells, since is arises from point charges, and the binding energy of 2.7 eV might then be considered as an upper limit. The energy increase in moving the B and H apart within a cell single unit does not require the Madelung correction because of charge neutrality, and one notes that the values derived this way are smaller (coincidentally in agreement with experiment for the 216 atom cell). However, the distance between B and H sites even in the larger cell is relatively short, and will underestimate the energy required to fully separate the impurities. Therefore, the binding energy is therefore calculated to lie somewhere between 2.2 and 2.7 eV.

Typically the dissociation energy of a complex is given by the sum of the binding energy ($\leqslant$2.7 eV) and the migration energy ($\sim$0.2 eV), the calculated dissociation energy is therefore $\leqslant$2.9 eV. The migration energy is taken from static calculations of the motion of $\text{H}^+$ through the lattice. $^{34}$ This estimate assumes that there is not a larger barrier to move the H within the first few hops, which need not be the case. However, we have examined the first step using constrained minimization method. In this approach the atoms are moved along a vector joining the start and finish strucutres for the diffusion steps $\vec{v}$. At each stage all atoms are relaxed but constrained to move only in the plane perpendicular to $\vec{v}$. We have moved the H atom from the equilibrium site to a

nearby bond center, and at least within this approximation there is no additional barrier to dissociation.

### 2. Binding of B-B pairs
We next turn to the boron pair. We have relaxed the boron pair in a 64 atom cell where the boron atoms are at either nearest- or next-nearest-neighbor sites. In the neutral charge state the boron pair is at the lowest energy where the B atoms are nearest neighbors, with the next-nearest neighbor pair being around 0.2 eV higher in energy. The B atoms relax apart with a B-B separation of $1.94\ \mathring{\text{A}}$. The six equivalent B-C backbonds are within 1% of the bulk C-C bond lengths, but form an angle of around only $101^\circ$ with the $\langle 111 \rangle$ axis.

Charging the B pair destabilizes the nearest-neighbor pair, so that in the $-1$ and $-2$ charge states the energies are lowered by Coulomb repulsion of around 0.4 and 1.2 eV, respectively, when the B ions are separated by one C atom. However, if the pairs are formed in the neutral charge state where the nearest-neighbor pair is bound, then the properties of the boron-dimer will be those of the nearest-neighbor pair. Using Eq. (12) yields a binding energy for neutral $\text{B}_2$ of 0.7 eV relative to two separated, neutral $\text{B}_s$ centers. This binding energy is small in comparison to, for example, the nearest- neighbor nitrogen pair in diamond at around $4\ \text{eV}.^{36,37}$ This reflects the fact that the binding of boron pairs arises mainly from the elastic energy (the B-C bond lengths drop from 1.55 and $1.57\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ in $\text{B}_s$ to $1.52\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ in the pair), whereas for the nitrogen-pair, although there is an elastic term, a considerable contribution arises from the lowering of the occupied gap states (i.e., this is substantially an electronic effect).

Adding one or two electrons to the neutral nearest- neighbor $\text{B}_2$ defect leads to a partial and complete reconstruction, respectively, of the bond between the two B atoms. The B-B separations are 1.73 and $1.60\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ with bond angles of $105^\circ$ and $109^\circ$ for the $-1$ and $-2$ charge states, respectively.

### 3. Binding of di-boron-hydrogen complexes
We have decorated the boron pair with one or two hydrogen atoms. The lowest energy structure for $\text{B}_2\text{H}_1$ is shown in Fig. 5(c). The structure resembles that of the bond-centered hydrogen in bulk diamond in the positive charge state, $^{34}$ in that the hydrogen atom lies in a puckered bond-centered structure. The energy is around 0.9 eV higher when the H atom is on the $\langle 111 \rangle$ axis. The axial and puckered structures have B-H bond lengths of 1.1 and $1.2\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, respectively, with B-B-C bond angles of $99^\circ$ and $103^\circ$, respectively. $\angle\text{BHB}$ is calculated to be $102^\circ$. The ground-state structure can be understood as $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{B}_2^-$, since theory predicts that $\text{H}_{\text{BC}}^+$ adopts the puckered-bond-centered structure. $^{34,38}$ Adding an electron to form $(\text{B}_2\text{H}_1)^-$ results in a small perturbation to the geometry with the B-H bonds becoming fractionally shorter. We also examined antibonding structures which all had higher energies.

The binding energy of the first H atom to the neutral nearest-neighbor boron pair is calculated to be 3.8 and 3.3 eV from neutral and charged sources, respectively. The latter represents a binding energy 0.6 eV greater than that of the $\text{B}_1\text{H}_1$ relative to $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{B}_s^-$. A further possibility arises when one considers the probable electronic activity of $\text{B}_2$ (see Sec. III B 4). As implied above, this defect is likely to be a double acceptor and this suggests a possible reaction
$$(\text{B}_2)^{2-}+\text{H}_{\text{BC}}^+\rightarrow(\text{B}_2\text{H})^-.$$

This reaction is likely to be the case where there is a substantial excess of hydrogen donors, yielding a binding energy of 4.9 eV relative to $\text{B}_2^{2-}$ and $\text{H}^+$ dissociation product, a factor of around 2 greater than the binding energy of $\text{B}_1\text{H}_1$ (2.2–2.7 eV, Sec. III B 1).

This defect has overall $C_{2h}$ symmetry and is lower in energy than structures made up from antibonded H, or where the symmetry is lowered by perturbing the H atoms. As with the $\text{B}_2\text{H}_1$ structure, the puckered-bond-center configuration can be understood to come from that of the positively charged interstitial H configuration. The binding energy for this defect can be calculated in three significant ways. First one might want to know the binding of $\text{B}_2\text{H}_2$ relative to $2\times\text{B}_1\text{H}_1$, and second relative to $\text{B}_2\text{H}_1$ and bond-centered hydrogen. Finally, since the lowest energy form of two H atoms in diamond has been found to be the $\text{H}_2^*$ complex,we have also calculated the binding energy relative to $\text{B}_2$ and $\text{H}_2^*$. The results are summarized in Table I. The binding energy (2.7 eV) of the second H atom $(\text{H}_{\text{BC}}^+)$ to $(\text{B}_2\text{H}_1)^-$ is the same as the binding energy calculated for $\text{B}_1\text{H}_1$. The dissociation into two separated $\text{B}_1\text{H}_1$ complexes cost less energy, but the barrier to such a process is likely to be larger than the loss of a single H atom, since typically more bonds are disrupted in migration processes involving substitutional species than interstitial ones. For example, the motion of substitutional nitrogen is associated with a $5\ \text{eV}$ barrier. $^{39}$

Both structures for $\text{B}_2\text{H}_1$ and $\text{B}_2\text{H}_2$ have some precedence in molecular systems. The diborane molecule $(\text{B}_2\text{H}_7)^-$ has a bonding very close to that of $\text{B}_2\text{H}_1$, and is consistent with the idea that the $\text{B}_2\text{H}_1$ in diamond is an acceptor. $\text{B}_2\text{H}_6$ has a similar structure bridging the two B atoms although the coordination of the boron atoms in this molecule differs from that of $\text{B}_2$ in diamond. The H- and D-related modes for B₁H₁ using AIMPRO in a cluster geometry have been published previously.³⁴ However the B-related modes were not. We therefore have recalculated the modes for this center. The results are listed in Table II. The spring constant calculated for the interaction between the H and B atoms are very similar from the earlier calculation, which is reflected in the similarity of the high-frequency modes. However, the coupling between the H atom and the nearest C neighbor is calculated to be much smaller than previous one, resulting in a shift of the H modes by some 470 cm⁻¹. There are previously unreported B-related modes slightly higher in frequency to those of the B₂ defect. There is also evidence for a low-frequency hydrogen-related mode with peaks around 110 and 130 cm⁻¹. This mode of vibration represents the motion of the H/D atom between the two nearest C atoms. For completeness we have also calculated the vibrational modes for isolated Bₛ in the neutral and negative charge states, but none lie above the Raman frequency, as expected since the Bₛ absorption band lies at 1290 cm⁻¹.⁴⁰

The modes for the B₂Hₘ, $m{=}1,2$ are listed in Tables III and IV for the various isotopic combinations. For B₂H₁ in the negative charge state an additional mode is found when compared to the neutral defect. This arises from a B-H bend mode at around 1868 cm⁻¹, and is present due to a strengthening of the B-H bonds. For both charge states of B₂H₁, and the B₂H₂ defect, there is also evidence for the ¹⁰B related mode just above the Raman frequency. As with other complexes, there is evidence for resonant modes, with peaks around 150, 175, and 200 cm⁻¹ related to hydrogen, and modes in the 350–500 cm⁻¹ range localized mainly on the B atoms (c.f., Fig. 8).

In summary, all B₂Hₘ complexes that we have examined are expected to give rise to Raman and infrared active modes above the Raman frequency, exhibiting isotopic fine structure that could be used to unambiguously identify the defects experimentally. Since heavily boron doped material is optically opaque, Raman spectroscopy seems the more promising technique. Furthermore, it should be noted that we have not estimated the effective charges of IR-active oscillations, and it has been previously shown that many H–related modes are effectively invisible due to their small absorption coefficient.³⁴

### IV. CONCLUSIONS

Gaussian-like deuterium diffusion profiles have been found in boron doped diamond ($[\text{B}]{=}2{\times}10^{19}{-}1.3$ ${\times}10^{20}$ cm⁻³) in conditions where the deuterium dose is kept constant. From an analysis of the effective diffusion coefficient in a regime controlled by trapping-detrapping, we have deduced a dissociation energy of (B,H) pairs of 2.5 eV. In more highly boron-doped diamond ($[\text{B}]{=}4{\times}10^{20}$ cm⁻³), the deuterium concentration exhibits a plateau around 6 ${\times}10^{18}$ D-cm⁻³, then a steep decrease. This originates from additional hydrogen deep traps which was of unknown origin. Our work suggest that one serious candidate for these deep trap is a boron pair at nearest-neighbor sites.

Using $ab$ initio methods, we have calculated a dissociation energy between 2.4 and 2.9 eV for the B-D pair, in good agreement with the experimental value. We have also shown

The nearest-neighbor boron pair possesses a deeper acceptor leVel, $\sim E_{v}+1.1$ eV, than the single acceptor. It may also possess a second acceptor level around midgap. The binding energy of di-boron-hydrogen complexes represents a deeper trap for hydrogen than that of the isolated boron acceptors. The boron pair traps two hydrogen atoms, the second being less strongly bound than the first.

For $4×10^{20}$ B-cm⁻³, the boron dimer concentration is estimated at $2×10^{18}$ cm⁻³, which is expected to trap 2 $×10^{18}$ cm⁻³ (B₂D₁) or $4×10^{18}$ cm⁻³ (B₂D₂) deutrium, while a plateau at $5×10^{18}$ D-cm⁻³ is measured. This suggests that in these experimental conditions deuterium diffusion is controlled by boron dimer traps with formation of B₂D₂ or may be of B₂D₁ complexes. Complementary IR and/or Raman measurements are required to discriminate between these two different complexes, as all di-boron-related defects studied in this paper give rise to characteristic local vibrational modes that are potentially the key to experimental identification of these centers. The singly hydrogenated boron pair behaves in a fashion very similar to the isolated boron acceptor.
