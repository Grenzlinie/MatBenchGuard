PHILOSOPHICAL MAGAZINE B, 1994, VOL. 69, No. 2, 359-376

# Light-induced metastable changes in amorphous silicon nitride

By H. FRITZSCHE and YOSHIKAZU NAKAYAMA†

The James Franck Institute, University of Chicago, Chicago, Illinois 60637, USA

[Received 18 June 1993 and accepted 23 August 1993]

## ABSTRACT
Light-induced metastable increases in subgap absorption have been studied in semi-insulating amorphous silicon nitride as a function of illumination temperature between 100 and 300 K. These metastable absorption changes can be partially bleached by subgap light. The metastable increase in subgap absorption is related to neutralization of charged dangling-bond defects. Thermal relaxation of the metastable state is modelled by phonon-assisted hopping of carriers between neutral defects. We explain the persistent photoconductivity of amorphous silicon/silicon nitride multilayers with metastable hole trapping by defects in the nitride layers. We explore the origin of the charge state of dangling-bond defects in silicon nitride and conclude that the origin is more likely potential fluctuations than a negative effective correlation energy. The similarity of light-induced metastable changes in other materials suggests an origin that is common to semi-insulating amorphous alloys.

## §1. INTRODUCTION
Essentially all amorphous semiconductors undergo changes in their physical properties when they are exposed to bandgap light. Many of these changes are reversed by annealing. In chalcogenide glasses, it has been found that the electron-hole pair producing light can induce three distinct metastable effects (Tanaka 1991):

(1) an increase in the concentration of native defects (these native defects are valence alternation defects which have a negative effective correlation energy $-U$); in equilibrium these defects are charged and diamagnetic;
(2) a metastable change in the charge of these $-U$ defects which makes them neutral and paramagnetic;
(3) a reversible change in structure.

In contrast, in hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si: H) and some of its alloys, one finds that bandgap light induces only an increase in the concentration of native defects. These defects are silicon dangling bonds (threefold coordinated silicon atoms) which have a positive effective correlation energy $+U$. The difference between the behaviours of these two classes of amorphous materials has been attributed to the difference between their bondings and in particular, to the presence of non-bonding p electrons in chalcogenide glasses.

During the past few years, evidence has been mounting which suggests that hydrogenated amorphous silicon nitride (a-SiN$_x$: H) contains $-U$ native defects (Curry *et al.* 1990, Warren *et al.* 1992b). These are silicon dangling bonds just as in a-Si: H but back bonded to nitrogen atoms. They are called K centres (Krick, Lenahan and Kanicki 1988, Lenahan and Curry 1990) to distinguish them from the D-centre

dangling bonds of a-Si: H. Exposure of a-SiN$_x$: H to bandgap light produces many of the metastable phenomena which are associated with process (2) in chalcogenide glasses, a change in the charge state of their native defects. This evidence, which includes electron spin resonance measurements (Kanicki *et al.* 1991), the observation of infrared bleaching of the metastable effects in a-SiN$_x$: H (Seager and Kanicki 1990, Crowder, Tober and Kanicki 1990), capacitance measurements (Curry *et al.* 1990) and charge trapping experiments (Warren *et al.* 1992b) indicates that the light-induced metastable changes in a-SiN$_x$: H originate from changes in the charge of the native K-centres defects, and not from an increase in their concentration.

However, a negative sign of the effective correlation energy is not necessary to explain a metastable neutralization of pre-existing charged defects. Alternatively, there might be potential fluctuations caused by structural and compositional heterogeneities in a-SiN$_x$: H. When these fluctuations are larger than the positive correlation energy, then the majority of $+U$ midgap defects will be positively and negatively charged in equilibrium (Branz and Silver 1990). Light exposure will render them neutral and paramagnetic and produce a number of metastable effects which are indistinguishable from those attributed to $-U$ centres. In the present work, we explore evidence for this alternative.

Some years ago we observed in multilayers, which consist of alternating ultrathin layers of amorphous silicon and silicon nitride, a light-induced metastable increase in dark conductivity which could be as large as a factor $10^5$ at room temperature (Hamed and Fritzsche 1991). This persistent photoconductivity (PPC), as it is called, continues for days at 300 K with only a slow decay. Annealing near 460 K restores the original low-conductivity state. Several models have been proposed to explain the PPC effect, which has been observed also in multilayers of a-Si: H with other wide-gap amorphous materials such as amorphous silicon oxide and carbide (Hamed *et al.* 1991a). Most recently it was suggested that light exposure creates metastable defects in the insulating layers, a-SiN$_x$: H or a-SiO$_x$: H, which raise the Fermi level in the multilayer and thus cause the excess PPC conductance. These metastable defects were considered by these workers to have a positive correlation energy similar to the D centres in a-Si: H (Hamed, Fritzsche and Kohler 1991b, Deng, Hamed, Fritzsche and Tran 1991).

In this work we explore whether $-U$ defects or potential fluctuations with $+U$ defects prevail in a-SiN$_x$: H and whether the PPC effect observed in a-Si: H/a-SiN$_x$: H multilayers can be explained by either of these models. For this purpose we have been studying the creation and anneal kinetics of the metastable effects in the same a-SiN$_x$: H materials that were used in the a-Si: H/a-SiN$_x$: H multilayers which showed the large PPC. The metastable effect studied here is a light-induced increase in subgap absorption measured by photothermal deflection spectroscopy (PDS) (Jackson, Amer, Boccara and Fournier 1981). The subgap absorption is a good measure of changes in either the concentration or the charge state of defects in the gap.

### §2. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

The a-SiN$_x$: H films about $1\,\mu$m thick were deposited on Corning 7059 glass substrates at 510 K in a 13·6 MHz glow discharge chamber. The reactive gases were mixtures of $\text{SiH}_4$ and $\text{NH}_3$ with composition ratios $R=[\text{NH}_3]/[\text{SiH}_4]=2$ and 4. The film with $R=4$ is the same as the layer used for the high performance a-Si: H/a-SiN$_x$: H multilayers (Hamed and Fritzsche 1991). The pressure in the chamber was 100 mTorr. The optical gap $E_{04}$, defined as the photon energy at which the absorption coefficient is

$\alpha=10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, is $E_{04}=2.7 \mathrm{eV}$ for the $R=2$ and $E_{04}=3.3 \mathrm{eV}$ for the $R=4$ a-SiN$_{x}: \mathrm{H}$ film at $300 \mathrm{~K}$.

The optical absorption coefficient in the subgap spectral region was measured using the PDS method (Jackson *et al*. 1981). For these measurements, the sample is mounted on a stainless steel sample holder that fits snugly into a quartz cuvette that holds the deflection medium $\mathrm{CCl}_{4}$. In order to assure good reproducibility the sample was kept on the holder at all times, even during annealing and illumination. For the studies of the creation of metastable defects the samples were first annealed at $500 \mathrm{~K}$ for $90 \mathrm{~min}$ to eliminate the effects of previous light exposure and then sequentially illuminated for specific periods of time in a cryostat using filtered and focused light from a $300 \mathrm{~W}$ tungsten-halogen lamp $(360 \mathrm{~nm}<\lambda<800 \mathrm{~nm})$. In order to study the annealing of the metastable defects the $E_{04}=2.7 \mathrm{eV}$ sample was illuminated with a filtered tungsten- halogen lamp and the $E_{04}=3.3 \mathrm{eV}$ sample with a $100 \mathrm{~W}$ low-pressure mercury lamp $(\lambda>280 \mathrm{~nm})$, until the changes in subgap absorption approached saturation. The samples were then isothermally annealed for specific periods of time. In both studies the samples were quickly brought back to room temperature and measured without delay by PDS. This was done within $1 \mathrm{~h}$, a time short compared with the decay of the change in subgap absorption at room temperature. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that some less stable defects, which were created at low temperatures, annealed within this $1 \mathrm{~h}$ delay and thus were not detected. In addition, the photobleaching effect was investigated using filtered light from the tungsten-halogen lamp having a spectral range of either $540 \mathrm{~nm}<\lambda<800 \mathrm{~nm}$ or $600 \mathrm{~nm}<\lambda<800 \mathrm{~nm}$.

## §4. Discussion

Our results on light-induced metastable changes in subgap absorption of a-SiN$_x$:H agree in all major respects with those of other workers. We find a metastable increase in absorption in a wide energy range $1\,\mathrm{eV}<h\nu<2.4$ or $3.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ respectively for our samples. Bleaching of the metastable effects by subgap light occurs at 300 K in the $E_{04}=$ $3.3\,\mathrm{eV}$ sample. This suggests that the metastabilities are the result of a light-induced disequilibration of charges among pre-existing defects rather than due to creation of new metastable defects as is believed is the case in a-Si:H.

Our results raise several issues which we would like to address in the following. The first is the question whether a negative effective correlation energy $-U$ of the K defects or potential fluctuations are the cause for the observed effects. We then try to explain the observations of persistent photoconductivity in a-Si:H multilayers containing insulating layers of a-SiN$_x$:H and of other silicon alloys. Finally we discuss the kinetics of creation and annealing of the light induced changes in subgap absorption.

### 4.1. $-U$ defects or potential fluctuations

For defects having a negative effective correlation energy the charge transfer reaction

$$2\mathrm{K}^{0} \to \mathrm{K}^{-}+\mathrm{K}^{+} \tag{5}$$

is exothermic. The defects are therefore charged and diamagnetic in thermal equilibrium. The observation that between 10 and 20% of the defects yield a spin signal in the dark annealed state (Kanicki *et al.* 1991) indicates that neutral paramagnetic $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ defects coexist with charged defects. This should not happen if eqn. (5) is exothermic and if $-U$ is larger than $kT$ and the spread in the distribution of $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ energies. The value of $-U=0.9\,\mathrm{eV}$ which has been suggested (Kanicki *et al.* 1991, Chen *et al.* 1992) would rule out the presence of paramagnetic $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ centres in thermal equilibrium.

Moreover, doping of semiconductors having $-U$ defects should not cause a change in conductivity because the Fermi energy remains pinned between the $\mathrm{K}^{-}$ and $\mathrm{K}^{+}$ energy levels until the dopant concentration exceeds the K-centre concentration. Dunnett, LeComber and Spear (1988) reported, however, a significant increase in the 300 K conductivity of nitrogen-rich a-SiN$_x$:H films with lithium doping. Unfortunately they did not report whether the lithium concentration incorporated was larger than $5 \times 10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, the concentration of K defects in undoped silicon nitride.

Finally, the similarity of the light-induced changes in the subgap absorption of a-SiO$_x$:H (fig. 7) and of a-SiN$_x$:H (fig. 2) suggest a similar origin for both. We cannot rule out the possibility that $-U$ defects exist in both materials but wish to explore an alternative and perhaps more plausible explanation in the following.

We start by recalling some recent thinking about the dependence of $E_{\text{opt}}$ on $x$ (Robertson 1993) which is illustrated in fig. 1. The composition $x=1$ (according to the scale of Maeda and Umezu 1991a, b) divides the slow rise of the optical gap at small $x$ from a very steep rise at larger $x$. The Tauc parameter $B$ in eqn. (1) passes through a minimum and rises rapidly at larger $x$. This striking change in behaviour at low and high nitrogen concentrations is related to a change in the character of the valence band and in the dominant covalent bonds (Guraya *et al.* 1990, Maeda and Umezu 1991a, Robertson 1993) in the following manner. Below $x<1$ the valence and conduction bands originate from Si-Si bonding and antibonding states. $E_{\text{opt}}$ increases slowly with increasing $x$ as stronger Si-N bonds replace Si-Si bonds and the hydrogen concentration increases.

At high nitrogen contents, say $x>1.4$ the valence band is formed by the $\mathrm{p}_{z} \pi$ lone-pair orbitals of nitrogen bonded by three $\mathrm{sp}^{2}$ hybrid bonds to silicon and hydrogen. It is interesting to note that $E_{\text{opt}}$ or other properties of a -SiN$_{x}$: H do not show any special behaviour as $x$ passes through $x=1.33$, the stoichiometry of $\mathrm{Si}_{4} \mathrm{~N}_{4}$. The reason is the very high hydrogen content of $30-40$ at. $\%$ at this composition which pushes stoichiometry to $x>1.5$ where $E_{\text{opt}}=5.3 \mathrm{eV}$.

In the range $1 \cdot 1<x<1 \cdot 5$ the average optical gap increases from $2 \cdot 7$ to $4.5 \mathrm{eV}$ and the Tauc parameter $B$ in eqn. (1) passes through a deep minimum (Ibaraki and Fritzsche 1984, Hasegawa, Matuura and Kurata 1986, Maeda and Umezu 1991a). In this composition range, major changes in bonds occur that involve large differences in bond energies and electronegativities; strong N-H bonds replace Si-H, and strong Si-N bonds replace Si-Si. The antibonding states of Si-N become the conduction band, and the valence band starts to be formed by the $\mathrm{p}_{z} \pi$ lone-pair orbitals of planar nitrogen.

We are dealing with a ternary alloy of silicon, nitrogen and hydrogen. The strong dependence of the effective bandgap on average composition at large nitrogen concentrations indicates that local composition fluctuations will lead to sizeable bandgap variations. The compositional fluctuations may be further enhanced over their random values by local strain relief mechanisms. Potential fluctuations will then arise from charge transfer between regions of different compositions. These fluctu- ations are partially screened by the charging of gap states near $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ such as the $\mathrm{K}$ centres. If the $\mathrm{K}$ centres have a positive correlation energy $+U$ which is less than half the peak to peak fluctuations of the potential, then most $\mathrm{K}$ centres become $\mathrm{K}^{+}$and $\mathrm{K}^{-}$with a certain fraction remaining $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ near the zero crossings of the potential fluctuations (Branz and Silver 1990). The widths of the inner core excitation peaks measured by X- ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Guraya *et al.* 1990) permit potential fluctuations of about $1 \mathrm{eV}$.

This would explain the coexistence of paramagnetic and diamagnetic defect centres in equilibrated a-SiN$_{x}$: $\mathrm{H}$ films. Compositional heterogeneities and spatial fluctuations in the bandgap would also explain the unusually broad tailing of the subgap absorption spectra and the similarity between alloys such as a-SiO$_{x}$: $\mathrm{H}$ and a-SiN$_{x}$: $\mathrm{H}$.

Bandgap fluctuations may also be the reason for the decrease in $B$ defined by eqn. (1). Ugur, Johanson and Fritzsche (1985) carried out effective-medium calculations of the optical parameters of silicon/silicon nitride multilayers. They found a similar decrease in the effective-medium $B$ to a value $B_{\mathrm{c}}=400 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1 / 2} \mathrm{eV}^{-1 / 2}$ as the nitride-to- silicon ratio was increased to unity. In fig. 1, one can see that, after passing through a minimum, $B$ increases again at large $x$ where $E_{\text{opt}}$ reaches a constant value of $5.3 \mathrm{eV}$. Here the bandgap fluctuations should vanish as the gap becomes dominated by nitrogen lone-pair states and Si-N antibonding orbitals. The N-H bonding and

antibonding states are expected to lie outside the gap. Compositional fluctuations, however, can still yield potential fluctuations even when the bandgap fluctuations cease at large $x$.

Capture of electrons and holes excited by bandgap light will produce a metastable increase in paramagnetic neutral $K^{0}$ centre (Branz and Silver 1990). Changes in subgap absorption are expected to result not only from the increase in $K^{0}$ centres but also from resulting increases in potential fluctuations which, in equlibrium, were partially screened by the charged centres. Bleaching of the light-induced excess absorption by subgap light and the capacitance measurements can be explained equally well by the potential fluctuation model. Both this and the $-U$ model agree that the metastable state results from a change in charge of pre-existing defect centre.

### 4.2. Persistent photoconductivity
Very large PPC effects are observed in amorphous silicon layers sandwiched between nitride layers. The effect is largest when the silicon layer thickness is small (about 2 nm) and the nitride thickness exceeds 50 nm. Similar PPC effects were observed when the silicon nitride was replaced by layers of a-SiO$_{x}$: H or a-SiC$_{x}$: H (Deng *et al.* 1991). The increase in the magnitude of the PPC effect with the insulator thickness and the association of the PPC anneal temperature with the insulator material (Hamed *et al.* 1991a) suggest that the metastable changes occur in the insulating layers and not in the silicon layers. Hamed and Fritzsche (1991) estimated that an increase in dark conductivity by a factor $10^{5}$ at 300 K corresponds to an increase of $3 \times 10^{15} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ electrons in the silicon layers 2.0 nm thick. This corresponds to an exchange of $6 \times 10^{8} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$ charges. These excess carriers were accumulated after a 1000 s light exposure which produced about $10^{20} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ electron-hole pairs in the silicon layers. At that time, Hamed and Fritzsche (1991) attributed the low efficiency of creating the metastable PPC state to the low probability of creating new metastable dangling-bond defects in the silicon nitride layers by hopping injection of the photoexcited carriers. On the other hand, such hopping injection can also alter the charge state of pre-existing defects in the nitride layers which may result in a small excess positive charge which then is balanced by the excess negative charge which produces the PPC in the silicon layers. A light induced positive charge in silicon nitride layers was also observed by Kanicki *et al.* (1990). The slow decay of the PPC effect at 300 K results then from the relative isolation of the strongly localized K centres in the silicon nitride. This model assumes a metastable charge trapping and therefore cannot distinguish between $+U$ and $-U$ defects.

The light-induced increase in subgap absorption of a-SiN$_{x}$: H and the PPC effect of silicon/silicon nitride multilayers seem to result from the same metastable effect. They both anneal near 460 K and are created with similar light exposures. There is, however, one significant difference. The PPC in silicon nitride multilayers can be excited with undiminished efficiency at 4.2 K (Hamed *et al.* 1991a, b) while the efficiency of changing the subgap absorption decreases by a factor of nearly ten between 300 and 100 K according to fig. 3. This difference requires some further study. Warren *et al.* (1992a) found that the creation of $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ centres in nitrogen-rich films is independent of temperature at least down to 100 K in agreement with our PPC results.

### 4.3. Creation and anneal kinetics
The slow accumulation of the metastable increase in subgap absorption shown in fig. 3 and of the changes in spin density or flat-band voltage shifts observed by Kanicki

et al. (1990) occur as a result of a large number of electron-hole pair generation and recombination events. Without understanding the competing recombination mechan- isms, which do not give rise to metastable changes, it is not possible to interpret or model the observed creation kinetics or its temperature dependence. The stretched- exponential parameters $\beta=0.64$ and $\tau=2000 \mathrm{~s}$ at $300 \mathrm{~K}$ observed by us for the $E_{04}=3.3 \mathrm{eV}$ sample agree well with the results of Kanicki et al. (1990) for an $E_{04}$ $=5.5 \mathrm{eV}$ sample.

We now turn to the annealing of the light-induced metastable changes shown in fig. 4. These anneal curves resemble those obtained by Tober, Kanicki and Crowder(1991) on a-SiN $N_{1.6}:$ H films except that the latter exhibited two distinct components: one that annealed with a stretched-exponential relaxation process and another that required a considerably higher anneal temperature. We also observe a faster and a somewhat slower component.

If the metastable state corresponds to a neutralization of lower-energy charged defects, then relaxation involves the motion of charge from one to another $K^{0}$ defect. The second very slow relaxation may be a metastable non-random remnant of the initial metastable charge distribution, which is left after the preferential recombination of closer $K^{0} K^{0}$ pairs (Bishop and Searle 1988). Such a remnant decays much more slowly until the charge distribution becomes stirred at higher temperatures. We disregard this very slow component in the following. In the Appendix we present a rough model for charge motion between $K^{0}$ centres by phonon-assisted hopping of electrons. Reasonable fitting parameters to eqn. (A 13) of the Appendix yield the broken curves in fig. 4 which describe the fast-relaxation components quite satisfactorily.

## §5. CONCLUSIONS

The metastable effects observed in insulating a-SiN $N_{x}:$ H deposited by plasma assisted chemical vapour deposition can be related to a metastable neutralization of charged native defects. These native defects can be either negative effective correlation energy $-U$ defects in a homogenous material or $+U$ defects if the material contains potential fluctuations exceeding the magnitude of $U$. Our experimental results cannot distinguish between these alternatives. However, we presented indirect evidence which makes the presence of spatial bandgap and potential fluctuations plausible. The fluctuations may result from statistical fluctuations in the random distribution of different chemical bonds having large differences in energy or they may be enhanced by spatial heterogeneities resulting from strain relief. Bandgap fluctuations may also explain the anomalous width of the subgap absorption spectra and the low values of the factor $B$ in the expression for the interband optical absorption.

The presence of potential fluctuations may also explain the second, very slow component of the thermal relaxation of the light-induced metastable effects in insulating a-SiN $N_{x}:$ H. The faster-relaxation component can be described by a simple phonon-assisted hopping of charges through localized band-tail states between metastable $K^{0}$ centres.

The PPC in multilayers consisting of alternating amorphous silicon and silicon nitride layers can be explained by the presence of either $-U$ or $+U$ defects in the nitride layers if these defects trap a small excess of photoexcited holes that are injected by hopping from the silicon layers. We cannot exclude the possibility that electron- hole pairs recombining in the nitride layers create new metastable defects which cause a rise in the Fermi energy of the multilayer and thereby the PPC effects.

The similarity of the metastable effects observed in a-SiN$_x$:H and in a-SiO$_x$:H as well as in multilayers formed by these materials suggest that they originate from a feature that is common to these materials and perhaps shared by other semi-insulating amorphous alloys.

## APPENDIX
### ANNEALING OF METASTABLE K$^0$ CENTRES
In the following we wish to explore the anneal kinetics of metastable K$^0$ centres. We assume the following.

(1) The metastable K$^0$ centres form a band with a Gaussian density G($\epsilon$) of states of width $\epsilon_{\rm w}$ near the gap centre.
(2) Only electrons are mobile.
(3) The equilibrium state is established by transferring charges to produce K$^-$ in the lower half and K$^+$ states in the upper half of the gap.
(4) The localized states of the conduction-band tail are distributed in the density of states

$$
g(\epsilon)=\frac{N}{\epsilon_{0}} \exp \left(\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon_{0}}\right), \tag{A 1}
$$

where the energy $\epsilon=0$ at the electron mobility edge, $N$ is the total concentration of localized states and $\epsilon_0$ is the exponential width.

Here we do not specify whether a negative effective correlation energy or potential fluctuations cause the reaction $2{\rm K}^0 \to {\rm K}^- + {\rm K}^+$ to be exothermic. We also neglect the spatial variations in eqn. (A 1) which are associated with potential and bandgap fluctuations. We no not know whether hopping of electrons or of holes contributes more to the charge-exchange relaxation of the metastable K$^0$ centres. We use electrons as the mobile carriers just for convenience.

After light exposure at $T \leqslant 300\rm K$ the charge carrier distribution is in a frozen metastable state. When the temperature is raised to an annealing temperature $T_{\rm a}$, electrons from K$^0$ centres are thermally excited to the localized states of the conduction-band tail. The differential conductivity in the localized tail states in the interval $d\epsilon$ at $\epsilon$ is proportional to the product of the diffusion constant $D(\epsilon)$ of hopping

transport and the density $d n(\epsilon)$ of electrons. Since $d n(\epsilon)$ decreases and $D(\epsilon)$ increases steeply with increasing $\epsilon$, the differential conductivity has a sharp maximum at

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}}=3 \epsilon_{0} \ln \left(\frac{3 \epsilon_{0}}{k T_{\mathrm{a}}} \frac{a}{2} N^{1 / 3}\right), \tag{A 2}
$$

which is called the transport energy (Shapiro and Adler 1985a, b, Shklovskii, Levin, Fritzsche and Baranovskii 1990); $a$ is the localization radius of the states. The average hopping rate at this energy is

$$
v_{\mathrm{t}}=v_{0} \exp \left(-\frac{3 \epsilon_{0}}{k T_{\mathrm{a}}}\right), \tag{A 3}
$$

where $v_{0}=10^{12} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ is the phonon frequency. Relaxation of the metastable charge distribution occurs when an electron is activated, $\mathrm{K}^{0} \rightarrow \mathrm{K}^{+}+\mathrm{e}$, to the transport energy and is finally caught by another $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ centre, $\mathrm{e}+\mathrm{K}^{0} \rightarrow \mathrm{K}^{-}$. The rate-limiting step seems to be the thermal activation from the $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ centres at energy $\epsilon$. The rate is given by

$$
v(\epsilon)=v_{\mathrm{t}} \exp \left(\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}}}{k T_{\mathrm{a}}}\right). \tag{A 4}
$$

At a time $t$ after raising the sample's temperature to the annealing temperature $T_{\mathrm{a}}$, only those $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ states for which $v(\epsilon) t \geqslant 1$ will have had a chance to release their electron. These are the $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ states which lie above the demarcation energy $\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}$, where

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}}=k T_{\mathrm{a}} \ln \left(v_{\mathrm{t}} t\right). \tag{A 5}
$$

As $\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}$ moves down through the density $G(\epsilon)$ of states of the $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ centres, electrons are emitted to $\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}}$ at a rate

$$
G\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\right) \frac{\mathrm{d} \epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}}{\mathrm{d} t}=k T_{\mathrm{a}} G\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\right) t^{-1}, \tag{A 6}
$$

Therefore the rate equations for the mobile electron concentration $n$ near $\epsilon_{\mathrm{t}}$ and the densities of $\mathrm{K}^{0}, \mathrm{~K}^{+}$and $\mathrm{K}^{-}$centres can be written as

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} n}{\mathrm{~d} t}=\frac{k T_{\mathrm{a}} G\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\right)}{t}-n b^{0}\left[\mathrm{~K}^{0}\right]-n b^{+}\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right], \tag{A 7}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\mathrm{K}^{0}\right]}{\mathrm{d} t}=n b^{+}\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right]-n b^{0}\left[\mathrm{~K}^{0}\right]-\frac{k T_{\mathrm{a}} G\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\right)}{t}, \tag{A 8}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right]}{\mathrm{d} t}=\frac{k T_{\mathrm{a}} G\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\right)}{t}-n b^{+}\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right], \tag{A 9}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\mathrm{K}^{-}\right]}{\mathrm{d} t}=n b^{0}\left[\mathrm{~K}^{0}\right]. \tag{A 10}
$$

We also have the neutrality condition and the constancy of the K-centre concentration:

$$
\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right]=\left[\mathrm{K}^{-}\right]+n, \tag{A 11}
$$

$$
\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right]+\left[\mathrm{K}^{0}\right]+\left[\mathrm{K}^{-}\right]=\mathrm{constant}, \tag{A 12}
$$

where the square brackets denote concentration, and $b^{0}, b^{+}$and $b^{-}$are the capture coefficients. As the relaxation $2 \mathrm{~K}^{0} \rightarrow \mathrm{K}^{+}+\mathrm{K}^{-}$proceeds, the density $G(\epsilon)$ of states of $\mathrm{K}^{0}$

centres diminishes with time. This occurs for $\epsilon>\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}$ by electron excitation and at all $\epsilon$ by electron capture. We can set $\mathrm{d} n / \mathrm{d} t \approx 0$ in eqn. (A 7) and obtain from eqns. (A 7) and (A 8)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\mathrm{K}^{0}\right]}{\mathrm{d} t}=-2 G\left(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\right) k T_{\mathrm{a}} t^{-1} \frac{b^{0}\left[\mathrm{~K}^{0}\right]}{b^{0}\left[\mathrm{~K}^{0}\right]+b^{+}\left[\mathrm{K}^{+}\right]}. \qquad \text{(A13)}
$$

The broken curves in fig. 4 are calculated from eqn. (A 13) using the following fitting parameters: $\epsilon_{0}=0.07 \mathrm{eV}, \epsilon_{\mathrm{w}}=0.35 \mathrm{eV}$ and $v_{0}=3 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ for the $E_{04}=2.7 \mathrm{eV}$ sample, and $\epsilon_{0}=0.10 \mathrm{eV}, \epsilon_{\mathrm{w}}=0.29 \mathrm{eV}$ and $v_{0}=10^{12} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ for the $E_{04}=3.3 \mathrm{eV}$ sample. Furthermore we assumed that the $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ centres are near midgap and that $b^{+} / b^{0}=0.01$.

The slow long-time tails of the annealing curves at $T_{\mathrm{a}}<470 \mathrm{~K}$ may result from the larger potential barriers surrounding a fraction of the $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ defects or the clustering of charges which is expected at the lower temperatures where the relaxing distribution is insufficiently stirred (Bishop and Searle 1988). The annealing curves of Tober *et al.* (1991) exhibit a fraction of metastable $\mathrm{K}^{0}$ that essentially cease to anneal below $T_{\mathrm{a}}=500 \mathrm{~K}$. We do not observe such an extremely slow component.
