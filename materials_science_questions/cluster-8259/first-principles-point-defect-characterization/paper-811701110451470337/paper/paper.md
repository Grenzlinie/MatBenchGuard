# Recoverable degradation in InAs/AlSb high-electron mobility transistors:
The role of hot carriers and metastable defects in AlSb

S. T. Pantelides $^{1,3}$

(Received 25 June 2010; accepted 27 September 2010; published online 7 December 2010)

Recent observations of electrical stress-induced recoverable degradation in InAs/AlSb high-electron mobility transistors (HEMTs) have been attributed to metastable defects in the AlSb layer generated by the injected holes. Here we present a detailed theoretical analysis of the degradation mechanism. We show that recoverable degradation does not require the presence of hot carriers in the vicinity of the defects but the degradation is enhanced when the injected holes become more energetic. The metastable degradation arises without the presence of an energy barrier. A comprehensive survey of candidate defects suggest that substitutional and interstitial oxygen are responsible for the degradation. Therefore, reducing the oxygen contamination during device fabrication is likely to significantly improve the reliability of InAs/AlSb HEMTs. (C) 2010 American Institute of Physics.
[doi:10.1063/1.3505795]

## I. INTRODUCTION

Degradation of many types of semiconductor devices under electrical stress has generally been attributed to the release of hydrogen by hot carriers, which depassivates pre-existing defects. $^{1-3}$ Such degradation is not usually possible to recover completely. On the other hand, easily reversible hot-carrier degradation has been observed in AlGaAs/GaAs and AlGaAs/InGaAs high-electron mobility transistors (HEMTs); a mechanism that involves carrier trapping/detrapping at deep levels has been suggested. $^{4,5}$

Recently, we performed electrical stress tests on InAs/ AlSb HEMTs (Fig. 1) and observed degradation in some of the devices. $^{6}$ The degradation is manifested as negative shifts in threshold voltage and transconductance peak (Fig. 2); the former indicates an increase in donor concentration or a decrease in acceptor concentration in the AlSb layers (defects in the InAs are ruled out because no significant change in mobility was observed). The degraded devices almost completely recover after being left at room temperature with no bias for about two days (Fig. 2). It was suggested that the degradation was caused by existing defects in the top AlSb layer being converted to more positively charged metastable states by the injected hot holes. Preliminary results of first-principle calculations suggested that oxygen defects might play a significant role in the degradation.

In this article, we present detailed theoretical results and analyze the degradation mechanism. We show that, in contrast to typical hot-carrier damage that involves hydrogen release, where the carriers that directly interact with defects must have enough kinetic energy to knock off hydrogen atoms, in recoverable degradation, the carriers that directly interact with the defects do not need to be hot. Instead, the hot carriers play an indirect role: they create holes in the InAs channel by impact ionization. These holes are then injected into the top AlSb barrier and are captured by the defects. We demonstrate that the metastability of the defect does not require an energy barrier as in various DX centers, $^{7}$ but instead can originate from the low electron concentration in AlSb and a large upward shift in the pertinent electronic level caused by lattice relaxation. We survey the properties of candidate defects, and identify the ones that likely are responsible. Furthermore, we discuss the effects of the kinetic energy of the injected holes on the amount of observed degradation. These results demonstrate the importance of reducing oxygen contamination during device fabrication, in order to improve the performance and reliability of InAs/AlSb HEMTs.

When the original Fermi level is much higher than the transition level, the absolute change in the steady state concentration of $B^{0}$ due to hole injection will still be small and its effect on device characteristics can be negligible.

### B. Origin of long lifetime

Since $B^{0}$ is not the thermodynamical ground state of the defect, it will capture an electron and relax back to $A^{-}$ when the injection of holes is stopped. However, the $B^{0}$ state may have a very long lifetime because the electron capture can be very slow in this kind of structure. As shown in Fig. 3, there are three possible processes for $B^{0}$ to capture an electron. The first process is capturing an electron from the conduction band. Since the Fermi level is far below the conduction band, the free electron concentration is very low. Thus, a defect can stay in configuration $B^{0}$ for a long time before it captures a conduction band electron. In the second process, the empty defect level gets occupied by an electron thermally excited from the valence band (thermal hole emission). This process can also be slow because the empty defect level is now far above the valence band. In the third process, the empty state gets occupied by an electron tunneling across the interface. Since the empty electronic level is far above the Fermi level, the tunneling process can also be slow, especially for defects that are far from the interface. Thus, in the situation depicted in Fig. 3, a slow rate for overall electron capture can be achieved. Although the large shift in electronic level is usu- ally found in a DX center, $^{7}$ which also exhibits a sizable energy barrier for electron capture [Fig. 4 (left)], a barrier is not needed to ensure a slow rate of electron capture. In principle, this kind of metastability can exist in bulk semiconductors, but it may be realized more easily in a heterostructure like an InAs/AlSb HEMT, where the Fermi level is controlled by other layers, and a flux of carriers can be injected across the interface to generate a nonequilibrium concentration of metastable states.

The relationship between the long defect recovery time and the low electron concentration in the top AlSb layer is supported by recovery trends under negative gate bias and no drain bias. Figure 5 shows the recovery of two devices with similar stress-induced degradation (transconductance peak changes in 110 and 130 mV). The device biased at $V_{gs}=-1$ V [Fig. 5(a)] shows significantly less recovery than the one biased at $V_{gs}=-0.7$ V [Fig. 5(b)]. Figure 5(c) shows the fractional recovery in devices with $\sim$110 mV poststress degradation as a function of gate bias. Since more negative gate bias implies Fermi levels closer to the valence band and hence fewer electrons, the slower recovery under negative gate bias suggests that the defect recovery time depends on the electron density in the top AlSb layer.

### III. SURVEY OF DEFECTS IN ALSB

In the following section, we present a comprehensive survey to identify the defect(s) that are responsible for the behavior illustrated in Fig. 3 for these devices. We examine a range of candidate defects, using available results in the literature, $^{8-10}$ where available, and new calculations. For each candidate defect, we explore various configurations and calculate the formation energies of all charge states for each stable configuration. Then we check the behavior of the candidate defect against the experimental results of Ref. 6.

The new calculations are based on density functional theory (DFT). We employ the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) (Ref. 11) version of the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) exchange-correlation functional for most calculations. The more accurate but computationally costly Heyd-Scuseria-Ernzerhof 06 (HSE06) (Ref. 12) hybrid functional is used only for the antimony antisite (Sb$_{\text{Al}}$). We use projector augmented wave potentials$^{13}$ and plane wave basis as implemented in the VIENNA AB INITIO SIMULATION PACKAGE code.$^{14}$ The kinetic energy cutoff of plane wave basis is 400 eV for GGA-PBE calculations and 240.3 eV for hybrid HSE06 calculations. The ionic relaxations are converged to $10^{-3}$ eV for the total energy difference between two ionic steps. A shifted $2\times2\times2$ k-point mesh is used for Brillouin zone sampling. For GGA-PBE calculations, the valence band maximum (VBM) of the host AlSb cell is the average value of all special k-points.$^{15}$ For hybrid DFT HSE calculations, the VBM is taken at the $\Gamma$ point. For charged defects, the potential alignment correction scheme described in Ref. 16, and the image charge correction scheme described in Ref. 17 are applied. The diffusion barrier is calculated using the nudged elastic band method.$^{18}$

The formation energy $E^f$ of a defect with charge $q$ is calculated as:$^{19}$ $E^f=E^{tot}+q(E_F-E_V)-\sum_i n_i\mu_i$, where $E^{tot}$ is the total energy of the cell containing the defect, $q$ is the charge of defect, $E_V$ is the position of the VBM of the host supercell, $E_F$ is the Fermi level measured from VBM, $n_i$ is the number of atoms of the ith element in the supercell, and $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential of the ith element. The formation energies are calculated under Al rich conditions.

The top AlSb layer is grown after the InAs layer, and, as a result, during its growth, the Fermi level is always at the lower part of the band gap. Thus we only need to consider the defects that have low formation energies when the Fermi level is low.

We start by considering common intrinsic defects. Previous DFT calculations⁸⁻¹⁰ have shown that at the lower part of the band gap, the intrinsic defects that have low formation energies are the aluminum interstitial (Alᵢ) and the antimony antisite (Sbₐₗ). We first look at Alᵢ. It is an interstitial defect with a small [1.28 eV (Ref. 10)] energy barrier, therefore, it may anneal out during the growth. The diffusion length of this defect can be calculated as $x=2\sqrt{Dt}$, where $D$ is the diffusivity and $t$ is the diffusion time. The diffusivity $D$ can be calculated as $D=l^{2}v\exp((-E_{a}/kT))$, where $l$ is the hopping distance between minima, $v$ is the attempt frequency, $E_{a}$ is the barrier for the diffusion, $k$ is the Boltzman constant, and $T$ is the temperature. For interstitials in AlSb, $l$ =4.35 Å, and we use $10^{13}$ s⁻¹ as the approximate value of $v.^{20}$ A typical growth temperature of AlSb top layer is $510\ ^{\circ}\text{C}.^{21}$ For an interstitial that enters the AlSb layer during the growth, it will diffuse at that temperature for about $10^{3}$ s considering the remaining growth time and postgrowth cooling. The diffusion length of such defect during that time is estimated to be $7\ \mu\text{m}$, which is much greater than the thickness of the top AlSb layer ($\sim$10 nm). Therefore Al interstitials will diffusion out of the top AlSb layer during growth and will not play any role in the device performance .

The antimony antisite Sbₐₗ has a low formation energy.⁸⁻¹⁰ Therefore, it may exist in the AlSb layer in large concentrations. Its ground state has Td symmetry, and Du has discovered a metastable configuration with C₃ᵥ symmetry.¹⁰ The formation energies of Sbₐₗ(Td) and Sbₐₗ (C₃ᵥ) obtained using the HSE06 hybrid functional are shown in Fig. 7. At the beginning of the stress, the defects are in the double donor state Sbₐₗ(Td)²⁺. The charge states of metastable Sbₐₗ (C₃ᵥ) configuration are all more negative. Thus Sbₐₗ cannot account for the observed degradation where the defects are supposed to become more positive.

Now we examine extrinsic defects that are likely to be present. We first consider oxygen, which is a known contaminant in AlSb;⁸,¹⁰ energy-dispersive analysis of x-rays (EDAX) measurements in an InAs/AlSb HEMT show a large O peak.²² According to Du,¹⁰ substitutional oxygen Oₛᵦ and interstitial oxygen Oᵢ have similar formation energies when the Fermi level is near the VBM, so they can both exist in large number. When the Fermi level is above the VBM +0.25 eV, we find that the thermodynamical ground state of Oᵢ is Oᵢ(C₃ᵥ)²⁻. It has a diffusion barrier of 2.4 eV. Under the growth condition discussed above, its diffusion length is estimated to be $\sim$2 nm, which is smaller than the thickness of the top AlSb layer ($\sim$10 nm), so it can remain in the AlSb layer.

For substitutional oxygen Oₛᵦ, we found a number of stable configurations. The formation energies in various charge states are shown in Fig. 8 (left). Near the VBM, the most stable states are the Oₛᵦ(C₃ᵥ) donor and a DX-like donor structure that has C₂ᵧ symmetry, similar to a proposed model for Oₐₛ⁺ in GaAs.²³ We name the latter OBB-DX (orthorhombic broken-bond DX) structure to distinguish it from the original BB-DX acceptor structure²⁴ that has C₃ᵥ symmetry. When the Fermi level moves away from the VBM, the α-CCB-DX (Ref. 25) and β-CCB-DX (Ref. 26) acceptors become most stable configurations. During the stress, holes generated in the InAs channel enter the top AlSb layer. If the defects are present in the Oₛᵦ(α-CCB-DX)⁻ or Oₛᵦ(β-CCB-DX)⁻ states, they can capture two holes and transform to Oₛᵦ(OBB-DX)⁺ (shown as the arrow) or Oₛᵦ(C₃ᵥ)⁺. One such transition removes an acceptor and creates a donor, which can account for the observed degradation naturally.

## IV. DISCUSSION

As stated before, the reversible degradation mechanism considered above does not require injected holes to be hot. However, it is observed experimentally that there is more degradation when the injected holes are more energetic. $^{6}$ Clearly, the kinetic energies of the holes in the barrier play a role in the degradation. There are two ways in which the temperature of the carriers can change the kinetics. First, the capture rate for carriers increases with the carrier tempera- ture when electron-lattice coupling is strong. $^{30}$ Second, hot carriers can deposit energy into the lattice and create meta- stable defects in the same charge state, which may have a different capture or emission rate. For example, in the case of $O_{Sb}$ , hot holes can convert $O_{Sb}(\alpha / \beta-CCBDX)^{-}$ to $O_{Sb}(C_{3 ~V})^{-}$ , which has a smaller electron emission barrier, thereby in- creasing the rate of generating $O_{Sb}(C_{3 ~V})^{+}$ . Therefore, the steady state concentration of more positive defects increases as the kinetic energy of holes increases. This effect is not as significant if the formation energy difference is large, which corresponds to a higher Fermi level in the AlSb barrier layer. This is consistent with the experimental observation that de- vices operating at higher Fermi levels (the ones that do not pinch off in Ref. 6) do not show significant degradation, in contrast to the devices operating at the lower Fermi levels.

## V. SUMMARY

In summary, the recoverable degradation in InAs/AlSb HEMTs is explained by defects in AlSb that transform into metastable states upon capturing the injected holes. We show that the mechanism itself does not require the injected carri- ers to be hot, but hot holes lead to enhanced degradation. We demonstrate that the metastable states can have long life- times at room temperature because of the very low concen- tration of the free electrons and the structural relaxations that shifted the emptied electronic levels far above the Fermi level. Theoretical investigation of possible intrinsic and ex- trinsic defects in AlSb shows that metastable states of sub- stitutional and interstitial oxygen have the charge states and lifetimes to account for the observed degradation and recov- ery. We suggest that this kind of degradation may be avoided by reducing the oxygen content of the as-fabricated devices.
