# Dopant compensation in $HfO_2$ and other high K oxides

H. Li, Y. Guo, and J. Robertson
Engineering Department, Cambridge University, Cambridge CB2 1PZ, United Kingdom

(Received 11 February 2014; accepted 3 May 2014; published online 15 May 2014)

The theory of doping limits in semiconductors and insulators is applied to the case of wide gap oxides, crystalline, or amorphous, and used to explain that impurities do not in general give rise to gap states or a doping response. Instead, the system tends to form defect complexes or undergo symmetry-lowering reconstructions to expel gap states out of the band gap. The model is applied to impurities, such as trivalent metals, carbon, N, P, and B, in $HfO_2$, the main gate dielectric used in field effect transistors. © 2014 AIP Publishing LLC. [http://dx.doi.org/10.1063/1.4878503]

The addition of aliovalent impurities to crystalline silicon or other $sp^3$ bonded semiconductors leads to doping, because the high stability of the tetrahedral substitutional site causes many impurities to occupy this site. On the other hand, amorphous $SiO_2$ has a continuous random network structure¹ and this allows impurity atoms, such as B, Al, or P, to occupy a network site whose coordination satisfies its classical valence, so there is no doping. Similarly, amorphous semiconductors form a continuous random network,² so Mott³ proposed that additional atoms would also adopt sites that satisfied their classical valence, with all their electrons in bonds, so there would be no doping. (a-Si can be doped inefficiently, but this is partly compensated⁴).

Recently, high dielectric constant (high K) oxides, such as $HfO_2$, are replacing $SiO_2$ as the gate dielectric in metal oxide semiconductor field effect transistors (MOSFETs) to lessen the gate leakage currents.⁵ These oxides have ionic bonding and can be crystalline or amorphous. The oxides may also contain impurities, either intentionally or unintentionally from the deposition process.⁶⁻¹⁰ It is important to know if these impurities will give rise to gap states or cause Fermi level shifts that could degrade device performance. We find here that the impurities rarely give rise to doping, and indeed they often do not give rise to gap states. This is important, as the absence of gap states will reduce device instabilities and leakage currents and may avoid flat band voltage shifts.¹¹

We show that this lack of doping follows from a general principle of wide band gap materials that any impurity state acting to shift the Fermi energy $E_F$ will tend to create a compensating intrinsic defect which negates this effect, so there is no doping.¹²⁻¹⁷ There is a second effect that the interaction of the impurity and defect states tends to repel any defect states out of the band gap. An impurity occupying a pure substitutional or interstitial site in $HfO_2$ will usually give rise to gap states, but the lowest energy configuration of the impurity is generally a complex of the impurity and an intrinsic defect, so that the substitutional site is greatly modified.

The defect structures were relaxed using the density functional theory (DFT) plane wave code CASTEP, using ultra-soft pseudopotentials. The density of states was calculated using the generalised gradient approximation to the exchange correlation energy. However, as this underestimates the band gap, the density of states was then calculated by the hybrid functional screened exchange method.¹⁸,¹⁹ This has previously been used for calculations on many semiconductors and insulators, and gives a good description of defects in $HfO_2$.

We first consider the trivalent La impurity in $HfO_2$ as an example.²⁰ $La^{3+}$ substituting for $Hf^{4+}$ in $HfO_2$ will create a valence band hole state. This will shift $E_F$ to the valence band edge. Such a shift would give a large driving force for the spontaneous creation of an oxygen vacancy, a donor. This can be expressed as the formation energy of the oxygen vacancy becoming negative for $E_F$ in the lower gap, as in Fig. 1(a). The oxygen vacancy is a double donor, so the most stable combination is for two substitutional $La_{Hf}$ sites and a doubly positive oxygen vacancy, Fig. 2(a). The two electrons of the neutral vacancy fall to fill the two hole states, Fig. 1(b), leaving a filled valence band. The second point is that the lattice relaxation due to the positive charge on the oxygen vacancy acts to repel its states to just above the conduction band edge, so there are no gap states, Fig. 2(b). The general explanation of this is the theory of doping limits in semiconductors. $^{12-17}$ This states that as impurity doping shifts the Fermi energy away from midgap into the bands, it will reach an energy limit where there is the spontaneous creation of compensating defects, which limits further doping. $^{12,14}$ For normal semiconductors like Si, this energy limit lies well into the bands, Fig. 1(c). There are separate energy limits for n- and p-type doping. As the band gap increases, as in ZnSe, the energy limits start to fall into the gap, and then the semiconductor cannot be doped in that polarity (that is, p-type for ZnSe). In wide gap oxides like HfO₂, the two doping limits both lie well within the gap,¹⁷ so there is no doping response, and there will always be a spontaneous creation of compensating defects, Fig. 1(c).

Fig. 1(a) shows the action of the doping limits principle for the specific case of HfO₂, E_F is stable within the green shaded zone in Fig. 1(a), but if it moves below or above this zone, then the formation energy of a compensating defect (V_O or I_O) will become negative, the defect forms, and this keeps E_F within the stable zone shown in green in Fig. 1(a). We take the oxygen chemical potential in Fig. 1 to be that of the Si/SiO₂ equilibrium, to correspond to conditions of annealing the HfO₂ film next to an excess of Si, as in the Si MOSFET channel.

We now consider some other examples of the absence of doping and the expulsion of gap states, for the cases of Al, N, C, P, and B. Fig. 2(a) shows the complex of two La_Hf sites next to the compensating oxygen vacancy,²⁰ and the outward relaxation of the La and Hf sites adjacent to the vacancy is clear. Fig. 2(b) shows the partial density of states (PDOS) for this structure. The vacancy state that lies on the adjacent metal sites has now moved from the upper gap into the bottom of the conduction band, due to this relaxation, so there is no state in the gap.

Al behaves like La and other trivalent metals. It substitutes at the Hf site. Its hole state leads to the spontaneous formation of an oxygen vacancy, and two Al_Hf sites plus one O vacancy form the neutral complex in which any gap states have also been repelled out of the gap (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)). The cases of substitutional La and Al in HfO₂ extend to many systems such as yttrium stabilised zirconia.²¹

Nitrogen is an intentionally added impurity which can have beneficial effects.⁹,¹⁰,²² It can act as a diffusion barrier to reduce oxygen diffusion.⁸ Nitrogen is pentavalent, with one less electron than oxygen. N at an oxygen site leads to a valence band hole.²²,²³ Thus, like La_Hf, N_O centers are compensated by oxygen vacancies, and a pair of N_O sites will form a neutral, closed shell complex with an oxygen vacancy,²²⁻²⁴ Fig. 2(e). Again, the outward relaxation of Hf ions away from the charged vacancy and level repulsion causes the O vacancy level to move upwards from the upper gap to above the conduction band edge, as seen in Fig. 2(f). Experimentally, the removal of the gap states associated with the O vacancy reduces the leakage current.¹⁰,²² Nitrogen addition may also be used to reduce the positive bias stress instability (PBTI) in HfO₂-base gate stacks, which is associated with the injection of electrons from the Si conduction band into the O vacancy states, whose energy lies near the Si conduction band energy.¹¹ Removal of these states reduces this instability. La addition is also found to reduce the PBTI²⁵⁻²⁷ because of its ability to raise the vacancy level out of the gap.

Carbon is a possible impurity in HfO₂ because metal-organic precursors are often used in the atomic layer deposition process.⁵⁻⁸ C has the same valence as Hf, so it can substitute at the Hf site as C_Hf, as in Fig. 3(a).⁷,²⁸,²⁹ Fig. 3(b) shows the PDOS for this site in cubic HfO₂ where a breathing mode relaxation is allowed. This site has two gap states, one just above the oxide valence band and one below the conduction band edge.

Carbon can also substitute at the O site, but the difference in valence means that the C_O site is an acceptor, Fig. 4(a). This configuration leaves many gap states. This charge can be compensated by an additional oxygen vacancy, as shown in Fig. 4(c). This compensation leaves E_F in midgap. However, in this case, the C 2p levels remain in the lower gap, and the vacancy states also remain in the upper gap region, Fig. 4(d), so it is not a good combination. In Fig. 4(c) the Hf sites around the O vacancy are shown as dark blue.

Carbon can exist in two more stable and interesting configurations not previously noticed. First, the C_Hf site of Fig. 3(a) can relax off-center, to form three stronger bonds to its neighboring oxygens, but breaking the other five C-O bonds. This gains 7 eV. The center now looks like a CO₃ carbonate ion in the Hf space, Fig. 3(c). Here, the oxygens that have broken their bonds to the C site are shown as pink. The effect is mostly clearly seen in monoclinic $HfO_2$ where the Hf site has seven neighbours, three on one side and four on the other. A simple off-center displacement creates the carbonate-like entity, as in Fig. 3(e). Fig. 3(f) is the associated PDOS which again shows no gap states. The presence of a carbonate ion was noted for oxidised $SiC.^{30}$

It is also possible to substitute a $CO_3^{2-}$ ion for a $O^{2-}$ ion. Here, the C is surrounded by three oxygens, Fig. 4(e). Unfortunately, this does leave a localised C $p\pi$ state in the upper gap, Fig. 4(f).

The stability of the neutral configurations of these various C-based defects is plotted against oxygen chemical potential in Fig. 5. The $C_{Hf}$ and $(CO_3)_O$ defects are both oxygen-excess sites, so their formation energy increases as the O chemical potential decreases. On the other hand, the $C_O$ center compensated by an O vacancy is an O deficient center, whose formation energy falls as the O chemical potential goes more negative. As carbon does not introduce gap states if it stays in the distorted $C_{Hf}$ configuration, this requires the O chemical potential to be above $-3.8$ eV. This is a low O chemical potential but not as low as that of the $Si/SiO_2$ equilibrium. The $C_{Hf}$ is more stable than the carbonate ion at the O site because the latter site has too many anions in the same space.

The $C_{Hf}$ and $(CO_3)_O$ sites each have the same formal oxygen deficiency. They can also be considered as $HfO_2$:$CO_2$ alloys, isovalent to $HfO_2$:$SiO_2$ alloys. The silicates have a wider band gap than $HfO_2$ itself, because of the strong Si-O bonds, but the prevalence of weaker $\pi$ bonding means that this is not so for C.

We finally consider other possible impurities P and B. P substituting at a Hf site is the more stable option as this allows P to bond to O. A simple substitutional $P_{Hf}$ is a donor. This complex has no states in the main gap, Fig. 6(b), but it does leave some states localised on these under-coordinated O sites that lie just above the valence band edge.

For the case of boron, B also substitutes at the Hf site, so as to make B–O bonds. It is trivalent, but is less electropositive than the trivalent metals considered earlier, and prefers to form covalent bonds to O. Pairs of borons are compensated by an O vacancy, which strongly rebonds to leave 3-fold coordinated B sites, Fig. 6(c). The under-coordinated oxygens are again shown as pink. (These configurations are the same as those found in earlier work on the dipole layers at $\text{SiO}_2/\text{HfO}_2$ intefaces. $^{31}$) There are no B-related gap states, but there remain some states just above the valence band edge lying on the under-coordinated oxygens. We note that states close to the valence band are less critical in $\text{HfO}_2$, it is states in the upper gap, and especially those near the silicon gap that are most dangerous. In that sense, B and P impurities have well passivated gap states. This model can be extended to cover the case of amorphous $\text{SiO}_2$ as well. Introducing dopants into $\text{SiO}_2$ is known to cause a rearrangement of its lattice, changing the coordination of the dopant sites, and sometimes creating O dangling bonds, so as not to shift $E_F$. Thus, the doping limits model can be viewed as the most general model, that encompasses crystalline and amorphous structures.

In summary, we have applied the theory of doping limits in semiconductors and insulators to wide gap oxides and used to argue that impurities in general do not give rise to gap states or a doping response. The impurities form a variety of defect complexes or undergo symmetry-lowering reconstructions, which keep $E_F$ near midgap and lead to the expulsion of states out of the band gap. The model is applied to impurities such as trivalent metals, carbon, N, P, and B in $\text{HfO}_2$. It is valid more generally, for example, accounting for the prevalence of oxygen vacancies near impurities in $\text{SrTiO}_3$. $^{32}$
