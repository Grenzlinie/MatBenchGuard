# Strong exchange anisotropy in orbitally degenerate complexes. A new possibility for designing single-molecule magnets with high blocking temperatures

Vladimir S. Mironov*

Institute of Crystallography, Russian Academy of Sciences, 117333, Leninskii prosp, 59 Moscow, Russia

## Abstract
A new approach to designing single-molecule magnets (SMM) with high spin-reorientation barriers is discussed. It is based on the use of orbitally degenerate metal complexes with a pronounced spin-orbit coupling exhibiting strongly anisotropic exchange interactions. Incorporation of such building blocks into an SMM cluster can drastically increase its magnetic anisotropy and blocking temperature. The use of the exchange anisotropy is an attractive alternative to the use of the single-ion anisotropy only.

Designing single-molecule magnets (SMM) with high blocking temperatures is a topical problem in the field of molecular magnetism [1]. The blocking temperature is mainly determined by the spin-reorientation barrier separating the $M=+S$ and $M=-S$ states of an SMM spin cluster. This barrier is closely related to the magnetic anisotropy of the cluster causing the splitting of the ground spin state. For the axial symmetry this splitting is described by the $DS_{z}^{2}$ pattern; the spin cluster exhibits SMM properties when $D<0$. At present, the largest barrier of $45\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and the blocking temperature of $\sim3\mathrm{K}$ are obtained for the $\mathrm{Mn}_{12}\mathrm{Ac}$ SMM cluster [2]. Much higher barriers are therefore needed to increase the blocking temperature.

The microscopic origin of the magnetic anisotropy in spin clusters is still poorly understood. The global magnetic anisotropy of SMM clusters is usually associated with the single-ion anisotropy only (zero-field splitting and/or the $g$-tensor anisotropy) combined with more or less isotropic spin-spin coupling between metal centres [3,4]. The role of the exchange anisotropy was scarcely discussed.

In this communication, an alternative way to increase the spin-reorientation barrier is discussed, which is based on the use of the exchange anisotropy rather than the single-ion zero-field splitting. In most of the cases, the ground state of transition metal ions is a spin-only state, so the exchange interaction is described by the isotropic spin Hamiltonian $-JS_{1}S_{2}$; anisotropic spin terms are usually of minor importance. However, in orbitally degenerate metal centres with a pronounced spin-orbit coupling, exchange interactions become strongly anisotropic. This occurs for most of lanthanide (4f) and actinide (5f) ions and for some orbitally degenerate transition metal complexes, such as octahedral $\mathrm{Co}^{\mathrm{II}}$ complexes [5] or the $[\mathrm{Mo}^{\mathrm{III}}(\mathrm{CN})_{7}]^{4-}$ heptacyanimetallate (a pentagonal bipyramid) [6]. It is important to note that the exchange anisotropy is distinct from the single-ion anisotropy; moreover, the exchange anisotropy does not correlate with the anisotropy of the $g$-tensor of interacting metal ions. Strong exchange anisotropy was also found for other orbitally degenerate ions [5].

The new possibility to increase the spin-reorientation barrier is based on the idea that the spin spectrum of a polyatomic magnetic cluster involving strongly anisotropic exchange bonds can differ drastically from the usual $D S_{z}^{2}$ pattern. This can be best shown for a dimer composed of an orbitally degenerate magnetic centre with a Kramers doublet as the ground state (the effective spin $S_{1}=\frac{1}{2}$ ) and a spin-only $3 \mathrm{~d}$ ion, such as $\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}$ $\left(S_{2}=\frac{5}{2}\right)$. For the antiferromagnetic $(J<0)$ isotropic exchange $-J \boldsymbol{S}_{1} \boldsymbol{S}_{2}$, the spin spectrum of this dimer has only two levels, $S=2$ (lower) and $S=3$ (upper), Fig. 1a. Since the ground level $S=2$ is not split, there is no barrier between the $M=2$ and $M=-2$ states, corresponding to the opposite magnetizations of the dimer.

The splitting of the ground level can come only from the zero-field splitting on the $S_{2}=\frac{5}{2}$ ion, which is rather small $\left(\sim 10^{-2} \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}\right.$ for $\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}$ ions). For the Ising-like spin coupling $-J S_{1}^{z} S_{2}^{z}$ the spectrum is described by the $-J m M_{S}$ pattern resulting in six doubly degenerate levels, Fig. 1b; such a spin coupling was really found for the $\mathrm{Mo}^{\mathrm{III}}-\mathrm{CN}-\mathrm{Mn}^{\mathrm{II}}$ dimer shown in Fig. $1, H=$ $-J_{z} S_{\mathrm{Mo}}^{z} S_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{z}$ [9]. The ground state at $-5|J| / 4$ (represented by two wave functions $\left|-\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle_{1} \otimes\left|+\frac{5}{2}\right\rangle_{2}$ and $\left|+\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle_{1} \otimes\left|-\frac{5}{2}\right\rangle_{2}$ ) is separated by $|J| / 2$ from the first excited state at $-3|J| / 4$. Therefore, in the latter case the dimer shows an SMM behaviour with a spin-reorientation barrier of order of the exchange parameter $J$, which can reach a value of several tens of $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. Magnetic characteristics of this dimer ( $g$-tensor and magnetic susceptibility) are strongly anisotropic (Ising-like).

The barrier and the magnetic anisotropy can be further increased in going from dimers to polyatomic spin clusters involving one or several orbitally degenerate building blocks. In this case, the barrier can be much larger (probably, several hundreds of $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ), even for rather small clusters (about 10 magnetic ions or less). This is evidenced from numerical modelling spin spectra of small spin clusters of several topologies with various combinations of isotropic and anisotropic building blocks. Spin energy spectra and the barrier are sensitive to the number anisotropic exchange bonds and the topology of the cluster.

There are several groups of orbitally degenerate magnetic building blocks, which can be used for constructing spin clusters with high SMM characteristics:
(a) $4 \mathrm{f}$ ions (all open-shell $\mathrm{Ln}^{3+}$ ions except $\mathrm{Gd}^{3+}$ ) and some $5 \mathrm{f}$ ions $\left(\mathrm{U}^{3+}, \mathrm{U}^{4+}\right.$, and $\left.\mathrm{U}^{5+}\right)$.
(b) Orbitally degenerate $3 \mathrm{~d}$ complexes: octahedral $\mathrm{Co}^{\mathrm{II}}$ complexes, $\left[\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN})_{6}\right]^{3-},\left[\mathrm{FeCp}_{2}\right]^{+}$.
(c) $4 \mathrm{~d}$ and $5 \mathrm{~d}$ orbitally degenerate complexes with a strong spin-orbit coupling, such as pentagonalbipyramidal $\left[\mathrm{Mo}^{\mathrm{III}}(\mathrm{CN})_{7}\right]^{4-}[6]$ and $\left[\mathrm{Re}^{\mathrm{IV}}(\mathrm{CN})_{7}\right]^{3-}$ [10] cyanide complexes, and some octahedral complexes, such as $\left[\mathrm{Os}(\mathrm{CN})_{6}\right]^{3-}$.

This list may be further extended. The most preferable are those building blocks which combine strong exchange anisotropy with high exchange parameters. From this point of view, $5 \mathrm{~d}$ complexes (like $\left[\mathrm{Re}^{\mathrm{IV}}(\mathrm{CN})_{7}\right]^{3-}$ ) are very promising since they have a diffuse and high-energy $5 \mathrm{~d}$ orbitals favouring for high exchange parameters and exhibiting a strong spin-orbit interaction $\left(\zeta_{5 \mathrm{~d}} \approx 2000 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}\right.$ or more). Magnetic properties of such clusters can be tuned by combining magnetically anisotropic building blocks (a)-(c) with appropriate spin-only ions, such as $\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}, \mathrm{Cr}^{3+}$, or $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$. Various combinations of magnetically anisotropic and isotropic building blocks and various topologies can be used. Apart from a high spin-reorientation barrier, such clusters can potentially exhibit other unusual magnetic properties.
