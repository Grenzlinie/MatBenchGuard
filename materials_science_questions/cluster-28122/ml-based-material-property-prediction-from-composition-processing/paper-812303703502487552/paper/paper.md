# CALCULATION OF THE HIGH-TEMPERATURE STRENGTH OF COMPLEXLY ALLOYED NICKEL ALLOYS USING EQUATIONS OF THE SYSTEM OF NONPOLARIZED IONIC RADII

S. V. Ovsepyan, $^{1}$ B. S. Lomberg, $^{1}$ and E. V. Baburina $^{1}$

Translated from Metallovedenie i Termicheskaya Obrabotka Metallov, No. 6, pp. 9 - 11, June, 1995.

Modern complexly alloyed high-temperature nickel-base alloys contain up to 14 alloying elements. The complex composition ensures fulfillment of strict and often contradictory requirements imposed on the materials of critical parts of gas turbine engines (GTE). However, multi-component alloying creates consider- able difficulties in the development of new compositions with specified characteristics or in the optimization of existing alloys. The present work is devoted to calculating the high-temperature strength of nickel alloys by means of the system of nonpolarized ionic radii (SNIR).

The development of alloys requires evaluation of the ef- fect of the composition on the properties when the concentra- tion of several alloying elements is changed simultaneously. The solution to this problem is often sought using semi-em- pirical methods of representing the chemical composition of the investigated alloy in the form of one or a few parameters. The parameters may be the type and amount of reinforcing phases, the temperature of their dissolution or segregation, the lattice parameters of the $\gamma'$-phase and the matrix, the num ber of electron vacancies, etc. [1 - 3].

The PHACOMP method [4, 5] is the most popular. It is based on the calculation of the mean concentration of elec- tron vacancies for the matrix solid solution, which makes it possible to evaluate the composition and to predict the segre- gation of the TPS-phases, which worsen the properties. We believe that the most essential drawback of the phase calcula- tion method is the insufficiently correct determination of the concentration of vacancies, the dependence of the calculation accuracy on the assumptions made on the distribution of ele- ments in the matrix and the reinforcing phases, and the lim- ited possibilities for evaluating the properties of the material.

In [6, 7] the composition of the alloy is evaluated by the data of phase analysis based on the relation between the mean atomic mass of the alloying elements and the concentration of their valence electrons.

Another approach envisages the use of the equations of the SNIR of the effective charges of the partners of the intera- tomic bond as a function of its length [8 - 10]. It is assumed that different crystallographic directions in densely packed lattices are characterized by different electron densities not only due to the nature of the packing (in the case of pure met- als) but also due to different neighboring atoms (in the case of alloys). Therefore, the excess electrons in the directions where the atomic orbitals overlap find a place on unfilled lev- els of other directions. The excess mean charge is denoted by $Zx$, and the ability to accept electrons is denoted by $Zy$. A constant relationship between $Zy$ and $Zx$ exists for each type of crystallographic lattice, for which the lattices are stable. Proceeding from this condition a system of equations is solved for a given composition of elements. The solution gives the mean values of $Zx$ and $Zy$ and the lattice parameter $A$ that characterizes the alloy as a single whole. For multi- phase alloys the parameter $A$ is a size factor [8].

In [8, 10] the electron equivalent of the composition ($Zy$) was used to describe "composition - property" dependences for nickel alloys. An additional parameter was the total con- centration of the alloying elements (for example, W + Mo+ Nb + Al + Ti). In our opinion this approach does not use all the possibilities of the SNIR.

The purpose of the present work was to estimate the pos- sibility of using the SNIR theory (the parameters $Zy$ and $A$) to describe the properties of complexly alloyed high-tempera- ture nickel alloys without resorting to the concentrations of the alloying elements.

We investigated an experimental high-temperature nickel alloy of the system Ni - Co - W - Al - Cr - Nb - Mo - Ti- Ta - Hf - V with additions of B and C. We analyzed 18 com- positions obtained by the method of vacuum induction melt- ing with different contents of Nb, Hf, V, and Ta. The base composition of the alloy was close to ÉP975 alloy [11]. The concentration of the changed elements by the data of chemical analysis, the endurance of test specimens at $750^{\circ} C$ and $\sigma=750 ~N / mm^{2}$ , and the computed values of $A$ and $-2 Z y$ are presented in Table 1. Analysis of the endurance as a func- tion of the parameter $Z y$ (Fig. 1) shows that this repre sentation of the data has no practical value without separating the set of points into groups. We cannot predict the properties of the material using just the parameter $Z y$ . For example, al loys 4 and 9 have a different endurance at close values of $Z y$ . Therefore, we should take into account one more parameter, namely, the size factor $A$ , in order to characterize the compo sition of the alloy.

Let us represent the compositions of alloys in the coordi- nates $(A, Z y)$ (Fig. 2). As the degree of alloying is increased, the parameters $A$ and $Z y$ grow. For example, alloys 5, 7, 9-11 preferentially contain the maximum sum of $Nb, Hf, Ta$ , and $V$ . Analysis of the computed data allowed us to distin guish three groups of alloys (straight lines in Fig. 2) for each of which we have chosen an linear regression equation. It can be seen from the data of Table 1 and Fig. 2 that alloys $1-4$ ,15 are less alloyed than alloys $6,7,9-11$ . The concentra tions of $Hf$ and Ta affect the parameters $A$ and $Z y$ most of all.

With allowance for these facts analysis of the data in Fig.1 becomes much easier. All compositions are divided into three groups. The endurance $(\tau_{m})$ of a melt can be evaluated by known dependences $\tau_{m}=f(Z y)$ by referring it to a group of compositions in the coordinates $(A, Z y)$ .

In order to compare the possibilities of the descriptions of alloy properties using the SNIR parameters and the concen- trations of the alloying elements we chose the coefficients in the regression equations that relate the endurance and the composition. In one case the parameters for evaluating the composition were the mass fraction $(\%)$ of niobium, hafnium, tantalum, and vanadium, and in the other the parameters were $A$ and $Z y$ . The results of the analysis are presented in Table 2. The models were evaluated by the deviation $\delta$ of the com puted values from the experimental ones. We analyzed sev- eral types of dependences of the endurance $\tau_{m}$ on the compo sition. Table 2 presents two types of models, namely, linear(1,2) and exponential (3,4). It can be seen that models 1, 2 and 3,4 have close deviations $\delta$ . This means that the SNIR parameters can be used for predicting the properties of nickel alloys. When choosing compositions of modern high-tempera- ture nickel alloys we should avoid excess alloying of the solid solution, which worsens the properties of the alloys. In our case, metallographic and phase analyses have shown the ap- pearance of lump particles of the excess $\gamma^{\prime}$ -phase in alloys 10,11, and 5. Therefore, for $(-2 Z y)>4.65$ the high-temperature strength of the materials is reduced.

Based on the data presented above we can give some rec- ommendations on the choice of the compositions of the alloysthat have the highest high-temperature strength at $750^{\circ} C$ :4.59 < -2Zy < 4.65; 0.3538 < A < 0.3541 nm. Using Table 1, we can easily pass from the conventional parameters to spe- cific concentrations of alloying elements. In our case, the best high-temperature alloys should contain $1.4-1.5 \% Hf$ with an addition of up to $2.5 \% Nb$ and up to $0.5 \% Ta$ . The concentra tion of tantalum can be increased to $2.4 \%$ only with a simul taneous decrease in the niobium concentration to $1.4 \%$ . The mass fraction of vanadium should be at most $0.3 \%$ .

## CONCLUSION

Two parameters, namely, $Z y$ and $A$ , can be used for the description of the high-temperature strength of nickel alloys instead of the concentration of the alloying elements. This will make the choice of the composition with the requisite high-temperature strength much easier.
