## ABSTRACT

HCN is an important precursor of nitric oxide (NO) during pulverized coal combustion. A thorough theoretical exploration with density functional theory and wave functional theory was performed to elucidate the effect mechanism of Fe on HCN heterogeneous formation from nitrogen-containing char. Based on X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis, an aromatic cluster model consisting of pyridinic nitrogen was chosen for simulation. Electronic structure analysis showed that abundant $\pi$ electrons were sufficiently delocalized above and below the basal model plane. Maxima of electrostatic potential existed at every hollow site at the center of a hexagon on van der Waals surface. Calculation results indicated that chemisorptions of iron atoms on seven different hollow sites were exothermic spontaneous reactions with the formation of interstitial iron carbides and iron nitrides. It was verified by reduced density gradient analysis that the Fe atom and nitrogen containing char were mainly combined by electrostatic attraction. Heterogeneous desorption of HCN from nitrogen containing char was endothermic, whether iron was in presence or not. The rate-determining step was the ring-opening process of the nitrogen heterocyclic ring when iron not bonding to the nitrogen atom, while it was the breakage of Fe-N bond when iron combined with the nitrogen atom. Thermodynamic and kinetics analyses found that the participation of Fe had inhibiting effect on heterogeneous formation of HCN except the direct binding of iron to nitrogen.

## 1. Introduction

Nitrogen oxide ($\text{NO}_x$) is one of the main pollutants during pulverized coal combustion, which can result in acid rain, photochemical smog and the destruction of ozone layer. HCN is a major nitrogenous product from pyrolysis and is an important precursor of $\text{NO}_x$

in the subsequent combustion process [1–3]. Clarifying the formation and conversion mechanism of HCN during combustion is of great significance for NOₓ control.

The metal minerals in coal could affect the heterogeneous conversion of nitrogen in coal [4,5]. Iron (Fe) is one of the intrinsic metal elements with high content existing in coal [6,7]. The influence of Fe on the release of HCN in solid fuel pyrolysis and combustion has been extensively studied. Zhao et al. [8] obtained through experiments that Fe and its compounds could suppress HCN formation during pyrolysis and combustion of char. Sun et al. [9] found that impregnating iron compounds as inhibitors seems to be effective for decomposition of HCN. The results from Tsubouchi and Ohtsuka [4] showed that Fe not only had an inhibitory effect on HCN formation, but also the function of promotion for HCN decomposition. It is also speculated by Tsubouchi [10] that iron could firstly interact with char-N (pyridinic functionalities in char) to form interstitial iron nitrides (FeₓN or FeₐCᵦN), which may subsequently decompose to yield N₂. However, Yi et al. [11] found that iron compounds facilitated thermal cracking of heterocyclic N with the release of HCN. It can be seen that the effect mechanism of Fe on conversion of HCN during pyrolysis and combustion of char is still not well understood. Unfortunately, further validation and understanding are limited by the complexity of experimental phenomena and current experimental measuring methods.

Quantum chemistry, one of the most reliable methods to research microscopic reaction mechanisms, has been introduced to investigate nitrogen conversion mechanism during pyrolysis and combustion of coal. The detailed catalytic reduction mechanism of NO without NH₃ injection by graphene-based single atom Fe catalyst was investigated by Yang et al. [12] through density functional theory (DFT) calculation. Liu et al. [13] demonstrated via DFT calculation that alkali earth metal Ca had a stronger influence on the pyrolysis of pyrrole and indole to form HCN. Hou et al. [14] and Ren et al. [15] analyzed heterogeneous adsorption properties of HCN on CaO(100) and Fe(100) through the first-principles calculation. It was found that the adsorption of HCN molecule on perfect Fe(100) surface proved to be chemisorption [15]. The H-downward configuration was the most stable chemisorption on Ca(100) for HCN [14]. The Ca vacancy surface was benefit for adsorption while the temperature increase was negative for HCN adsorption [14]. It can be seen that quantum chemistry calculation plays an important role in elucidating the mechanism of chemical reaction. However, the role of Fe playing in HCN desorption from char has never been discussed in the reported works. Herein, density functional theory was applied in this work to investigate the effect mechanism of Fe on the heterogeneous desorption of HCN from char. It is hoped that an improved understanding of the reaction mechanism could be obtained by this work.

## 2. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses of nitrogen functionalities in char

In order to establish a reasonable model of nitrogen-containing char, the nitrogen functionalities in char were analyzed firstly. Datong coal (bituminous coal) was used in this study to produce char. The coal was crushed and particles between 45 and 74 µm were selected. Preparation of char was conducted in an electrically heated horizontal tube furnace. Before pyrolysis, high-purity Ar (> 99.999%) was introduced into the furnace, which was heated to 1050 °C. The coal sample was place in a quartz boat and rapidly pushed into the constant temperature zone to be calcinated in Ar by maintaining the temperature for 90 min. Further, the char was cooled down to ambient temperature by the continuous supply of Ar and collected for subsequent analysis. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (ESCALAB MARK) was used to determine the valence state of N with radiation ($h\nu = 1253.6$ eV) at 150 W. The N 1 s spectra of Datong coal and the derived char were shown in Fig. 1, which can be fitted by four peaks corresponding to pyridinic N (N-6 at 398.6 ± 0.2 eV), pyrrolic N (N-5 at 400.5 ± 0.2 eV), quaternary N (N-Q at 401.4 ± 0.2 eV), and oxidized N (N-X at 403.2 ± 0.2 eV). The percentage of each nitrogen functionality was calculated based on the area of the fitted peaks and was listed in Table 1. N-5, the dominant nitrogen functionality in Datong coal, was decreased after high temperature pyrolysis. The major nitrogen functionality remaining in char was present as N-6, which was in accordance with results in references [16–18].

### 3.2. Calculation methods

All geometry structures were fully optimized using the B3LYP functional [28]. The effective core potential (ECP) is usually used for heavier elements starting from the fourth period to obtain a balance between calculation accuracy and computational cost. The Lanl2DZ valence electron basis set [29,30] and the pseudo potential basis set of Lanl2DZ inner layer electrons were chosen for Fe atoms. Both valence and sub-valence electron were considered when generating the ECP's for the Lanl2DZ basis function [29,30]. It has been validated that the overall quality of potential energy surfaces and electronic energies for Fe computed from ECP's does not differ significantly from the all-electron calculations [30]. Nonmetallic atoms C, H, O and N were optimized at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level. Previous studies have shown that the problem of spin contamination can be effectively reduced; meanwhile a good balance between calculation accuracy and cost could be achieved at this level [31-33]. The well-established D3 method was introduced to describe London dispersion interaction [34]. The nature of optimized stationary points was characterized by frequency calculations at the same computational level, ensuring that all reactants, intermediates, and products had no imaginary frequency and that transition states had only one imaginary frequency. The intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) paths were traced in both forward and backward directions to confirm that located transition states truly connected two corresponding minima and to obtain the potential energy profiles.

The single-point energy and zero-point correction energy of each stable structure are calculated at the same level. The recommended scale factors 0.9806 for calculating fundamental vibration frequencies and zero-point vibrational energies (ZPVEs) were applied to eliminate the overestimation of ab initio harmonic vibrational frequencies [35]. All quantum chemical calculations were carried out using the Gaussian09 D01 package with the ultrafine integration grids and the tight SCF convergence criterion [36]. Multiwfn was applied for wavefunction analyses [37]. VMD was used to visualize some of the results [38].

The adsorption energy $(E_{ads})$ can be calculated according to Equation (1).
$$
E_{ads}=E_{Fe+sur}-E_{Fe}-E_{sur} \tag{1}
$$
where $E_{Fe+sur}$ is the total energy of the system after one Fe atom adsorbed at different sites on the surface of nitrogen-containing char; $E_{Fe}$ is the energy of one Fe atom; $E_{sur}$ is the energy of C(N).

According to transition state theory [39], the reaction rate constant was calculated to study the kinetic properties of reactions. The calculation formula was shown in Equation (2).
$$
k^{T S T}=\kappa(T) × \sigma × \frac{k_{B} T}{h} × \frac{Q_{T S}}{Q_{A} Q_{B}} × \exp \left(\frac{-E_{b}}{k_{B} T}\right) \tag{2}
$$
where $E_{b}$ is the energy barrier of each reaction step, $kJ·mol^{-1}$; $T$ is reaction temperature, $K$; $k_{B}$ is Boltzmann constant, $J·K^{-1}$; $h$ is Planck constant, $J·s$; $Q_{TS}$, $Q_{A}$ and $Q_{B}$ are the partition function of transition state TS, reactant A and B, respectively; $\sigma$ is the reaction-path symmetry number; $\kappa(T)$, the transmission coefficient, is quantum tunneling correction factor for motion along the reaction coordinate, which can be calculated according to tunneling models of Wigner [40],
$$
\kappa(T)=1+\frac{1}{24}\left[\frac{h v}{k_{B} T}\right]^{2} \tag{3}
$$
where $v$ is the imaginary frequency at the transition state, $cm^{-1}$.

## 4. Results and discussion

Heterogeneous release paths of HCN from char with and without Fe were explored and discussed in this section. In order to comprehensively and accurately analyze the effect mechanism of Fe on HCN desorption from nitrogen-containing char, several different possible adsorption configurations of Fe on the surface of nitrogen-containing char were considered. Then heterogeneous desorption path of HCN from each adsorption configuration was further studied. Thermodynamic and kinetic analyses were also conducted based on calculation results.

### 4.2. Adsorption of Fe

Doping metal atoms into char models have been widely adopted in exploring the effect of metal elements on chemical reactions [43-48]. Li et al. found that the Fe-C bond formed after activation at $300^{\circ}\text{C}$ by experimental and theoretical analyses [11]. Because of the thermal activation procedure, the valence state of the bonded Fe was between Fe(0) and Fe(III). Domazetis et al. found that Fe-C bond formed in mimic pyrolysis of a brown coal model with iron complexes [49]. The adsorption of Fe on graphene monolayers has been investigated in references [50-53] using density functional theory. The Fe atom was found to be most prone to be adsorbed at the hollow (H) site at the center of a hexagon [50]. The electronic structure analyses of the C(N) model in section 4.1 showed that the abundant $\pi$ electrons above the benzene rings were favorable for the adsorption of Fe. The same conclusion, that the Fe atom was most likely to be adsorbed at hexagonal sites of C(N), was also obtained from analysis of ESP on the vdW surface. Therefore, adsorptions of the Fe atom at 7 different hexagonal sites (labeled as H1 to H7 in Fig. 2) were studied in this work.

The Fe$^{\circledR}$M1 to Fe$^{\circledR}$M1 in Fig. 5 were optimized geometric structures from Fe adsorbed at H1 to H7 sites on the C(N) model, respectively. ### 4.3. Heterogeneous desorption of HCN

#### 4.3.1. Microscopic reaction paths

To consider the effect of Fe on heterogeneous desorption of HCN from nitrogen-containing char, we quested various reaction paths based on adsorption configurations Fe<sup>①</sup>M1 to Fe<sup>⑧</sup>M1. The optimized intermediate structures in reaction paths of HCN desorption were drawn in Fig. 7. The abbreviations M, TS, P, and C(N) represented intermediates, transition states, products, and the nitrogen-containing char model, respectively. In addition, the imaginary frequency value of each transition state and some relevant bond lengths were also given in Fig. 7. The corresponding reaction potential energy surfaces were plotted in Fig. 8. The energies shown in Fig. 8(b)-(h) were relative to the sum of energies of C(N) and one Fe atom in free state.

Desorption of $HCN$ from $C(N)$. It was quite obvious in Fig. 7(a) that there were two transition states in the path of HCN desorption from C(N). The elementary reaction C(N) → M1 was a ring-opening process, with the length of C5-N bond increasing from 1.363 Å (C(N)) to 2.247 Å (TS1), which needed to overcome an energy barrier of 349.87 kJ/mol and was 258.11 kJ/mol endothermic. Then the C1-C2 bond was stretched from 1.454 Å (M1) to 2.434 Å (TS2). A complex M2 was formed, from which HCN was released. The whole reaction C(N) → HCN + P was 484.72 kJ/mol endothermic.

Desorption of $HCN$ from $Fe^{②}M1$. We can see from Fig. 7(b) that desorption of HCN from Fe<sup>②</sup>M1 also undergone two elementary reactions. Firstly, the intermediate Fe<sup>②</sup>M1 was converted to Fe<sup>②</sup>M2 via Fe<sup>②</sup>TS1. An energy barrier of 357.89 kJ/mol was needed. The length of C5-N bond elongated from 1.358 Å (Fe<sup>②</sup>M1) to 2.360 Å (Fe<sup>②</sup>TS1). Meanwhile 263.65 kJ/mol of heat was absorbed. Subsequently, the C1-C2 bond was destroyed with an energy barrier of 211.92 kJ/mol via Fe<sup>②</sup>TS2. Finally, HCN was released from the complex Fe<sup>②</sup>M3 with the Fe<sup>②</sup>P formed. The reaction Fe<sup>②</sup>M1 → HCN + Fe<sup>②</sup>P was 487.13 kJ/mol endothermic. The total heat absorbed in the whole process of Fe + C(N) → HCN + Fe<sup>②</sup>P was 266.83 kJ/mol.

Desorption of $HCN$ from $Fe^{③}M1$ to $Fe^{⑧}M1$. As shown in Fig. 7(c)-(g) and Fig. 8(c)-(g), the pathways of HCN desorption (Fe<sup>③</sup>M2) to Fe<sup>⑧</sup>M1 to Fe<sup>⑧</sup>M1 were very similar with that from Fe<sup>②</sup>M1. It would not be described in detail here. The relevant data was summarized in Table 3.

Desorption of $HCN$ from $Fe^{⑨}M1$. According to Fig. 7(h), firstly the intermediate Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M1 overcome 77.93 kJ/mol through Fe<sup>⑨</sup>TS1 to break the C5-N bond, forming a stable intermediate Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M2. About 20.45 kJ/mol of heat was absorbed. The bond length of C5-N was extended from 1.442 Å (Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M1) to 2.001 Å (Fe<sup>⑨</sup>TS1). Secondly, Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M2 conquered 57.92 kJ/mol via Fe<sup>⑨</sup>TS2 to form Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M3. The length of C1-C2 bond was extended from 1.540 Å (Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M2) to 1.903 Å (Fe<sup>⑨</sup>TS2), whereas the length of Fe-C1 bond was shortened from 2.267 Å (Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M2) to 1.917 Å (Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M3), which was 16.25 kJ/mol endothermic. Then Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M3 only needed to overcome 38.31 kJ/mol via Fe<sup>⑨</sup>TS3 breaking the Fe-C1 bond and simultaneously forming the Fe-C2 bond, from which 14.08 kJ/mol was released. Finally, HCN was desorbed from Fe<sup>⑨</sup>M4. The remaining structure recombined to Fe<sup>⑨</sup>P. The whole reaction C(N) + Fe → Fe<sup>⑨</sup>P + HCN was 17.13 kJ/mol endothermic.

According to transition state theory (TST), elementary reaction with the highest activation energy is the rate-determining step of the whole reaction [56]. The reaction rate-determining step for each HCN desorption path can be determined from Fig. 8 and Table 3. We found that the rate-determining steps of HCN heterogeneous desorption from C(N) and from FeⓇM1 to Fe®M1 were the ring-opening process of the six-membered heterocycle, i.e. breakage of the C5-N bond. However, the rate-determining step of HCN desorption from Fe⑦M1 depended on the breakage of the Fe-N bond. In view of this difference, the characteristic of C5-N bond was studied in detail. Mayer bond order can be used to characterize the relative strength of chemical bonds in molecular structures [57]. The formation and breakage of bonds can be inferred by calculating bond orders. So, the C5-N bond orders for C(N) and FeⓇM1-Fe®M1 were calculated and summarized in Table 4. Obviously, the C5-N bond was weakened by the combination of Fe and N. That made it easier to break for the C5-N bond in Fe®M1. ### 4.3.3. Change of Mayer bond orders along IRC paths

In this study, IRC calculations were performed for each transition state in HCN desorption paths to confirm whether they linked the corresponding intermediates correctly. Mayer bond orders of several key bonds of each point in IRC paths were calculated and extracted. Mayer bond orders along IRC of TSs in desorption of HCN from C(N)

Changes of Mayer bond order of the C5-N bond in IRC path of TS1, Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS1-Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS1 were shown in Fig. 9(a). We can see that the bond order of C5-N gradually decreased to zero along the reaction path, which indicated that the C5-N bond was weakened and eventually was broken. According to Fig. 9(b)-(c), both the bond order of C1-N and C1-C2 maintained high value but there was no obvious consistent change trend, indicating that the C1-N and the C1-C2 bond were relatively stable in this process. Fig. 9(d), (e) showed the Mayer bond order change of C1-C2 and C1-N in IRC path of TS2, and Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS2-Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS2. Obviously, the C1-C2 bond order gradually reduced from 0.6-0.8 to 0, corresponding to the breakage of C1-C2 bond. Meanwhile, we found from Fig. 9(e) that the C1-N bond order was getting higher and higher, and finally was closed to 3 along the IRC path, which represented that the C1-N bond was strengthened and the structure of HCN molecule tended to be stable. Fig. 9(f)-(g) showed the energy profiles for IRC paths of the TSs. It can be seen that each TS with the highest energy connected structures with lower energy in both forward and backward reaction directions.

Fig. 10 showed the Mayer bond order change and the energy profiles in IRC paths of HCN desorption from Fe<sup>⊕</sup>M1. It was found that the C5-N bond order along IRC path of Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS1 gradually decreased and finally approached to 0. The continuous decline of C5-N bond order meant that the interaction force between the C5 atom and the N atom become weaker and finally the C5-N bond was broken. In addition, the C1-C2 bond was weakened as its bond order appeared decreased. On the contrary, the C1-N bond was strengthened as the bond order remained high and slightly increased. As can be seen in Fig. 10(b), the C1-N bond order was further increased via Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS2, indicating that interaction force between the C1 atom and the N atom become stronger. The Fe-C1 bond order increased from approximately 0 to 1, corresponding to the formation of Fe-C1 bond. However, the C1-C2 bond order continued to decrease from 1 to 0, revealing the breakage of C1-C2 bond. Furthermore, the Fe-C1 bond was broken via Fe<sup>⊕</sup>TS3 as displayed in Fig. 10(c), because its bond order eventually reached to 0 as the reaction going on. The Fe-C2 bond continued to be strengthened and the five-membered heterocycle consisting of the Fe atom and four C atoms tended to be stable. In Fig. 10(d), the bond order of Fe-N decreased to 0, while bond order of the C1-N increased to 3, corresponding to the desorption of HCN.
