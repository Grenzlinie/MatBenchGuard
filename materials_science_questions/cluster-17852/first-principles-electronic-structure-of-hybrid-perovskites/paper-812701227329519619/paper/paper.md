# Charge Transport between Coaxial Polymer Nanorods and Grafted All-Inorganic Perovskite Nanocrystals for Hybrid Organic Solar Cells with Enhanced Photoconversion Efficiency

Dhirendra K. Chaudhary, Anima Ghosh, Md. Yusuf Ali and Sayan Bhattacharyya*

Department of Chemical Sciences, and Centre for Advanced Functional Materials, Indian Institute of Science Education and Research (IISER) Kolkata, Mohanpur - 741246, India

* Email for correspondence: sayanb@iiserkol.ac.in


### Abstract

The versatile optoelectronics properties of perovskite nanocrystals (NCs) have provided a strong surge for their utilization in different classes of solar cells, organic photovoltaic (OPV) systems being no exception. In an unprecedented approach, a hybrid solar cell with $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs strategically grafted on poly(3-hexylthiophene-2,5-diyl) (P3HT) nanorods (NRs) is shown to have a photoconversion efficiency (PCE) of $9.72 \pm 0.4$ %, with only 1.5 wt% NCs. The improvement is twice more than the P3HT:PCBM reference devices ($4.09 \pm 0.2$ %). The choice of NC composition is validated by Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations which show decent charge carrier mobility in $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$, besides having better stability than $CsPbI_3$, making $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs suitable contender for hybrid device architecture. A trivial blending of the NCs in P3HT:PCBM matrix results in their non-uniform distribution, escalating charge carrier trapping, albeit maintaining a device efficiency of $8.07 \pm 0.3$ % with 1 wt% NCs. Uniform NC grafting is propitious over inhomogeneous blending since $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs not only act as additional light harvesters, but their chemical grafting onto the P3HT NRs improves the charge transport by creating better charge percolation pathways. The higher crystallinity of the P3HT NRs than P3HT also helps in reducing the trap states.

## Introduction

Besides the emergence of perovskite solar cells (PSCs),¹ OPV technology is a fast evolving energy harvesting option due to their advantageous properties such as low cost, large area device, high mechanical flexibility, light weight, compatibility with roll-to-roll processing.²⁻³ The potential of this technology could be understood from the improvement in device efficiency, reaching a record value of ~17.3% via solution processed tandem device architecture.² OPV devices are fabricated using $\pi$-conjugated molecules whose light absorption within a very specific range limits the spectral response. Hence the concept of organic-inorganic hybrid solar cell devices came into the picture which allows a wide range of tunability in absorption and other optoelectronic properties of hybrid organic-inorganic photoactive layer using suitable nano/quantum structures.⁴⁻⁵ In fact a large number of nanomaterials consisting of binary, ternary and quaternary metal chalocogenides, pyrite, carbon nanostructures, wide band gap semiconductors have been used already.⁶⁻¹¹ By employing CdTe NCs in poly(p-phenylenevinylene) (PPV) blend a device efficiency of ~5.64% is reacheable.¹² Hybrid devices with iodine passivated colloidal PbS NCs provide ~4.8% device efficiency,¹³ and with Si NRs/PEDOT:PSS photoactive layer, ~8.40% is achieved.¹⁴ These results clearly show that hybrid solar cell technology has the potential to come up with commercially viable technology. In this context, the marriage between PSC and OPV is highly fascinating to design stable and efficient alternatives.¹⁵

In the hybrid OPV devices, efficiency improves due to improvement in short circuit current density ($J_{SC}$) or fill factor ($FF$) of the devices whereas in most reports, open circuit voltage ($V_{OC}$) remains invariant with nanoparticle blending.¹⁶ In fact the $V_{OC}$ of P3HT based devices could be improved by tuning the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of

fullerene acceptors through derivatization to say, indene-C₆₀ bisadduct (ICBA) and IC₇₀BM etc.¹⁷,¹⁸ These fullerene multiadducts however possess lower mobility in comparison to the monoadduct besides favoring exciton recombination.¹⁹ Higher $V_{OC}$ can be obtained through appropriate photoactive layer engineering such as using P3HT:ICBA. $V_{OC}$ is also enhanced by certain fullerene-free electron transport materials.²⁰,²¹ However synthesis of these materials requires multi-step processes which increases the overall production cost in case of large scale production. The exciton diffusion length in organic semiconductors is very small; within <30 nm, and therefore while synthesis one has to consider the domain size of organic donor and inorganic acceptor that should be less than or equal to the exciton diffusion length. Grafting of inorganic NCs on polymeric nanostructures is a practical solution to overcome this limitation, for example facile charge transport was facilitated when CdSe NCs were grafted on P3HT NRs.²² The phase separation of inorganic NCs from non-polar conjugated polymers at higher loading concentrations decreases the interfacial area, which can be partly mitigated by suitable solvent and processing conditions.⁵ A monodispersed NC grafting can be better achieved on one- or two-dimensional (1D or 2D) polymeric materials with efficient photochemical generation of spatially separated charge carriers providing an efficient donor/acceptor interface to achieve improved PCE.²³

Among the NCs to be grafted, all-inorganic halide perovskite ($CsPbX_3$, $X=Br^-$, $I^-$, $Cl^-$) NCs are the suitable choice because of their low exciton dissociation energy, ambipolar charge transport, long electron–hole diffusion length and high absorption coefficients with tunable optical band gap.²⁴⁻²⁶ It has been observed that PV devices fabricated using these NCs have high $V_{OC}$ due to their inherent defect tolerance.²³,²⁷ Moreover, the conduction and valence band positions of $CsPbX_3$ are suitable for its application as ternary blend in organic solar cells. While

the fabrication of all-inorganic perovskite NC sensitized solar cells require sequential multilayer deposition for making a ligand free photoactive layer, the hybrid solar cell device architecture allow single step deposition and polymeric matrix may render the desired film uniformity. In our earlier finding,⁠²³ we observed that the $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ is more stable as compared to other halide compositions and based on this understanding, herein we have fabricated organic-inorganic hybrid solar cells using $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs in an inverted device architecture such as $FTO/TiO_2/P3HT:PCBM:CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}/MoO_3/Au$ by NC grafting onto P3HT NRs. The grafting of NCs onto P3HT NRs improves the charge transport which yields a significant improvement in device performance in contrast to the inhomogeneous NC blending within the polymeric matrix. This is indeed a prodigious strategy in OPV since P3HT is already known to be a useful hole-transporting material for fabricating stable PSCs,⁠²⁸⁻²⁹ but their combined light harvesting ability has been seldom utilized.

Experimental and Computational Methods

Materials: Lead (II) bromide ($PbBr_2$, 99.999% trace metal basis), lead (II) iodide ($PbI_2$, 99.999% trace metal basis), cesium carbonate ($Cs_2CO_3$, reagentplus, 99%), octadecene (ODE, technical grade, 90%), oleylamine (OAm, 70%), and oleic acid (OAc, 90%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. P3HT (regioregular), PCBM (>99.5%), Ti-isopropoxide (97%), lead nitrate ($\geq$99.9% trace metals basis), hexane (fraction from petroleum), ethanol (synthesis grade), isopropyl alcohol (synthesis grade), acetone (synthesis grade), cyclohexanone (CHN, ACS reagent, $\geq$99.0%), 1,2-dichlorobenzene (DCB, anhydrous, 99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl,ACS reagent, 37% ) and methyl acetate (MeOAc, Synthesis grade) were purchased from Merck.

**Synthesis of Cs-Oleate:** A mixture of 0.18 g $Cs_2CO_3$, 1 ml of OAc and 8 ml of ODE was heated at $120^o$C under vacuum for 30 min. When the temperature reached $120^o$C, the reaction medium was stirred for another 30 min followed by $N_2$ purging till a clear solution was obtained. The solution was kept at this temperature for further use.

**Synthesis of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs:** 50 ml of ODE was added to a mixture of $PbBr_2$ and $PbI_2$ (0.5 mmoles each) and stirred under vacuum at $120^o$C for 1 h and then at $140^o$C for 30 min followed by $N_2$ purging. 5 ml each of OAm and OAc was added to the above solution under $N_2$ flow to make a clear solution. 4 ml Cs-oleate was added and the reaction medium was introduced in an ice-water bath after 5 sec to quench the reaction.

**Isolation of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs:** NC isolation was performed by centrifuging the NC solution at 7000 rpm for 10 min. The precipitate was separated and dissolved in hexane. MeOAc was added to the above solution and centrifuged again at 700 rpm for 10 min. A hexane solution of the precipitate was then kept at $4^o$C for 12 h. The supernatant was centrifuged and the precipitate dried under vacuum. The $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs were washed with lead nitrate, dissolved in MeOAc and used for device fabrication after vacuum drying.

**Synthesis of P3HT NRs:** The P3HT NRs were prepared by adding 200 µl cyclohexanone into a pre-dissolved solution of 12.5 mg P3HT in $800\mu$L DCB. After adding cyclohexanone, the solution was kept for 10 min for molecular self-assembly and used after 5 min rigorous stirring at room temperature.

**Blending of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs with P3HT:** For blending $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs with P3HT, the as washed NCs were added in different wt% in the P3HT:PCBM solution.

Grafting of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs onto P3HT NRs: $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs were grafted on the coaxial P3HT NRs by adding 200 µl of the NCs (from a stock solution) to a cyclohexanone medium containing different concentrations of P3HT.

Preparation of $c$-$TiO_2$ precursor: A dilute acidic solution was prepared by drop wise mixing diluted HCl (3:1) in 2.5 ml ethanol. This acidic solution of ethanol was mixed drop wise under constant stirring with titanium isopropoxide in ethanol. The resulting solution was stirred for 30 min to yield a homogenous, clear and transparent precursor solution.

Device Fabrication

The devices were fabricated onto pre-patterned FTO coated glass substrate of sheet resistivity 5-7 $\Omega$/sq. Prior to deposition of any layer, the substrates were cleaned by soft soap solution, deionized water, acetone, isopropyl alcohol for 10 min each and then dried under $N_2$. For the removal of residual organic contaminants, the substrates were cured in ultraviolet ozone light for 15 min. Thereafter the $TiO_2$ thin film of thickness $40 \pm 3$ nm was deposited by spin coating using the $TiO_2$ precursor solution at 2000 RPM for 30 sec. Active layer of the control devices (organic solar cells) was prepared by blending P3HT:PCBM (12.5:10 w/w mg) in 1,2-dichlorobenzene and stirred overnight. In case of NC blended system, different amounts (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 wt%) of NCs were blended in the active layer solution. Thin films of P3HT NRs and $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NC grafted P3HT NR solutions were prepared by spin coating at 800 RPM for 30 s. The as deposited films were dried for 20 sec at room temperature (inside the $N_2$ filled glove box). After the slow drying step, the films were annealed at $100^\circ$C for 10 min on preheated hot plate. Then the substrates were transferred in thermal evaporator chamber for the sequential deposition of 5 nm $MoO_3$ at deposition rate 0.1-0.2 $\mathring{A}$/sec and 100 nm gold using a shadow mask at the rate of 0.2-

$0.5$ Å/sec at a base pressure of $1.8\ (\pm0.2)\times10^{-6}$ torr. The thickness of thermally deposited $\text{MoO}_3$ and Ag films were estimated *in situ* via quartz crystal thickness monitor. The active device area was maintained at $0.25\ \text{cm}^2$.

### Characterization and Measurements

UV-vis absorbance spectra were recorded using Jasco V-670 spectrophotometer. Room temperature PL spectra were measured with Horiba JobinYvon Fluorolog using a Xe lamp as the excitation source with an excitation wavelength near the band edge. The XRD measurements of thin films were carried out by in grazing incidence mode with $\text{Cu K}\alpha$ radiation whereas the crystallinity of $\text{CsPbBr}_{1.5}\text{I}_{1.5}$ NCs was recorded by Rigaku (mini flex II, Japan) powder X-ray diffractometer having $\text{Cu K}\alpha=1.54059$ Å radiation. The FESEM images were obtained using Carl Zeiss SUPRA 55VP FESEM. TEM images were recorded with JEOL, JEM-2100F microscope using 200 kV electron source equipped with the energy dispersive analysis of X-rays (EDAX) setup at the DST-FIST facility, IISER Kolkata. To record the TEM and STEM-HAADF images, SAED patterns, EDAX and elemental mapping, the NCs were dispersed in hexane.
Photovoltaic performances were measured under simulated AM 1.5 sunlight using Batsol solar simulator model no. PEC-L01, a class AAA solar simulator calibrated for $100\ \text{mW cm}^{-2}$ light intensity (in constant intensity mode) calibrated using National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) silicon reference cell. EQE of the solar cells were recorded using calibrated AM 1.5G solar photon flux in the spectral range of 300 nm to 800 nm. *J-V* and CV studies were performed with BioLogic electrochemical workstation (Model No. SP-300). The CVs were recorded in conventional three electrode cell; the three electrode cell was assembled by using Pt wire as the counter electrode, $\text{Ag/0.01 M AgNO}_3+0.1\ \text{M TBAPF}_6$ in acetonitrile as the reference electrode and glassy carbon as the working electrode.

### Computational details

The crystal structure, DOS and band structure calculations were carried out using the all-electron, full potential, linear augmented plane wave (FP-LAPW) method as implemented in the WIEN2k code. The Brillouin zone was sampled using $6 \times 6 \times 6$ $k$-points to obtain the electronic band structures and total DOS for all the structures. $R_{mt} \times K_{max}$ was set to 9, where $R_{mt}$ is the smallest muffin-tin radius. The radii of the muffin-tin spheres are $2.5$ $a_0$ for Cs, Pb, I, and Br, where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius. Inside the muffin-tin spheres the valance electron wave functions were expanded with spherical harmonics up to $l_{max} = 10$.The convergence of the self-consistent calculations remained the same with earlier calculation of a total energy tolerance of $10^{-5}$ Ry. Since Pb is a heavy element with strong relativistic effect, SOC effect is strong and was therefore considered in the study of electronic properties of Pb-containing compounds. SOC severely affects the band gap value, underestimated from GGA/PBE results. In order to have more accurate band gap values, TB-mBJ potential was considered.

### Results and Discussion

The self-assembly of NRs is driven by the addition of a poor solvent and explained by rearrangements of polymeric moieties.$^{30}$ When cyclohexanone (CHN; as poor solvent) is added to P3HT dissolved in 1,2-dichlorobenzene (DCB), the P3HT molecules start minimizing the unfavorable contacts between the backbone and hydrocarbon solvent due to solvophobic interaction which populate the chain-chain interaction and results in the unidirectional growth of P3HT molecules.$^{30,31}$ With a CHN dispersion of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs, the self-assembled P3HT NRs are grafted by the NCs in a very compact manner due to non-covalent interaction between P3HT NRs and $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs (Figure 1a). The NC grafted molecular self-assembled NRs

allow precise control over the molecular rearrangement which significantly impact the PV performance (discussed later). The high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) image in Figure 1b clearly shows the uniform grafting of NCs and the elemental mapping demonstrates spatial distribution of the individual elements.

The phase indexing matches to the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Figure S1a). The Cs:Pb:Br:I compositional ratio of 21:19:31:28 is verified from energy dispersive analysis of X-rays (EDAX) showing iodine deficiency relative to the precursor ratio (Figure S1b). HAADF-STEM mapping shows the absence of any phase segregation or secondary phase formation in the NCs (Figure S1c). The NCs show an absorbance band at ~560 nm which corresponds to its band gap of ~2.21 eV and the photoluminescence (PL) maximum near 566 nm is very close to absorption band edge (Figure 2d).

DFT calculations on different compositions of $CsPbBr_xI_{3-x}$ demonstrate the compatibility of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs for hybrid device architecture (Figure 2e,f). Crystal stability and electronic structure calculations of three different systems $CsPbI_3$, $CsPbI_{1.5}Br_{1.5}$ and $CsPbBr_3$ were performed based on DFT formalism as implemented in Wien2k code. $^{32}$ To replicate $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ we have considered half bromination in $CsPbI_3$ with the constituent elements in $2 \times 2 \times 2$ super cell containing 8 Cs, 8 Pb, 12 Br and 12 I. Starting from the respective experimental lattice parameter $a$ of 5.868, 6.348 and $6.07$ Å for $CsPbBr_3$, $CsPbI_3$ and $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ cubic unit cells having space group $Pm-3m,^{33-35}$ their crystal structures were optimized at the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), $^{36}$ with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange co-relation potential, and Tran and Blaha modified Becke and Johnson (TB-mBJ) potential. $^{37,38}$ The calculated formation energies of the optimized structures of $CsPbI_3$, $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ and $CsPbBr_3$ are -13.02, -14.32 and -15.67 eV, respectively. These results show better stabilization of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ over $CsPbI_3$ structure validating the experimentally observed results of enhanced stability with $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ over $CsPbI_3.^{23}$

Figure S2a,b show the density of states (DOS) and band structure with the spin–orbit coupling (SOC) of cubic $CsPbI_3$ where the direct band gap open at point $R$. The conduction band minimum (CBM) originates primarily due to Pb $6p$ orbital whereas its valence band maximum (VBM) is derived from the mixture of Pb $6s$ and I $5p$ states. Without SOC, the calculated band gap is 1.79 eV (considering Tb-mBJ potential) and CBM is six-fold degenerate including spin degeneracy. SOC on Pb splits its CBM into doublet ($j = 1/2$) and quartet ($j = 3/2$) and this splitting lowers the CBM and elevates the VBM providing a smaller band gap of 0.83 eV. Figure 2e,f show the band structure and DOS with SOC effect of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ where the band gap is 2.1 eV and increases further to 2.42 eV for $CsPbBr_3$. However considering SOC effect, the band

gap reduces to 1.19 eV for $CsPbI_{1.5}Br_{1.5}$ and 1.97 eV for $CsPbBr_3$. In case of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$, CBM arises from Pb $6p$ and the most dominating states in VBM come from Pb $6s$. The contribution of I $5p$ is greater in VBM than that of CBM as seen from the partial DOS (Figure S2 e). Here Br $4p$ lies just below 0.86 eV from band edge in VBM and 0.3 eV above the CBM edge. In $CsPbBr_3$ the contribution of Br $4p$ in both VBM and CBM is more intense than the $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ system (Figure S2c,d).

The physical consequence of the electronic band structure is in the form of effective masses of charge carriers, which can be predicted from the shape of VBM and CBM correlated to the mobility of charge carriers. When electron is excited from VB to CB by an incident photon, the CB and VB curvatures determine the effective mass of the electron and hole, respectively. $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ has the optimum balanced CB and VB curvatures at $R$ point in comparison to $CsPbI_3$ and $CsPbBr_3$. A mixed hybridization of half Br $4p$ with I $5p$ balances the optimum curvature of CB with VB in $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$. In case of $CsPbI_3$ CBM is more flattened in nature as compared to VBM. Because of the optimum shape of VB and CB, $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs are expected to provide a decent mobility of charge carriers.

When the self-assembled growth is carried out for 10 min, average diameter of the P3HT NRs is 22 nm (Figure S3a) which is very close to the exciton diffusion length (20-25 nm) of P3HT. The uniform grafting of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs on P3HT coaxial NRs is clearly observed in Figure 3a. The NC lattice fringes observed from the HR-TEM images in Figure 3b,c, SAED pattern in Figure 3d and the lattice spacing of (110) reflection of $\alpha$-CsPbI$_3$ phase in Figure 3e give further evidence of their successful grafting. XRD pattern of the grafted NRs shows P3HT reflections at lower $2\theta$ $(^o)$ and due to a preferred arrangement of the self-assembled P3HT molecules by $\pi$-$\pi$ stacking, the peak intensity increases (Figure 3f). The preferred arrangement of

the molecules through $\pi$-$\pi$ stacking is affirmed by recording the P3HT and P3HT NR thin films (Figure S3b). The absorption spectra of NC grafted NRs in Figure 3g show the convolution of absorbance from both P3HT NRs and $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs. Combining the Tauc plot (not shown) from absorbance data with cyclic voltammogram (CV) plots (Figure 3h),$^{39}$ it is observed that the positions of highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of P3HT remain unchanged after formation of the NRs.

Before adjudging the advantages of NC grafting on P3HT NRs, it is necessary to understand the consequences of NC blending within P3HT:PCBM photoactive layer (Figure S4). During both blending and grafting of NCs, four different NC loadings are used viz. 5, 10, 15 and 20 mg which correspond to 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 wt% of NCs with respect to P3HT, respectively. Figure 4a shows the schematic diagram of the NC blended device architecture and the cross-sectional FESEM image in Figure 4b shows the thickness of photoactive layer to be ~120 nm. The energy band diagram of the device (Figure 4c) suggests that the excitons generated within P3HT layer could be dissociated either at the P3HT/PCBM or P3HT/NC interface. As observed from the J-V characteristics in Figure 4d, blending of the perovskite NCs in different ratios significantly alter the $V_{OC}$, $J_{SC}$ and other device parameters (Table 1). P3HT:PCBM devices without the NCs show $V_{OC}$ ~0.63 V whereas upon incorporation of 0.5 wt% NCs, $V_{OC}$ jumps to ~0.96 V with observable improvement in $J_{SC}$ and $FF$. The improvement of $J_{SC}$ could be attributed to the generation of additional charge carriers by the perovskite NCs. The conduction band of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NC lies between P3HT and PCBM to allow a better cascading pathway for charge extraction from P3HT (Figure 4c). Due to the strong visible light absorption of $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs, photon generation is significantly enhanced in the range 550-300 nm, also validated from the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of devices fabricated with 1 wt% NCs in P3HT:PCBM (Figure 4e). Without PCBM, the devices with P3HT:$CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs (1:1 w/w ratio) show a significant drop in $J_{SC}$ and $FF$ due to poor charge transport although $V_{OC}$ remains at ~0.92 V (Figure S5).

A maximum PCE of 8.07 ± 0.30 % is obtained by blending 1 wt% NCs blended in P3HT:PCBM, represented as P3HT:PCBM:NCs(1 wt%) (Table 1). Junction capacitance of these devices is calculated from depletion approximation, using capacitance-voltage (C-V)

measurements (Table S1). According to the depletion approximation there are no free carriers in the space charge region and junction capacitance can be expressed as:

$$
\frac{1}{C^{2}}=\frac{2}{q \varepsilon \varepsilon_{0} N A^{2}}\left(V_{b i}-V\right) \tag{1}
$$

Figure 4f shows the Mott-Schottky plot (1/C² versus V) of devices where $V_{bi}$ estimated from the intercept of the linear region on $x$-axis is found to be ~0.62 and 1.0 V for P3HT:PCBM and P3HT:PCBM:NCs(1 wt%) blended devices, respectively, in good agreement with $V_{OC}$ of the respective devices. The microscopic interfaces can be further explored by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).⁴⁰ The solid lines in Nyquist plots measured at 0 V under 100 mW/cm² illumination (Figure 4g) show a good fit of experimental data using the equivalent circuit shown in Figure S6. The resistance $R_g$ is typically interpreted as the resistance associated to electron transport, $C_g$ represents dielectric component of the diode, $R_{rec}$ is associated with the recombination resistance and $C_\mu$ is the distributed chemical capacitance. The fitted parameters of the impedance equivalent circuit are summarized in Table S1. In consideration of the recombination centers and impurity induced spatial in-homogeneities at the interfaces, the constant phase element (CPE) has been used instead of ideal capacitance. Series resistance ($R_S$) is the intercept point of real part of Nyquist plot and associated with external contact resistance and sheet resistance of the electrodes. Among the two semicircles, the smaller semicircle at high frequency region corresponds to the charge transfer process between active layer and selective contacts whereas the larger semicircle at low frequency domain shows the recombination of charge carriers in photoactive layer. The surface recombination lifetime ($\tau_{surface}$) of charge carriers has been estimated from the product of $R_{rec}$ and capacitance. In presence of NCs, comparatively high charge trapping occurs due to random distribution of the blended NCs.

Figure 4h shows the sub-linear trend in the enhancement of $J_{SC}$ as a function of light intensity, $P_{Light}$ in log-log scale. The experimental data is fitted with equation (2) to determine the recombination coefficient ($\alpha$) in the photocurrent loss mechanism.

$$
J_{SC}(P) = P_{light}^{\alpha} \tag{2}
$$

If $\alpha$ is close to 1, nongeminate recombination such as trap-assisted recombination processes under short-circuit conditions are negligible, and a value smaller than 1 indicates the presence of both trap-assisted recombination and interfacial recombination in the solar cells. For P3HT:PCBM and P3HT:PCBM:NCs photoactive layer based devices, $\alpha$ is found to be 0.63 and 0.51, respectively which suggest the prevalence of trap-assisted recombination and interfacial recombination in both the device configurations. Figure 4i shows the $P_{Light}$ dependence of $V_{OC}$, which can be fitted according to equation (3):

$$
V_{OC}(P)=\frac{nkT}{q}\ln\left(P_{Light}\right)+C \tag{3}
$$

where $n$ is the ideality factor, $k$ is Boltzmann constant, $q$ is the absolute value of electron charge, $T$ is temperature and $C$ is a constant. In an ideal $p$-$n$ junction when the band-to-band recombination dominates, $n$ is close to 1 and when trap assisted midgap or interfacial recombination occurs, it ranges between 1 and 2. In our case the ideality factor is found to be 1.24 and 1.61 for P3HT:PCBM and P3HT:PCBM:NC based devices, respectively, which suggests the prevalence of trap assisted recombination with blended NCs. The results of intensity dependent $J_{SC}$ and $V_{OC}$ strongly corroborate the EIS results.

Systematic grafting of the NCs on P3HT NRs can resolve the disadvantages of trap assisted recombination in an inverted device architecture shown in Figure 5a. The NRs of 20 nm diameter, synthesized by 10 min self-assembly, have been employed for device fabrication. When devices are fabricated with 0.5 and 1 wt% NCs grafted on P3HT NRs, $J_{SC}$ of the devices increases whereas a partial reduction in $FF$ is observed as compared to the case of NC blending (Figure 5b and Table 2). $J_{SC}$ increases due to increased charge separation between the chemically grafted NCs and the NRs whereas the small drop in $FF$ arises from insufficient NC coverage on

the NRs. This is evident from the increase in FF to 0.7 with higher NC loading of 1.5 wt%, which however drops again with 2 wt% NCs. Higher loading of NCs on the P3HT NR surface renders increased NC-NR interfacial roughness with more probability of charge trapping.

With a solar cell configuration such as FTO/TiO$_2$/P3HT:PCBM:NCs (1.5 wt%)/MoO$_3$/Au, a maximum PCE of $9.72 \pm 0.4$ % is achieved. The Nyquist plots (Figure S7) with 1.5 wt% NC grafted P3HT NRs as the photoactive layer suggest low charge transfer resistance (Table S2).

The trap state passivation is firstly assisted due to higher crystallinity of P3HT NRs than bulk P3HT and secondly due to the chemical grafting of NCs resulting in stronger NR-NC electronic interactions (Figure 5c). This interpretation is also supported by the steady increase in $J_{SC}$ and $V_{OC}$ with increasing light intensity (Figure S8). Moreover on increasing the NC concentration the

photon harvesting efficiency increases in the 300-550 nm wavelength region as validated by the EQE spectra (Figure 5d). When the self-assembly growth is maintained for 2 h, P3HT NRs with thicker diameters are formed. Devices fabricated with ~250 nm diameter P3HT NRs grafted by 1.5 wt% NCs however demonstrate a lower PCE (Figure S9). Since the exciton diffusion length in P3HT is very short (20-25 nm), most of the photogenerated excitons harvested in P3HT participate in geminate recombination (Figure S9e). The comparative literature presented in Table S3 with data from devices fabricated using ternary P3HT:PCBM photoactive layers containing different nanomaterials and polymers clearly shows that perovskite NC is the best candidate to improve the PCE of the widely investigated P3HT:PCBM based devices.⁴¹⁻⁶⁹

The mastery of NC grafting on the NRs is also evident from the self-life time and degradation studies of the devices, which were performed following the International Summit on Organic Photovoltaic Stability (ISOS)-D1 protocols, under which the devices were stored in the dark under ambient conditions (temperature 24ºC ± 3ºC and relative humidity 44 ± 6%) and the performances were measured periodically under simulated solar light. Inverted organic solar cell devices without $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs show a three-stage temporal degradation (Figure 5e and Figure S10a-c). In the absence of NC grafting, rapid degradation occurs at stage (I) in the initial 100 h with respect to $J_{SC}$ (~35%), $FF$ (~20 %) and relatively smaller drop in $V_{OC}$ (<5%), which collectively affect the PCE by ~40%. Stage (I) degradation attributes to interfacial degradation whereas the slow degradation in stage (II) from 100 to 500 h is due to intrinsic or oxidation driven degradation of the photoactive layer. Due to degradation of photoactive layer a significant drop in $FF$ and $V_{OC}$ has been observed after 500 h in stage (III) because of partial degradation in anode buffer layer and the photoactive layer which rapidly drops the $FF$, $J_{SC}$ and $V_{OC}$. In contrast, the devices fabricated with NC grafted P3HT NRs, the initial degradation in stage (I) is

rapid because of interfacial degradation whereas the degradation rates in stage (II) and stage (III) are slow because of the P3HT NR coverage by ambient stable NCs. The life time of the NC grafted photoactive layer is ~380 h in comparison to only ~120 h for P3HT:PCBM based devices. Even under constant illumination, $J_{SC}$ of the devices with NC grafting remains unchanged whereas that of P3HT:PCBM based devices drops by ~4% within 2000 s (Figure S10d) due to molecular agglomeration during substrate heating. This provides strong evidence to believe that the NCs are facilitating significant device stability even under biased and constant illumination conditions.

## Conclusions
To summarize, all-inorganic perovskite $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs are grafted on P3HT NRs in an unprecedented approach to boost the performance of organic-inorganic hybrid solar cells. Besides grafting, blending of the NCs in P3HT:PCBM results in their non-uniform distribution within the photoactive layer which although improves the $V_{OC}$, but suffer from significant charge trapping. To counter charge trapping at the random NC-P3HT interfaces, an optimal grafting ratio of NCs on P3HT NRs is employed in the photoactive layer and the devices with 1.5 wt% NCs yield the best PCE of $9.72 \pm 0.40$ %. Herein the strategically grafted NCs increase the light harvesting efficacy in addition to trap state passivation due to higher crystallinity of P3HT NRs and chemically bound NCs coverage resulting in stronger NR-NC interactions. The NC grafted P3HT NR devices are more stable in ambient atmosphere and the lifetime is found to be at least ~380 h. Our findings suggest that the versatile $CsPbBr_{1.5}I_{1.5}$ NCs can be used as photo-harvesting as well as charge transport materials for the fabrication of ambient stable hybrid solar cells.
