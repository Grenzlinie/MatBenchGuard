## BaGe₈As₁₄ - a semiconducting sodalite type compound†

Valentin Weippertᵃ, Thanh Chauᵃ, Kristian Witthautᵃ, Lucien Eisenburgerᵃ and Dirk Johrendt*ᵃ

Received 00th January 20xx,
Accepted 00th January 20xx
DOI: 10.1039/x0xx00000x

The new sodalite type compound BaGe₈As₁₄ was synthesized via solid-state reactions and structurally characterized with single crystal X-ray diffraction (space group $\overline{4}3m$). Vertex-sharing GeAs₄-tetrahedra form the β-cages with additional Ge/As mixed sites located slightly above or below the six-membered rings. The structure is similar to the borate mineral rhodizite. The barium atoms are disordered by a slightly shift off the centers of the large β-cages. This partially disordered structure together with a narrow band gap of 0.43 eV in line with low resistivity ($2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\Omega$cm), and a high carrier concentration ($1.6 \cdot 10^{20}\, \text{cm}^{-3}$) at 300 K qualifies BaGe₈As₁₄ as a potential thermoelectric material.

Cage compounds represent a fascinating class of solids and attract considerable interest.¹ The group of the zeolites, although first mentioned in 1756, is one of the most intensely researched fields in chemistry, tendency increasing. According to *SciFinder* it produced over 900 publications annually since 2016. Zeolites allow a multitude of applications ranging from ion exchange or -absorption, energy storage, thermoelectric materials to catalysts in petrochemical cracking.²⁻⁶ The variety of the zeolite frameworks bases on variations of the Al/Si ratio, substitution of framework atoms, and different ways to connect cages and other structural motifs. As of today the *Database of Zeolite Structures* lists 252 different framework types.⁷ A base motif of many zeolites is the sodalite cage, originating from the equally named mineral Na₈(Al₆Si₆O₂₄)Cl₂.⁸ Combined with other cages it occurs in many frameworks.¹ The sodalite framework (SOD) itself is composed of all-face-sharing β-cages packed in a bcc arrangement. Different coordinations of the cage positions and incorporation of guest atoms lead to a large variety of sodalite type compounds.¹, ⁹ Almost all are oxides, like Zn₄O(BO₂)₆, [GaGe₅O₁₂]⁻ or Na₈(Si₉Be₃O₂₄)Cl₂,¹⁰⁻¹² while non-oxide sodalite frameworks are rare. Known examples are Cu₁₂Sb₄S₁₃, Zn₇(P₁₂N₂₄)Cl₂¹³⁻¹⁶ and [Cu₂Zn₁₀Cl₂₄].²,¹⁷ Consequently, semiconducting intermetallic compounds with sodalite cages are largely unexplored. Just these could be of particular interest for high performing thermoelectric materials as they offer the possibility to incorporate so-called "rattling atoms" in the large void of the sodalite cage. This concept already lead to promising results in skutterudite compounds like Fe₃CoSb₁₂ and doped CoSb₃, IrSb₃ or Ba₈Ga₁₆T₃₀ ($T$ = Si, Ge) for clathrates.¹⁸⁻²¹ Herein we present BaGe₈As₁₄, the first Ge-As based sodalite type compound. It features a disordered Ba position in an unusually large coordination sphere as well as promising semiconducting properties.

Figure 2 shows the environment of the disordered Ba position. The $\overline{4}3m$ symmetry at the center results in a tetrahedral array of Ba atoms each occupied by 25 %. The coordination is a pseudo Frank Kasper polyhedron with $CN = 9{+}7$ comprising four Ge/As and twelve As with two sets of distances: $d_{(CN\ 9)} = 3.607(2)$ Å - 3.869(2) Å and $d_{(CN\ 7)} = 4.113(2)$ - 4.271(2) Å (Figure 2).

Low temperature single crystal X-ray experiments show that the displacement of the Ba atoms is static. Corresponding crystallographic data, atomic positions and anisotropic displacement parameters are in Tables S5-13. Only the disordered Ba position yielded excellent results with small residuals. Refinements with Ba at the centre (2a) position result in large displacement parameters, which do not extrapolate to zero at low temperatures (Figure 3).

![](./images/812527243396907010_7.jpg)

Figure 3. $U_{eq}$ of an ordered Ba position at $2a$ (0, 0, 0) and the Ge and As positions in BaGe₈As₁₄ at 293, 218, 189, 143 and 103 K with regression lines.

Since Ge and As are indistinguishable through laboratory scale X-ray diffraction, therefore we checked a possible ordering of the Ge/As site with selected area electron diffraction (SAED) experiments. SAED simulations with the ordered structure in space group $P\overline{4}3m$ with As and Ge on different 4e sites show weak additional spots with $h + k + l \neq 2n$, which are not visible in the measured patterns (Figure S1). Thus, the body-centred space group is correct. STEM-HAADF images (Figures 4 and S2) perfectly coincide with the structure overlay, which further supports the structure.

![](./images/812527243396907010_8.jpg)

Figure 4. Crystal structure of BaGe₈As₁₄ in [111] direction combined with a projection of one unit cell from the STEM HAADF image in Figure S2 (Ba: yellow, Ge: brown, As: black).

Given the charge neutral formula, we expected semiconducting properties. The optical bandgap from UV-Vis-NIR measurements is 0.43 eV or 0.45 eV assuming indirect or direct transitions in the Kubelka-Munk function, respectively (Figure 5). The resistivity of a compacted and sintered BaGe₈As₁₄ pellet is $2.2{\cdot}10^{2}\,\Omega$cm at 300 K and increases with decreasing temperature (Figure S3). From a fit of the Arrhenius-plot (Figure S4) we get very small activation energies of 0.01-0.03 eV, similar to what we found in related compounds.²³ This indicates extrinsic conductivity through dopant levels close to the conduction band. Small amounts of impurities hardly contribute to the optical absorption, so the optical band gap is much larger and closer to the intrinsic value. Hall Effect measurements indicate the p-type nature of BaGe₈As₁₄ (Figure S5-6) and yield carrier concentrations of $1.6{\cdot}10^{20}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}$ (300 K) and $2.3{\cdot}10^{20}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}$ (400 K ) typical for a doped semiconductor (p-type GaAs: $8{\cdot}10^{18}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}$)²⁴ with low mobility values of $\approx 2\,{\rm cm}^{2}{\rm V}^{-1}{\rm s}^{-1}$.


![](./images/812527243396907010_9.jpg)

Figure 5. Tauc plots of a diffuse reflectance spectrum of BaGe₈As₁₄ for direct and indirect band gap transitions.

![](./images/812527243396907010_10.jpg)

Figure 6. PBE band structure of BaGe₈As₁₄ indicating an indirect band gap.

DFT band structure calculations reveal indirect band gaps of 0.36 eV, 0.52 eV (Figure 6) and 0.88 eV using PBESol,²⁵ PBE,²⁶ and SCAN²⁷ functionals, respectively (for computational details see the ESI). We do not expect an accurate calculated gap because we cannot treat the disorder and have used an ordered structure in space group $P\overline{4}3m$. However, the calculations indicate that the measured optical gap of 0.43 eV is indirect. The density of states plot (DOS, Figure S7) shows high contributions of Ge and As states below the Fermi level, while the Ba states are in the conduction band.

The Bader analysis yields the charge neutral formula $Ba^{1.47+}(Ge^{0.38+})_6(Ge^{0.13+})_2(As^{0.05-})_2(As^{0.26-})_6(As^{0.39-})_6$. While the Ba position has ionic character, the charges of the polyanionic network are significantly lower when compared to the oxidation numbers $Ba^{+2}(Ge^{+4})_6(Ge^{+2})_2(As^0)_2(As^{-2})_6(As^{-3})_6$. This indicates a strong covalent character of the Ge/As network.

Rietveld refinements of X-ray powder diffraction data (Figure 7) confirm the single crystal structure (Table S15). The sample consists of 96 wt.-% BaGe₈As₁₄ with 3 % GeAs₂ and 1 % Ge impurity phases, respectively. High temperature powder X-ray diffraction patterns (Figure S9) reveal that BaGe₈As₁₄ is stable up to 873 K.

![](./images/812527243396907010_11.jpg)

Figure 7. Powder X-ray diffraction pattern and Rietveld refinement of BaGe₈As₁₄ with minor impurities of GeAs₂ and Ge.

EDX measurements confirm the composition BaGe₈As₁₄ within 5 %, which is the typical error of the method: Ba 4.4(3) at.-% [4.3], Ge 33.0(7) at.-% [34.8] and As 62.6(8) at.-% [60.9]. Values in square brackets were calculated from the formula.

BaGe₈As₁₄ is to the best of our knowledge the first Ge/As based sodalite type compound. The barium atoms are too small to fit the large sodalite framework, and are therefore disordered around the center. A $Ge_{0.5}As_{0.5}$ mixed site introduces additional disorder. Such structural disorders act as phonon scattering centers, which typically reduce the thermal conductivity $\kappa$. BaGe₈As₁₄ is however a relatively good electronic conductor with low resistivity ($\rho_{300K}=2.2{\cdot}10^{-2}\Omega cm$). Both properties together are promising prerequisites for a thermoelectric material, where the product $\rho{\cdot}\kappa$ needs to be small for high efficiency, while it is difficult to optimize.
