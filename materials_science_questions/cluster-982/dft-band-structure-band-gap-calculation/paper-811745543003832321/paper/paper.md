# Role of Electron-Hole Interactions in the Optical Spectra of Metals
Gianni Mula, $^{(a)}$ Roberto Car, $^{(b)}$ and Sokrates T. Pantelides
IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center, Yorktown Heights, New York 10598
(Received 24 December 1986)

It was recently argued that, contrary to what has been universally believed, single-particle theory is not adequate to describe the optical spectra of metals and that electron-hole (excitonic) effects play a dominant role. Here, we present theoretical calculations using two different conceptual frameworks which indicate that the opposite is in fact true. We also identify the inappropriate approximations that exaggerated the role of excitonic effects and conclude that the latter play only a minimal role in the optical spectra of metals.

PACS numbers: 78.50.Jg, 71.35.+z, 78.40.Kc

Optical excitations in crystalline solids can be described, to a first approximation, as single-particle interband transitions, i.e., transitions of electrons from occupied to empty single-particle Bloch states. Inclusion of electron-hole interactions is, in general, difficult, so that their role has been the subject of many controversies. By now, it has been established that electron-hole effects play a dominant role in both the optical and x-ray absorption spectra of insulators. These spectra generally exhibit strongly bound excitons below the interband threshold and a strongly bound modified interband continuum. $^{1,2}$ In semiconductors, electron-hole effects are considerably weaker. They generally introduce weakly bound Wannier excitons below interband thresholds and modify the interband continua far less than they do in insulators. $^{3,4}$ In metals, where screening is more effective, it has been universally believed that electron-hole effects are still weaker, introducing edge singularities in the x-ray spectra but having a negligible effect on optical spectra. Recently, however, this point of view was challenged. First, Bakshi et al. $^{5}$ presented an analysis of the optical spectra of Mg-Li alloys and suggested that the observed peaks, including the peak in the spectrum of pure Mg, are due to localized states similar to excitons in insulators. In a subsequent paper, Kunz and Flynn $^{6}$ reported a theoretical interband optical spectrum for Mg, calculated from correlation-corrected Hartree-Fock energy bands. They found poor agreement with the experimental spectrum and attributed this discrepancy to the omission of electron-hole interactions. They then included electron-hole interactions by treating the interaction strength as an adjustable parameter and were able to reproduce the experimental spectrum. They concluded that electron-hole effects are essential for a proper description of the optical spectra of metals. These conclusions were discussed further in the two later papers of Bakshi et al. $^{7}$ and Boisvert et al. $^{8}$ In this paper, we present a series of calculations and corresponding analysis of experimental data which lead us to the opposite conclusion. We also identify the inappropriate approximations in the work of Refs. 5 and 6 and thus conclude that electron-hole effects play only a minimal role in the optical spectra of most metals.

We begin by presenting our calculations and results. We have employed two very different conceptual frameworks for the calculation of the optical conductivity of Mg in the limit of negligible electron-hole interactions: (1) We used density-functional theory in the local-density approximation. $^{9}$ We carried out a self-consistent band-structure calculation $^{10}$ for fcc Mg, which is the structure assumed in Ref. 6, and then computed the interband optical conductivity. (2) We used a superposition of Abarenkov-Heine model potentials $^{11}$ which are free-ion pseudopotentials screened by dielectric functions that incorporate electrostatic, exchange, and correlation effects. With this potential, we obtained the interband optical conductivity of Mg in both the hypothetical fcc and the actual hcp structures.

It is clear that single-particle theory, as implemented by our calcu- lations, is in satisfactory agreement with experiment, ob- viating the need to invoke electron-hole interactions. We note that the square-shaped peak at about 1.5 eV in the theoretical fcc spectrum goes away in the hcp calcula- tion, in agreement with experiment. This small discrepancy is consistent with the fact that the local-density approximation yields semicon- ductor band gaps which are somewhat smaller than ex-perimental values. $^{14}$

The above results demonstrate the importance of hav- ing a reliable single-particle theory before its failure to agree with experiment is taken as evidence for significant many-body effects. We wish to argue that the approxi- mations employed in Ref. 6 are responsible for the failure of that interband spectrum to agree with the cal- culations reported here and with experiment. We note two crucial approximations: (1) Though exchange is treated exactly in the Hartree-Fock calculation, correla- tion is then added by perturbation theory. The correc- tions are, however, very large, making perturbation theory questionable; the main rise in the Hartree-Fockoptical spectrum is lowered from about 7 eV to about 3 eV. (2) The correlation corrections were calculated by the treatment of Mg as a homogeneous electron gas hav- ing the average density of real Mg. One can immediate- ly see that this is a poor approximation. The sum of ex- change plus correlation of the homogeneous electron gas is essentially dispersionless. $^{15}$ Thus, if one were to treat both exchange and correlation in the manner that corre- lation was treated in Ref. 6, one would conclude that a Hartree calculation is a good approximation and that ex- change plus correlation merely introduce a rigid shift of the band structure. That is not, of course, true. Ex- change and correlation are equally important $^{15}$ and must be treated to a comparable degree of accuracy by one's taking into account the variations in the charge density present in a real crystal. The local-density approxima- tion $^{9}$ appears to be the best way currently available to treat exchange and correlation on an equal footing, espe- cially for free-electron-like metals. It has been found satisfactory for ground-state properties of metals $^{10}$ and semiconductors. $^{16}$ It has also been found satisfactory for the optical spectra of semiconductors $^{17}$ (see discussion below). The work reported in this paper and earlier cal- culations $^{18}$ confirm that the same general approach also works well for the optical spectra of metals.

In the more recent paper by Boisvert et al., $^{8}$ small clusters of atoms (up to nineteen atoms) were used to in- vestigate the localization of excitations. It is not at all clear that such small clusters can satisfactorily simulate a metallic environment. Furthermore, it is not at all surprising that localized states are obtained when the central atom is an impurity. It is, however, impressive that even in a nineteen-atom cluster simulating pure Mg metal, the excitations are found to be delocalized over the entire cluster, supporting our conclusions.

The conclusions of Ref. 6 can also be questioned on the basis of the magnitude of the electron-hole interac- tion strength needed to reproduce the experimental spec-trum, namely 3.5 eV. $^{19}$ Earlier, Kunz and Flynn $^{20}$  found an effective electron-hole interaction strength of4.7 eV for the optical spectrum of LiF, which is a wide- gap insulator. We consider it very unlikely that electron-hole interactions have roughly the same strength in metals and wide-gap insulators. One should also com- pare with the optical spectra of semiconductors, which should reflect electron-hole interactions of intermediate strength. In a detailed calculation on Si, Hanke and Sham $^{4}$ found that electron-hole interactions alter the heights of peaks, but produce minor shifts in their posi- tions (~0.2 eV). This result is consistent with our con- clusions, but contrasts sharply with Ref. 6, according to which electron-hole effects in Mg shift the primary peak by about 3-4 eV.

Finally, we reexamine the analysis of Ref. 5 which was the first to suggest that optical spectra of metals aredominated by electron-hole interactions. Bakshi et al. $^{5}$  examined the optical conductivity of Mg-Li alloys at compositions ranging from $100 \%$ to $0.55 \% Mg$ . They pointed out that the sharp peak of the spectrum of pure Mg can be tracked continuously to higher energies in the

spectra of the alloys all the way to the alloy with only 0.55% Mg. At that limit, one actually has a dilute con- centration of Mg impurities in metallic Li so that it seemed proper to interpret the observed peak as largely an intra-atomic Mg excitation. The continuous tracking of this peak all the way to pure Mg led to the suggestion that, even in that case, the peak is likely to be due large- ly to an intra-atomic Mg excitation. A careful scrutiny of this analysis, however, reveals a flaw. In their Fig. 2, Bakshi et al. $^{5}$ displayed the conductivity for $Mg_{x} Li_{1-x}$ alloys for $x=1$ to $x=0.189$ as measured by Mathewson and Myers, $^{13}$ omitting the spectra for smaller values of x and x=0. Instead, on the same figure, for $0.0055 \leq x$ ≤ 0.081, they displayed their own difference spectra, i.e., the optical conductivity of the alloy after subtracting from it the optical conductivity of pure Li. Thus, the plot of Ref. 5 manifestly treats the two metals on different footings. This difficulty was recognized in Ref.7, but an internally consistent plot over the entire alloy range was not examined. In Fig. 3, we show just such a plot, i.e., the optical conductivities of several Mg-Li al- loys measured by Mathewson and Myers, $^{13}$ ranging from pure Mg to pure Li. We note that the optical threshold moves smoothly to higher energies in the entire alloy series. Also, the peak broadens and moves to higher en- ergies as the Li content increases. Though in the last two curves there are no data beyond ~3.8 eV, the avail- able data suggest that, for x=0.047, the drude absorp- tion is followed by a broad peak centered at ~3.3 eV, and for x=0 (pure Li), the corresponding peak is fur- ther out (~4 eV). New, more complete data for small values of x, including x=0, would certainly be valuable. The available data, however, indicate that the main peak can be tracked continuously from pure Mg all the way to pure Li or vice versa. Thus, one cannot conclude that the peak is largely due to intra-atomic Mg excitations. Instead, the proper explanation, given in fact in Ref. 13, is that the alloy data reflect a change in the Fermi-level position in a nearly free-electron band structure. We tested this notion further by computing the optical con- ductivity of pure Li using the Abarenkov-Heine model potential. We find an optical threshold at ~2.7 eV, in very good agreement with the data, and a smooth rise into a very broad peak. The value of the optical conduc- tivity is roughly 2 orders of magnitude weaker than in Mg, as in the data.

In closing, it is useful to give a qualitative explanation of the shapes of the observed spectra (see, e.g., Ref. 13). As is well known, the interband optical conductivity of a homogeneous electron gas vanishes because the opticaloperator does not couple two different plane waves; $\langle k|p| k'\rangle=\delta_{k k'}$ . In real metals, contributions to the op tical conductivity come primarily from the regions near the gaps at the Brillouin-zone boundaries where plane- wave admixture occurs. Now, Mg is strikingly free- electron-like $^{21}$ and the Fermi level passes through small gaps at the Brillouin-zone boundary. $^{21}$ The sharp peak at ~0.7 eV is, therefore, due to transitions across this small gap. As Li is mixed in, the nearly-free-electron band structure remains largely unchanged, but the Fermi level moves to lower energies because of the smaller number of electrons. Thus, optical transitions involve larger energies and occur between states far from the gaps where plane-wave admixture is weak, leading to smaller values of the optical conductivity.
