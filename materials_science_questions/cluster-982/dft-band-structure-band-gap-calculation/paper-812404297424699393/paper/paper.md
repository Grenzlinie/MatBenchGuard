# POSITRON DEFORMATION POTENTIAL AND DIFFUSION CONSTANT IN InP

Z. BOUSAHLA*, B. ABBAR and H. AOURAG

Computational Materials Science Laboratory, Department of Physics, University of Sidi, Bel Abbes, 22000-Sidi Bel Abbes, Algeria

(Received 29 October 1996; revised 7 June 1997; accepted 26 June 1997)

**Abstract**—On the basis of the empirical psudopotential method and independent particle model, electronic and positronic band structures are obtained. The calculated electron and positron chemical potentials give the positron and positronium work functions. The deformation potential model is used in the treatment of the contribution to the relaxation by a positron-acoustical phonon, which is needed to calculate the positron diffusion constant. Calculations of the diffusion-related quantities are presented. © 1998 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

**Keywords**: electronic and positronic band structures, deformation potential, diffusion constant

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The properties of solid surfaces and the near-surface regions have recently been investigated by slow-positron-beam techniques [1,2]. An aspect of paramount importance in this area is the transport of positrons occurring in, or close to, the energy regions of thermal equilibrium with the medium where interactions are most sensitive to the structural details of the solid. The knowledge of the positron diffusion coefficient in solids is of great interest for fundamental understanding of positron interactions in solids. The absolute value of $D^{+}$ is a measure of the strength of the various positron–solid interaction mechanisms. The study of positron motion in elemental semiconductors began with the drift-velocity measurements of Mills and Pfeiffer [3,4]. Another detailed study of positron diffusion was carried out for several Si crystals by Nielsen *et al.* [5]. Jorch *et al.* [6] have also experimentally studied the motion of positrons in the near-surface region of Ge. In this work, the authors showed that the positrons exhibit behaviour which is not consistent with conventional weak carrier phonon scattering theory. The literature gives several examples of measurements addressing this specific point that have been interpreted in terms of simple diffusion mechanism involving thermal positrons (see, for instance, the recent measurements of positron diffusion constants in a number of metals by Soininen *et al.* [7]). But so far there are few measurements that are providing new insights on positron interactions with surface in compound semiconductors. Quantitative analysis is more difficult, but progress is being made in this area, which is more advanced for the case of GaAs [8–16] than for InP. It should prove useful that investigations on the limits of the diffusion approximation are certainly desirable in InP. In order to give additional information on compound semiconductors, we have chosen for our study InP as a prototype.

The main purpose of this work is to extend a theoretical analysis on a positron diffusion from longitudinal acoustical phonons contribution [17], which can predict quantities directly measurably by slow-beam techniques and then to discuss the possibilities of surface emission in InP.

First, we calculate the positron band structure, in particular the bottom of the lowest-energy band, parallel with electron band structure. The relative positions of the energy levels of delocalized positron and the electron chemical potentials in perfect bulk InP determine the positronium work functions and, if the electron work function is known, so is the positron work function. These quantities are very important parameters in slow-positron-beam experiments. Moreover, the volume dependence of these levels (the deformation potential) can be used to estimate the positron phonon coupling strength and thereby the positron diffusion constant.

The firm theoretical basis of this work lies in calculations of electron structure using the empirical pseudo-potential method (EPM) with adjusting form factors [20], whereas the calculation of the delocalized positron Bloch states is based on the independent particle model (IPM). The calculation of the electronic structures of solids and the corresponding positron states can be elegantly formulated using the density-functional theory [18]. Unfortunately, this is not yet the practical calculation method for positrons in semiconductors. The positron screening by electrons is not perfect owing to the existence of the band gap. This has to be taken into account

We remark, at this point, that while the positron in the solid is a part of an inherently many particle system with important many- body interactions, the quantum independent particle model is often very useful. Bloch positrons in a solid can be scattered by the lattice vibrations, i.e. phonons, by impurities, defect, or disorder, and by excited electrons or holes, in particular, if we assume that acoustic phonon scattering is the prevailing lattice scattering mechanism and the interaction is weak [21]. The lattice distortion surrounding the positron is small. The positron thus retains its independent particle character with some scattering due to phonons. These aspects make the study of the positron's motion a fascinating one and have prompted us to take calculations based on the EPM coupled with IPM in InP.

## 2. CALCULATIONS
The local pseudopotential method and the independent particle approximation are used to calculate the electron and positron band structures, respectively [22, 23].

The potential for a thermalized positron inside the solid relative to the vacuum level is a combination of the positron chemical $\mu_{+}$and the surface dipole $\Delta$, which yields a general expression for the positron work function [24, 25]
$$\phi_{+}=-\Delta-\mu_{+}. \tag{1}$$

Note that $\Delta$ and $\mu_{+}$in eqn (1) have the same reference. The present bulk calculations performed in the EPM coupled with the IPM give the positron and electron levels relative to the crystal zero (see Fig. 1 for a schematic picture). The chemical potential $\mu_{+}$is equal to the energy of the lowest positron state, measured from the crystal zero. The definition of $\phi_{+}$is analogous to that for the electron work function,
$$\phi_{-}=\Delta-\mu_{-}. \tag{2}$$

The above definitions imply that surface changes (involv- ing only the dipole $\Delta$) would be reflected equally in $\phi_{-}$ and $\phi_{+}$, as was demonstrated experimentally by Murray et al. [26]. In this work, however, the electron chemical potential is the distance between the Fermi level and the crystal zero. The positronium work function $\phi_{\mathrm{ps}}$ is another fundamental quantity that characterizes the posi- tron energetics in a perfect solid. The formation of Ps has not been observed either directly or indirectly inside defect-free metals or semiconductors. In such materials Ps will form only at the surface via the Coulomb inter- action between incident positrons and surface electrons of the atomic layer of the surface. The energy balance gives
$$\phi_{\mathrm{ps}}=\phi_{-}+\phi_{+}-0.5 \mathrm{Ry}, \tag{3}$$
which is less than the positron work function because the electron work function is usually less than $\frac{1}{2}$ Ry. With use of eqn (1) and eqn (2) we can further write
$$\begin{aligned}
\phi_{\mathrm{ps}} & =\left(-\mu_{+}-\Delta\right)+\left(\Delta-\mu_{-}\right)-0.5 \mathrm{Ryd} \\
& =-\mu_{+}-0.5 \mathrm{Ryd}.
\end{aligned} \tag{4}$$

![](./images/812404297424699393_2.jpg)

Fig. 1. Different energy levels for positrons and electrons in InP. The positron work function $\phi_{+}$consists of the electrostatic surface dipole barrier $\Delta$ and the chemical $\mu_{+}=E_{\text {corr }}+E_{0}$. The surface dipole is the same as for electrons but it has an opposite sign. The chemical potential $\mu_{-}$is the electron Fermi energy.

Theoretically, in a perfect solid, positron coupling to a acoustic phonons has long been recognized as the dominating scattering process [17]. $D_{+}$ depends on the strength of the positron-phonon interaction which is described by the deformation potential $\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{d}}^{+}$ [27]

$$
D_{+}=[8 \pi / 9]^{1 / 2}\left(\hbar^{4}\langle\mathrm{Cii}\rangle\right) /\left(m^{*^{5 / 2}}\left(k_{\mathrm{B}} T\right)^{1 / 2} \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{d}}^{+^{2}}\right). \quad(5)
$$

where $m^{*}$ is the positron effective mass, $T$ the absolute temperature, and $\langle\mathrm{Cii}\rangle$ the elastic constant associated with longitudinal waves over the directions of propagation. The calculation of the average in $\langle\mathrm{Cii}\rangle$ has often been approximated with the formula [28]

$$
\langle\mathrm{Cii}\rangle=\mathrm{C} 12+2 \mathrm{C} 44+3 / 5(\mathrm{C} 11-\mathrm{C} 12-2 \mathrm{C} 44). \quad(6)
$$

The deformation potential $\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{d}}^{+}$ is defined as $\Omega \partial E^{+} / \partial \Omega$, where $E^{+}(\Omega)$ is the total energy of the crystal in volume $\Omega$, with the positron in its lowest Bloch state. Bergersen et al. [17] calculated $\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{d}}^{+}$, following Hodges and Stott [25] as

$$
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{d}}^{+}=\Omega \frac{\mathrm{d} \mu_{+}}{\mathrm{d} \Omega}+\Omega \frac{\mathrm{d} \mu_{-}}{\mathrm{d} \Omega}. \quad(7)
$$

The first term describe the volume dependence of the positron chemical potential which includes the positron zero-point energy $E_{0}$ and the electron-positron correla- tion energy $E_{\text {corr }}$. The second term is included in order for the electron chemical potential to remain constant in the presence of the lattice distortion. For a long wavelength deformation, this contribution to the positron deforma- tion potential takes the form given in eqn (7).

Finally, the deformation potential and the positronium work function are not dependent on the surface barrier potential $\Delta$, which, being an important ingredient in surface electronic states, is more difficult to calculate accurately.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

The calculated electron and positron band structures in InP are displayed in Figs 2 and 3, respectively. The positron band structure is rather free-particle-like and resembles closely to the lowest valence electron band. Also, the positron effective band mass at the $\Gamma$ point is near the free-particle mass, $m^{*}{ }_{\mathrm{b}}=0.9 m_{\mathrm{e}}$. A more complex effect is the contribution to the effective mass of the $\mathrm{e}^{-}-\mathrm{e}^{+}$ interaction [29]. On the other hand, so far the phonon part has not been calculated in detail, although this is expected to be small [30,31]. Since no firm values exist for the positron effective mass, we here use $m^{*}=1.3 m_{\mathrm{e}}$ throughout, which is a compromise over the various theoretical and experimental determinations. The adjusted form factors and the calculated and measured principal energy levels are collected in Table 1 and Table 2, respectively. It is found in com- paring the theoretical calculations and the experimental measurements of gaps that there is a good agreement between theory and experiment [32].

InP seems to have a larger negative value of $A_{+}$. As a consequence, the Coulomb repulsion felt by positrons in the large open interstitial regions results in strong positron affinity.

A theoretical proposal for the direct experimental determination of the electrostatic dipole barrier of a metal was discussed in detail by Oliva [33]. In this work, however, $\Delta$ is estimated by the help of the calculated $\mu_{-}$ and the measured electron work function [34]. Using 5.65 eV for $\phi_{-}$, the value of the dipole surface in InP is determined to be 4.98 eV. An important point is the smaller magnitude of as compared with $\mu_{+}$ and, therefore, the positron energy level in bulk is lower than in vacuum. Fig. 1 reveals that for electrons in bulk InP $\mu_{-}$ is negative and that the electron work function is due predominantly to the surface dipole (i.e. $\Delta \gg |\mu_{-}|$). According to Table 4, one should note that our theoretical work predicts a positive positron work function (2.6 eV). The positronium work function is given by eqn (4). The value of $\phi_{\text{ps}}$ obtained in this study is 1.45 eV. As a result InP is one of III-V compound semi-conductors from which neither positrons nor positronium would be produced from thermalized positrons that diffuse to the sample surface. The localized electron states within the forbidden energy gap and the larger density can affect positronium formation. On the other hand, we know that InP is used as a material for devices of fast electronics and opto-electronics. Defects or impurities play an important role in its production or performance. It would be better to know the experimental values of $\phi_{+}$ and $\phi_{\text{ps}}$ for more certainty. We hope the accuracy of the experimental determination will improve, and more experiments will be done on InP as well as on other zinc blend-type semiconductors.

The diffusion constant $D_{+}$ at 300 K is readily evaluated from the deformation potential using eqn (5). The results are given in Table 5. The theoretical estimate for $\mathbf{E}_{d}^{+}$ in InP is based upon the calculation of the electron and positron energy levels. It is indeed due essentially to changes in $\mu^{+} + \mu_{-}$ with volume. We can calculate the positron parameter $\mathbf{E}_{d}^{+}$ from the slope in Fig. 4, we find a value of 5.14 eV. Substituting into eqn (5) using $T =$ 300 K gives us the diffusion constant $D_{+} = 3\ \text{cm}^{2}\text{s}^{-1}$ due to longitudinal acoustic phonon scattering. In turn, the diffusion length in InP is $L_{+} = (D_{+} \tau)^{1/2} = 3000\ \mathring{\text{A}}$, where $\tau$ is the bulk lifetime and assumed to be 244 ps [35]. Also, mobility calculations can tell us whether a particle moves by hopping or by propagation in a band, and whether scattering from impurities or phonons is dominant. In the latter case we may determine the strength of the positron interaction with phonons. The positron mobility was expressed in terms of the diffusion coefficient by means of the Einstein relation $\mu = D_{+} e/k_{\text{B}}T = 115\ \text{cm}^{2}\ (\text{V.s})^{-1}$. It is interesting to compare our results with the theoretical deformation potential in other compound semiconductors.
