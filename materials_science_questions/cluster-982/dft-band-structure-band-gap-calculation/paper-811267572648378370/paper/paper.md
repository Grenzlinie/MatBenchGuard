## Engineering organic semiconducting solids. Multicomponent access to crystalline 3-(4-aryl-1,2,3-triazolyl)coumarins

J.E. de la Cerda-Pedro, $^{a}$ R. Arcos-Ramos, $^{c}$ M. Maldonado-Domínguez, $^{a}$ S. Rojas-Lima, $^{b}$ M. Romero-Ávila, $^{a}$ M.P. Carreón-Castro, $^{c}$ R. Santillan, $^{d}$ N. Farfán, $^{a*}$ and H. López-Ruiz $^{b*}$

Crystalline 3-(4-aryl-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)coumarins (ATCs) were prepared from commercial materials using a four-component methodology as key step. In the present work, a feasible and environmentally friendly route to the title compounds was developed through the reaction between salicylaldehydes, ethyl bromoacetate, phenylacetylenes and sodium azide under mild conditions, with short reaction times and simple workup. Crystalline solids are readily accessed from the featured products *via* solution processing and their arrays in the solid-state were elucidated through SXRD; these molecules display periodic overlap of $\pi$-systems, which facilitates carrier transport in organic electronic devices. Semiconductor band gaps for the obtained solids were derived through plane-wave DFT and compared with reference systems known to display superior performance in organic electronics. Thus ATCs represent attractive systems for research and implementation in molecular materials, a task which will be facilitated by the concise route herein described.

### Introduction

Coumarins have been extensively investigated regarding their outstanding pharmacological activity, $^{1}$ such as anticancer, $^{2}$ anti-influenza $^{3}$ and anti-HIV agents. $^{4}$ Additionally, their remarkable optical properties $^{5}$ have allowed their use as laser dyes $^{6}$ and fluorescent molecular tags. $^{7}$ Tuning the electronic properties of these compounds, with donor and acceptor groups, gives control over electronic structure in the $\pi$-conjugated system. $^{8}$ Coumarins may be tuned further by linking an aryl or heteroaryl moiety to position C-3, as Branco *et al.* recently reported. $^{9}$ Particularly, donor-acceptor couples at positions C-3 and C-7 display maximum synergism, red-shifting absorption, accentuating charge-transfer and strengthening the nonlinear-optical (NLO) response of coumarin derivatives. Approaches to build NLO materials are based on intermolecular interactions or charge transfer processes between chromophores. $^{10}$

We recently designed a family of *push-pull* donor-(amide bridge)-acceptor systems based on coumarins, $^{11}$ featuring self-assembly into periodically stacked lattices. Since organic electronics require materials to be in the solid state, it is desirable to have a facile and reproducible means of getting good quality crystals. In this regard, coumarins represent an interesting motif for exploration, as these compounds display extensive $\pi$-stacking in the solid state, an interaction to take advantage of during the design of molecular crystalline materials. $^{12}$ Molecular crystals constitute promising materials in organic electronics, given the precise order in their structure and their natural lack of grain boundaries. $^{13}$ Organic electronics is a continuously growing area of research, and the development of new and efficient devices, such as single crystal transistors (SCTs), relies on the development of crystalline dielectrics with adequate properties, high stability and solubility in common solvents for low-cost processing.

The construction of diverse and elaborated heterocycles from simple starting materials, for application in materials science, remains a continuing challenge in organic synthesis. Multiple component reactions (MCRs), due to their convergent nature, are ideal tools to address this issue. $^{14}$ One of the principal axes in our research group is the development of accessible and versatile one-step transformations. $^{15}$ In general, the preparation of 3-(4-aryl-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)coumarins, ATCs, has been described as a multistep procedure that requires the synthesis of 3-azidocoumarins (see Scheme S1). $^{16}$ However, 3-

The regioselectivity and efficiency of click-chemistry reactions under mild conditions opens the possibility to multi-component protocols using accessible starting materials to engineer complex crystalline solids, with well-defined structures and photophysical properties. Herein we describe an exceptionally mild one-step, piperidine and Cu-catalyzed synthesis of ATCs **5a-l** in the presence of sodium azide. The reaction showed considerable tolerance to different functional groups; its operational ease, compared to protocols already described, makes it even more interesting and readily applicable. The chromophores obtained were studied through UV-Vis absorption and emission techniques, to record their excitation-relaxation fingerprints in solution. Time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) was applied to describe the observed electronic excitations for each compound. All the single-crystals obtained were studied through X-Ray diffraction and plane-wave DFT, elucidating periodic structures and approaching the electronic features of these organized solids. As will be shown, these molecules represent interesting systems for the development of low molecular weight materials, due to their facile preparation and ease of crystal growth from solution in common solvents.

## Results and Discussion
### Multicomponent synthesis of 3-(1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)coumarin ATCs derivatives (5a-l).

Initially, studies were carried out using salicylaldehyde **2a**, ethyl bromoacetate **3**, phenylacetylene **4a** and sodium azide in the presence of varying amounts of piperidine and CuI at reflux temperature (80 °C); prototypical substrates for reaction parameters optimization were chosen as shown in Scheme 1. Therefore, a 3:1 mixture of iPrOH/DMF was chosen as the solvent, and several experiments were carried out (Table S1). The iPrOH:DMF system was selected because it has been proved effective for rate acceleration of copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC).¹⁵ᵇᶜ Data clearly shows that the best conditions for the formation of coumarin **5a** required 0.1 equiv. of CuI and 1.25 equiv. of piperidine and 1.1 equiv. of Et₃N at 80 °C for 4 h (entry 4 Table S1). Although all solvents are tested useful for the process, reaction proceeds faster and usually in better yields in a 3:1 solvent mixture of iPrOH/DMF. The generality of the method was examined by using different substituted salicylaldehydes **2** and phenylacetylenes **4**; the results are summarized in Table 1. The one-step MCR methodology showed a considerable tolerance to different functional groups. The introduction of an electron-donating group (EDG = methoxy or diethylamino) in the *meta* position of the salicylaldehyde provided better yields, in comparison to *para* substitution; this can be rationalized as increased nucleophilicity of OH groups, due to delocalization of nonbonding pairs from EDGs.

The absorption spectrum of chromophore **5a** shows three bands located at 252 nm, 290 nm and 338 nm. These bands are very sensitive to substitution and, according to TDDFT studies, correspond to π-π* excitations of the 3-(1,2,3-triazolyl)coumarin core. Inclusion of different substituents (electron-donor at C-7 and chlorine at C-6) over the coumarin unit, as featured by **5c, 5e, 5f** and **5k**, induces a red-shift of the π-π* transition band. In the case of compounds **5b, 5d** and **5g-j**, in which halogen atoms and methoxy groups are located in the phenyl ring and also at C-6, a decrease in intensity of the same band is observed (see ESI).

The starker spectral changes are observed in compounds **5c** and **5f**, whose absorption profiles which shift towards a "camel back" shape with two strong absorption bands between 255-275 nm and 415-425 nm (longest/maximum), contrasting with the parent **5a**. According to TDDFT results, the most important shifts arise from a π-π* transition, involving polarization of the coumarin core along the mutually conjugated positions 3 and 7. This excitation shifts from a HOMO-3 -> LUMO topology in **5a** to a HOMO -> LUMO transition in both **5c** and **5f**, where a polarization bias, parallel to the ground-state dipole moment, is present, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

ATCs **5a-l** display fluorescence in dilute solution, with λ<sub>max</sub> between 415-501 nm. The fluorescence of coumarins **5d, 5f, 5i** and **5k** is strongly dependent on substitution with electron-donor groups over the coumarin core and the remote phenyl ring, as can be appreciated in Fig. 1.

DFT study of electronic properties of ATCs (5a-l).

Among the most intensively studied molecular crystals for organic electronics are those derived from acenes and derived compounds. Pentacene is probably the most representative member of this family of semiconductors.¹⁷ The chemical instability of pentacene, which is prone to oxidation and Diels-Alder cycloaddition,¹⁸ has prompted the search for alternative, tunable molecules with attractive electronic features and increased stability, thus the present research was conducted with this goal in mind.

A set of compounds 6a,¹⁹ 6b²⁰ and 6c,²¹ was chosen for benchmarking purposes (See ESI). All molecules in the reference set are organic, composed of aromatic or heteroaromatic rings linked through single bonds, and thus possess inherent conformational freedom in solution, as the featured compounds 5a-l. HOMO and LUMO energies for these reported compounds, allowed us to test our computational methods. The reference set has shown superior performance on experimental devices, and thus represents an adequate standard to compare our compounds.

To study frontier orbital values, a set of theoretical methodologies was tested against reported values for the reference set (See ESI). The best results were obtained from the hybrid B3LYP functional, as implemented in Gaussian '09.²² This was chosen, thus, as the standard functional for single molecule computations throughout this work, with the 6-31+G(d) basis set. Frontier orbital energies are shown in Fig.3.

Compounds 5f and 5k present computed HOMO levels which suggest they can behave as p-semiconductors with Au electrodes (work function of -5.10 eV), if an adequate crystal packing is obtained.²³

Single X-ray diffraction studies.

Suitable crystals for SXRD studies of 3-(4-aryl-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)coumarins, ATCs: 5a, 5b, 5c, 5f, 5h, 5j, 5k and 5l were grown during slow evaporation of ethyl acetate or methylene chloride solutions in open glass vials. Crystallographic data are summarized in Table 3. A relevant feature is that all crystal structures are solvent-free (Fig. 4). Solvates are common in organic crystals, and such impurities may severely affect device performance and stability.¹³

The featured compounds prone to crystallization, and soluble in organic solvents and thus suitable for solution-processing, important due to low cost, applicability to printable electronics and the possibility of growing large-area single crystals. Different intramolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions S(5), S(5) and S(6) with average distances 2.46 Å, 2.59 Å and 2.36 Å, respectively, were found as common structural motifs, holding together all crystal structures obtained. Dihedral angles between selected planes: (1) coumarin core, (2) triazole and (3) benzene were calculated using all non-hydrogen atoms through the graphical interface from SHELXL²⁴ and Mercury²⁵ programs; the results are summarized in Table S2. The dihedral angle values found confirmed the quasi-planarity of the molecules. In general, smaller values for dihedral angles between the coumarin core and the aryl-substituted ring favor intramolecular electron density delocalization over the $\pi$-conjugated system.

The crystal structure of coumarin 5a was solved in the monoclinic space group P 2₁/n. As expected, the triazole ring established intramolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions C4-H4···N2 (2.43 Å), C12-H12···N3 (2.59 Å) and C9-H9···O2 (2.34 Å). The crystal packing of compound 5a is dominated by hydrogen-bonding interactions between the nitrogen atoms of the triazole ring with the coumarin hydrogens, C4-H4···N3 (2.60 Å) and C5-H5···N2 (2.58 Å) aligning the molecules orthogonally slipped along the crystallographic bc plane as shown in Fig. 5.

The methoxy groups break the planarity of the systems as evidenced by the values of torsional angles: C4-C3-N1-C9 (-166.4 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (162.5 °) for **5b** and C4-C3-N1-C9 (-157.3 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (166.0 °) for **5c**. The coumarins **5b-c** formed different hydrogen-bonding interactions C5-H5···N2 (2.49 Å), C12-H12···O3 (2.59 Å), C15-H15···O1 (2.63 Å) for **5b**, and C5-H5···N3 (2.46 Å), C8-H8···O2 (2.43 Å), C17-H17···O2 (2.63 Å) for **5c**; for this reason, the coumarin molecules in both cases are aligned in an *anti-parallel* fashion mode forming one-dimensional layers. For coumarin **5b**, the layers are stacked (intercalated one onto another) producing a columnar herringbone arrangement as in the case of coumarin **5a**. For **5c**, the layers are self-assembled (intercalated one onto another) over the crystallographic *ac* plane.

The molecular structure of coumarin derivative **5f** was solved in the monoclinic space group P $2_1$/c (Fig. 5). In comparison with coumarins **5b-c**, the compound **5f** adopts a more planar conformation confirmed by the lower dihedral angles values and torsion angles: C4-C3-N1-C9 (-163.9 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (178.0 °). The coumarins are held by hydrogen-bonding interactions C8-H8···O2 (2.68 Å) aligning the molecules in an *anti-parallel* manner, *head-to-tail* (3.31 Å) disposition. The displaced *face-to-face* stacking displayed by **5f** formed layers with intermolecular contacts between planar $\pi$-systems. Moon and co-workers correlated this structural feature with efficient hole and electron mobility.$^{13}$

Coumarin 5h crystallized in the triclinic space group P-1 (Fig. 6). The molecular structure was slightly deviated from planarity as evidenced by the torsion angles: C4-C3-N1-C9 (-174.9 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (173.7 °). The crystal packing of coumarin 5h was dominated by hydrogen-bonding interactions: C5-H5…N3 (2.60 Å) creating one-dimensional layers along the crystallographic $bc$ plane. The layers are held by hydrogen-bonding interactions C6-H6…F1 (2.58 Å) and are stacked in an anti-parallel fashion (plane to plane distance 3.46 Å).

The crystal structures for 5j and 5k were solved in the triclinic P-1 (Z = 2) and monoclinic P $2_1/n$ space groups, respectively. The torsion angle values C4-C3-N1-C9 (-174.5 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (173.8 °) for 5j, C4-C3-N1-C9 (-169.0 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (177.9 °) for 5k, demonstrated the planarity of these systems. Hydrogen-bonding interactions C12-H12…O2 (2.59 Å) and C12-H12…O3 (2.65 Å) aligned the molecules of coumarin 5j in an anti-parallel manner, and are complemented by C5-H5…N3 (2.63 Å) interactions extending the layer along the crystallographic $ac$ plane. The crystal packing of coumarin 5k is dominated by dimeric hydrogen bonds [corresponding to a graph set notation: $D_2^2$ (6)]²⁷ between the methoxy groups at C-7. This supramolecular motif is complemented by multiple hydrogen-bonding interactions C6-H6…O2 (2.50 Å), C17-H17…O2 (2.70 Å), C18-H18…O4 (2.60 Å) and C18-H18…N3 (2.66 Å) that self-assemble the molecules in a multilayered arrangement between planar $\pi$-systems as shown in Fig. 6.

Finally, the crystal structure of 5l was solved in the orthorhombic P $2_12_12_1$ space group. Compared with the previous coumarins, 5l presented the lowest torsion angle values C4-C3-N1-C9 (-179.3 °) and C9-C10-C11-C12 (177.8 °) due to the formation of different intramolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions C4-H4…N2 (2.40 Å), C9-H9…N1 (2.40 Å), C9-H9…O1 (2.82 Å) and C12-H12…N3 (2.60 Å). In the crystal packing of 5l the molecules are slipped and stacked in a head-to-tail manner, which in combination with different hydrogen bonding interactions, [C4-H4…N3 (2.58 Å), C5-H5…N2 (2.70 Å), C8-H8…N4 (2.51 Å) and C15-H15…N4 (2.50 Å)] hold the molecules orthogonally producing a grating self-assembly (Fig. 6).

Plane-wave DFT analysis and theoretical band structure of featured molecular crystals.

From the reference set of organic semiconductors, 6a afforded a tight crystal packing with extensive $\pi$-stacking along the periodic lattice. This feature is responsible for its superior performance, and thus, this molecular crystal was used as reference in the DFT study of molecular crystals obtained from the featured ATCs. All crystal cells were optimized through DFT, using the PBE functional with Grimme's dispersion correction,²⁸ as implemented in Materials Studio 8. Synthetic procedures.

General procedure for the synthesis of 3-[1,2,3-triazol- yl]coumarins derivatives (5a-5l).
To a round-bottom flask containing a magnetic stirring bar were added ethyl bromoacetate (1.5 equiv) and $NaN_3$ (1.0 equiv) in 10 mL of iPrOH:DMF (3:1). Then, the corresponding salicylaldehyde (1.0 equiv) and piperidine (1.25 equiv) were added and the mixture was stirred during 10 minutes. After this time, the phenylacetylene derivative (1.0 equiv) and CuI (10 % equiv) and $Et_3N$ (1.1 equiv) were added and the reaction mixture was heated to reflux during 4 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, ethyl acetate (25 mL) was added. The solid was filtered, washed with cold ethyl-acetate. The crude compounds were purified by recrystallization with solvent hexanes-ethyl acetate mixtures.

## Conclusions
We have successfully developed a novel one-step four-component reaction strategy leading to functionalized 3-(4-aryl-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)coumarins, ATCs, starting from readily available materials. This process shows broad synthetic scope and is thus amenable to the generation of large libraries of the title compounds, which are obtained with high purity and can be processed in solution in common solvents. Highly crystalline solids can be readily accessed, presenting desirable periodic stacking, and a semiconducting character as predicted through DFT studies, displaying a good comparison between the featured compounds and molecules with excellent performance in organic electronics. ATCs are promising candidates to develop self-organized solid materials for implementation in organic electronic devices.
