# CONDUCTION AND VALENCE BAND DENSITY OF STATES OF SnS₂: THEORY AND EXPERIMENT

G. Margaritondo,* J.E. Rowe, M. Schluter and H. Kasper†

Bell Laboratories, Murray Hill, NJ 07974, U.S.A.

(Received 21 February 1977 by M. Cardona)

The band structure of SnS₂ has been investigated over a wide energy range by pseudopotential band structure calculations and synchrotron radiation photoemission spectroscopy techniques. A good correspondence has been found between energy positions of the theoretical density of states features and structure in the constant initial state (CIS) and energy distribution curves (EDC's) for the conduction and the valence bands respectively. In the energy region between - 8 eV and 15 eV from the top of the valence band we observe four valence band and six conduction band peaks.

TIN DICHALCOGENIDES are a particularly interesting kind of non-transition-metal layer compound. Their CdI₂-like crystal structure is similar to those of transition-metal compounds such as ZrS₂ and HfS₂ but the theoretical description of their electronic structure is simpler since d electrons do not play a major role in the valence band formation. Band structure calculations based on pseudopotential [1-4] and tight-binding [5] techniques have been able to clarify the nature of the chemical bonding and the valence electronic charge distribution of SnS₂ and SnSe₂. Previous experimental input for these calculations has been optical data [6] and photoemission spectra taken with conventional u.v. and X-ray sources [7]. The optical data are related to the valence and conduction bands through the joint density of states function which at low energy appear strongly affected by the electron-hole interaction [6]. Conventional photoelectron energy distribution curves (EDC's) of reference [7] compare quite favorably with the theoretical density of states for the top of the valence bands but they do not provide detailed information about the conduction bands. Moreover, the energy position of the cation s-like states shows a discrepancy of 1-2 eV between theory [1-5] and experiment [6] and this raises doubts about the validity of the current theoretical models in energy regions more than ~5 eV away from the top of the valence band, $E_{V}$. Interpretation of deeper valence states is made difficult in the available X-ray photoemission experiments of the valence bands [5, 7] since there is an overlapping contribution due to the excitation of Sn 4d electrons by the Al $K\alpha_{3}\alpha_{K}$ satellite.

The present experiments use synchrotron radiation photoemission spectroscopy techniques to study the band structure of SnS₂ over a wide energy range extending ~8 eV below and ~15 eV above the Fermi level.
The valence band density of states has been explored by taking EDC's at several photon energies while constant-initial-state (CIS) [8] spectra have provided information on the conduction band density of states. In general if one neglects matrix element effects the peaks in a photoemission spectrum correspond to critical points in the joint density of states, i.e. $\nabla_{k}(E_{i}-E_{f})=0$. Therefore the information on the initial density of states is convoluted with that on the final density of states as is the case for optical spectroscopy. However, with photoemission spectroscopy one can achieve experimental conditions such that spectral peaks simply correspond to maxima in the valence or in the conduction band density of states. This of course makes the interpretation of experimental data one obtains much more straightforward. For example at large enough photon energies the EDC's perform an average over several plane-wave-like final states and their peaks simply correspond to maxima in the initial density of states, i.e. $\nabla_{k}E_{i}=0$. In the present experiments we have taken EDC's in the photon energy range $\hbar\omega=18-35$ eV and their initial state peaks correspond to final states in the energy region 11-32 eV above $E_{V}$. As we shall see below the structure in the conduction band density of states of SnS₂ is confined to an energy region less than ~15 eV above $E_{V}$ and thereafter no final-state effects are observed in the photoemission spectra. Therefore the EDC's we obtain for photon energies above ~23 eV should not be affected by final-state effects and their peaks are related to maxima in the valence band density of states. conduction band density of states by means of CIS spectroscopy. This technique which has been successfully applied to several III-VI compounds such as GaSe, GaS and InSe [9] consists of measuring the number of photoelectrons of energy $E_{f}$ as a function of the photon energy, $\hbar \omega$, while keeping the difference $E_{f}-\hbar \omega=E_{i}$ constant. One should observe that $E_{i}$ is the initial state energy of the optical transition for primary photoionization processes. In the present experiment $E_{i}$ has always been selected to coincide with one of the maxima in the valence band density of states as determined by EDC's at sufficently high photon energies. Therefore we have $\nabla_{k} E_{i}=0$ so that the condition $\nabla_{k}(E_{i}-E_{f})=0$ simplifies to $\nabla_{k} E_{i}=0$ and the peaks in our CIS curves correspond to maxima in the condution band density of states. Of course the above conditions for having peaks in the EDC's and CIS curves should be somewhat relaxed if one takes into account the energy dependence of the matrix elements that have been neglected so far. Indeed we observe small changes in the position of a given peak* in different CIS curves and similarly small excursions of the EDC peaks even at large photon energies when final state effects are negligible. Nevertheless we shall see that within the experimental uncertainty our approach does provide the correct position of the structure in the valence and in the conduction band density of states as it is confirmed by their overall agreement with the theoretical predictions.

All the experiments were carried out on samples freshly cleaved *in situ* under ultrahigh vacuum conditions (pressure, $p \lesssim 1 \times 10^{-9}$ torr). The u.v. source was the University of Wisconsin storage ring equipped with a vertical Seya-Namioka monochromator. The experimental equipment allowed us to select either $s$- or $p$-polarized photons without changing the photoelectron collection geometry as described in reference [9]. As we shall see, however, no strong photon polarization effects have been observed in the present experiments. Figure 1 shows several EDC's taken with photons in the energy range $18-30 \mathrm{eV}$ and of both $p$- and $s$-polarization. $E_{V}$ has been determined by averaging the linear extrapolations of the high-energy edges of several different EDC's. Four structures ($A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$) are present in all the EDC's at $\sim 1.3 \mathrm{eV}, \sim 3.2 \mathrm{eV}, \sim 4.6 \mathrm{eV}$ and $\sim 7.4 \mathrm{eV}$ below $E_{V}$ respectively. As discussed above the small changes in the energy position of these peaks with photon energy are due to matrix element effects at least for $\hbar \omega>23 \mathrm{eV}$. Peaks $A$, $B$ and $C$ in the higher photon energy EDC's of Fig. 1 have been selected as initial states for the CIS curves of Fig. 2. Following the discussion above we expect the primary photoionization processes to give CIS peaks related to maxima of the conduction band density of states. These CIS peaks can be distinguished form those due to secondary photoionization [8] since they must appear at the same energy for spectra with different initial states that have been plotted as a function of the *final state energy*. Six features of this kind are observed in Fig. 2 at $5.1,6.25$, $\sim 7.4, \sim 9.5, \sim 11.45$ and $\sim 13.9 \mathrm{eV}$ above $E_{V}$ respectively. For curves $C$ a shoulder of uncertain origin at $\sim 8.0 \mathrm{eV}$ overlaps the right side of the peak at $7.4 \mathrm{eV}$. Note that the matrix element effects discussed above cause small excursions of the structures around the above positions in different CIS curves. The last five structures have been labeled as $d$ through $h$ following the same convention already employed for III-VI materials [9]. Above $\sim 15 \mathrm{eV}$ all the CIS curves become smooth due to a featureless behavior of the conduction band density of states. We point out that none of our experimental techniques is sensitive to the energy region between the Fermi level and the vacuum level for which optical spectroscopy or Auger processes must be employed. The first structure at $5.1 \mathrm{eV}$ above $E_{V}$ could be spurious and due to the onset of the vacuum level but we believe that it is more likely related to a theoretical peak appearing $5.4 \mathrm{eV}$ above $E_{V}$. In the present paper we shall only analyze the energy position of the CIS structures. No attempt will be made here to analyze their relative intensity which is determined by several factors such as the $\hbar \omega$-dependence of the photoionization matrix elements close to the threshold [10] and the secondary emission background signal. One can see from Figs. 1 and 2 that unlike the III-VI compounds, $\mathrm{SnS}_{2}$ does not exhibit strong polarization-dependent selection rules.

The calcualtions are done using local pseudopotentials as defined in references 1, 4 and 6. The bandstructure of $SnS_{2}$ is evaluated at 196 k-points in the irreducible part of the Brillouin zone. Some 25 bands are considered which cover energies up to about 25 eV above the valence band maximum. The density of states is computed using Gilat-Raubenheimer techniques [11]. The computed wavefunctions are used to construct (hypothetical) charge density contour plots for states within given energy intervals. Thus the nature of structures in the conduction band density of states may be investigated in real space.

Theory and experiment are directly compared to each other in Fig. 3 where the dashed line shows the calculated density of states and the solid line shows the $\hbar\omega=22$ eV EDC and the CIS curve having peak $C$ in the EDC's as initial state. It emerges from Fig. 3 that the theoretical results are in surprisingly good overall agreement with experiment in the wide energy range displayed. Table 1 summarizes the spectral energy positions of the present work and of previous theories and experiments. The close correspondence of the experimental structures and the theoretical structures suggests direct identification.

On the basis of chemical bond arguments and quantitative charge density calculations [4] one expects cation $s$ electrons to participate to the valence band density of states together with anion $s$ and $p$ electrons and some minor $d$-like contributions. The anion $s$ states are expected at a binding energy of $\sim-12$ eV and do not appear in most of our EDC's. The experimental peak $D$ can be identified with the theoretical structure at $-6.0$ eV mostly originating from Sn $(5s)$ electrons although as already mentioned these peaks exhibit the largest discrepancy of 1.4 eV between theory and experiment. The upper valence bands (i.e. peaks $A,B$ and $C$) are mostly due to sulphur $(3p)$ electrons. The hypothesis of a crystal-field splitting of the $p_{xy}$ and $p_{z}$ components has been previously advanced on the basis of the optical reflectivity data [6]. A splitting greater than $\sim0.5$ eV of these components in the valence bands would result in strong photon polarization effects in our experiments as found for III-VI compounds [9]. Peaks $d$ and $e$ originate mainly from Sn $(5p)$ orbitals (with some $d$-contribution). A small amount of $S$ $p$-character, $p_z$ for peak $d$ and $p_{x,y}$ for peak $e$, is admixed. Peaks $f$ show essentially the same behavior, however, the states are more delocalized. Peak $g$ contains mostly Sn $d$-character (with some $p$-contribution). Peaks $h$ show rather delocalized states with some accumulations around the chalcogens. In summary only peaks $d$ and $e$ show pronounced atomic character, i.e. Sn $5p$-character, while the wavefunctions corresponding to other structure are rather delocalized in nature. It is thus expected that atomic optical selection rules for these latter peaks are weakened and rather initial state independent. Therefore, polarization independent spectra should be obtained.

In summary we have demonstrated that the simultaneous use of pseudopotential band structure calculations and of several synchrotron radiation photoemission spectroscopy techniques may give a complete picture of the valence and condution bands of a material over a very wide energy range. It certainly appears worth while applying the same method to a systematic study of materials with non-free-electron-like conduction band density of states. Particularly interesting candidates for such an investigation are the group IVA and VA chalcogenides and halides and the corresponding transition metal compounds.
