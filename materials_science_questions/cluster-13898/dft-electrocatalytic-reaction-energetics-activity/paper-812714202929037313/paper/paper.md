### Abstract

Nitrogen-doped carbon nanostructures ($\text{CN}_x$) have been shown to be promising catalysts to replace Pt-based materials for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in PEM fuel cell cathodes. This work reports the use of a combination of post-reaction X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and density functional theory (DFT) aimed at examining changes to the nitrogen species in $\text{CN}_x$ after potential application in an oxygen saturated acidic electrolyte. The results hence obtained reveal interesting changes to the pyridinic, quaternary and pyridinic $\text{N}^+\text{O}^-$ binding energy envelopes as a result of protonation of sites and formation of ORR intermediates after the $\text{CN}_x$ electrodes were subjected to electrochemical potential holds in the kinetic and mass transfer limited regimes of the ORR cyclic voltammogram of the catalyst.

The emergence of proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell technology in the past few decades has led to the development of carbon-based non-precious metal catalysts (NPMCs) to replace the Pt-based materials on the PEM fuel cell cathode for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). Among NPMCs, nitrogen-doped carbon nanostructures (CNₓ) have emerged as promising cathode materials for PEM fuel cells $^{[1]}$. These catalysts have been shown to be resistant to many poisons, such as CO, H₂S, Cl⁻, and CN⁻, that rapidly deactivate Pt-based and iron-nitrogen coordinated carbon supported (FeNC) catalysts $^{[2]}$. However, the ORR performance of CNₓ materials is still far from that of the state-of-the-art Pt catalysts and efforts have continued to improve their electrocatalytic performance $^{[2e, 3]}$. In order to gain insights into a rational design of CNₓ catalysts, it is important to understand the nature of ORR active sites in these materials $^{[4]}$.

The role of nitrogen functionalities such as pyridinic, quaternary and pyrrolic N has been previously explored using *ex-situ* characterization techniques such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) in several studies using probe molecules such as phosphate anions and by employing strategies to modify catalyst synthesis procedures $^{[1h, 2h, 4b, 5]}$. While these studies have provided insights into the structure-activity relationship for ORR on CNₓ catalysts, researchers remain divided on the nature of nitrogen moieties responsible for the ORR activity. While several reports suggest that higher pyridinic and pyrrolic N species contribute to higher ORR activity $^{[1c, 2f, 2g, 4b, 6]}$, others indicate that the graphitic or quaternary N sites provide ORR activity to these materials $^{[6c, 7]}$. Previous studies performed in our research group suggest that ORR activity correlates with the amount of pyridinic N sites present in CNₓ catalyst in acidic media $^{[1a, 1c, 1h, 2g, 5a]}$. Nakamura and co-workers examined model CNₓ catalysts with controlled pyridinic N edge sites and demonstrated that the activity on a per site basis is higher for pyridinic N and that the active site is associated with oxygen species interacting with the Lewis basic sites on the C atom adjacent to the pyridinic N species $^{[4b]}$. However, it is known that catalysts undergo changes under reaction conditions and an understanding of these changes will be important to continued efforts to develop a mechanism for ORR on CNₓ catalyst.

XPS is the most commonly used surface-sensitive technique to quantify the relative distribution of nitrogen species in carbon-based NPMCs $^{[8]}$. Recent works of Atanassov and co-workers have proposed the use of density functional theory (DFT) for accurate identification of nitrogen

functionalities through XPS measurements and developing correlations between the ORR activity of the catalyst and nature of nitrogen sites $^{[8-9]}$. Based on the assertion that the nitrogen functionalities undergo transformations under ORR conditions, techniques such as near ambient pressure XPS (NAP-XPS) with potential application capabilities would be ideal for investigating the nature of ORR active sites in $\text{CN}_x$. However, performing NAP-XPS measurements with potential application in the presence of aqueous electrolytes can be extremely challenging experimentally. A step towards in-situ electrochemical characterization and an alternative to NAP- XPS is to perform post-ORR XPS measurements on $\text{CN}_x$ that could be useful in identifying permanent changes that occur on the catalyst electrode in the presence of an electrolyte and application of potential. Therefore, in this manuscript, we examine changes to nitrogen species in $\text{CN}_x$ catalyst as a function of applied potential in an acidic medium using a combination of post reaction XPS and DFT calculations.

Figure 1 shows the cathodic scan of a cyclic voltammogram collected on $\text{CN}_x$ under ORR conditions in an acidic electrolyte. The catalyst exhibits an onset potential of 0.78 V and half-wave potential of 0.59 V vs. RHE. Based on this cyclic voltammogram, the potential holds for collection of XPS spectra were performed at the following potentials:

(i) 1.2 V corresponding to the thermodynamic ORR potential, but no ORR activity

(ii) 0.7 V corresponding to the kinetic limited regime near the onset potential where ORR activity is very low

(iii) 0.5 V corresponding to the kinetic and mass transfer limited mixed regime with higher ORR activity as compared to the kinetic limited regime, and

(iv) 0.2 V corresponding to the mass transfer limited regime which also exhibits high ORR activity

For comparison, XPS data was also collected on $\text{CN}_x$ catalyst that was not exposed to any potential (i.e. pre-reaction $\text{CN}_x$ catalyst).

Figure 2(i) shows the experimentally-collected X-ray photoelectron spectrum (XPS) of $\text{CN}_x$ in the N-1s region fitted to three major nitrogen species: (i) pyridinic N centered at 398.2 eV with FWHM of 1.6 eV, (ii) quaternary N centered at 400.8 eV with FWHM of 1.4 eV and (iii) pyridinic $\text{N}^+\text{O}^-$ centered at 402.2 eV with FWHM of 1.9 eV. While the XPS data was fit to these major binding energy (B.E.) envelopes, DFT calculations were used to predict binding energies for specific forms

of nitrogen moieties in $CN_x$. This approach is illustrated in Figure 2 where the DFT-predicted binding energies of various nitrogen site models (Figure 2(a-h)) are marked on the experimentally- fitted XPS N-1s spectrum in Figure 2(i). The exact DFT-predicted B.E. values can be found in the supporting information in Table S1. From Fig. 2, it is clear that specific nitrogen models can deviate from the ideal assignment associated with the N moiety. For instance, among the nominally "quaternary N" species, the basal and armchair quaternary sites have binding energies ~ 401.5 eV versus the experimental peak centered at 400.8 eV. On the other hand, the zigzag pyridinium and armchair pyridinium sites, also nominally "quaternary N" species, have binding energies close to ~ 400.7 eV, which is closer to the peak assignment experimentally. The DFT values for the pyridinic oxide and pyridinic sites are also higher by ~ 0.5 eV than the center of the experimental XPS peaks. It is not straightforward to separate errors in the DFT calculation from actual dispersion and variation in XPS binding energies due to the range of N moieties that fall nominally under the pyridinic, quaternary, and pyridinic oxide classifications. However, there is generally good agreement between the binding energies observed experimentally and those predicted by DFT calculations in terms of the relative positions of the peaks. This agreement gives us confidence that the DFT-predicted changes in the binding energies can be used to understand the changes observed in the post-reaction XPS data. It should be noted that the focus is not on the absolute values of the binding energies, but rather on the relative B.E. shifts observed experimentally versus those predicted by DFT.

Table 1 and Figure S1 show the relative distribution of various nitrogen functionalities and their corresponding binding energies obtained on $CN_x$ catalyst electrodes held at different potentials under ORR conditions. DFT calculations were used to attempt to explain these experimental observations by screening through all considered sites to discover candidate sites that could be responsible for the experimental XPS B.E. shifts at pertinent potentials. To explain the changes observed in the nitrogen distribution at different potentials, two phenomena were considered:

(i) Protonation of sites
(ii) ORR proceeding via the associative mechanism

The results obtained from DFT for each type of change to the various $CN_x$ models depicted in Figure 2 are shown in detail in the SI in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.


### Potential hold at 1.2 V:

On applying a potential of 1.2 V, the percentage of pyridinic N on the CNx surface decreases and that of quaternary N increases as compared to the electrode that was not exposed to any potential. Based on DFT calculations, this change could be a result of the protonation of the zigzag pyridinic site to zigzag pyridinium site (Scheme 1) manifesting itself in a B.E. shift of 1.6 eV. While this is smaller than the shift for pyridinic to quaternary observed experimentally (2.6 eV), it matches the trend for a N moiety to go from pyridinic to quaternary. In addition, based on the DFT values of $\Delta$G, armchair pyridinic, zigzag oxide and armchair oxide sites cannot protonate at this potential (Table S1).

It is also important to note that while there is no ORR activity at this potential, ORR intermediates can still form, particularly for sites that may have rate-limiting steps further along the ORR mechanism, such as strongly bound OH* intermediate requiring higher overpotentials to completely turn over the ORR to form water. Examining Table S2, we can identify the following sites as having ORR intermediates by 1.2 V: zigzag pyridinic, armchair pyridinium, armchair quaternary, and zigzag oxide. Armchair pyridinium can be excluded because the conversion of armchair pyridinic to armchair pyridinium does not occur until 0.31 V. The other three sites all have steps that suggest at 1.2 V the ORR mechanism can proceed to OH* before being prevented from turning over the ORR reaction. Zigzag pyridinic shows a small positive shift (+0.4 eV) and armchair quaternary shows a small negative shift (-0.4 eV) for OH*. We classify these shifts as small relative to the shift for protonation and therefore a minor contribution to the changes in the experimental XPS at 1.2 V. Zigzag oxide with OH* shows a shift of around +0.4 eV and as shown in Table 1 there is a small positive shift (+0.1 eV) associated with the pyridinic oxide, which could tentatively be assigned to the zigzag oxide (a fraction of the overall pyridinic oxide sites) undergoing the shift due to OH*.

The basal quaternary site shows an onset potential of 1 V to begin the formation of ORR intermediates, and while DFT predictions of the onset potential cannot be considered exact, we observe that even if ORR intermediates did form at 1.2 V on basal quaternary sites, most of them would not have a major shift except for O*. Since no major increase is observed in the pyridinic N⁺O⁻ envelope experimentally, we assume that ORR intermediates do not form on basal quaternary sites until more negative potentials. Overall, the DFT results suggest an interesting observation –

ORR intermediates can form on several sites at 1.2 V but do not result in noticeable XPS shifts. Instead, the observed experimental shift from pyridinic to quaternary N seems to be primarily associated with protonation of zigzag pyridinic. This process likely competes with ORR intermediate formation on zigzag pyridinic and some fraction only become protonated. That would match the relatively small percentage of pyridinic N sites that show a shift to quaternary.

Potential hold at 0.7 V:

The potential hold at 0.7 V results in a major reduction in the quaternary N envelope and increase in the pyridinic $N^+O^-$ envelope along with a shift in the latter to lower binding energies, as compared to the potential hold at 1.2 V. Since 0.7 V lies close to the ORR onset potential in the kinetic limited regime, ORR is not substantial here, but has started. Looking at the overall ORR onset potentials for different species, DFT predicts that the quaternary sites would contribute to ORR activity at this potential. While we caution that the DFT predicted onset potential within a continuum solvation model might not capture all the factors of the more complex material and electrochemical environment, if the basal quaternary sites were beginning to turn over ORR at 0.7 V, we could expect that all three ORR intermediates would be present in some population on these sites. Based on predictions from DFT reported in Table S2, the presence of adsorbed O* on the basal quaternary site results in a positive shift (0.4 eV) in its B.E. towards the pyridinic $N^+O^-$ signal (Scheme 2). While this is a smaller shift than the nominal difference between quaternary and pyridinic oxide, the experimental fit of the XPS shows the difference between these 2 sites dropping from 1.5 eV to 0.9 eV when going from 1.2 to 0.7 V. The B.E. of the basal quaternary sites shifting towards that of pyridinic oxide, but with a smaller magnitude, would lead to an overall downward shift of the B.E. for the pyridinic oxide, as observed experimentally.

Potential hold at 0.5 V and 0.2 V:

The next step involves potential hold at 0.5 V where the relative distribution of pyridinic N envelope decreases slightly, pyridinic $N^+O^-$ envelope increases and quaternary N envelope does not change. The subsequent stage of potential hold at 0.2 V results in no significant change in the relative distribution of nitrogen functionalities. At these overpotentials, both zigzag pyridinic and

zigzag pyridinium sites can turn over the ORR. As noted earlier, zigzag pyridinic species might see a split where some sites early in the potential sweep get occupied by OH* groups resulting in no noticeable shift in the XPS, but as the ORR starts to turn over on these sites, they would also have the potential to go through all four states (bare, OOH*, OH*, O*). As this occurs, the zigzag pyridinic site also can proceed down an alternative pathway through protonation illustrated in Scheme 3. Overall, the observed experimental shift from pyridinic to pyridinic oxide can be associated with zigzag pyridinic with OH* that converts to zigzag pyridinic (due to significantly faster ORR kinetics at 0.5 V) and then some fraction of these sites begins to go down the pathway illustrated in Scheme 3. In this scenario OOH* or OH* intermediates on zigzag pyridinium sites would result in the observed drop in pyridinic sites and rise in pyridinic oxide sites (Table S2). When the ORR is turning over, a small fraction of sites is expected to have OH* or OOH* intermediates (others would have O* or nothing but these do not show any noticeable shifts from zigzag pyridinium), explaining the small change at 0.5 V.

It is important to note here that at these high overpotentials other sites including armchair quaternary and zigzag oxide are also capable of turning over ORR, based on the free energy calculations (Table S2). However, formation of intermediates on these sites does not result in large B.E. shifts that can be observed experimentally.

The results presented herein indicate that a combination of post-reaction XPS and DFT calculations can be useful in identiying changes that occur to nitrogen functionalities in $CN_x$-type catalysts under ORR conditions, and understand the role of various nitrogen sites in providing ORR activity to these materials. The phenomena such as protonation and formation of ORR intermediates that take place on the electrode surface at various applied overpotentials in acidic electrolytes can result in significant changes in the nitrogen distribution in $CN_x$ that can be quantified and explained using post-reaction XPS measurements and DFT calculations. An important conclusion from this study is that even before reaching the onset potential during electrochemical measurements, ORR intermediates are formed on the carbon sites adjacent to nitrogen in the $CN_x$ catalyst, however, strongly adsorbed intermediates prevent the reaction from turning over completely. It is also important to note that this study only examined some possible $CN_x$ sites, and there may be other possible sites that could contribute to the ORR activity of these materials.

The methodology reported in this work can be extended to the identification of the nature of active sites in carbon-based NPMCs with the help of poisoning probe moelcules/ions such as CO, H₂S, CN⁻, halides and phosphate anions. This work is a positive step towards using in-situ electrochemical surface-sensitive characterization techniques to gain insights into the nature of active sites in these materials.
