# Fluid-thermal-electric multiphysics simulation of air-to-water thermoelectric generator system

## Problem background
Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) convert waste heat from hot fluids (e.g., engine exhaust, industrial process streams) directly into electricity via the Seebeck effect. Designing efficient TEG systems requires accurate numerical models that capture the intricate coupling among fluid flow, convective and conductive heat transfer, and thermoelectric phenomena (Peltier, Joule, Thomson effects). Crucially, parasitic heat generated by the electric field feeds back into the temperature distribution and the fluid flow, so a truly predictive model must solve the fluid, thermal, and electric equations simultaneously rather than sequentially. This work focuses on an air-to-water TEG system, where hot air passes through a finned aluminum heat exchanger to heat one side of a thermoelectric module, while cooling water maintains a low temperature on the other side. The main challenge is to build a fully coupled fluid-thermal-electric multiphysics simulation that can faithfully reproduce the experimentally measured output power of such a system over a range of load resistances, demonstrating that the simultaneous coupling captures essential physics that sequential approaches miss.

## Approach
The method is a fully coupled steady-state numerical simulation of the air-to-water TEG system using an open-source multiphysics finite-element solver (ElmerFEM recommended). The geometry comprises a 60×60×45 mm aluminum heat exchanger with 12 inner fins, steel inlet/outlet connectors, an aluminum water-cooled heat sink, and a commercial thermoelectric module (TEG-127020) containing 128 pairs of Bi₂Te₃-based semiconductor legs with copper electrodes and ceramic plates. A load resistance completes the electrical circuit.

All three physical fields are solved simultaneously:
- Fluid flow: incompressible turbulent flow of hot air and cooling water, modeled by the RNG k‑ε turbulence equations.
- Heat transfer: conduction in solids, convection in fluids, and thermoelectric heat sources (Peltier, Joule, Thomson) in the semiconductor legs, using temperature-dependent material properties.
- Electric field: current continuity with Seebeck voltage generation; the load resistance governs the operating point.

Material properties for the Bi₂Te₃ legs are given as fourth-order polynomial functions of temperature for the Seebeck coefficient, thermal conductivity, and electrical resistivity. Boundary conditions are fully specified: natural convection to ambient air (h=15 W/m²·K, 300 K); hot air inlet at 500 K and velocity corresponding to 40 g/s mass flow (≈45.67 m/s); cooling water inlet at 300 K and 7.04 m/s; outlet pressure boundary; electrical ground on the cathode side of the TEM.

The simulation is run for several discrete load resistances spanning 2–8 Ω, producing an output-power-versus-load curve that can be directly compared with experimental data.

## How you are scored
Your submission is scored by an automated verifier that inspects the artifacts under `/app/outputs`. The primary scoring comes from the CSV produced in Step 3. The verifier will:
- Read your `load_resistance_ohm` and `output_power_W` columns.
- Compare your predicted output power to a hidden experimental reference (digitized from the paper's figure) at the same load resistances, computing the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) over all data points.
- Additionally, evaluate the relative error at a load resistance close to 4 Ω (by interpolation if needed).
- Combine these error metrics into a final score according to hidden thresholds that reward greater accuracy (lower errors yield higher scores).

The evidence files from Steps 1 and 2 (`mesh_info.txt` and `setup_log.txt`) are checked for existence and basic correctness but carry very low weight relative to the simulation outputs. The exact scoring formula, thresholds, and weights are defined in the hidden grading specification. Simply reporting numbers from the paper is insufficient; the simulation must be actually executed, and the verifier will not fetch any remote resources during scoring.
