The simultaneous conversion of solar radiation into thermal and electrical energy in a thermoelectric (TE) solar-assisted heat pump is, for the purposes of this study, referred to as hybrid conversion. To capture more thermal and electrical energy, flat-plate reflectors have been mounted on a TE solar collector. To obtain higher solar radiation intensity on the TE solar collector, the position of the reflectors has been changed and the optimal position of the reflectors determined by both experimental measurements and numerical calculation so as to obtain maximal concentration of solar radiation intensity. The calculated values have been found to be in good agreement with measured ones. Improvements to the thermal energy and electrical power outputs of the system can be achieved by the use of the TE solar-assisted heat pump with reflectors. For the optimum position of the reflectors, the coefficient of performance (COP) of the system formed from a TE solar collector integrated with a heat pump (TESC-HP) was 5.60. The power output and conversion efficiency of the TE modules can reach 10.09 W and 2.40%, respectively, being improved by 34.5% and 18.2%, respectively, compared with the TESC-HP without reflectors.

**Key words:** Heat pump, reflectors, thermal energy, electrical power

## INTRODUCTION

The rapid consumption of fossil fuels to meet energy demands leads to environmental damage and shortage of fossil fuels, prompting a search for alternative resources for a sustainable future. Solar energy is perhaps the most interesting and promising source among the renewable energy sources. Solar radiation is freely available over the whole earth. A significant amount of research and development work on thermoelectric (TE) solar collectors has been done over the past 10 years. TE solar systems have been the subject of intensive numerical and experimental investigations. In a review of these trends in technology development, in particular the advancements in recent years, Scherrer et al. $^{1}$ presented a series of mathematical models based on optimal control theory to assess the electric performance of a solar TE generator based on skutterudites as a function of sun–spacecraft distance. The design parameters (dimensions, weight, etc.) were optimized for operation at a distance of 0.45 astronomical units (a.u.) from the sun with 400 W electrical output power and a required load voltage of $30\ \text{V}_\text{DC}$. The simulation results indicated that the skutterudite-based solar TE generator offered attractive performance features as a primary or auxiliary power source for spacecraft in near-sun mis-

Kraemer et al. $^{2}$ presented a high-performance flat-panel solar TE generator using high thermal concentration (area of the absorber plate divided by the cross-sectional area of the thermoelectric ele- ments). The efficiency was seven to eight times higher than for a conventional solar TE generator. According to literature reviews, solar TE generators convert a few percent of all incoming solar radiation into electricity. More than 90% of the solar radiation incident on a TE is reflected or converted to thermal energy. To increase the electrical and thermal out- put of TE solar collectors, reflectors are used to concentrate the solar radiation onto the surface of the collector. A few research studies have been reported on TE generators with a solar-concentrat- ing collector. As an example, Fan et al. $^{3}$ investi gated a parabolic dish concentrator TE generator. A parabolic dish collector with aperture of 1.8 m was used to concentrate sunlight onto a copper absorber plate. Four BiTe-based TE modules were used to convert the concentrated solar thermal energy directly into electric energy. A water-cooled heat sink was used to remove waste heat from the cold side of the TE modules. Experimental results showed that the concentrator TE generator was able to produce electric power of up to 5.9 W for a $35^{\circ}C$ temperature difference with hot-side temperature of $68^{\circ}C$. Lertsatitthanakorn et al. $^{4}$ studied a double pass TE solar air collector with flat-plate reflectors. The flat-plate reflectors were used to concentrate solar radiation onto the TE solar air collector. Experimental results showed that the optimum po- sition of the reflectors was $60^{\circ}$, which gave signifi cantly higher thermal energy and electrical power output than the TE solar collector without reflec- tors. This article presents the performance of a solar parabolic concentrator coupled to a TE module. The effects of air cooling, flow rate, and fan orientation are investigated. However, the efficiency of TE de- vices is dependent on applying a significant tem- perature difference across the TE device and the material's dimensionless figure of merit $(ZT).^{5}$ To achieve a higher temperature difference across the TE device, a TE solar collector integrated with a heat pump (TESC-HP) was constructed by our re- search group. $^{6}$ The refrigerant in the evaporator of the heat pump system can be used to release heat at the cold side of the TE device. In the present experiment, the hot side of the TE device was at- tached to the back side of a flat-plate solar collector to receive heat from solar radiation. It was found that the maximum COP was 5.48, with hot water capacity of 1300 L/day. Meanwhile, the power out- put and conversion efficiency from the TE modules reached 7.5 W and 2.03%, respectively, represent- ing improvements of 110% and 133.3%, respectively, compared with the conventional TESC. The TESC- HP also had a hot water capacity 44.45% higher than that of the conventional TESC. In the present work, flat-plate reflectors were used to concentrate the solar radiation onto the hot side of the TE modules, and the heat pump was used to release heat at the cold side of the TE modules. The tem- perature difference across the TE modules was in- creased by using both methods. The optimal position of the flat reflectors was investigated. A comparison between the TESC-HP with and without flat reflectors is also presented.

## SYSTEM DESCRIPTION

A schematic view of the system with the TESC and flat reflectors is shown in Fig. 1. The TE solar col- lector was composed of a glass cover, air gap, galva- nized iron plate, and TE modules. The hot side was attached directly to the back side of the galvanized iron plate acting as an absorber. A copper heat sink integrated with a heat pump was directly attached to the cold side of the TE modules. The space between the TE modules, absorber, and heat sink was insu- lated using a closed-cell elastomeric thermal insulator (thermal conductivity 0.039 W/m-K). The collector was 0.8 m wide and 1.2 m long, with an aperture area of $0.96m^{2}$. The absorbing surface of the collector was made of galvanized iron, 2.5 mm thick, painted dull black. Forty-eight TE cooling modules (model TEC1-12708, China) made of bis- muth-telluride-based alloys were used. Each module had an area of $4cm×4cm$. Twelve TE modules were connected in series and arranged in four rows, with each row connected in parallel. To cool the TE modules, a heat pump system was used to assist the release of heat from the heat sinks at the cold side of the TE modules. Two aluminum sheets, with dimensions of $1.2m×0.8m$, acted as reflectors for diffuse solar radiation. They were mounted in the north and south direction of the TESC with changeable position in relation to the TESC. To ob- tain the highest solar radiation intensity on the TESC, the positions of the bottom and top reflectors were changed and the optimal position of the reflec- tors determined. The advantage of this concentration technique is that there is no need to track the sun. A schematic diagram of the TESC-HP with the reflec- tors is presented in Fig. 2. The heat pump system consisted of a R-134a piston-type hermetic compres- sor with rated power input of 350 W, a water storage tank, a water circulation pump, a water-cooled plate- type condenser, the heat sink (back side of the TESC) evaporator, and a throttling device (capillary tube). The condensed liquid refrigerant from the condenser passed through the throttling device directly into a copper tube to the inlet of the TE modules' heat sinks, where it evaporated by heat release from the heat sinks. Each heat sink was a square $0.05m×0.05m$ copper plate onto which each ther- moelectric module was mounted. The refrigerant temperature in the heat sink was lower than the ambient temperature. The vaporized refrigerant then passed through the compressor, and finally the high-temperature/pressure vapor was pumped into the condenser, where it condensed. The energy re-

jected by the condenser was absorbed by water as a cooling medium through a refrigerant to the water plate-type heat exchanger. A water pump was used to circulate the water from the water storage tank to the condenser. The total volume of water in the storage tank was 100 L. The water capacity is the amount of hot water at the set point per day. A small outlet at the bottom of the tank was used for drain- age. Water from the storage tank entered the con- denser and captured the heat released from the refrigerant. In this study, the water flow rate was fixed at 0.33 kg/s to maximize the heat rejection from the condenser. Therefore, the pump consumed 75 W. Water at the outlet of the condenser entered the storage tank and continued to feed to the condenser until the water temperature in the tank reached the water set point temperature (40°C). The pump and compressor were turned off, and the water was drained out of the system. Fresh water was used to refill the tank, then the pump and compressor were started again.

The collector was oriented to the south and tilted so as to adjust an angle of 16° from the horizontal, which was considered suitable for the geographical location of Mahasarakham, Thailand (16°14'N, 103°15'E).⁷

The collector was instrumented with T-type (accuracy ±0.5°C) thermocouples to measure the temperatures of the glass cover, the flowing water, the water in the tank, the surface of the absorber plate, the refrigerant at various locations in the system, and the heat sink. A thermocouple to mea- sure the ambient temperature was kept in a shelter to protect it from direct sunlight. Four pyranometers (model CM 11, accuracy ±10 W/m²; Kipp & Zonen) were used to measure the total solar radiation incident on the collector, the global solar radiation on a horizontal surface, the global solar incident radiation, and the diffuse radiation on a horizontal surface as measured with a shading ring to shade direct radiation. The low and high pressures of the refrigerant system were measured by pressure transducers (accuracy ±0.08 bar). The water flow rate was measured by a flow meter (model Z-4003, accuracy ±4%; Nitto) at the collector inlet. The output current and voltage were measured using a multimeter (model 189, accuracy: V_DC ±0.025%, A ±0.5%; Fluke). The experiments were performed from November 2012 to February 2013. Experi- mentation started at 9 a.m. and ended at 5 p.m.

# ANALYSIS

## Calculation of Solar Radiation Incident on the TESC

The total solar radiation $G_{\text{tot\_ref}}$ on the TESC with tilted plane angle $\beta$ was calculated as⁸

$$
G_{\text{tot\_ref}} = G_{\text{dir}} + G_{\text{dif}} + G_{\text{ref\_g}} + G_{\text{ref1}} + G_{\text{ref2}}, \quad (1)
$$

where $G_{\text{dir}}$ is the direct radiation on the collector surface, $G_{\text{dif}}$ is the sky diffuse radiation, $G_{\text{ref\_g}}$ is the radiation reflected from the ground, $G_{\text{ref1}}$ is the radiation reflected from the bottom reflector which reached the collector surface with tilted plane angle $\alpha_1$, and $G_{\text{ref2}}$ is the radiation reflected from the top reflector which reached the collector surface with tilted plane angle $\alpha_2$.

$$
G_{\text{dir}} = G_{\text{in}} \sin(\alpha + \beta), \quad (2)
$$

$$
G_{\text{dif}} = G_{\text{difh}} \left(\frac{1 + \cos \beta}{2}\right), \quad (3)
$$

$$
G_{\text{ref\_g}} = \rho_{\text{g}} G_{\text{h}} \left(\frac{1 - \cos \beta}{2}\right), \quad (4)
$$

$$
G_{\text{ref1}} = \rho_{\text{al}} G_{\text{in}} \sin \chi \sin(\alpha - \alpha_1), ; \quad \chi = \beta + 2\alpha_1 - \alpha, \quad (5)
$$

$$
G_{\text{ref2}} = \rho_{\text{al}} G_{\text{in}} \sin \tau \cos(\alpha + \alpha_2), ; \quad \tau = \alpha + 2\alpha_2 - \beta, \quad (6)
$$

where $G_{\text{in}}$ is the global solar incident radiation, $G_{\text{difh}}$ is the diffuse radiation on a horizontal surface, $G_{\text{h}}$ is the global solar radiation on a horizontal surface, $\alpha$ is the solar altitude angle, $\rho_{\text{g}}$ is the ground reflectance (ground albedo 0.2 without snow, 0.6 with snow), and $\rho_{\text{al}}$ is the reflectance of the aluminum sheet reflectors (0.8).

The solar zenith angle $(\theta_z)$ for a south-facing surface is given by⁹

$$
\sin \theta_z = \cos(\varphi - \beta) \cos \delta \cos \omega + \sin(\varphi - \beta) \sin \delta, \quad (7)
$$

where $\delta$ is the declination angle of the sun, which can be found in Ref. 9. $\varphi$ is the latitude of the location of the TESC and $\omega$ is the sun hour angle.

The solar altitude angle is determined as

$$
\sin \alpha = \cos \varphi \cos \delta \cos \omega + \sin \varphi \sin \delta. \quad (8)
$$

The solar azimuth angle is given by

$$
\cos \gamma = \frac{\sin \varphi \cos \delta \cos \omega - \cos \varphi \sin \delta}{\cos \alpha}. \quad (9)
$$

The analytical model given by Eqs. 1–9, after appropriate adaptation to our case, was used to calculate the optimal angle of the flat reflectors.

The concentration ratio $(C)$ was defined as the ratio of the total solar radiation incident on the TESC-HP with reflectors $(G_{\text{tot\_ref}})$ to the total solar radiation incident on the TESC-HP without reflectors $(G_{\text{tot}})$, which is given by

$$
C = \frac{G_{\text{tot\_ref}}}{G_{\text{tot}}}. \quad (10)
$$

## Heat Pump System

The heating capacity of the condenser $(Q_c)$ yielded¹⁰

$$
Q_{\text{c}} = m_{\text{w}} C_{\text{pw}}(T_{\text{wo}} - T_{\text{wi}}), \quad (11)
$$

where $m_{\text{w}}$ is the water flow rate, $C_{\text{pw}}$ is the specific heat of water, and $T_{\text{wo}}$ and $T_{\text{wi}}$ are the outlet and inlet water temperatures of the condenser, respectively.

The coefficient of performance (COP) of the heat pump system is defined as follows:

$$
\text{COP} = \frac{Q_{\text{c}}}{W_{\text{com}} + W_{\text{p}}}, \quad (12)
$$

where $W_{\text{com}}$ and $W_{\text{p}}$ are the power input to the compressor and water pump, respectively.

## TE Power Generation

The electrical output of the TE solar collector $(P)$ was calculated from the measured data as follows:

$$
P = I \cdot V, \quad (13)
$$

where $I$ and $V$ are the current and voltage of the TE modules at matched load.

Vorobiev et al.,¹¹ Peterson,¹² and Miller et al.¹³ suggested that the conversion efficiency $(\eta_{\text{e}})$ is as follows:

$$
\eta_{\text{e}} = \eta_{\text{c}} \frac{M - 1}{M + \frac{T_{\text{c}}}{T_{\text{h}}}}, \quad (14)
$$

where $M = \sqrt{1 + ZT_{\text{m}}}$ with $T_{\text{m}} = 0.5(T_{\text{h}} + T_{\text{c}})$.

$T_{\text{h}}$ and $T_{\text{c}}$ are the hot- and cold-side temperatures of the TE module, respectively, $T_{\text{m}}$ is the average temperature, $Z$ is the figure of merit of the TE material ($Z = 1.6 \times 10^{-3}$ 1/K),¹¹ and $\eta_{\text{c}}$ is the Carnot efficiency: $\eta_{\text{c}} = \frac{T_{\text{h}} - T_{\text{c}}}{T_{\text{h}}}$.

Note that $ZT_{\text{m}}$ is a characteristic parameter of the thermoelectric element and essentially governs its internal conversion efficiency. It is well known that the value of $Z$ can have strong variations with

temperature. In this study, to gain insight into the optimal collector operating temperatures, the value of $Z$ was assumed to be constant. Although this may be an oversimplification of the actual situation, it provides tractable solutions for the solar collector temperature and operating efficiency of the thermoelectric element.¹²

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The values of the optimal positions of the top and bottom reflectors for a given tilted plane angle ($\beta$) equal to $16^\circ$ were determined by means of the analytical model described by Eqs. 1–9. These values change during the year with the varying declination angle ($\delta$) of the sun. The optimal positions of the top and bottom reflectors during the year in the sun peak are presented in Fig. 3. As can be seen, the maximum calculated value of the optimal angle of the bottom reflector is $65.72^\circ$, obtained on 21 June (summer solstice), when the declination angle is at its maximum of $23.45^\circ$, whereas the minimum calculated value is $33.32^\circ$, obtained on 22 December (winter solstice), when the declination angle is at its minimum of $-23.45^\circ$. Contrary to the top reflector, the maximum calculated value of the optimal angle position of the top reflector is $7.98^\circ$, obtained on 22 December (winter solstice), whereas the minimum calculated value is $-24.41^\circ$, obtained on 21 June (summer solstice).

Figure 4 shows good agreement between the numerical results and experimental data for 4 months (November 2012 to February 2013). The experimental and calculated total solar radiation incident on the TE solar collector differ by less than $30\ \text{W/m}^2$.

## CONCLUSIONS

This work considered a TESC-HP with and without flat solar radiation reflectors. The changing optimal positions of the reflectors during the year were determined analytically and experimentally for a collector fixed at angle of $\beta = 16^\circ$ to obtain the maximum concentration of solar radiation intensity. Systematically good agreement between the numerical calculation and experimental measurements during the tested period was found, allowing reliable prediction of the values of the optimal reflector positions for different collector tilted plane angles. Daily test results of the TESC-HP with flat reflectors in their optimal positions are presented. At a water set point temperature of $40^\circ$C, the maximum COP was 5.60 and the hot water capacity was 1400 L/day. Meanwhile, the power output and conversion efficiency of the TE modules reached 10.09 W and 2.40%, respectively, representing improvements of 34.5% and 18.2%, respectively, compared with the TESC-HP without reflectors. These results show the positive effect of the reflectors made of aluminum sheet even when considering the additional small cost of the reflectors. Such economic evaluation would be an interesting topic for further study.
