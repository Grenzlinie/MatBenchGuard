# The influence of high pressure on transformation equilibria in iron meteorites*

A. E. RINGWOOD† and LARRY KAUFMAN‡

(Received 15 September 1961)

Abstract-Phase diagrams for the $\alpha-\gamma$ transformation equilibria in iron-nickel alloys at high pressures have previously been calculated by the authors. The phase compositions and structures of iron meteorites are discussed in terms of the high pressure diagrams, using arguments which were first advanced by H. H. Uhlig. It is concluded that the structures of iron meteorites were developed within the pressure range 30-60 kilobars. The new 50 kilobar diagram permits the time interval for formation of the Widmanstatten texture to be less than $10^{8}$ years, thus resolving a current controversy between advocates of high pressure and low pressure crystallization for iron meteorites.

The influence of minor components, principally phosphorus, on the crystallization equilibria is also considered. The small amounts of phosphorus usually occurring in meteorites do not change the binary Fe-Ni equilibrium sufficiently to modify the principal conclusions. However, the stabilizing effect of phosphorus on the $\alpha$ phase may account for the fact that meteoritic kamacite is slightly richer in nickel than would be expected from either the high pressure or the low pressure binary Fe-Ni phase diagram.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

THE phase relationships in iron meteorites are capable of providing valuable information about the crystallization conditions in the parent bodies from which the meteorites were derived. UHLIG (1954) has studied these relationships and has found that they cannot be interpreted in terms of the 1 atm iron-nickel phase diagram. He concluded that high pressures have influenced the crystallization equilibria, resulting in a depression of the $\alpha-\gamma$ transformation by about $200^{\circ} C$. However, this interpretation has led to difficulties, since UREY (1956) pointed out that such a depression would lead to impossibly long times being required for the formation of the Widmanstatten pattern. Evidently, a fundamental disagreement has arisen on this important subject.

A weakness in UHLIG's argument is the phase diagram with which he represents the Fe-Ni system at high pressure. UHLIG estimates that a pressure of $10^{5}$ bars would depress the $\gamma-\alpha$ transformation in pure iron by $200^{\circ} C$. He then assumes that a similar pressure would uniformly depress the whole Fe-Ni phase diagram without altering its shape. Since the pressure effect on the binary system depends upon molar volumes, which behave anomalously in the $\gamma$ alloys (Fig. 1), this assumption is unrealistic. Accordingly it appeared desirable to calculate the high pressure equilibria as a first step towards resolving the controversy.

Subsequent careful investigations failed to reveal the eutectoid. (OwEN and LIU, 1949). However, there is a possibility that a eutectoid might be developed at high pressure. ## 2. The System Fe-Ni at High Pressure

The free energies of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ Fe-Ni alloys as a function of composition, temperature, and pressure, may be expressed as follows:
$$
F_{(x, P, T)}^{\alpha}=F_{(x, T)}^{\alpha}+23 \cdot 9 P V_{(x, T)}^{\alpha} \mathrm{cal} / \mathrm{mol}
\tag{1}
$$
$$
F_{(x, P, T)}^{\gamma}=F_{(x, T)}^{\gamma}+23 \cdot 9 P V_{(x, T)}^{\gamma} \mathrm{cal} / \mathrm{mol}
\tag{2}
$$
where $F_{(x, T)}^{\gamma}$ is the free energy of the $\gamma$ phase as a function of composition and temperature at $1 \mathrm{~atm}, P$ is the pressure in kilobars, and $V_{(x, T)}^{\gamma}$ is the molar volume of the $\gamma$ phase as a function of composition and temperature. Equations for $F_{(x, T)}^{\alpha}$ and

$F_{(x,T)}^{\gamma}$ have previously been derived from the 1 atm thermodynamic properties of Fe-Ni alloys (KAUFMAN and COHEN, 1956, 1958). When these are used to calculate the Fe-Ni phase diagram at 1 atm, excellent agreement with the experimental results of OWEN and LIU is obtained (Fig. 2). This demonstrates that the expressions for $F_{(x,T)}^{\alpha}$ and $F_{(x,T)}^{\gamma}$ are satisfactory and internally consistent.

It is fortunate that extensive molar volume data for Fe-Ni alloys as a function of $x$ and $T$ are available. These have been calculated from the lattice parameter measurements of OWEN and YATES (1937), JETTE and FOOTE (1936) and BASINSKI *et al.* (1955), thus yielding $V_{(x,T)}^{\alpha}$ and $V_{(x,T)}^{\gamma}$. A further test of the adequacy of the thermodynamic data used above is a comparison of the calculated and experimentally determined pressure effects on the $\alpha-\gamma$ transition in pure iron. This has previously been carried out by KAUFMAN (1961), using the experimental data of several authors corrected for the new pressure calibrations of KENNEDY and LA MORI (1961). Excellent agreement over the pressure range 0-80 kilobars was obtained.

Equations (1) and (2) can now be used to calculate the free energies of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ alloys as functions of $P$, $T$ and $x$. Application of the rule of common tangents to these functions allows the compositions of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ alloys in equilibrium at specified pressures and temperatures to be calculated, and a phase diagram to be constructed.

The relevant calculations have been made by KAUFMAN and RINGWOOD (1961) at pressures of 50, 100 and 150 kilobars. Calculations were made independently by the above authors, and results were found to be in good agreement. The resultant Fe-Ni phase diagrams at 50 and 100 kilobars are shown in Figs. 3 and 4.

The most serious errors in the calculated diagram are introduced by the assumptions of constant compressibilities for $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ phases, and by ignoring

ordering reactions in $Ni_3Fe$. Whilst these errors may be significant, and must prevent the high pressure diagram from being applied too rigidly, they are unlikely to alter the major qualitative features. The errors are smallest in the 50 kilobar diagram, and in particular, on the iron rich side of this diagram. As it appears

## 4. APPLICATION TO METEORITIC IRONS

Most meteoritic irons contain more than 99 per cent of iron and nickel. Accordingly their constitution can be discussed in terms of the Fe-Ni phase diagram. The three principal constituents of meteoritic irons are kamacite, ( $\alpha$ phase), taenite ( $\gamma$ phase) and plessite, which is a fine intergrowth of kamacite and taenite. Iron meteorites are classified in three principal groups-hexahedrites,* octahedrites and nickel-rich ataxites. The former consist of kamacite, and contain up to 6 per cent of nickel. Octahedrites contain between 6 and 13 per cent of nickel and are composed of kamacite, taenite and plessite in varying proportions. The kamacite and taenite display a characteristic structural orientation which gives rise to the Widmanstatten pattern. The structure has probably been derived by very slow cooling through the $\gamma-\alpha$ transition. Nickel-rich ataxites contain more than 13 per cent of nickel and are composed mainly of plessite and scattered kamacite.

UHLIG (1954) has extensively discussed the structures and phase compositions in iron meteorites and has pointed out several features which give rise to inconsistencies when interpreted in terms of the 1 atm Fe-Ni phase diagram. (Fig. 2.)

* Another group-the nickel-poor ataxites-are often regarded as reheated hexahedrites.

Subsequently FELLER-KNIEPMEIER and UHLIG (1961) and LOVERING and PARRY (in press) have provided further compositional data on the various phases, which supports UHLIG's earlier conclusions. For an adequate exposition of these conclusions, the reader is referred to the original source. The following summary of UHLIG's arguments must suffice for the present. The arguments are based upon the following observations:

(i) Widmanstatten (W) textures are not found in irons containing more than 13 per cent of nickel. This implies that these irons (nickel-rich ataxites) have never cooled slowly through the $\gamma-\alpha$ transformation, which at one atmosphere begins at $650^{\circ}C$ for a 13 per cent nickel alloy (Fig. 2). If they had, then W-textures should have formed, since diffusion rates are still sufficiently high below $650^{\circ}C$. If Fig. 2 is applied, rapid cooling or quenching of meteorites from $650^{\circ}C$ would be required.

(ii) Plessite, which usually contains 15-17 per cent of nickel, is common in octahedrites and is the most abundant constituent of nickel-rich ataxites. It is probable that plessite was derived from an earlier, low nickel taenite. Application of Fig. 2 would require that it was quenched suddenly from around $600^{\circ}C$. Slower cooling would have caused a Widmanstatten pattern to form in the plessite and would have resulted in coarser crystallization.

(iii) The metastable $\alpha_{2}$ martensitic phase sometimes occurs in nickel-rich ataxites. In the alloys containing less than 25 per cent of nickel this can be produced only by sudden cooling of taenite from a high temperature or by the sudden release of high confining pressure (KAUFMAN et al., 1961).

(iv) Taenite from octahedrites commonly contains about 30 per cent of nickel at its borders, implying that transformation and Widmanstatten pattern formation occurred between $650^{\circ}$ and $500^{\circ}C$.

(v) Taenite lamellae in octahedrites are characteristically inhomogeneous, often with 15-20 per cent nickel plessitic cores grading continuously into 30 per cent nickel borders. A transition from 15 to 30 per cent nickel may take place in a distance of only 0.02 mm. If Fig. 2 applies, such taenites must have cooled slowly from $650^{\circ}$ to below $500^{\circ}C$, in equilibrium with neighbouring homo- geneous kamacite. Yet it is known that diffusion is comparatively rapid in this temperature interval, and slow cooling should have homogenized the taenite.

Observations (i), (ii) and (iii) above might be explained if iron meteorites had cooled slowly to $650^{\circ}C$, and had then been subjected to very rapid cooling or quenching. However, it is widely believed that the parent bodies of iron meteorites were of substantial size—the weight of Canyon Diablo, which itself is only a fragment of a parent body, was probably in the vicinity of 100,000 tons (NININGER, 1956). Most workers believe that the parent bodies were at least of asteroidal size. Such bodies cannot be cooled quickly. Their thermal inertia is too large by many orders of magnitude to explain (i), (ii) and (iii), which require cooling of $200^{\circ}C$ to take place in periods of minutes, hours, or at the most, days. At the

same time, the quenching hypothesis does not provide an explanation for (iv) and (v) above, which appear to be inconsistent with (i), (ii) and (iii).

UHLIG shows that observations (i) to (v) imply an attempted equilibrium in meteoritic irons at temperatures lower than are provided by Fig. 2. He suggests that irons have crystallized under high pressure, which has depressed the $\gamma-\alpha$ transformation equilibria uniformly by about $200^\circ$C. This would cause meteorites containing more than 13 per cent of nickel to enter the $\gamma-\alpha$ transformation region below $500^\circ$C. Under these conditions, diffusion rates in Fe-Ni alloys are very slow, and formation of Widmanstatten textures would not be possible within the time available. Furthermore, since final equilibrium is reached in a temperature region where diffusion is very slow, the preservation of plessite, $\alpha_2$ phase and inhomogeneous taenite becomes explicable.

If the meteoritic parent body had been broken up, and the high internal pressures released, so that the phase boundaries moved to the positions shown in Fig. 2, the above phases would remain kinetically stable because of the low temperature. In addition, sudden release of pressure would cause adiabatic cooling. At 50 kilobars, this might amount to $40^\circ$C (RocCA and BEVER, 1950). In the temperature range between $400^\circ$ and $300^\circ$C, such a cooling would cause a further large decrease in diffusion rates.

UREY (1956) has criticized UHLIG's hypothetical high pressure diagram. The lower temperatures imply that the time interval required for formation of the W-textures was greater than $10^9$ years. There is strong evidence from meteorites that this pattern was formed in less than $10^8$ years.

In the previous sections, we have seen that high pressure considerably alters the shape of the Fe-Ni phase diagram, in addition to causing a general depression. It is possible that this alteration of shape may reconcile UHLIG's arguments with UREY's criticism.

We will now consider meteoritic equilibria and UHLIG's arguments in terms of our high pressure diagrams (Figs. 3 and 4).

It does not appear likely that the 100 kilobar diagram will be applicable to meteorites. Although a eutectoid is present and has the right composition to explain plessite, the transformation temperatures are depressed below $300^\circ$C, and UREY's objections apply. Furthermore, the diagram requires the presence of coexisting high $(>60\%$ Ni) and low nickel* taenites, due to immiscibility of the $\gamma$ alloys. These are not observed. Finally, the diagram cannot explain the inhomogeneous taenite lamellae characteristic of octahedrites. These are composed of plessitic cores passing into 30 per cent nickel margins. It therefore appears that meteoritic irons have crystallized under pressures which were substantially lower than 100 kilobars. However, this makes it difficult to interpret plessite as a eutectoid, since the nickel content at the eutectoid would exceed that of plessite at pressures much below 100 kilobars. With some regret, therefore, the authors have discarded their earlier suggestion that the *common varieties* of meteoritic plessite might represent a high pressure eutectoid. However, we do not wish to completely

* The low nickel taenites would probably invert to martensitic $\alpha_2$ phase or plessite during subsequent cooling.

abandon this suggestion. It is possible that some rather rare types of coarse plessite might be of eutectoid origin (Section 5).

The 50 kilobar diagram (Fig. 3) appears more relevant. Because the relative depression of the $\gamma/\alpha+\gamma$ boundary decreases with increasing nickel content (Table 1) it is possible to reconcile the views of UHLIG and UREY. A pressure of 50 kilobars lowers the beginning of transformation temperature of a 13 per cent nickel alloy from $650^\circ$C to $490^\circ$C. This satisfies the most important requirement of UHLIG's interpretation—*viz.* that transformation of meteorites with more than 13 per cent of nickel occurred below $500^\circ$C where diffusion rates were sufficiently low to prevent the formation of W-textures within the time available and preserve plessite, inhomogeneous taenite and $\alpha_2$ phase.

At the same pressure, the transformation for 30 per cent nickel alloys begins at $385^\circ$C, compared with $300^\circ$C in UHLIG's model. Thus, the formation of zoned taenite lamellae occurs between $470^\circ$ and $385^\circ$C according to Fig. 3, compared to $420$-$300^\circ$C according to UHLIG's diagram. There is a very large difference in average diffusion rates between these two ranges, and the long period required for W-pattern formation according to UHLIG's diagram is greatly reduced for Fig. 3.

A further property of the new diagram is the high rate at which the $\gamma/(\alpha+\gamma)$ boundary changes with decreasing temperature. A temperature fall of only $85^\circ$C causes the taenite composition to change from 15 per cent nickel to 30 per cent nickel. This makes it possible to understand the high concentration gradients which are observed in taenite. Furthermore, it increases the difficulties associated with Widmanstatten pattern formation for alloys in this region.

The selection of 50 kilobars as the pressure at which to calculate the Fe-Ni phase diagram was largely fortuitous. By interpolation, it appears that UHLIG's observations can be satisfied in a 10 kilobar range on either side of this value although the range 40-50 kilobars is rather more satisfactory than 50-60 kilobars. Considering the uncertainties in the diagrams, we may conclude that the major structures of iron meteorites were probably developed under pressures ranging from 30 to 60 kilobars.

The taenite two phase field at 50 kilobars does not intersect the $\gamma/(\alpha+\gamma)$ boundary. However, high nickel taenites (25-40% Ni) should pass into this field if they cooled below $300^\circ$C. This may possibly explain a common feature of meteoritic taenites. The marginal regions of many taenites appear dark when etched. Electron probe microanalysis (FELLER-KNIEPMEIER and UHLIG, 1961) shows that these darkly etching regions contain about 30 per cent of nickel. Since such high nickel contents should confer corrosion resistance in a homogeneous taenite the dark etching presumably reflects phase inhomogeneity. This may possibly be due to the taenite cooling under pressure into the immiscibility field of Fig. 3, giving rise to an intergrowth of two taenites.

## 5. THE ROLE OF MINOR COMPONENTS

Iron meteorites usually contain small quantities of minor components—particularly P, C, S and Co. It is necessary to enquire into the possible effects which they may have on the binary Fe-Ni equilibrium. In order to affect this equilibrium significantly, they must be substantially soluble in either the kamacite

or taenite. Thus, sulphur is eliminated, since it occurs as a separate immiscible phase (troilite). In most irons, carbon is present in amounts less than 0.01 per cent. Such amounts are insufficient to affect the equilibria significantly. Cobalt averages about 0.5 per cent in meteoritic irons. It is enriched by a factor of two in kamacite compared to taenite (YANVEL et al., 1958). However, it can be shown that this degree of enrichment, in conjunction with the amount of cobalt present, cannot affect the binary Fe-Ni equilibrium significantly.

It appears that the only element which merits serious consideration is phosphorus, which occurs in small amounts in most irons, averaging about 0.2 per cent. It has sometimes been claimed that the phosphorus content exerts a drastic effect on the equilibria, and that the constitution of iron meteorites can only be considered adequately in terms of the ternary system Fe-Ni-P (VOGEL, 1951). This view is no doubt correct when applied to the rare irons which contain more than 0.5 per cent of phosphorus. However, the following arguments show that previous conclusions based upon consideration of the binary system Fe-Ni are unlikely to require substantial modification because of the phosphorus which is present in most irons.

(a) Phosphorus is preferentially soluble in the $\alpha$-phase, and has the effect of stabilizing it relatively to $\gamma$ phase. Accordingly it increases the transformation temperature of pure iron. It is instructive to compare the relative effects of phosphorus and nickel, The isothermal (martensitic) $\alpha-\gamma$ transformation temperature in iron is increased by about $60^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ by 0.2 per cent phosphorus (HANSEN, 1958). It requires $2 \frac{1}{2}$ per cent of nickel to decrease the $\alpha-\gamma$ martensitic transition by a similar amount. Thus, as a very rough guide, we might expect 0.2 per cent of phosphorus to alter the zero pressure phase boundaries by an amount equivalent to $2 \frac{1}{2}$ per cent of nickel but in the opposite direction. Since phosphorus stabilizes the $\alpha$ phase, its effect in meteorites will be to increase the nickel content of kamacites in equilibrium with a given taenite.

(b) The phosphorus contents of irons are highly variable, mostly ranging between zero and 0.5 per cent. If such quantities of phosphorus exerted a drastic effect upon the Fe-Ni equilibrium, the remarkable compositional uniformity of phases and structures which is observed in meteoritic irons (OwEN and BURNS, 1939; PERRY, 1944; FELLER-KNIEPMEIER and UHLIG, 1960) would be quite inexplicable.

It is highly improbable that such small concentrations of phosphorus could cause substantial changes in the binary Fe-Ni equilibria.

(c) OwEN and BURNS (1939) experimented extensively upon meteoritic irons and found that they behaved similarly to pure artificial Ni-Fe alloys during heat treatment. The effects of phosphorus were not noticeable.

There is probably a natural restriction on the amount of phosphorus which is available to affect the Fe-Ni equilibrium in meteorites. The solubility of phosphorus in nickel $(\gamma)$ is negligible, whilst the maximum observed solubility of phosphorus in $\gamma$ iron is only about 0.2 per cent. (HANSEN, 1958). Thus, any phosphorus in excess of 0.2 per cent probably crystallizes as schreibersite, whilst the meteoritic iron is in the $\gamma$ state.* During the $\gamma-\alpha$ transformation, this phosphorus is effectively trapped, and unable to enter the equilibrium owing to kinetic effects. The only phosphorus which affects the equilibrium is the small amount which was originally in solid solution in the $\gamma$ phase. This eventually precipitated from the $\alpha$ phase at low temperature in the form of rhabdite crystals.

It is just possible that phosphorus may have a relatively greater effect upon the Fe-Ni phase diagram at higher pressures. The free energy curves for $\gamma$ alloys at 50 kilobars and $300-400^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ are broad and flat, and of such a shape that quite small changes in free energy could substantially affect the shape and position of the immiscibility region. Thus, a small amount of phosphorus dissolved non-ideally in $\gamma$ phase might raise the top of the miscibility gap of Fig. 3 sufficiently to cause a eutectoid to appear in the 50 kilobar diagram. Some rare types of coarse plessite with structures resembling eutectoids, might conceivably result from this cause.

## 6. The Composition of Kamacite

The compositions of meteoritic kamacites are somewhat anomalous, and cannot be explained by the one atmosphere Fe-Ni phase diagram, nor by either of the high pressure diagrams. The nickel content of kamacite ranges between 6 and 7.5 per cent, whilst the *mean* composition of the coexisting taenites are between 20 and 25 per cent nickel. (OwEN and BURNS, 1939; FELLER-KNIEPMEIER and UHLIG, 1961). From the 1 atm diagram (Fig. 2) we would expect that kamacite in equilibrium with taenite of this composition would contain 4 per cent of nickel. In the 50 kilobar phase diagram the kamacite should contain only 3 per cent of nickel.

Although it was previously concluded that phosphorus will not grossly affect the equilibria in meteoritic irons, it may have a small, but significant effect upon the kamacite composition. In the previous section it was estimated that 0.2 per cent of phosphorus could increase the nickel content of kamacite by about $2 \frac{1}{2}$ per cent. This may be sufficient to account for the discrepancy.
