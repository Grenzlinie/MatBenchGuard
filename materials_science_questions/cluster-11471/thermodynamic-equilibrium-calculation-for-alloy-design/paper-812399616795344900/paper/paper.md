Transmission microscopy studies⁽²⁾ have revealed that enhanced dispersion strengthening is inadequate to account for the incremental increase in yield strength of martensite by deforming metastable austenite at temperatures near one-half the absolute melting temperature. The purpose of this note is to illustrate that strength increases in the as-quenched structure are obtained not only by a more effective strain-induced carbide dispersion, but also by an increase in dislocation density. The combined beneficial effects are partially negated by a loss in interstitial solid solution strengthening due to the formation of carbides at the expense of depleting carbon from the matrix. The argument will be based on previously documented results,⁽²⁾ given in more detail in another report,⁽³⁾ used in conjunction with the mechanistic approach taken by Conrad.⁽⁴⁾

Conrad⁽⁴⁾ states that the yield strength is given by the sum of three contributions:

$$\text{(1) } \quad \tau_{\rho}=\alpha_{1} \mu b \rho^{\frac{1}{2}}: \text{ dislocation density}$$

$$\text{(2) } \quad \tau_{\lambda}=\alpha_{2} \mu b(1 / \lambda): \text{ precipitated particles}$$

$$\text{(3) } \quad \tau_{f}=\left(\alpha_{3} / b a_{0}{ }^{3}\right) f: \text{ interstitial atoms}$$

where $\alpha_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2} \cong 0.3$ to 0.4 and $\alpha_{3} \cong 38 \times 10^{-20}$ dyn-cm², $f$ is the atom fraction of interstitial atoms and $a_{0}=2.87 \mathring{A}$. $\mu=8.25 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{dyn} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ or $12 \times 10^{3} \mathrm{ksi}$ and $b=2.48 \mathring{A}$.

Results⁽²,³,⁵⁾ have illustrated the existence of particles, suggested to be alloy carbides, in the as-quenched structure of a Cr-Mo-V steel (H-11). Also present was a fine dislocation network.⁽²,³,⁵⁾ Twenty-five per cent rolling deformation of austenite at $1000^{\circ} \mathrm{F}$ prior to quenching in oil produced an increase in dislocation density and an increase in particle density with little or no change in particle size.⁽²,³⁾ The particle size was about $40 \mathring{A}^{(3)}$ and the corresponding increase in particle density,⁽³⁾ $\Delta(1 / \lambda)$, was $(1.64-1.41=0.23) \times 10^{6} \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ where $\lambda$ was taken as the center-to-center inter-particle spacing minus the particle diameter. The corresponding increase in dislocation density,⁽³⁾ $\Delta\left(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)$, was $(1.47-1.15=0.32) \times 10^{6} \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The corresponding incremental increase in yield strength of the Cr-Mo-V steel with a $400^{\circ} \mathrm{F}$ temper was experimentally determined to be 50-70 ksi in tension.

Under the conditions described above, a $65^{\circ} \mathrm{F}$ increase in $M_{s}$ was recorded due to the 25 per cent deformation at $1000^{\circ} \mathrm{F} .^{(2,3)}$ If this increase in $M_{s}$ is assumed to result from carbide formation at the expense of depleting carbon and the carbide forming alloying element from solid solution, the decrease in atom fraction of interstitial carbon can be estimated. Assuming the strain-induced carbides to be $\mathrm{M}_{2} \mathrm{C}$, as suggested,⁽¹⁾ and using the relationship:⁽⁶⁾ $\Delta M_{s}\left({ }^{\circ} \mathrm{F}\right)=650 \times \Delta(\mathrm{wt} . \% \mathrm{C})+K \times \Delta(\mathrm{wt} . \% \mathrm{M})$, where $K$ equals 70 for Cr and 50 for Mo, the weight percent depletion of interstitial carbon can then be calculated as 0.05. This value is only slightly dependent on the type of carbide. The change in atom fraction $(\Delta f)$ is then found to be 0.0022 for the particular low alloy steel of composition:⁽³⁾ 0.39 C-5.08 Cr-1.40 Mo-0.54 V-0.26 Mn-0.98 Si-0.011 P-0.008 S.

If these variations in substructural parameters are responsible for the incremental increase in yield strength due to ausforming, the contribution from each effect can then be estimated from equations (1), (2) and (3). Thus,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{(1-1) } \Delta \tau_{\rho} & =\alpha_{1} \mu b \Delta\left(\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\right) \\
& =2.0 \text{ to } 2.6 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{dyn} / \mathrm{cm}^{2} \text{ or } 29-38 \mathrm{ksi}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{(2-2) } \Delta \tau_{\lambda} & =\alpha_{2} \mu b \Delta(1 / \lambda) \\
& =1.4 \text{ to } 1.9 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{dyn} / \mathrm{cm}^{2} \text{ or } 21-27 \mathrm{ksi}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{(3-3) } \Delta \tau_{f} & =\left(\alpha_{3} / b a_{0}{ }^{3}\right) \Delta f \\
& =-1.4 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{dyn} / \mathrm{cm}^{2} \text{ or }-21 \mathrm{ksi}
\end{aligned}
$$

The sum $\Delta \tau_{\rho}+\Delta \tau_{\lambda}+\Delta \tau_{f}=\frac{1}{2} \Delta \sigma$ predicts 60 to 88 ksi increase in tensile yield strength which agrees extremely well with the experimentally determined value of 50-70 ksi.

The implication from these calculations is that the net gain in yield strength due to the strain induced dispersion is almost entirely offset by the concomitant decrease in yield strength due to the depletion of interstitial carbon. The depletion of substitutional alloying elements is neglected since they play a minor role in the strength of martensite, but they would make the competing effects even more comparable.

One might be inclined to conclude that the incremental increase in yield strength results solely from an increase in dislocation density with carbide formation unnecessary. Quite the contrary, the role of carbide formation in ausforming is probably necessary for two very important reasons: (1) As shown by Lewis and Martin,⁽⁷⁾ dislocation densities are increased with a decrease in inter-particle spacing which is the condition created by the strain-induced increase in particle density and (2) The precipitated particles pin

the substructure developed at a relatively high deformation temperature.

## Substructures in melt-grown zinc-0.1% thallium alloy crystals*

Damiano and Tint^(1) have examined the substructure on polished and suitably etched surfaces in a Zn-0.1% Cd alloy, grown in a Pyrex crucible. Two substructures could be distinguished just behind the interface, a "primary" structure of large pits arranged in cells, the cells being elongated in the direction of the basal trace, and a "secondary" structure of smaller pits. The secondary structure consisted both of long rows of small pits approximately parallel to the primary cell boundaries, and short rows of pits perpendicular to the primary boundaries. There were also some random small pits. A short distance behind the decanted interface the primary structure faded out and the secondary structure formed an equiaxed array. Kratochvil and Sichova,^(2) examining sectioned surfaces in crystals of 99.95% Zn, grown by the Czochralski method, observed pits outlining elongated cells throughout the specimen; the pits moved on annealing to give equiaxed sub-grains. It appears that the primary structure is associated with impurities, and as homogenization proceeds dislocations in the impurity cell walls are freed to take up equilibrium positions.

In the present investigation crystals were prepared from an alloy of 0.1% Tl in Zn, using the Czochralski method (speed of growth 24 cm/hr). Purity was 99.999% for both metals. The following observations were made:
