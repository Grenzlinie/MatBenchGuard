# ANALYSIS OF CARBIDE FORMATION IN A CYANIDED LAYER AND METHODS FOR CALCULATING EQUILIBRIUM COMPOSITIONS OF SATURATING ATMOSPHERES

N. V. Bogdanova and P. É. Shenderei

Translated from Metallovedenie i Termicheskaya Obrabotka Metallov, No. 11, pp. 11 - 15, November, 1996.

It is known that one of the defects of a carburized (cyanided) layer is a carbide network, which worsens considerably the characteristics of parts. The present paper is devoted to the causes of its formation in a cyanided layer in steel 35G2. The boundaries of deposition of carbon black and the carbon potential in a cyaniding furnace are calculated. The effect of the alloying elements and the carbon content in steels 08kp and 35G2 on the final distribution of carbon in the diffusion layer is determined. The equilibrium composition of a saturating atmosphere that provides cyaniding without deposition of carbon black in the functional zones of the furnace or carbide formation (as a network or as a solid layer) on the surface of the metal is calculated.

In order to eliminate carbide formation on the surface of a part the carbon potential of the saturating atmosphere should be controlled in the entire carburization (cyaniding) process. It is especially important to control the carbon potential in the final zone of through furnaces before hardening the parts.

The carbon potential in carburization (cyaniding) in Hol- croft furnaces used in the Volzhskii Automobile Plant (VAZ) is controlled with the help of infrared gas analyzers supplied by Leeds Northrup (USA). The composition of the atmos- phere is controlled with respect to $CH_{4}$ in the saturation zone and with respect to $CO_{2}$ in the precooling zone (before hard ening).

Despite a well-working system of control of the composi- tion of the furnace atmosphere there are cases where carbides are formed in the carburized (cyanided) layer. For example, in joint treatment of parts of different steels in a Holcroft three-zone through installation carbides in the form of an in- terrupted network have been observed in the cyanided layer of parts of steel 35G2. The same parts produced of steel 08kp did not contain carbides.

The aim of the present work consists in analyzing the thermodynamic conditions (causes) of the formation (or ab- sence) of carbides in cyaniding of the strengthened layer of parts of steels 08kp and 35G2 and determining the equilib- rium composition of an atmosphere that should provide de- fectless chemical heat treatment of parts.

The specimens studied were $10 × 20 × 2$ mm in size and included $0.05-0.11\%$ C, $<0.03\%$ Si, $0.25-0.5\%$ Mn for steel 08kp and $0.31-0.39\%$ C, $0.16-0.37\%$ Si, $1.4-1.8\%$ Mn for steel 35G2. The experimental specimens were cy- anided in a three-zone through installation of the Holcroft firm in accordance with the regimes presented in Table 1.

A metallographic analysis has shown that the cyanided layer of specimens made of steel 35G2 contained carbides, whereas in specimens made of steel 08kp they were absent.

We analyzed the causes of formation of carbides by com- paring the calculated carbon content in the surface layer of the specimens and the "boundaries of carbon black."¹

The boundaries of deposition of carbon black (carbide formation in unalloyed austenite) under the operating temperature of the cyaniding regime were determined by the formula for evaluating the carbon potential of a furnace atmosphere (i.e., the carbon content in unalloyed austenite in equilibrium with the furnace atmosphere) [1], namely,

$$
C_{\mathrm{b}}=\frac{A_{\mathrm{C}} \cdot 100}{1.07 \cdot \exp \left(\frac{4196.6}{t_{\mathrm{f}}+273}\right)+19.5 A_{\mathrm{C}}}, \tag{1}
$$

where $t_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the furnace temperature, $^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, and $A_{\mathrm{C}}$ is the activity of carbon in iron relative to graphite, which is determined by the formula

$$
A_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{[\mathrm{CO}] \cdot\left[\mathrm{H}_{2}\right]}{100 \cdot\left[\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right] \cdot K}, \tag{2}
$$

where $[\mathrm{CO}],\left[\mathrm{H}_{2}\right],\left[\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right]$ are the partial pressures of the components of the gas atmosphere, $K$ is the equilibrium constant of the reaction $\mathrm{CO}+\mathrm{H}_{2}=[\mathrm{C}]+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(K=0.025,10.36$, and 11.85 at $t_{\mathrm{f}}=810,820$, and $830^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, respectively), $[\mathrm{C}]$ is the carbon concentration in the solid solution.

In the given case at $A_{\mathrm{C}}=1$ the carbon potential $C_{\mathrm{b}}$ is the boundary of carbon black deposition, i.e., the potential of the atmosphere at which carbon black begins to be deposited in the functional space of the furnace and carbide formation begins in unalloyed austenite.

Calculated results are presented in Table 2. An analysis of the data has shown that in order to eliminate carbon black deposition in the zones of the furnace (and carbide formation in unalloyed austenite) in carburization (cyaniding) the carbon potential of the saturating atmosphere should not exceed $0.91,0.94$, and 0.97 for 810,820, and $830^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ respectively. The carbon potentials of the saturating atmosphere for the zones of the furnace for the regime of cyaniding of a lever of steel 35G2 presented in Table 1 have been calculated by formulas (1) and (2).

An example of the calculation of $A_{\mathrm{C}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{b}}$ for the third zone of the cyaniding unit is given below:

$$
A_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{17.2 \cdot 18.0}{100 \cdot 0.189 \cdot 10.36}=1.58 ;
$$

$$
C_{\mathrm{b}}=\frac{1.58 \cdot 100}{1.07 \exp (4196.6 \cdot 1093)+19.5 \cdot 1.58}=1.349,
$$

where 17.2 and 18.0 are the contents of $\mathrm{CO}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ in the third zone of the furnace (Table 1) (at a pressure of the saturating medium equal to $1 \times 10^{5} \mathrm{~Pa}$ the partial pressures of the components virtually equal their volume percentages), 0.189 is the content of steam corresponding to a dew point $t_{\mathrm{d}}=15^{\circ} \mathrm{C}, 10.36$ is the equilibrium constant for a heterogeneous reaction of the water gas (at $820^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ ).

An analysis of the data of Table 2 has shown that in cyaniding of parts of steel 35G2 the third zone of the furnace is under conditions favorable for deposition of carbon black, because the carbon potential of its atmosphere exceeds the boundary of carbon black deposition for $820^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ $\left(C_{\mathrm{b}}=1.35 \%>0.94 \%\right)$.

The next stage consists in calculating (and plotting) the distribution of the carbon concentration over the thickness of the surface layer of specimens of steels 35G2 and 08kp after cyaniding in a Holcroft furnace by the regime presented in Table 1.

The mass exchange between the gas atmosphere and the surface of the part is determined by the following method: the carbon potential of the atmosphere is known but equilibrium between the gas phase and the surface has not been attained; this is a boundary condition of the third kind, and the mass flow of carbon $(J)$ is determined by the formula

$$
J=b t \cdot\left(C_{\mathrm{b}}-C_{\text {surf }}\right), \tag{3}
$$

where $b t$ is the coefficient of mass transfer, which characterizes the total rate of all processes of mass exchange on the surface of the part, $C_{\text {surf }}$ is the carbon content on the surface of the part with a structure of unalloyed austenite attained while the parts reside in the furnace.

The coefficient of mass transfer has been calculated using the measured carbon contents in 0.1-mm-thick foils of steel 10 after different holds in the saturating atmosphere of the furnace $(10,20,40,60,80 \mathrm{~min})$, including the hold needed for attaining a carbon concentration in the foil that is in equilibrium with the atmosphere. For a Holcroft three-zone furnace the coefficient of mass transfer $b t=0.2 \mathrm{~mm} / \mathrm{h}$.

The processes of transfer of carbon atoms from the surface into the depth of the part were calculated by [1] Fick's equation for a diffusion flow

$$
J=D \frac{d C}{d x}=D \frac{C_{\text {surf }}-C_{\mathrm{ib}}}{h} \tag{4}
$$

and Arrhenius's one-dimensional diffusion equation

$$
D=D_{0} \exp (-E / R T), \tag{5}
$$

where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient for unalloyed austenite, $b$ is the thickness of the saturated surface layer, $C_{\text {surf }}$ is the carbon concentration on the surface of the part, $C_{\mathrm{ib}}$ is the carbon concentration on the internal boundary of the saturated layer, $C_{\text {surf }}-C_{\text {ib }}$ is the concentration gradient, $D_{0}$ is a constant quantity, $E$ is the experimentally determined activation energy of diffusion, $R$ is the universal gas constant.

Since the effect of the alloying elements is not allowed for, the calculation was conducted for unalloyed austenite with a content of carbon equal to its concentration in steels AS35G2 and 08kp, i.e., for steels of types 35 and 08:

Calculated data are presented in Table 3 and Fig. 1.

It can be seen from Table 3 that after a 0.33-h hold in an atmosphere of the third zone of the furnace with a carbon potential $C_{b}=1.35\%$ at $820^{\circ}C$ the carbon content on the surface of a part of steel of type 08 has not attained the critical value ($C_{surr}=0.907\% < 0.94\%$), whereas for steel of type 35 the carbon content exceeded the maximum solubility ($C_{surr}=0.97\% > 0.94\%$).

The data obtained show that the initial concentration of carbon in unalloyed steel affects its final distribution after cyaniding. With an increase in the initial carbon content (other conditions being equal) the carbon concentration increases over the entire thickness of the diffusion layer, and if the cyaniding is below the boundary of deposition of carbon black the probability that carbides will be formed increases.

Fig. 1. Distribution of carbon concentration over the thickness of the diffusion layer in unalloyed steel with 0.08% (a) and 0.35% (b) carbon after cyaniding by the regime presented in Table 1 (h is the distance from the surface, the numbers at the curves denote the zones).

This feature is explained in [1] by the direct proportionality of the diffusion coefficient of carbon in carburization to its initial content in the steel. The diffusivity increases with growth of the carbon concentration due to the decrease in the activation energy of carbon diffusion and the displacement of carbon by carbon in the solid austenite solution (established in [2]). This creates a situation where "the same concentration gradient at a higher concentration of carbon is accompanied by a higher gradient of its activity and a more efficient driving force of the diffusion" [1].

If the hold time in the zone of carbon black deposition (in zone 3) is increased to 0.5 h or more, carbides begin to form even in steel of type 08, where the initial carbon content is lower (Table 3).

The carbon concentration on the surface of parts of steels 35G2 and 08kp after cyaniding was calculated by a formula that allows for the proportion of the maximum dissolution of the alloying elements in austenite, namely,
$$
\begin{align*}
\log \frac{C_{g}}{C_{l}} &= 0.058(\%\ \text{Si}) + 0.01(\%\ \text{Ni}) \\
&\quad - 0.02(\%\ \text{Mn}) - 0.04(\%\ \text{Cr}), \tag{6}
\end{align*}
$$
where $C_{g}$ is the carbon potential of the gas atmosphere or the carbon content in unalloyed austenite in equilibrium with the atmosphere (in the given case $C_{g}$ is understood as the concentration of carbon $C_{surr}$ on the surface of steels of types 35 and 08 attained in the time τ, because equilibrium does not occur under conditions of formation of carbon black), $C_{l}$ is the carbon content in the steel in equilibrium with the atmosphere, i.e., in alloyed austenite (in the given case $C_{l}$ is the concentration of carbon $C_{a}$ attained on the surface of steels 35G2 and 08kp in the time τ).

The effect of the alloying components on the carbon concentration in steel compared to the carbon content in unalloyed austenite was evaluated with the help of the alloying coefficient $f$ determined by the formula
$$
f = \frac{C_{g}}{C_{l}}. \tag{7}
$$

For a nonequilibrium state
$$
f = \frac{C_{surr}}{C_{a}}.
$$

In the next stage of the investigation we calculated optimum characteristics of the furnace atmosphere that should provide cyaniding without carbides in the steel or carbon black in the furnace.

The optimum values of the dew point and the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere of the second zone of the furnace were calculated by formula (2) and the equation

$$
A_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{[\mathrm{CO}] \cdot[\mathrm{CO}]}{100 \cdot\left[\mathrm{CO}_{2}\right] \cdot K}, \qquad (8)
$$

where [CO] and $[CO_{2}]$ are the partial pressures of the components of the gas atmosphere (at a pressure of $\sim 1 \times 10^{5}$ Pa the partial pressures are virtually equal to the volume percentages of the components in the furnace), $K$ is the equilibrium constant of the reaction $2CO=CO_{2}+[C]$ ($K=10.337$ for $820^{\circ}C$), [C] is the carbon concentration in the solid solution.

The concentrations of carbon monoxide and hydrogen were mostly chosen to be equal to or slightly different from the values determined in the technological control of the composition of the atmosphere (see Table 1).

An analysis of the calculated results (Table 4) has shown that in order to maintain the carbon potential in the second zone of the furnace at a level sufficient for saturation ($C_{b}>0.75\%$) but not exceeding the boundary of deposition of carbon black ($C_{b}<0.94\%$), the dew point should range from $-5.5$ to $-9.5^{\circ}C$ or the concentration of $CO_{2}$ should be maintained within $0.28-0.415\%$ range.

However, these data cannot be treated as exact because the concentrations of hydrogen and carbon monoxide have been chosen approximately and the calculated composition of the atmosphere (Table 4) probably differs from the equilibrium one and can be used only as a standard for the technological operation of cyaniding.

Table 5 presents several variants of an equilibrium composition of a cyaniding atmosphere ($C_{b}=0.75-0.94\%$) for $t=820^{\circ}C$, calculated with the help of equations of gas equilibria.

An analysis of the data obtained has shown that the optimum parameters of a cyaniding atmosphere at $820^{\circ}C$ are as follows: the dew point $t_{d}$ ranging from $-5.3$ to $-8.4^{\circ}C$, the concentration of carbon dioxide ranging from 0.314 to 0.403%.

We have thus established that the main reasons behind the segregation of carbides in the surface layer of steel 35G2 and their absence in steel 08kp after cyaniding by the same regime in a Holcroft furnace are as follows:

– the higher carbon content in steel 35G2 than in steel 08kp;

– the conduction of the cyaniding in the third zone of the furnace in a regime of carbon black deposition.

In order to eliminate the formation of carbides in the surface layer and the deposition of carbon black in the furnace the cyaniding of parts of different steels under diminished saturation ($830^{\circ}C$) and quenching ($820^{\circ}C$) temperatures should be conducted while maintaining the carbon potential of the furnace atmosphere in both zones at a level not exceeding the boundary of formation of carbon black.
