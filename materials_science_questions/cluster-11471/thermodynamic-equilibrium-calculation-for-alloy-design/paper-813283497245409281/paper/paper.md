# Correlation between Microstructures and Yield
Strength of a High Strength Enameling Steel

Ming Jiang$^{1)}$, Xuefeng Yang$^{1)}$, Shiyan Pan$^{1)}$, Bruce W. Krakauer$^{2)}$ and Mingfang Zhu$^{1)\dagger}$

1) Jiangsu Key Laboratory for Advanced Metallic Materials, School of Materials Science and Engineering, Southeast University, Nanjing 211189, China
2) AO Smith Corporate Technology Center, Milwaukee, WI, 53224, USA

[Manuscript received June 10, 2011, in revised form September 10, 2011]

Numerical estimates of the components of yield strength of a high strength Fe-C-Mn-P-N-Si enameling steel were determined using empirical relationships between microstructure and yield strength. Results are reported for both the hot rolled (HR) and cold rolled (CR) forms before and after simulating an enamel-fire anneal (EFA). To determine the solubilities of alloying elements, thermodynamic calculations were performed in combination with the considerations of process conditions and the element diffusivities. The results show that the main solid solution strengtheners were the elements Mn, Si, and P, while the elements C and N were nearly completely tied up as precipitates. The yield strength reduction, due to the EFA, resulted primarily from an increase in grain size and a decrease in dislocation density, and the EFA appeared to have a negligible effect on the element solubilities.

**KEY WORDS:** Enameling steel; Enamel-fire anneal; Microstructure; Yield strength;
Thermodynamic calculation

## 1. Introduction

Glass-lined thin-walled pressure vessels are predominantly used for water heater storage tanks. The minimum thicknesses used for these pressure vessels are dictated by a safety requirement that stipulates minimal permanent deformation when a tank is subject to a static pressure load$^{[1]}$. Low-carbon Al-killed steels are usually used in their construction, and to increase the yield strength, elements such as Mn, Si, P, Ti, and Nb are added$^{[2-6]}$.

As steel has become increasingly expensive, there is a need to drive the thicknesses to lower values. The ability to use thinner steels is hampered by the enamel-fire annealing (EFA) process that requires heat treating the pressure vessels in the range of 800-$900\ ^{\circ}\text{C}^{[7]}$, since the yield strength decreases during the EFA$^{[4,5]}$. Thus, in order to figure out how to reduce the steel thicknesses used to produce glass-lined thin-walled pressure vessels, the reasons for yield strength reduction due to the EFA should be understood for the specific enameling steels being used to construct the vessels.

Some studies have been performed on the mechanical properties of the enameling steels. Takahashi *et al.*$^{[7]}$ reported that the after-fire yield strength is lower than the before-fire strength, but the reasons for yield strength reduction were not discussed. Researchers have reported that during firing, critical grain growth may occur with a simultaneous loss of yield strength$^{[4,8]}$. For the Ti-bearing enameling steels, the volume fraction and the size of fine precipitates also change during firing$^{[5,6]}$. These experimental studies about the relation between microstructures and yield strength are, however, not complete enough to identify possible strategies for minimizing the yield strength reduction during the enameling firing process, because they do not report on any of the metallurgical factors responsible for yield strength

It is known that the solubility of alloy elements and dislocation density are also important factors of the strength of enameling steels, yet limited research has been reported on the changes of these factors during the EFA. In addition, the previous studies did not attempt to establish numerical estimates of the met- allurgical components of yield strength reduction.

In this work, the microstructures of a high strength Fe-C-Mn-P-N-Si enameling steel, in both cold rolled (CR) and hot rolled (HR) forms, were sys- tematically characterized by combining experiments and thermodynamic calculations. Based on the char- acterized microstructures, numerical estimates for the components of yield strength were determined. The goal is to develop strategies for modifying the chem- ical composition or processing to minimize the yield strength reduction during the enamel firing process.

## 2. Experimental

The materials used for the present investigation are two commercial Fe-C-Mn-P-N-Si enameling flat rolled steels: CR and HR. The CR and HR steels were hot rolled to a finishing temperature of about $900\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ and then rapidly cooled and coiled at about $600\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. The CR steel was subject to four additional process- ing steps after hot rolling: pickling, cold rolling, box annealing, and temper rolling. The box annealing process was performed at about $700\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 20 h. The chemical compositions are shown in Table 1.

The EFA was simulated by annealing the spec- imens in a box type furnace. The CR and HR specimens, with dimensions of 200 mm$\times$80 mm$\times$1.4 mm and 200 mm$\times$80 mm$\times$4.1 mm, were annealed at $860\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 5 min. The average heating rate and cooling rates were about $20\ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{min}$ and $170\ ^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{min}$, respectively.

Tensile tests were performed on standard sheet tensile specimens at a crosshead speed of 6 mm per min with a CMT5105 tensile machine. The mi- crostructures were observed with a FEI-Sirion scan- ning electron microscope (SEM). The average grain sizes and pearlite volume fractions of the specimens were measured with the ImageTool software$^{[9]}$.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis was performed on thin foils with a TECNAI G2 20 trans- mission electron microscope (TEM). The thin foils were prepared by mechanically thinning to $50\ \mu\text{m}$, and then perforated by twin-jet electropolishing using a solution of $7\%$ perchloric acid in ethanol at $-25\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$.

The thermodynamic calculations were performed using Pandat, a phase equilibrium calculation soft- ware of multi-component alloys$^{[10]}$.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Microstructure characterization

Fig. 1 shows SEM images of the CR and HR steels before-and-after the EFA. Before the EFA, the mi- crostructure of the CR steel is ferrite and free cemen- tite. The microstructure of the HR steel is composed of ferrite, and a small volume fraction of lamellar pearlite and free cementite. The ferrite shape of the HR steel is equiaxed polygonal, which is more regular and uniform than that of the CR steel.

After the EFA, grain growth is observed in both the CR and HR steels, but the growth is not classi- fied as critical grain growth. For the CR steel, a small amount of pearlite appears at the grain boundaries, and the quantity of free cementite decreases. For the HR steel, the volume fraction of pearlite slightly in- creases and free cementite decreases. For both the CR and HR steels, the pearlite is lamellar, and the shape of the ferrite grains does not show significant changes.

The grain sizes and pearlite volume fractions be- fore and after the EFA were measured, and are shown in Table 2. The EFA increases the average grain size by about $2\ \mu\text{m}$ for both the CR and HR steels, from 8.7 to $10.5\ \mu\text{m}$ for the CR steel, and from 7.4 to $9.5\ \mu\text{m}$ for the HR steel. The percentage changes in grain sizes of the CR and HR steels are $21\%$ and $28\%$, re- spectively. The pearlite volume fraction increases in the CR steel from 0 to $2.51\%$ and in the HR steel from $6.89\%$ to $7.66\%$.

Fig. 2 shows TEM micrographs of dislocations in the CR and HR steels before and after the EFA. Be- fore the EFA, the two steels have a moderate disloca- tion density, and dislocation tangles are observed in some areas (see Figs. 2(a) and (b)). After the EFA, the dislocation density decreases significantly in both steels (see Figs. 2(c) and (d)).

The dislocation density was determined by the line intercept method. The number of the intersec- tions between concentric circles and dislocations were counted, and the dislocation density is calculated us-ing the following equation$^{[11,12]}$:

$$
\rho=2N/(lt) \tag{1}
$$

where $\rho$ is the dislocation density, $N$ is the number of the intersections of dislocations, $l$ is the length of the secant, and $t$ is the foil thickness. The thin foil sample preparation method is expected to cause a variation in the thickness of 100 to $150\ \text{nm}^{[13]}$.

### 3.2 Element solubilities

To estimate the contribution of the solid solution component to the yield strength reduction, the solu- bilities of the alloy elements before and after the EFA should be determined. Since it is difficult to experi- mentally measure the solubilities of elements, in the present work, the element solubilities of the CR and HR steel before and after the EFA were estimated by thermodynamic calculations using Pandat software in combination with calculations of the element diffusiv- ities and knowledge of the steel processing conditions. The elements included in the analysis were C, Mn, P, N, and Si. The residual elements Al, Cu, Cr, Mo, Ni are not strong solid solution strengtheners, and the

Since the concentration distribution in the CR steel is similar to that in the HR steel, only the distributions in the CR steel are presented. Pandat predicts that the precipitates are (Mn,Fe)₇C₃, Mn₂₃C₆, Fe₃P, etc. However, these phases were not observed during the microstructure analyses. Fig. 3(c) shows that the element Si is in complete solid solution. Figs. 3(d) and (e) indicate that a significant portion of C and N is tied up as the precipitates of cementite and AlN.

Since the mechanical properties were measured at room temperature, the solubilities of Mn, P, Si, C, and N at room temperature should be obtained by using the data in Fig. 3. It is known, however, that the thermodynamic calculations of solubility shown in Fig. 3 are based on the equilibrium state, while the practical samples are mostly in the nonequilibrium.

It should be reasonable to estimate the element solu- bilities using the data in Fig. 3 in combination with the considerations of the element diffusivities and the processing conditions.

For the CR steel before the EFA, the steel was stress relief annealed at 700 °C for 20 h in a box an- neal at the steel mill. Under those conditions, it is reasonable to assume that the solubilities of the ele- ments Mn, Si, and P in matrix have reached equilib- rium. In addition, since the diffusivities of the ele- ments Mn, Si, P are very low, around the magnitudes of $10^{-47}$-$10^{-16}$ m²/s when the temperature is below 700 °C, we assume negligible diffusion distances upon cooling. Accordingly, the solubilities of Mn, Si and P were determined from the data in Fig. 3 at 700 °C for the CR steel before the EFA. The calculated sol- ubilities of Mn, Si, and P are 1.2849%, 0.45%, and 0.048%, respectively.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the CR and HR steels before and after the EFA are shown in Table 5. Since the minimum thicknesses used for the pressure vessel parts are dictated by the yield strength of the enameling steels, only the yield strength data is presented. For both steels, the EFA causes the yield strength to decrease. For the HR steel, the yield strength, as measured by the lower yield point, decreases by 49 MPa, from 419 to 370 MPa. For the CR steel, the yield strength also decreases, but the comparison between before and after the EFA is complicated due to the fact that the CR steel does not exhibit a well defined yield point before the EFA.

### 3.4 Numerical estimates of the components of yield strength reduction during EFA

The components of the total yield strength, $\sigma_y$, of low carbon steels, exhibiting a ferrite-pearlite microstructure, can be represented by$^{[15–17]}$:
$$
\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + \sigma_{\text{s}} + \sigma_{\text{g}} + \sigma_{\text{p}} + \sigma_{\text{d}} + \sigma_{\text{transf}} \tag{2}
$$
where $\sigma_0$ is the inherent friction stress, $\sigma_{\text{s}}$ is the solid solution strengthening, $\sigma_{\text{g}}$ is the grain size strengthening, $\sigma_{\text{p}}$ is the precipitation strengthening, $\sigma_{\text{d}}$ is the dislocation strengthening, and $\sigma_{\text{transf}}$ is the phase transformation strengthening. In general, $\sigma_0$ is constant for a given steel. In the present work, $\sigma_0$ was taken as 30 MPa$^{[12]}$ and assumed to not change before and after the EFA.

The contribution of solid solution strengthening, $\sigma_{\text{s}}$, in Eq. (2) was determined using the following empirical equation$^{[18]}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{\text{s}} = 4570[\text{C}] + 4570[\text{N}] &+ 37[\text{Mn}]+ \\
83[\text{Si}] + 470[\text{P}] &+ 38[\text{Cu}]
\end{aligned} \tag{3}
$$
where $[\text{X}_i]$ is the concentration of element $\text{X}_i$ dissolved in the matrix, in wt%. The values of $\text{X}_i$ are shown in Table 4.

The grain size strengthening component in Eq. (2) was determined using the Hall–Petch equation$^{[18–20]}$:
$$
\sigma_{\text{g}} = k_y d^{-1/2} \tag{4}
$$
where $k_y$ is the grain boundary unlocking term, usually taken as $15.1–18.1$ Nmm$^{-3/2}$ for high-angle boundaries$^{[19]}$. In the current work, $k_y$ was taken as $17.4$ Nmm$^{-3/2[18,20]}$. $d$ is the ferrite grain diameter.

The contribution of dislocation strengthening was calculated with the equation$^{[12,21–23]}$ below:
$$
\sigma_{\text{d}} = aGb\rho^{1/2} \tag{5}
$$
where $a$ is a constant taken as 0.38, $G$ is the matrix shear modulus and equals to 8.3 GMPa for ferrite

For conventional C–Mn steels, the contribution of precipitation strengthening is usually negligible$^{[15]}$, and our microstructure observations corroborate this fact. Thus, the precipitation strengthening, $\sigma_\text{p}$, is not considered in the present work. The quantity $\sigma_{\text{tranf}}$ in Eq. (2) is introduced to account for the pearlite microstructure. Pickering disclosed that pearlite has a minor effect on yield strength when the pearlite volume fraction is under 20%$^{[24]}$. As shown in Table 2, the volume fractions of pearlite of all samples are under 7.7%. Therefore, the effect of $\sigma_{\text{tranf}}$ is neglected.

The estimates of the contributions of various sources to the total yield strength of the CR and HR steels are given in Table 6. For the before and after EFA conditions, grain size strengthening constitutes approximately 44% to 50% of the yield strength, solid solution strengthening contributes approximately 24% to 30%, and dislocation strengthening contributes approximately 16% to 23%. Thus, for both steels, before and after the EFA, the grain size strengthening provides the most important contributions to the yield strength. The second important factor is the solid solution strengthening, followed by the dislocation strengthening.

Before the EFA, the dislocation strengthening in the CR steel is higher than that in the HR steel due to higher dislocation density in the CR steel induced by temper rolling. The initial concentrations of Mn, Si, and P in the CR steel are higher than that in the HR steel, causing the solid solution strengthening of the CR steel to be higher than that of the HR steel. Since the HR steel has a smaller average grain size than the CR steel, the grain size strengthening of the HR steel is slightly higher than that of the CR steel.

Based on the data in Table 6, the changes of the different strength factors after the EFA were calculated and are shown in Table 7. The change in dislocation density and grain size are the primary factors responsible for yield strength reduction of both the CR and HR steels. The magnitudes of the changes are different for the two steels. The EFA increases the average grain size by about 2 $\mu$m for the CR and HR steels. While the change in the absolute grain size is nearly the same for both steels, the inverse-square-root relation of the Hall–Petch equation means that the HR steel will experience a greater yield strength reduction due to the smaller initial grain size. Consequently, the calculated reduction in yield strength from grain growth is about 17 MPa for the CR steel and 23 MPa for the HR steel. Regarding the effect of dislocation strengthening, the CR steel exhibits a larger decrease than that of the HR steel and this difference is due to the temper rolling process. The dislocation density decrease produces the primary contribution (about 70%) to the yield strength reduction for the CR steel, while the dislocation density decrease and grain growth create a similar level of yield strength reduction for the HR steel.

### 4. Conclusions

For the Fe–C–Mn–P–N–Si enameling steels analyzed in this study, dislocation density decrease and grain growth are the major contributors to the yield strength reduction in the HR and CR steels during the EFA. For the CR steel, dislocation density decrease is responsible for about 70% of the reduction in yield strength. For the HR steel, the change in dislocation density and grain size are each responsible for about 50% of the reduction in yield strength. This difference between the CR and HR products is not surprising considering that the CR steel is temper rolled before the EFA. The elements that are the primary contributors to the solid solution strengthening mechanism are Mn, P, and Si at concentration levels close to the bulk concentration levels, and the EFA appears to have a negligible effect on this strengthening component of the yield strength. On the other hand, the elements C and N are, most likely, nearly completely

tied up as precipitates in the form of cementite and AlN. Thus, they have a negligible contribution to the solid solution strengthening, but are probably critical to achieve small grain sizes and to limit grain growth during the EFA.

The results obtained from the present study reveal potential approaches to the development of a new al- loy with a higher after-fire yield strength. The chemi- cal composition and processing should be improved to achieve a small grain size and, at the same time, cre- ate a microstructure that impedes grain growth dur- ing the enamel firing process. Any new alloy should also maintain or enhance the benefits of using the el- ements Mn, P, and Si as solid solution strengtheners, which are not affected significantly by the steel mak- ing and firing processes. For both the cold rolled and hot rolled forms, a study is needed to determine the optimum levels of residual strain prior to the enamel firing process, since it is clear that a large fraction of the yield strength reduction is a result of releasing this stored energy. Closely related work about these issues is currently underway.
