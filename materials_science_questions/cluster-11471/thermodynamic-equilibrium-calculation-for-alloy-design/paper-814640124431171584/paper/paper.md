# A Composite Modeling Analysis of the Deformation Behavior of Medium Manganese Steels

Radhakanta Rana, Paul J. Gibbs, Emmanuel De Moor, John G. Speer, and
David K. Matlock*

A composite model with different assumed flow behaviors for the individual microstructural constituents and stability parameters for the metastable austenite transformation is presented and shown to provide design insight into the development of third-generation advanced high strength steels with a wide spectrum of tensile properties. The deformation behavior of a Fe-7.09 Mn-0.099 C-0.13 Si (wt%) steel is evaluated with uniaxial tensile testing and the results are correlated with predictions of the composite model. It is shown that the present simple composite model can predict tensile strength and uniform elongation with good accuracy over a range of austenite volume fractions in the steel. The analysis is based on Mn enrichment into austenite during intercritical annealing of the steel resulting in microstructures with significant variations in the amount and stability of austenite. Strategies for controlling the stability and amount of austenite in medium Mn steels are also presented in low carbon, nominally 5-10 wt% Mn-containing steels with/without Al as an alloying addition.

## 1. Introduction

The development of automotive steels is increasingly driven by property targets vis-à-vis strength and formability. These targets are governed mainly by the stringent emission legislations set by various governments and passenger safety requirements. As for example, the United States Department of Energy recently set two property targets as shown in Figure 1 for a development of automotive steels: $^{[1]}$ 1200 MPa ultimate tensile strength (UTS) with 30% total elongation (TE) and 1500 MPa ultimate tensile strength with 25% total elongation. These ambitious property targets differ from property ranges characteristic of the first and second-generation advanced high strength steels (AHSS), necessitating exploitation of alternate processing routes to achieve the targeted property combinations within the third-generation AHSS property band.

Medium Mn transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) steels are promising candidates for third-generation AHSS as these steels have been shown to exhibit exceptional levels of ductility (TE > 30%) and reasonably high strength (UTS > 800 MPa).$^{[2,3]}$ These steels typically contain 3-10 wt% Mn and are generally subjected to an intercritical annealing treatment to enrich austenite with high levels of Mn (and C, if present) in order to ensure very high fractions of austenite (up to about 60 vol%) in the microstructures at room temperature.$^{[4]}$ Depending on its stability, austenite transforms to martensite through displacive transformation during deformation of the steels leading to high strain hardening rates and a delay in the onset of instability, resulting in simultaneous increases in strength and ductility. These attractive mechanical properties resulting from intercritical annealing have generated significant scientific and industrial interest in medium Mn TRIP steels in recent years.$^{[5-11]}$

Depending on the microstructural variables, such as austenite amount and stability, the presence of other constituent phases (e.g., ferrite and martensite) and heat treatment parameters which influence constituent grain sizes, medium Mn TRIP steels can show wide ranges in mechanical properties.$^{[2-11]}$ Therefore, it would be useful to develop a methodology to predict the mechanical properties of these steels incorporating these variables in order to guide development of new third-generation AHSS grades. As one approach, Matlock and Speer$^{[12,13]}$ proposed a composite model to predict the tensile properties of AHSS with an extended range of strengths and elongations. In the present study, this composite model has been applied to analyze the mechanical properties of a medium Mn TRIP steel as a function of microstructural variables. ## 2. Background of Composite Model

### 2.1. Determination of Flow Stress

The composite model used for predicting the tensile properties of AHSS microstructures follows the approach developed for continuous fiber composites by Mileiko $^{[14]}$ and Garmong and Thompson. $^{[15]}$ The model applies the "rule of mixtures," Equation 1, to describe the flow behavior of a multi-component composite where the deformation behavior of each individual constituent is described by a unique flow equation, assumed here to be the Hollomon power law equation in Equation 2. $^{[12,13]}$

$$
\sigma_{c}=\sum \sigma_{i} V_{i} \tag{1}
$$

$$
\sigma=K \varepsilon^{n} \tag{2}
$$

In Equations 1 and 2, $\sigma$ is the true stress ($\sigma_{i}$ for the "i-th" component of the microstructure, $\sigma_{c}$ for the composite), $\varepsilon$ is the true strain, $V_{i}$ is the volume fraction of the $i$-th component of the composite, $K$ is the strength coefficient, and $n$ is the strain hardening exponent. Application of this composite model to a multiphase AHSS microstructure assumes all constituents (i.e., ferrite, austenite, martensite, etc.) are load bearing, deformation is isostrain, and interfaces between constituents are contiguous up to the point of plastic instability, i.e., deformation occurs without premature debonding.

The UTS and uniform elongation (UE) for the composite can be predicted $^{[13]}$ by applying the tensile instability condition described by Equation 3 to the composite flow curve in Equation 1.

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} \sigma}{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}=\sigma \tag{3}
$$

### 2.2. Transformation of Austenite

Multiphase AHSS are often designed to retain high amounts of metastable austenite at room temperature in an ultrafine matrix of martensite, bainite, or ferrite. $^{[12,17]}$ Metastable austenite may affect the mechanical response of AHSS to strain via the so-called "TRIP effect," whereby metastable austenite transforms to hard martensite resulting in advantageous work hardening behavior. Knowledge of the response of any retained austenite in the microstructure to strain is, therefore, critical to accurately describe the deformation behavior of AHSS using the model described above. The extent of the TRIP effect depends on factors such as amount, stability and morphology of austenite, deformation temperature, defor- mation rate, stress state, etc. $^{[18-21]}$ Morphologically, a film type austenite is mechanically more stable than a blocky type of austenite. $^{[21]}$ Austenite is also more stable at a higher deformation temperature $^{[20]}$ and it becomes less stable in a multiaxial stress state than under uniaxial stress. $^{[19]}$ Deformation at higher strain rates may increase the strain hardening rate of TRIP steels when a high amount of retained austenite (>11 vol%) is present in the microstructure due to an increase in the TRIP effect. $^{[22]}$ However, adiabatic heating during high strain rate deformation $(>10^{-2} \mathrm{~s}^{-1})$ is also known to increase the austenite stability $^{[20]}$ countering the effect of higher strain rate on the TRIP effect.

To accurately predict mechanical properties using the composite model, it is necessary to incorporate the strain- dependent transformation of austenite into the model because the initial phase fractions continuously evolve as deformation progresses. Numerous models have been proposed to mathematically express the transformation of metastable austenite with applied strain in TRIP steels. $^{[23-27]}$ The model proposed by Olson and Cohen (OC), $^{[26,27]}$ which has been applied successfully to a range of steels with metastable austenite and varying mechanical stability, is used here to represent the strain induced austenite transformation. $^{[5]}$ The OC model assumes that shear band intersections act as nuclei for martensite and that martensite evolution with strain is described by the following equation:

$$
f_{\alpha^{\prime}}=1-\exp \left[-\beta(1-\exp [-\alpha \varepsilon])^{m}\right] \tag{4}
$$

where $f_{\alpha'}$ is the fraction of martensite and $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $m$ are model parameters. The parameter $\alpha$ is related to the rate of shear band formation and thereby to the stacking fault energy of austenite and the strain rate, while $\beta$ corresponds to the probability of martensite nucleation at a shear band intersection which is a function of chemical driving force for austenite to martensite transformation and the necessary strain energy of the transformation. The constant $m$ is a geometric parameter relating the orientation of shear bands and the probability of shear band intersections acting as martensite nuclei. Further description of these parameters can be found in the original work by Olson and Cohen. $^{[26,27]}$

For a fixed austenite morphology, austenite stability is determined by the chemical composition of austenite and the deformation temperature. The chemistry effect on the austenite stability can be elaborated from the following. Both steels were cold rolled (50% thickness reduction) and annealed at 640 °C for 16 h before water quenching to room temperature. ## 5. Application of Composite Model to Medium Mn Steel

### 5.1. Experimental Background

The 7.1 Mn steel was vacuum cast, reheated, hot rolled to approximately 4 mm, surface ground to remove the oxide layers, and cold rolled to 1.5 mm thick sheet.[³⁷] Cold rolled specimens were annealed at 600, 625, and 650 °C for 168 h and quenched in water. Microstructures were recorded from the longitudinal cross-section containing rolling and normal directions in a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) with 5 kV operating voltage and 10 mm working distance, after etching the metallographic specimens in 2 vol% Nital solution. Room temperature tensile tests were conducted on transverse ASTM E8 subsized specimens of the annealed material with a strain rate of $5.74 \times 10^{-4}$ s⁻¹. The effects of strain on austenite content were measured using in situ neutron diffraction in a SMARTS diffractometer at the Lujan Center of Los Alamos National Laboratory, NM. Diffraction data were recorded with the samples under uniaxial tension transverse to the rolling direction with fixed strain increments. Retained austenite contents from the diffraction data were determined by Rietveld analysis.[³⁷] Further details of the neutron diffraction experiments can be found elsewhere.[³⁷]

Figure 10 reveals that, as the annealing temperature increases, the 7.1 Mn steel exhibits a decreased stability of austenite. Thus, 7.1 Mn steel annealed at $600\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ possesses the highest austenite stability, whereas anneal- ing of the steel at $650\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ shows the lowest austenite stability. This behavior can be explained with Figure 4 where the partitioning of alloying elements in austenite at annealing temperatures is shown for 7.1 Mn steel. Figure 4 suggests that, in the annealing temperature range of $600$–$650\,^{\circ}\text{C}$, the concentrations of both C and Mn decrease with increasing temperature. Therefore, the decrease in room temperature austenite stability with increasing annealing temperature is due to the less enrichment of the austenite stabilizers in austenite at annealing temperatures higher than $600\,^{\circ}\text{C}$.

Alternate expressions which independently include yield stress, such as a Ludwik-type function,$^{[16]}$ to describe stress–strain curves of annealed conditions with strain localization behavior might be necessary to better predict the initial part of the tensile curves for situations involving discontinuous yielding. It should be noted that the discontinuous yielding during tensile deformation of the medium Mn steels as observed in Figure 7, 8 and 12 is possibly due to localization of plastic flow owing to the ultrafine ferrite grain size present in the material after annealing.$^{[6]}$ In fact, the 7.1 Mn steel exhibited a ferrite grain size in the range of $1$–$1.5\ \mu\text{m}$ as is evident from Figure 9. However, the stability of austenite can also influence the plastic flow localization.$^{[38]}$ Presence of a less stable austenite in the microstructure with ultrafine ferrite grains may suppress the plastic flow localization through transformation-initiated yielding. The 7.1 Mn steel exhibited a decrease in austenite stability with increasing annealing temperature (Figure 10), resulting in an early martensitic transformation during deformation. It is interpreted that this transformation-initiated yielding caused a transition of yielding behavior of the steel from discontinuous after annealing at $600\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ to continuous for $675\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ annealing as revealed in measured tensile curves in Figure 12.

Alternate approaches to predict AHSS mechanical properties have also been reported, as for example, by Lee and De Cooman$^{[4]}$ and Choi et al.$^{[39,40]}$ While these studies have employed different aspects of the detailed phase properties, they have all employed a composite approach for incorporating individual phase characteristics into the overall property model. Choi et al.$^{[39,40]}$ developed a model to predict the mechanical properties of AHSS, based on a micromechanical approach. In their model, they used a finite element analysis (FEA) of deformation fields, and incorporated the deformation function of each of the constituent phases into the model through ‘representative volume elements’ (RVE) of the phases. However, in a similar manner, as it is done in the present composite model, Choi et al.$^{[39]}$ have also considered the volume fraction and stability of austenite that influence the deformation behavior of AHSS. Recently, Lee and De Cooman$^{[4]}$ developed a dislocation density-based constitutive model to predict the mechanical properties as well as the stress–strain curves of a $10\ \text{wt}\%$ Mn-containing steel. In their model, a composite approach has essentially also been applied incorporating the microstructural details.

As evident from the examples provided in Figure 11, the composite model presented and used to predict the mechanical properties in the present paper is simple and yet robust, given all the assumptions in the model. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 2, such composite modeling can be used to guide the design of new AHSS with mechanical properties bridging the gap in the property map of automotive steels. Therefore, the present model can be effectively used to provide insight into the development of new third-generation advanced high strength steels for the automotive industry.

The mechanical properties of medium Mn steels are greatly influenced by the presence of metastable austenite in the microstructure, through the amount and stability of austenite. 2. The retained austenite content in medium Mn steels initially increases with increasing annealing temperature, shows a peak, and then decreases due to changing austenite stability caused by varying partitioning of alloying elements in austenite as a function of annealing temperature.

3. The amount of austenite in medium Mn steels can be increased by increasing the Mn content and selecting the annealing temperature corresponding to the peak amount of retained austenite. Alternatively, Al can be added in lower Mn steels (e.g., 5 wt% Mn) to shift the retained austenite peak to higher temperatures to obtain higher partitioning of Mn into austenite due to enhanced diffusivity of Mn at higher annealing temperatures.

4. For a specific medium Mn steel, the stability of austenite can be controlled by optimizing the annealing temperature as the chemical composition of high temperature austenite varies with annealing temperature.

5. The stability of austenite in medium Mn steels decreases with decreasing deformation temperature leading to increase in tensile strength. However, obtaining the highest uniform elongation is dependent on selection of deformation temperature showing optimum metastability of austenite.
