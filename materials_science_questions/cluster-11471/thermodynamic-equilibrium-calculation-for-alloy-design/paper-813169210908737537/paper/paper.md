# Evaluation of Pitting Behavior on Solution Treated Duplex Stainless Steel UNS S31803

Yiming Jiang¹), Tao Sun¹), Jin Li¹), Jie Xu²)*

1) Department of Materials Science, Fudan University, Shanghai 200433, China
2) School of Information and Electrical Engineering, Zhejiang University City College, Zhejiang 310015, China

[Manuscript received December 6, 2013]

*Corresponding author: Prof., Ph.D.; Tel.: +86 571 88284312; Fax: +86 571 88284312; E-mail address: xujie@zucc.edu.cn (J. Xu).

The pitting corrosion resistance of duplex stainless steels UNS S31803 annealed at different temperatures ranging from 1050 ℃ to 1 200 ℃ for 24 h has been investigated by means of potentiostatic critical pitting temperature (CPT). The microstructural evolution and pit morphologies of the specimens were studied through optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy. The potentiostatic CPT measurements show that the CPT was elevated with the annealing temperature increased from 1050 ℃ to 1150 ℃ and decreased as the temperature further increased to 1200 ℃. The specimens annealed at 1150 ℃ exhibited the highest CPT and the best pitting corrosion resistance. The pit morphologies show that the pit initiation sites transfer from austenite phase to ferrite phase as the annealing temperature increases. The results were explained by the variation of pitting resistance equivalent number (PREN) of ferrite and austenite phases as the annealing temperature was varied.

**KEY WORDS:** Duplex stainless steel; Solution treatment; Pitting corrosion; Critical pitting temperature; Pitting resistance equivalent number

## 1. Introduction

Duplex stainless steel (DSS) presents desirable mechanical properties and corrosion resistance in extensive application fields including marine construction, chemical industries and power plants¹,². Many applications of DSS are services where common austenitic stainless steel would have corrosion problems³. It is generally accepted that pitting is one of the most detrimental and concealed forms and usually in the incentive for stress corrosion cracking (SCC)³,⁴. As a general rule, approximately equal volumetric fractions of ferrite ($\alpha$) and austenite ($\gamma$), defect-free microstructure and no deleterious phases are necessary for such good properties of DSS. Many researchers⁵,⁶ have demonstrated that once the chemical composition is established, mechanical properties and corrosion resistance determined by microstructure and composition of each phase of DSS should be controlled effectively by heat treatments, alloying techniques and steel making practice.

Deleterious phase formation avoidance and appropriate heat treatment condition selection are regarded to be of crucial importance during phase ratio adjustment⁵,⁷. Vannevik et al.⁶ found that

the critical pitting temperature (CPT) of five highly alloyed DSS with varying nitrogen concentrations showed good correlation with the pitting resistance equivalent number (PREN) in the weakest phase, i.e. the ferrite. Lai et al. $^{[8]}$ described that increasing solution treatment temperature increased the amount of ferrite. Using microelectrochemical methods, Perren et al. $^{[9]}$ documented a good correlation between the PREN of the single phases and corrosion behavior and with similar PREN to ferrite, the austenitic phase seems to be more susceptible to corrosion in strong acid. Garfias-Mesias et al. $^{[10]}$ reported that ferrite was always found to be the preferential pitting phase even though PREN in it was higher than that in austenite for solution treated alloy UNS S32550 at different temperatures.

A common cognition has been achieved from the results obtained in these researches that the weakest phase determines the corrosion resistance of the whole alloy for solution treated DSS. However, there still exist conflicts in some respects and it is necessary to draw conclusions under more sufficient experiment supports. With the aim for helping the producer to optimize the process and enhance the performance of DSS UNS S31803, the present work mainly focuses on a comprehensive investigation for the correlation among the volume fractions of each phase, main alloying element partitioning and the localized corrosion behavior of solution treated DSS at different temperatures $^{[7,11,12]}$.

To fulfill this task, a potentiostatic CPT method, an approach of rising the temperature at a given potential to determine the critical pitting temperature, is recommended in the present research. Potentiostatic CPT is a method to rank pitting corrosion susceptibility. With good reproducibility (with error no more than $\pm 1$ °C), sensitivity (better than more-often measured pitting potential) and efficiency (needless to prepare complex equipment), as demonstrated in many reports $^{[7,11,13-15]}$, this method has been brought into the ASTM standard in 1999. Great importance is that metastable pit morphologies can be observed clearly during potentiostatic CPT determination, which could provide us a more straight way to monitor the initiation site of pits.

## 2. Experimental

The investigated material was a commercial standard duplex stainless steel plate (6 mm in thickness) of the type UNS S31803 (SAF2205, EN1.4462), with the chemical compositions listed in Table 1.

Specimens were solution treated for 24 h at 1000 °C, 1050 °C, 1100 °C, 1150 °C and 1200 °C, respectively, and then water quenched. The pitting corrosion resistance of the samples was evaluated using potentiostat PARSTAT2273, which was comprised of a conventional three-electrode cell. A platinum foil was used as the auxiliary electrode and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode. The samples acting as a working electrode were embedded in epoxy resin with an exposure area of $0.5\ \mathrm{cm^{2}}$ and polished mechanically with successive grade emery papers from 600 to 2000 grit. Afterwards, they were polished with diamond paste of 2.5 and 1.5 μm granulation, degreased with ethanol and rinsed with distilled water and then blow-dried. In order to avoid crevice corrosion, special treatment was performed as described by Deng et al $^{[16]}$. Note that if any crevice corrosion was observed on the specimen after testing, the test results should be regarded invalid and must be discarded according to ASTM G150-99. The electrolyte was a solution of $1.0\ \mathrm{mol\ L^{-1}}$ sodium chloride. All test solutions used in this work were made up from analytical grade reagents and distilled water.

The potentiostatic CPT determination involving in this work was based on recording the current flowing in the investigated system at an applied constant potential as the function of the

temperature of the system. The system was deaerated with pure nitrogen for at least 30 min before the experiment start and throughout the whole test. The working electrode was firstly cathodally polarized at –900 mV vs. SCE for 5 min to deoxidize the oxide on the surface after 5 min immersion in the electrolyte at 25 °C. Then the specimen was allowed to stabilize at an open circuit potential for 10 min. Finally, an anodic potential of 750 mV vs. SCE was applied and the electrolyte temperature was increased at a rate of $(1\pm0.1)$ °C min⁻¹. The temperature was controlled by a programmable temperature controller (THCD-09). The current density was recorded simultaneously with increasing the temperature throughout the test (except for the first 5 s following the applied anodic potential). The experiment was not terminated until the current density increased to 100 μA cm⁻² and kept continuing for 1 min. The CPT was defined as the temperature at which the current started to increase rapidly with no repassivation after 1 min. Each electrochemical measurement for a special specimen was repeated at least three times.

The microstructure of DSS specimens were revealed by electrochemical etching in 30 wt% KOH solution for 15 s at an applied voltage of 2 V. The area fractions of austenite and ferrite were evaluated by quantitative metallography based on optical microscopy (OM). The final phase area fraction was the average value of at least 10 measurements, each at a different site. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SCE Phillips XL30 FEG) was used to observe the morphology of pitting initiation sites. The content of chromium, molybdenum and nickel in the ferrite and austenite phases was measured using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS); each value of the element distribution was an average of more than 10 EDS measurements. The nitrogen content of two phases was calculated by means of ThermoCalc computer program.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Microstructure evolution during solution treatment

The microstructures of UNS S31803 solution treated at 1050 °C, 1100 °C, 1150 °C and 1200 °C for 24 h are shown in Fig. 1. There are two phases present in the microstructures after the samples treated at the applied solution temperature: the austenite phase ($\gamma$) (shown as white “island”) and the ferrite phase ($\alpha$) (shown as gray matrix). There is no sigma phase precipitated in the ferrite/austenite interfaces or at the ferrite grain boundaries.

As can be seen from the morphologies, apparent evolutions are observed as solution temperature elevates, that is to say, the size of ferrite and austenite grains becomes larger and the area fraction of ferrite increases continuously. Moreover, the distribution of the two phases seems more homogenous simultaneously. When the solution temperature reaches 1200 °C, the $\gamma$ particles appear as the long plates with smooth and curved boundaries or the spherical-shaped particles (Fig. 1(d)). Table 2 illustrates the change in area fraction of ferrite and austenite as a function of solution temperature. It is evident that the amount of both phases is sensitive to solution temperature. These results show great uniformity with the phenomena found by Lai et al$^{[8]}$.

In order to make clear the relationship between microstructural evolution of the samples induced by solution treatment and the corresponding localized corrosion resistance, further examination is necessary to be carried out in the following text.

### 3.2. Influence of solution temperature on CPT

Fig. 2 reveals the typical curves of the current density versus temperature obtained from the solution treated UNS S31803 in 1.0 mol L⁻¹ sodium chloride solution. The final results were the average values of at least three times experiments under the same condition. It can be seen that with

increasing the solution temperature, the CPT of the samples solution treated at 1050 °C, 1100 °C and 1150 °C increases from 61 °C to 67 °C and then to 75 °C. However, when solution temperature is elevated to 1200 °C, the value of CPT does not increase continuously but decreases to 66 °C (this trend will be further demonstrated in section 3.3). It is also noted that some metastable pit transients with different sizes are observed below the CPT with increasing the electrolyte temperature during the experiments. It is well known that metastable pits can provide abundant information in the way of pit initiation sites and morphology of pit formation.

In order to identify the preferential initiation sites of pitting, metastable pits with small dimension were observed after the CPT measurements. For samples solution treated below 1150 °C, most of metastable pits were frequently found at austenite phase and small amount of metastable pits initiated at inclusions. Fig. 3(a) and (b) presents the representative SEM morphologies of pits on UNS S31803 subjected to solution treatment at 1050 °C and 1100 °C, respectively. However, for samples solution treated above 1200 °C, most of the metastable pits initiated in ferrite phase (Fig. 3(d)). For the samples treated at 1150 °C, more than half pits appear at ferrite/austenite boundaries (Fig. 3(c)). It is obvious that the values of CPT variation with solution temperature are accompanied by the pitting initiation sites transition.

The variation of the CPT values with solution temperature described in Fig. 2 seems to have no direct correlation with that of area fraction of ferrite shown in Table 2. Therefore, apart from the factor of microstructure evolution, there still exist other factors which dominate the changing of pitting corrosion resistance. Proverbially, solution treatment will induce to the redistribution of the elements in two phases；hence the composition of the two phases needs to be determined.

### 3.3. Influence of solution temperature on composition and PREN of both the single phases
It is well known that the chemical composition can strongly affect the tendency of a stainless steel to pitting. Although the fact that the effects of alloying elements are usually interactional makes the research intricate to a great extent, it has been turned out that some of alloying elements, such as chromium, molybdenum, nitrogen and tungsten, have a positive effect on the pitting resistance, whilst carbon, phosphor, sulfur and non-metallic inclusions are unfavorable ones to pitting resistance. In view of the main alloying elements including chromium, molybdenum and nitrogen are not equally distributed in ferrite and austenite in DSS: austenite is abundant in nickel and nitrogen, whilst ferrite, chromium and molybdenum. Special attention should be paid to the composition of ferrite and austenite phases as well as the whole alloy for the reason that the partitioning of these elements will unavoidably exert influence on the corrosion resistance of the entire alloy.

The average concentrations of major alloying elements in ferrite and austenite phase were analyzed by EDS for the specimens solution treated at various temperatures are shown in Fig. 4. Because of the insensitivity of EDS techniques to nitrogen, an approximate calculation was employed to determine the nitrogen content of single phase. The levels of nitrogen in the ferrite phase were assumed to be the saturation value (around 0.05%) and those in the austenite phase were thereby the rest which could be calculated based on the content of nitrogen in the whole alloy and the phase area fraction listed in Table 2 according to Garfias-Mesias et al$^{[10]}$. These data mainly show us the tendency of the distribution of the main alloying elements in two phases as solution temperature is elevated: in ferrite (Fig. 4(a)), chromium and molybdenum decrease while in austenite, chromium, molybdenum and nitrogen all increase (Fig. 4(b)).

In order to precisely evaluate the elemental influences on the resistance to pitting of both the corresponding phases, the pitting resistance equivalent number (PREN) was calculated according to

the formula (1)${}^{[17]}$.

$$\text{PREN} = \% \text{Cr} + 3. 3\% \text{Mo} + 16\% \text{N} \tag{1}$$

Although it has been proved by Garfias-Mesias et al.${}^{[10]}$ and Cleland et al.${}^{[18]}$ that PREN sometimes seems too simply to fulfill the task as a material selection reference, it has been admitted widely that higher PREN means better pitting corrosion resistance${}^{[19,20]}$. Fig. 5 illustrates the correlation between the calculating results of PREN for single phase and corresponding CPT and solution temperature as mentioned in section 3.2.

It has been clearly shown in Fig. 5 that the PREN of ferrite decreases, whilst the PREN of austenite increases as the solution temperature increases. The PREN of austenite is lower than that of ferrite when the solution temperatures below $1150\ ^\circ\text{C}$ while the reverse is true when it reaches $1200\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The opposite tendency equalizes the PREN of two phases of sample solution treated at $1150\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The elevation of the solution temperature allows the alloying elements to diffuse and repartition in the two single phases. Chromium and molybdenum are undoubtedly preferentially partition to the ferrite, but the ferrite volume fraction increases simultaneously to such an extent that the contents of the two main elements are ultimately diluted in ferrite. These results are in agreement with previous studies by Lai et al${}^{[13]}$. That is why the PREN of ferrite diminishes as solution temperature increases. The reason for the increase of PREN in austenite is that the content of nitrogen increases apparently as seen from Fig. 4.

It is worth noting that the trends of the evolutions of both the CPT (Fig. 5) and the site of pitting initiation (Fig. 3) exhibit an inflexion at $1150\ ^\circ\text{C}$, which is exactly the temperature that the PREN of the two single phases are almost the same. The existence of this inflexion can testify two points at least. Firstly, the pitting corrosion resistance is mainly controlled by the chemical composition of both the single phases but not directly determined by ferrite/austenite ratio since the variation of the latter is linear with solution temperature (Table 2). Secondly, it seems that the pitting preferentially occurs at the weaker phase when there is obvious difference between the PREN of the two phases, whereas appears more possibly at the boundary of the two phases when the PREN of the two phases are nearly the same. Thus, a conclusion could be drawn that the pitting corrosion resistance of the whole alloy is controlled by the weakest phase. That is to say, the highest CPT appears when the PREN of two phases is almost the same. Therefore, the whole attention should be paid to obtain the equivalence of PREN of ferrite and austenite during solution treatment. It should be emphasized that in order to fulfill this task better, further investigation on nitrogen partition at different temperatures with appropriate techniques should be reported instead of relative calculation applied in this research.

### 4. Conclusions

(1) The potentiostatic critical pitting temperature method is successfully applied to characterize the changes of pitting behavior, including CPT values and preferential sites for pitting initiation with varying microstructures for duplex stainless steel UNS S31803 suffering different solution treatments.

(2) The CPT value increases with increasing solution temperature from $1050\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $1150\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and decreases when the solution temperature reaches $1200\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The highest critical pitting temperature is $75\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at $1150\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

(3) The sites of pitting initiation transfer from austenite to ferrite with increasing the temperature from $1050\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $1200\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and are mainly located at the boundaries of ferrite/ austenite when the

solution temperature reaches $1150\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$.

(4) The variation of CPT and sites for preferential pitting nucleation can be reasonably explained with the evolution of both microstructure and composition of the two single phases. A comparison between the pitting resistance equivalent numbers of the two single phases reveals that the best pitting corrosion resistance of UNS S31803 is obtained at the point that the two single phases have the most similar PREN.
