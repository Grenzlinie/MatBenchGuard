## ABSTRACT

In the present work the commonly stated but not explicitly demonstrated association between the chemical composition of a material, its stacking fault energy (SFE) and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) behaviour, is established using thermo-mechanical testing, microstructural characterization and thermodynamic calculations on a Fe-Cr-Ni-Mo model alloy with varying interstitial content (0.1 $<$ (N + C) $<$ 0.25 wt%). The thermodynamic calculation shows that SFE decreases from $27\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ to $17\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ with increase in interstitial content at room temperature. The values of SFE rise upto $205\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ to $200\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ at 1323 K. The alloy with minimum interstitial content and maximum SFE exhibits a sluggish DRX behaviour in the strain rate domain $0.1-1\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The sluggishness gradually diminishes as the interstitial content is increased and SFE is decreased. The rate-dependency of nucleation is explained on the basis of interstitial content, which changes the SFE. The present investigation also elucidates the rate- dependent grain growth for the model alloy.

## 1. Introduction

The concept of atoms occupying interstitial sites in a crystalline lattice frequently arises in metallurgy, and is perhaps surpassed only by the concept of dislocations. First reported in 1926 [1,2], interstitial atoms are now central to diverse disciplines within materials science. Such impact can be attributed to the disproportionate impact that a small volume fraction of interstitials can have on material behaviour; be it steel strengthened by interstitial carbon [3], strain ageing phenomena promoted by interstitial solutes [4], or irradiation-induced defects [5].

The phenomena of interstitial-dislocation interaction and interstitial-grain boundary interaction necessitate meso-scale and macro-scale investigations. A suitable example of favorable interstitial-dislocation interactions is the beneficial effects resulting from addition of nitrogen to austenitic stainless steels. Prominent is the high degree of solid solution strengthening due to nitrogen [6], which cannot be explained on the basis of conventional interstitial-dislocation interactions, since N atoms in octahedral sites of FCC should cause symmetric distortions, and thus, should not be able to bind screw dislocations in the manner C does in the BCC matrix of carbon steel [7]. Instead, it is found that asymmetric distortions occur due to the formation of Fe-N complexes [7]. The phenomenon of complex formation is due to the unique electronic configuration of N, a feature that also results in short range ordering [8], secondary strengthening of N-containing austenitic steel [7] and affects the stacking fault energy (SFE). At elevated temperatures, N-containing austenitic steels have a good combination of strength, ductility and toughness. These are attributed to the influence of N on SFE as well as grain boundary strengthening [7,9].

It has also been seen that addition of N has significant impact on high temperature flow behaviour, and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) of steels [10]. The SFE is often cited as a key parameter in influencing DRX characteristics [11,12]. Thus, an explicit correlation is expected to exist between the addition of N, variation in SFE and evolution of DRX. In many metals, effects of interstitial elements on SFE and effects of interstitial elements on DRX have been separately investigated [13]; however, these do not provide a holistic view of the interstitial-SFE-DRX linkage.

DRX is most frequently characterized by the variation of DRX volume fraction and DRX grain size with respect to strain, temperature and strain rate [11,14,15]. In the DRX regime, with increase in temperature the DRX volume fraction generally increases, at all strain rates. Similarly, with increase in deformation strain rate, DRX volume

In such cases, the DRX volume fraction shows a gradual decrease to a minimum value followed by a smooth rise with increase in strain rate. In all these cases [16,17,20,21], the rate-sensitive DRX fraction reaches a minimum value at around $0.1-1\ \text{s}^{-1}$; such behaviour is attributed to the sluggish nucleation process [21]. The nucleation process in DRX is closely associated with the dislocation forest nucleation [22], which is dependent on the SFE. Thus, a correlation between the rate-sensitive behaviour and SFE is anticipated. Exploration of such a correlation is the primary motivation behind the current investigation.

It is not possible to demonstrate or comprehend the anticipated correlation, unless the SFE is systematically changed in a system which shows anomalous rate- sensitive DRX behaviour. It has been shown that the SFE of a system can be changed with minor variation of interstitial content [13,23-25]. In view of the above, a nitrogen containing stainless steel, which has reportedly shown the anomalous rate sensitive DRX behaviour [21], has been chosen as the model alloy for this study with the aim to explore the dependency of rate-sensitive DRX characteristics on interstitial content.

## 2. Experiments

The chosen model alloy has nominal chemical composition (in wt%) as given in Table 1. Three variants of the model alloy were received in plate form. Cylindrical bars machined from the plates were subjected to a common solution-annealing treatment at 1323 K for 30 min to ensure similar grain size in all three variants. From the solution-annealed bars,cylindrical specimens of 15 mm height and 10 mm diameter were machined for compression testing.

A servo-hydraulic controlled, constant true-strain rate compression testing machine, equipped with special quenching facility was used to carry out the isothermal compression testing at1323K with constant true strain rates of $0.001\ \text{s}^{-1}$, $0.01\ \text{s}^{-1}$, $0.1\ \text{s}^{-1}$, $1\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $10\ \text{s}^{-1}$. All specimens were heated inside a split muffle furnace equipped with resistance heating elements. Heat transfer modes were primarily radiation and natural convection. Soaking time of 5 min was provided for uniform temperature to be attained at all points in the specimen. Each specimen was deformed to a nominal strain of 50%. In all cases, deformation was found to be macroscopically homogeneous, with a complete absence of cracks and barreling. An additional set of tests have been carried out to ascertain the effect of temperature on growth of DRX grains and work hardening. The details of the additional test conditions are given in Table 2. All the tests were carried out in accordance with the ASTM E209 standard [26].

To minimize the effect of friction on deformation, graphite foils and Ni paste were used between specimens and platens. Before imparting the deformation, each specimen was heated at a rate of $5\ \text{K s}^{-1}$ to the desired deformation temperature, and soaked at that temperature for 2 min to achieve homogeneous temperature distribution throughout the specimen. The temperature during soaking and the adiabatic temperature rise during deformation were measured with the help of a thermocouple inserted into the center of each specimen. The temperature rise recorded for the tests has been given in Table 2. Each specimen was water-quenched at the end of deformation. The load-stroke data recorded during the deformation process were used to generate true stress-true plastic strain curves following the customary procedure [27]. In order to test the repeatability of flow stress measurements, tests were repeated at conditions yielding lowest and highest flow stress, respectively. In both cases, the variation in flow stress was found to be within 5 MPa. The deformed specimens were subsequently used for microstructural investigation.

Samples cut from the deformed specimens were prepared for investigation by Electron Back Scatter Diffraction (EBSD) technique following the standard metallographic procedure [28]. EBSD scans were acquired with step size of $0.5-1.5\ \mu\text{m}$ depending on the specific requirement. EBSD scans were conducted in the central part of the specimen over an area of $400\ \mu\text{m} \times 400\ \mu\text{m}$. The processing of the EBSD data were carried out using the open source software MTEX 4.5 by Bachmann, Hielscher and Schaeben [29]. The inverse pole figure (IPF) maps of 7 N, 11 N and 22 N variants in solution annealed condition are shown in Fig. 1. These figures indicate that the solution annealed materials consist of equiaxed grains with large fractions of twin boundaries. The average grain sizes after solution treatment are found to be $57 \pm 8$, $61 \pm 5$ and $58 \pm 6\ \mu\text{m}$ for 7 N, 11 N and 22 N, respectively.

X-Ray diffraction (XRD) technique was used to estimate the lattice parameter and stacking fault probability at selected conditions. Rietveld refinement [30]was implemented through the program MAUD [31] to estimate lattice parameter and stacking fault probability. Instrumental broadening was subtracted prior to refinement. Size-strain anisotropy and planar defects were accounted for using models given by Popa [32] and Warren [33],respectively.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. DRX characteristics

The flow curves obtained from the hot compression tests on all three variants of the model alloy are shown in Fig. 2. These curves are typical of materials undergoing DRX, i.e. the flow stress rises to a peak, followed by a stage of softening, after which the flow stress is expected to reach a constant value at sufficiently high strains [34]. These stages roughly correspond to work hardening, DRX-grain growth and a dynamic balance between hardening and softening, respectively. These broad stages are exhibited by all flow curves shown in Fig. 2. The specific shape of each curve contains valuable information about the DRX process, including the initiation stage. Thus, the subtle variations in shape of flow curve (across different deformation conditions as well as across different variants) convey information about differences in work hardening, DRX and grain growth. These are discussed in the following sections.

Poliak and Jonas [35] developed an analytical technique combining plasticity, irreversible thermodynamics and geometry to deduce the strain level at which DRX initiates. The strain level so determined is termed 'critical strain', and is a macroscopically averaged parameter representing the measurable initiation of DRX, and has been successfully applied to several materials [36-40]. In the present study, critical strain is used to analyse the macroscopic changes in DRX resulting from change in nitrogen content.

In addition to GOS, the average grain size and grain size distribution are essential descriptors of microstructure. Recrystallized microstructures can show a high variance in grain size when some parts of the microstructure undergo grain refinement and others simultaneously undergo grain growth. An extreme case of such high variance is abnormal grain growth, where the grain size distribution exhibits two modes, one at a low grain size and one at high grain size. The other extreme case is a narrow grain size distribution with a single mode; which is indicative of ongoing uniform recrystallization in the bulk of the microstructure.

The grain size distributions obtained in the present study lie between these extremes; neither abnormal grain growth nor perfectly uniform distributions are obtained at any deformation condition. All distributions are approximately log-normal. For ease of comparison, the size distributions at three strain rates for three variants have been plotted using identical axes in Figs. 5–7 (d)-(f).

For a given variant, the widest size distribution is found at lowest strain rates (0.001 s⁻¹). The wide distribution is a consequence of longer exposure to high temperature and corresponding high grain growth. With increasing strain rate, i.e. at 1 s⁻¹, the size distribution becomes narrower due to grain refinement stage of DRX. At the highest strain rate of 10 s⁻¹, either DRX or grain growth may be promoted depending on the individual progress of each mechanism. For instance, in 7 N variant, grain refinement continues till 10 s⁻¹, while in 11 N variant, some degree of grain growth is observed at 10 s⁻¹. The possible reasons are discussed in subsequent sections.

DRX fraction is plotted against strain rate in Fig. 8 for different variants used in this study. In agreement with the previously reported rate-sensitive DRX behaviour in other alloys [16–21], the 7 N variant shows lower DRX fraction at intermediate strain rates, when compared to that at lowest (0.001 s⁻¹) and highest (10 s⁻¹) strain rate. Similar decrease in DRX fraction to a minimum value followed by a rise with increase in strain rate was also noticed for the 11 N variant.

In contrast, the DRX fraction in the 22 N variant is nearly insensitive to change in strain rate. Comparison of these plots, as shown in Fig. 8, indicates that the DRX behaviour of the alloy at intermediate strain rates is highly sensitive to the interstitial (here N and C) content. Besides, comparison of the DRX fractions obtained for the three variants of the model-alloy at each strain rate indicate that the DRX fraction increases with increase in the interstitial content. DRX being a grain refinement mechanism [14], its complete characterization requires data regarding the grain size variation. While the grain size distribution in hot deformed alloys often displays considerable spread, the average grain size is a useful and convenient metric to interpret the overall grain size changes. As none of the microstructures show any indication of abnormal grain growth, which would necessitate discussion of size distribution, further results are limited to changes in average grain size.

Significant grain refinement with increase in strain rate can be noticed in the domain 0.001–0.1 s⁻¹. The degree of grain refinement reduces at strain rates higher than 0.1 s⁻¹. All three variants, i.e. 7 N, 11 N and 22 N show the same trend of grain growth with varying strain rate. In absolute terms, the difference in grain size at a given strain rate is sometimes too small to make meaningful comparisons across the three variants.

### 3.2. Prediction of SFE in hot working domain by varying interstitial content

The presence of interstitial elements in an alloy not only modifies the DRX behaviour [41,42] but also has significant influence on microstructural defects [22], such as stacking faults [13]. A stacking fault is a planar defect in an FCC crystal, wherein one HCP layer (with ABAB-type stacking) is embedded in the FCC matrix (having ABCABC-type stacking)[43]. The energy associated with the fault (the SFE) affects the ease of dislocation motion in an alloy, and hence, is deeply interlinked with work-hardening, rate-sensitivity and grain nucleation characteristics.

In the present study, the effect of interstitial elements – nitrogen and carbon – on the SFE of 7 N, 11 N and 22 N variants is studied. For each variant, the SFE is predicted by using chemical composition and elemental thermodynamic parameters as inputs to a thermodynamic model. Themodel used in the present study is based on the treatment proposed by Yakubtsov et al. [44] and further modified by Curtze et al. [45,46]. While the materials used by Yakubtsov et al. [44] and Curtze et al. [45] are Mn-rich systems, the model alloy used for the present study contains Mo. Lee et al. [25] has assessed SFE of various Fe-Cr-Ni with high Mn (> 10 wt%) system with varying nitrogen content (0.08–0.4 wt%). Therefore,the present work upgrades the model proposed by Yakubtsov et al. [44] and Curtze et al. [45,46]with three important modifications:

1. The incorporation of the BCC stabilizer molybdenum (Mo) for the first time as Mo is a crucial element in austenitic steels used at high temperatures.
2. Treatment of carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) as independent interstitial elements, which can have different effects on the SFE.
3. Extending thermodynamic treatment to high working temperatures, allowing the estimation of SFE at hot working conditions.

Keeping the brevity in view, thermodynamic model has not been described in detail, only an outline is given here. The SFE is related to change in free energy ($\Delta G$) due to the transition from FCC ($\gamma$) to HCP($\zeta$) phase in the faulted layer. The SFE is represented as [46]:

$$
\gamma_{SFE}=2 \sigma_{I}+2 \psi\left(\Delta G_{b u l k}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}+\Delta G_{M a g}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}+\Delta G_{S e g}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}\right)
\tag{1}
$$

where $\sigma_{I}$ is the interfacial energy in the faulted region, $\psi$ is the molar surface density, $\Delta G_{bulk}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$, $\Delta G_{Mag}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$ and $\Delta G_{Seg}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$ are the changes in bulk free energy, magnetic free energy and free energy due to segregation, respectively.

The bulk free energy term is comprised of contribution from substitutional elements and contribution from interstitial elements, the former being described by:

$$
\Delta G_{b(s u b)}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}=\sum_{i} \chi_{i} \Delta G_{(s u b) i}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}+\sum_{i j} \chi_{i} \chi_{j} \Omega_{i j}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}
\tag{2}
$$

where $\Delta G_{(sub)i}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$ represents the contribution from 'iᵗʰ' substitutional element and $\Omega_{ij}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$ is the excess free energy due to formation of stable compound by 'iᵗʰ' and 'jᵗʰ' elements. $\chi_{i}$ represents the mole fraction of 'iᵗʰ' element. The bulk energy contribution term given by Curtze et al. [46] is expanded to include $\Delta G_{(sub)Mo}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$ and $\chi_{Mo}\chi_{j}\Omega_{Moj}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}$ terms. Values of these terms were calculated using values from CALPHAD [47]. Similarly, interstitial contribution to free energy given in [44] is expanded to include carbon and nitrogen separately:

$$
\Delta G_{b(i n t)}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}=\chi_{C} \Delta G_{(i n t) C}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}+\chi_{N} \Delta G_{(i n t) N}^{\gamma \rightarrow \zeta}
\tag{3}
$$

The last two terms of Eq. (1), i.e. the magnetic and segregation contributions to free energy are treated using equations Eq. (3)-21 from Curtze et al. [46].

A computer code was specially written to collate and integrate the above equations. To ensure the applicability of the model, it is necessary to validate the model against the experimental results.

The experimental values of SFE are generated by performing diffraction experiments on all three variants, and analysis of the

Thus, X-ray diffraction line profile analysis is the tool used in this study to determine the SFE of 7 N, 11 N and 22 N variants at room temperature. The formalism of Schramm and Reed [48] is used:

$$
SFE \propto \frac{\text{Lattice parameter} * \text{Microstrain}^2}{\text{Stacking fault probability}} \tag{4}

The proportionality constant comprises shear modulus and anisotropy terms, details of which are described elsewhere [48]. For austenitic steels, we obtain the value $4960\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ which is in agreement with [49].

The remaining terms, i.e. lattice parameter, microstrain and stacking fault probability were determined using Rietveld refinement [30]implemented through the program MAUD [31]. In order to account for the size-strain anisotropy present in the XRD pattern, the anisotropic model of Popa [32]was used to determine microstrain on the faulting plane. Similarly, the stacking fault probability (probability of finding a stacking fault on (111) plane) was determined using Warren's [50] analysis of peak shifting due to planar defects. The program MAUD allows simultaneous implementation of Popa [32] and Warren [50] models, thereby treating any overlap between effects due to dislocations and due to stacking faults. Further, since the entire XRD pattern is used to obtain a best fit, all information contained in the pattern, i.e. peak position, area under peak, peak shape, tail of peaks, peak asymmetry are used to determine the values needed for Eq. 4. A recent analysis successfully used a similar simultaneous refinement approach to study microstructural evolution during tensile testing [49].

The only experimental input to the thermodynamic calculation is the lattice parameter determined from XRD patterns. The final calculation is not affected significantly if we use lattice parameter obtained from literature instead of measured lattice parameter. As such, the two calculations are independent and the reasonable agreement at room temperature is taken to indicate validity of the thermodynamic approach against an actual experimental technique.

### 3.3. Correlation between SFE and DRX

While DRX is often treated as the de facto softening mechanism during hot working of various materials [34], it is important to remember that the DRX process is predicated on a number of favorable conditions. Primary among these prerequisite conditions are: sufficiently high temperature and the accumulation of a critical degree of strain in the matrix [14]. These conditions depend upon factors such as the energy storing/dissipating property of the microstructure [51], the inherent tendency for dislocation annihilation [22], adiabatic temperature rise during deformation [17], and the time available for these processes to occur [14].

The balance between energy storage and energy dissipation in austenitic steel is determined by the lattice distortion (9) which is represented by micro strain [17]. The lattice distortion depends upon the substructure evolved during deformation. The nature of dislocation

For a low SFE material, cross-slip is hindered and the density of dislocation forests increases during deformation, leading to high energy sto- rage [22,52]. Upon reaching a critical value of stored energy, the ma- terial dissipates energy through the nucleation and growth of new strain-free grains [53,54]. While storage of energy is necessary, it is not sufficient for the initiation of DRX. The second necessary factor is adequate supply of thermal energy to the matrix through isothermal heating (imposed temperature) and/or adiabatic heating (temperature rise due to deformation at high strain rates)[55,56]. Like any other thermally activated process, DRX is time-dependent as it consists of a grain growth component (in addition to nucleation). The thermal ac- tivation for grain growth comes from a conjugate of temperature and time; while temperature provides the driving force for growth, the availability of sufficient time is essential for grain growth to proceed to completion. On the other hand, nucleation is more dependent on stored energy and temperature than on time, with which it shares a complex but weak correlation. Therefore, the overall DRX fraction is expected to have a functional relationship with the above three contributory fac- tors:

$$
DRXfraction = f(X_{\vartheta}^{i} \vartheta, X_{T}^{i} T, X_{t}^{i} t) \tag{5}
$$

where $X_{\vartheta}^{i}$, $X_{T}^{i}$, $X_{t}^{i}$ are weights assigned to lattice distortion characterized by microstrain $(\vartheta)$, temperature $(T)$ and time$(t)$, respectively for a variant 'i' of the model-alloy. During the deformation of a variant 'i', any change in $\vartheta$, $T$ or $t$ modifies the corresponding values of the weights assigned to these, in such a way that the following conditions are sa- tisfied:

$$
X_{\vartheta}^{i}>0, X_{T}^{i}>0, X_{t}^{i}>0 \tag{6}
$$

and

$$
X_{\vartheta}^{i}+X_{T}^{i}+X_{t}^{i}=1 \tag{7}
$$

As example, during deformation of a variant 'i', any increase in strain $(\varepsilon)$ at a specified temperature $(T)$ and strain rate $(\dot{\varepsilon})$, in absence of any adiabatic temperature rise, increases the weight assigned to lattice distortion, i.e.$X_{\vartheta}^{i}$, provided that the Eqs. (6)-(7) are satisfied. Similar rise in $X_{\vartheta}^{i}$ is expected from any source that causes formation of dis- location forests, as opposed to dislocation annihilation processes that decrease the value of $X_{\vartheta}^{i}$.

It has already been reported that the probability of dislocation an- nihilation is lower and the probability of dislocation forest formation is higher in a material with relatively lower SFE [57]. While formation of stacking faults during deformation at low temperatures is well known [45,58,59], the existence of these faults at very high tempera- ture is sparsely discussed in literature. Amongst these few reports, an investigation by Taylor et al. [12]has demonstrated that stacking faultscan form during the deformation at temperature of 973 K in SS304 austenitic steel.

The faulting model proposed by Warren [33]enables a quantitative comparison between the extent of faulting for different materials at similar deformation conditions. The extent of faulting is denoted by a 'stacking fault probability', which measures the probability of finding a stacking fault on a (111) plane. Stacking fault probability can be de- termined by Rietveld refinement of the complete diffraction pattern, as earlier demonstrated in [60]. The stacking fault probability so obtained is plotted for different variants in Fig. 11. The plot is overlaid with the corresponding micro-strain $(\vartheta)$ values on (111) plane to show the si- multaneous increase in both parameters with increasing interstitial content.

The fault probability for 7 N variant is $1.1 × 10^{-4}$, compared to $1.8 × 10^{-4}$ for 11 N variant and $3.3 × 10^{-4}$ for 22 N variant. It is evident that fault probability is strongly dependent on the interstitial content. The corresponding change in micro-strain value is lesser in magnitude, but follows the same trend as fault probability. It has been suggested by researchers that the formation of stacking fault needs a critical condition to be satisfied [12], which is related to the forest formation and dislocation clustering [61]. Dislocation accumulation (forest formation/dislocation clustering) is also the condition closely associated with DRX nucleation mechanism. Combining the importance of dislocation accumulation with the results of Fig. 11, it is inferred that faulting and DRX nucleation could be competing mechanisms. If so, the nucleation of strain-free grains could hinder the formation of faults in the recrystallization domain. The competing-mechanisms hypothesis explains why stacking faults have scarcely been reported in the DRX domain despite the commonly stated association between SFE of a material and its tendency to undergo DRX.

The correlation between stacking faults and formation of dislocation demonstrates that a material with low SFE (and with higher probability of stacking fault formation) has a higher chance of dislocation clus- tering compared to a material with high SFE (and with relatively lesser probability of stacking fault formation), i.e.:

$$
X_{\vartheta}^{22}>X_{\vartheta}^{11}>X_{\vartheta}^{7} \tag{8}
$$

Consequently, the following condition is valid at equal levels ofdeformation (temperature, strain, strain rate):

$$
X_{T}^{7}+X_{t}^{7}>X_{T}^{11}+X_{t}^{11}>X_{T}^{22}+X_{t}^{22} \tag{9}
$$

The higher value of $X_{\vartheta}^{i}$ at a given temperature signifies that the critical condition can be achieved with a lower value of $X_{t}^{i}$. Consequently, at fixed strain rate, the DRX nucleation occurs at lower strain levels for 22 N variant, followed by 11 N and 7 N, respectively as shown in Fig. 4. Eq. (9) also indicates that, at a given temperature, the DRX fraction for 22 N is more dependent on stored energy which causes nucleation, rather than the time, which favours grain growth. On the other hand, the reverse is true for 7 N variant of the alloy.

The variation of critical strain with strain rate is inconsistent with the variation of corresponding DRX fraction for all variants,as men- tioned in Section 3.1.

The DRX process in steels may be treated in classical terms as a nucleation and growth process, where nucleation of new grains occurs at different locations at different times. As time progresses nucleation occurs at more number of sites and during this period the already nu- cleated grains grow. With passing time, as the sites for nucleation get exhausted due to presence of already nucleated grains, no more nu- cleation occurs and the growth process becomes dominant. Thus, the process of nucleation for the polycrystalline occurs over a definite period. Initiation of this period is termed as the nucleation initiation (and is indicated by critical strain) following which periods of growth dominate.

It is reasonable to infer that the DRX fraction depends on factors other than nucleation initiation. These factors include initial grain size, density of nucleation sites,time available for grain growth and hin- drance of grain growth due to grain impingement. Since all the variants of steels used here have approximately similar initial grain size (as seen

in Fig. 1), effect of initial grain size can be neglected for the comparative analysis of DRX.Under strain-controlled deformation (a standard mechanical testing technique where the deformation is stopped once a prescribed level of strain is achieved), as in this study, the total deformation time is fixed. Therefore, early nucleation, as implied by a low critical strain, allows more time for the DRX process to proceed. Early nucleation could be promoted by the availability of favorable nucleating sites, such as dislocation forests near grain boundaries. The probable density of these sites would increase with increase in interstitial content of the alloy due to high dislocation-interstitial interactions. Such an increase in dislocation-interstitial interaction energy as a function of interstitial content has been shown by Yakubtsov et al. [44,62]. Formation of more number of new grains often leads to smaller grain size in the material as the growth of these grains is restricted by the presence of similarly growing grains in the neighborhood. However, comparison of the average grain sizes of the three variants shown in Fig. 9, indicates that the enhancement in interstitial content (C+N) from0.1 to 0.25 wt% has an insignificant influence on final grain size. The experimental finding can be understood if final grain size is viewed as a combination of initial grain nucleation and subsequent grain growth. Despite a relatively early nucleation in 22 N variant compared to 11 N and 7 N variants, the final grain sizes in all three variants are similar, possibly due to grain impingement and hindered grain growth in 22 N. In addition, the DRX fraction in 22 N is found to be the highest, suggesting most extensive nucleation in 22 N. It therefore appears that the number of nucleation sites, like the stacking fault probability, is influenced by interstitial content of the model-alloy. Therefore, it is reasonable to state that the DRX characteristics, particularly nucleation, largely depend on the SFE of the alloy. From the results, it is seen that, besides the existence of more nucleation sites, nucleation in 22 N variant also initiates at the lowest strain levels (Fig. 4). In view of these observations, it is concluded that nucleation plays the dominant role in DRX of the 22 N variant.

The dominance of nucleation decreases with decreasing interstitial content due to reduction in favorable nucleation sites and delayed nucleation. These differences are manifested in the variation of DRX fraction at different strain rates (shown in Fig. 8).Fig. 8 also shows varying sensitivity of DRX fraction to strain rate in the domain 0.1-10 s⁻¹. In the domain 0.1-10 s⁻¹, the DRX fraction is practically insensitive to strain rate in 22 N variant. On the contrary, in other variants the DRX fraction shows a decrease to a minimum value at 1 s⁻¹, followed by a rise with increase in $\dot{\varepsilon}$.

### 3.4. Interstitial content and rate dependent DRX behaviour

The variation of DRX fraction with strain rate has already been discussed by various research groups [18,19,21,63]. Conventionally, the DRX behaviour has been separately discussed in low $\dot{\varepsilon}$ domain and high $\dot{\varepsilon}$ domain. In the low $\dot{\varepsilon}$ domain, the high DRX fraction is attributed to low $\varepsilon_{c}$ and availability of more time (due to low $\dot{\varepsilon}$). On the other hand, the high DRX fraction at high $\dot{\varepsilon}$ is attributed to the adiabatic temperature rise and higher lattice distortion at these conditions. It is apparent that the deformation time has little impact on the DRX process at high $\dot{\varepsilon}$. In the framework of Eq. (5), the weight assigned to time, i.e.$X_{t}^{i}$, becomes significant only at low strain rates. In mathematical terms, a 'time threshold' $t_{c}$, below which $X_{t}^{i}$ is negligible may be used as:

$$
X_{t}^{i} \approx \begin{cases}
0 & \text { if } \quad t<t_{c} \\
0{<}c{<}1 & \text { if } \quad t>t_{c}
\end{cases}
\tag{10}
$$

In other words, there exists a time threshold, below which nucleation requires assistance from temperature ($X_{t}^{i}$) and stored energy ($X_{s}^{i}$) contributions. These contributions are provided by adiabatic temperature rise and increased dislocation density at high $\dot{\varepsilon}$. Therefore, the nucleation process is highly rate sensitive below the time threshold. When $t > t_{c}$, nucleation is found to be practically rate-insensitive. In the present study, the $t_{c}$ corresponds to strain rate of 0.1 s⁻¹. Below $t_{c}$, i.e. at strain rates 1 s⁻¹ and 10 s⁻¹, the role of adiabatic temperature rise becomes more important. Experimental measurements show that adiabatic temperature rise is 10 K at 1 s⁻¹ and 29 K at 10 s⁻¹, respectively. Therefore, the retardation of nucleation due to insufficient time is compensated by adiabatic temperature rise at 10 s⁻¹ but not at 1 s⁻¹, resulting in the trend shown in Fig. 8. The time-dependency dwindles at strain rates < 0.1 s⁻¹, where adequate time for nucleation eliminates the need for additional thermal energy. As per Eq. (9), at a given temperature, the time-dependency is also suppressed by higher interstitial content (or lower SFE and higher probability of fault), as more nucleation events can occur simultaneously due to availability of more nucleation sites. The above explanation addresses the insensitivity of DRX fraction to strain rate in 22 N variant in the domain 0.1-10 s⁻¹.

Like the variation of DRX fractions with strain rate, minima are noticed at 1 s⁻¹ in average DRX grain size vs strain rate curves. Though the average DRX grain sizes obtained at 10 s⁻¹ are relatively higher than those obtained at 1 s⁻¹, they are not similar in value, as was the case with DRX fraction. In addition, the curves do not show any variation in trend, at least in domain 0.001-0.1 s⁻¹, with increase in interstitial content of the model alloy. However, an erratic growth trend with interstitial content was noticed in 0.1-10 s⁻¹. The trend demonstrates that there exists no relationship between the interstitial content and the average DRX grain size.

While the SFE has a relationship with the DRX, it does not have a known relationship with grain growth. The grain growth is known to be dependent on time and temperature. A condition similar to Eq. (10) can be constructed to analyse the average DRX grain size vs. strain rate curve. As per such a condition, the curve can be divided into two parts: a time dependent part and a temperature dependent part, as shown in Fig. 12. Minimum grain size corresponding to the threshold time cited above can be considered as the critical grain size ($D_{c}$), beyond which the growth is sustained either by time or temperature. In view of the above, post-nucleation growth under dynamic conditions can be represented by the following general framework:

$$
D = D_{c} + X_{t}\Delta D_{t} + X_{T}\Delta D_{T}
\tag{11}
$$

Where $D$ is the final grain size, $D_{c}$ is the critical size, $\Delta D_{t}$ and $\Delta D_{T}$ represent the contributions of time and temperature to grain growth, $X_{t}$ and $X_{T}$ are weights assigned to these two parameters in a manner similar to Eq. (5). At low strain rate conditions, where there is adequate time for grain growth, $X_{t}$ assumes a high value, whereas at high strain rates, where the adiabatic temperature rise is significant, $X_{T}$ assumes a high value. The resultant of these two parameters controls the final grain size, as shown schematically in Fig. 12.

Within the framework of Eq. (11), the entire curve of Fig. 9 (b) can be interpreted on the basis of time-dependent and temperature-dependent grain growth. For long growth times (1-1000 s), $X_{t}$ is high and $X_{T}$ is insignificant; therefore the grain growth is consistently time-

## 4. Conclusion

The association between composition of a material and its SFE, and the dependency of DRX on SFE are commonly discussed. However, interstitial content-SFE-DRX linkage was not explicitly demonstrated earlier. An investigation was carried out to gain insights into such a linkage, using three variants of a Fe-Cr-Ni-Mo model alloy. Thermodynamic calculations were carried out to determine the effect of interstitial content on SFE. The thermodynamic calculations show that SFE decreases from $27\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ to $17\ \text{mJ/m}^2$ with an increase in interstitial content from 0.1 to $0.25\ \text{wt}\%$ at room temperature. These predictions by thermodynamic model were validated by XRD line profile analysis. SFE at 1323 K for variants of the model alloy was predicted using the same thermodynamic model. DRX behaviour of the alloy was characterized using EBSD technique. The findings from the investigation can be summarized as:

(i) With increasing total interstitial (here N + C) content, SFE reduces and the probability of forming stacking faults increases in the Fe-Cr-Ni-Mo system.

(ii) Since the stacking fault probability increases, the sites for dislocation forest formation increase during deformation. This is manifested as higher micro-strain in the variants having higher interstitial content. As a result, in these variants, DRX initiation occurs at lower strain levels.

(iii) The variation of critical strain with strain rate does not correspond to the overall DRX fraction in any variant. Thus, time and temperature, in addition to the accumulation of stored energy, control the DRX fraction in the alloy. As a consequence, the 7 N and 11 N shows sluggish DRX behaviour at strain rate around $1\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

(iv) The degree of dependency of DRX fraction on time, however, varies with interstitial content, and is highest in the alloy with lowest interstitial content. As the interstitial content increases in the alloy, the DRX fraction practically becomes rate-independent.

(v) In the present investigation, no relationship between interstitial content and grain growth was observed. However, like the variation of DRX fractions with strain rate, minima exist (at $1\ \text{s}^{-1}$) in average DRX grain size vs strain rate relation.

(vi) In the domain $0.001-1\ \text{s}^{-1}$ growth was found to be time dependent and in $1-10\ \text{s}^{-1}$, growth was temperature dependent. In low strain rate domain, due to availability of equal time to each variant at a specified strain rate, similar grain size for all was obtained at end of the deformation. However, due to variation in adiabatic temperature rise in high strain rate domain region, grain size varied among the variants.
