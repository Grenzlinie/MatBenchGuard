## Abstract

New interrupted cooling experiments have been designed to study the kinetics of bainitic ferrite formation starting from a mixture of austenite and bainitic ferrite. It is found that the kinetics of bainitic ferrite formation during the cooling stage is determined by the isothermal holding time. The formation rate of bainitic ferrite at the beginning of the cooling decreases with increasing prior isothermal holding time. An unexpected stagnant stage during the cooling stage appears when the isothermal holding time increases to a critical point. There are two reasons for the occurrence of the stagnant stage: (i) a solute spike in front of the interface; and (ii) kinetic transition. A so-called Gibbs energy balance approach, in which the dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion inside the interface and interface friction is assumed to be equal to the available chemical driving force, is applied to theoretically explain the stagnant stage. A kinetics transition from a fast growth mode without diffusion of Mn and Si inside the austenite-bainitic ferrite interfaces to a slow growth mode with diffusion inside the interface is predicted. The stagnant stage is caused by the transition to a slow growth mode. The Gibbs energy balance approach describes the experimental observations very well.

## 1. Introduction

The kinetics of the bainitic ferrite formation in lean and more alloyed steels is of great interest for the production of advanced high-strength steels, and has been widely studied both experimentally and theoretically [1–15]. Despite abundant efforts, the mechanism of bainitic ferrite formation is still one of the most controversial topics in the field of phase transformations [3,5,15–18]. Generally speaking, there are two competing views on the mechanism of bainite transformation: (i) first, the bainitic transformation is considered to be a diffusionless transformation [15,19], which in principle is the same as the martensitic transformation. During the growth of a bainitic plate there is no need for carbon diffusion, but such diffusion may take place after the growth; and (ii) secondly, the bainitic transformation is considered to be a diffusional transformation [2,3,20–23]. During the bainitic ferrite formation the carbon diffuses away from the growing ferrite into austenite, and the transformation rate is controlled by the diffusion of carbon and possibly other alloying elements. In the last decade or so, much effort has been paid to performing conventional isothermal experiments using modern techniques to validate either of the views. However, both schools seem to be able to explain the experimental results to their own satisfaction [8–10,12–14]. Recently, a series of cyclic partial phase transformation experiments [24], during which the transformation always starts from a mixture of austenite and ferrite (grain boundary ferrite) phase, have been designed to successfully discriminate between the classical local equilibrium model [25-27] and the paraequilibrium model [28,29]. It was found that the initial kinetics of austenite to ferrite transformation starting from a mixture of austenite and ferrite is determined by the distribution of the alloying element at the austenite/ferrite interface created earlier [30]. In other words, the measured austenite to ferrite transformation kinetics starting from the austenite and ferrite mixture can be used to demonstrate that substitutional element partitioning does take place at the austenite-ferrite interface.

In this work, the concept behind the cyclic partial transformation is extended to study the bainitic ferrite formation. A series of type IC (isothermal and cooling) dilatometric interrupted cooling experiments, in which the bainitic ferrite formation starts from a mixture of austenite and bainitic ferrite, are performed for two Fe-C-Mn and Fe-C-Mn-Si alloys. A so-called Gibbs energy balance approach, in which the dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion inside the interface and interface friction is assumed to be equal to the available chemical driving force, is proposed to explain the special features observed in the interrupted cooling experiments.

### 2. Experimental approach

The materials investigated here are an Fe-3Mn-0.1C (wt.%) alloy and an Fe-3Mn-0.1C-1.5Si (wt.%) alloy. In the interrupted cooling experiments, first an isothermal partial bainitic transformation is imposed to create a mixture of austenite and bainitic ferrite, then the mixture is slowly cooled down for further bainitic ferrite formation. This kind of interrupted cooling experiment is called a type IC experiment. The temperature program for type IC experiments is sketched in Fig. 1. The preceding austenization temperature and time for all type IC experiments are 1000 °C and 5 min, respectively. The cooling and heating rate during the type IC experiments are both $1\,\mathrm{^\circ C\,s^{-1}}$. Unlike conventional experiments, the bainitic transformation kinetics during the cooling stage, which is expected to reflect the austenite/bainitic ferrite interface condition at the end point of the isothermal holding stage, is the feature of interest here. This kinetics can be used to deduce the evolution of the austenite/bainitic ferrite interface condition during the isothermal bainite transformation. In order to study this evolution, different isothermal holding times are applied. It is expected that different austenite/bainitic ferrite interface conditions are obtained at the end of isothermal bainitic transformations.

A Bähr 805A dilatometer is used to measure the dilatation of the specimen (10 mm in length and 5 mm in diameter) during the type IC experiment, and the dilatation signal can be transferred into phase transformation kinetics using the rule of mixtures. In order to check the temperature difference along the length of the specimen, two thermocouples, spaced 4 mm apart, were used in the experiments, and the measured temperature gradient was typically smaller than 5 °C. The samples were mechanically polished and etched in 2% Nital solution for light optical microscopy. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies were conducted on a JEOL 2100F microscope operating at 200 kV. The TEM samples were prepared by electropolishing at 60-80 mV in a Struers TenuPol-5 electropolisher using a solution containing 950 ml acetic acid and 50 ml of perchloric acid.

## 4. Theoretical analysis

In the type IC experiments, after the isothermal formation of a mixture of bainitic ferrite and austenite, it is expected that the bainitic ferrite formation will continue immediately during the last cooling step according to both the diffusionless theory and the diffusional transformation theory with paraequilibrium assumption. However, as shown in the type IC experiments for both Fe–3Mn–0.1C–1.5Si and Fe–3Mn–0.1C alloys, for longer isothermal holding times a so-called stagnant stage is observed. At first sight, it is expected that the stagnant stage during the bainitic transformation here is also caused by the Mn spike (enrichment) and Si spike (depletion) ahead of the interface. The diffusion coefficient of Si and Mn in austenite ($D_{\text{Mn}}^{FCC}=5.3\times10^{-22}$, $D_{\text{Si}}^{FCC}=5.2\times10^{-21}$, $\text{m}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$) can be calculated by DICTRA based on the Mob2 database [31], and thus the thickness of the Mn and Si spikes can be roughly estimated by $(2Dt)^{0.5}$. Therefore, the Mn and Si spikes can be considered as the reasons for the retardation of the growth of the bainitic ferrite plates. However, during the bainitic ferrite formation the nucleation of bainitic plates is continuous, which means that during cooling there are possibly newly nucleated bainitic plates with no Mn or Si spikes. The presence of a spike is not sufficient to explain why the newly nucleated bainitic plates cannot move during the initial stage of cooling. In the following sections, a Gibbs energy balance approach will be formulated to explain the experimentally observed stagnant stage during the type IC bainitic ferrite formation.

### 4.1. A Gibbs energy balance approach

During a diffusional phase transformation, there is segregation of alloying elements into the migrating interface, which would cause dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion of alloying elements inside the interface, resulting in retardation of the interface migration [32-35]. This concept has been widely used in modeling the phase transformation kinetics [36-39]. The diffusion of alloying elements inside an interface moving with a quasi-steady velocity $v$ is governed by [34]

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[D \frac{\partial X}{\partial x}+\frac{D X}{R T} \frac{\partial E}{\partial x}+v X\right]=0 \tag{1}
$$

where $X$ is the concentration of the solutes, $x$ is the distance, $D$ is the diffusion coefficient of the solute inside the interface, $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is the temperature, $v$ is the interface velocity and $E$ is the free energy of interaction of the solute with the interface.

The dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion inside the interface during phase transformation can be evaluated by a solute drag model [32-34]:

$$
P V_{m}=-\int_{-\delta}^{+\delta}\left(X-X_{0}\right)(d E / d x) d x \tag{2}
$$

where $\delta$ is the half width of the interface, $P$ is the solute drag force that arises from the asymmetric solute distribution inside the interface, $V_{m}$ is the molar volume and $X_{0}$ is the nominal concentration of solutes in the alloys.

The dissipation of Gibbs energy can also be calculated from Hillert and Sundman's equation [35,36]:

$$
\Delta G_{m}^{\text{diff}}=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(u_{M}-u_{M}^{0}\right)\left[d\left(\mu_{M}-\mu_{\mathrm{Fe}}\right) / d x\right] d x \tag{3}
$$

where $\Delta G_{m}^{\text{diff}}$ is the dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion inside the interface, $u_{M}$ is the $u$ fraction of solute M, defined as $x_{M} /\left(1-x_{c}\right), u_{M}^{0}$ is the initial $u$ fraction of solute M, and $\mu_{\mathrm{M}}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{Fe}}$ are the chemical potential of M and Fe, respectively.

The equations by Cahn and Hillert-Sundman have been compared by Hillert [40], who found that they give the same result if the same physical parameter values are used in both calculations. In both equations, the dissipation inside the interface and the dissipation due to the spike of solutes in front of interface are taken into account.

In the classical diffusional models, it is generally assumed that the interface friction is zero, which means that the value of interface mobility is infinite and all the available driving force is only dissipated by diffusion of solutes. However, in reality, the value of interface mobility is finite [36,41], and the dissipation caused by the interface friction can be written as:

$$
\Delta G_{m}^{\text{friction}}=v V_{m} / M \tag{4}
$$

where $M$ is the intrinsic interface mobility and $V_{m}$ is the molar volume.

The chemical driving force per mole of atoms can gener-
ally be calculated from:
$$
\Delta G_{m}^{\text {chem }}=\sum_{i}^{n} x_{i}^{0}\left[\mu_{i}^{\gamma / \alpha}\left(x_{i}^{\alpha / \gamma}\right)-\mu_{i}^{\alpha / \gamma}\left(x_{i}^{\alpha / \gamma}\right)\right] \tag{5}
$$
where $x_{i}^{0}$ is the composition of material transfered over the interface when there is no diffusion in $\alpha, x_{i}^{0}=x_{i}^{\alpha / \gamma}, \Delta G_{m}^{\text {chem }}$ is the chemical driving force, $i$ is the element in the alloys, $n$ is the number of elements, $x_{i}^{\alpha / \gamma}$ and $x_{i}^{\gamma / \alpha}$ are the mole fractions of $i$ at the interface on the ferrite and austenite side, respectively (when $i$ is substitutional alloying elements, $x_{i}^{\gamma / \alpha}$ is chosen to be the concentration at the root of the spike, thus the spike is included in the calculations), and $\mu_{i}^{\gamma / \alpha}$ and $\mu_{i}^{\gamma / \alpha}$ are the chemical potential of $i$ in austenite and ferrite.

If formation of bainitic ferrite is controlled by carbon diffusion, the growth rate of a plate can be calculated from the Zener-Hillert equation [42]:
$$
\frac{v}{D_{\mathrm{C}}^{\gamma}}=\frac{R T\left(x_{\mathrm{C}}^{\gamma / \alpha}-x_{\mathrm{C}}^{0}\right)^{2}}{8 V_{m} \sigma x_{\mathrm{C}}^{0}} \tag{6}
$$
where $v$ is the growth rate, $D_{\mathrm{C}}^{\gamma}$ is the diffusion coefficient of $\mathrm{C}$ in austenite, $\sigma$ is the interfacial energy, $x_{\mathrm{C}}^{\gamma / \alpha}$ is the mole fraction of $\mathrm{C}$ at the interface in austenite and $x_{\mathrm{C}}^{0}$ is the mole fraction of $\mathrm{C}$ in austenite.

After coupling Eqs. (5) and (6), it is clear that the chemical driving force $\Delta G_{m}^{\text {chem }}$ will be a function of growth rate $v$ and $x_{\mathrm{C}}^{0} . x_{\mathrm{C}}^{0}$ depends on the fraction of austenite transformed here, and it increases with increasing fraction of austenite transformed. All calculations for the chemical driving force are performed in Thermo Calc TQ programming interface [43].

Based on the above equations, the dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion inside the interface, the interface friction and the chemical driving force can be calculated. The chemical driving force should be equal to the dissipation of Gibbs energy due to diffusion inside the interface and interface friction:
$$
\Delta G_{m}^{\text {chem }}=\Delta G_{m}^{\text {diff }}+\Delta G_{m}^{\text {friction }} \tag{7}
$$

### 4.2. Model application

The Gibbs energy balance approach will now be applied to model the phase transformation kinetics during the type IC experiments in the $\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Mn}-\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Mn}-\mathrm{Si}-\mathrm{C}$ alloys studied here. The type IC experiments in the $\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Mn}-\mathrm{C}$ alloy, in which only the dissipation due to Mn diffusion inside the interface is considered, is taken as the starting point.

In Fig. 7a, the dissipation of Gibbs energy due to Mn diffusion inside the interface at $550{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for the $\mathrm{Fe}-\mathrm{Mn}-\mathrm{C}$ alloy is plotted as a function of interface velocity. The thickness of interface $2 \delta$ is assumed here to be $0.5 \mathrm{~nm}$, and the diffusion coefficient of Mn inside the interface $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{i n t}$ is assumed to be the geometric average of the diffusion coefficient of Mn in austenite $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{F C C}$, in ferrite $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{B C C}$ and in the ferrite grain boundary $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{G B}$. The value of $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{F C C}$ and $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{B C C}$ can be calculated using DICTRA, while the value of $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{G B}$ is obtained from Ref. [44]. At $550{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}, D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{i n t}$ is about $10^{-18} \mathrm{~m}^{2} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$, which is about four orders of magnitude higher than $D_{\mathrm{Mn}}^{F C C}$. To our knowledge, there is no experimentally determined value of the intrinsic interface mobility for the austenite to ferrite transformation yet, while the value of the effective interface mobility has been measured by different research groups [45-48]. In the present calculation, the value of the effective interface mobility will be used for calculations, and it is expected that it is an underestimation of the intrinsic interface velocity. The value of the effective interface mobility used here is derived from Wits et al.'s equation [48]. The binding energy of Mn, $E_{0}^{\mathrm{Mn}}$, has been experimentally measured by Enomoto et al. [49], and three different values of binding energy are applied in the calculation to indicate the effect of the binding energy on the dissipation.

## 5. Discussion

During the growth of a bainitic ferrite plate there is some local plastic strain which also causes dissipation, called mechanical dissipation here. In order to better describe the experimental data, Bhadeshia [15] assumed that the mechanical dissipation during the bainitic ferrite transformation is a temperature-independent constant, $400\ \text{J mol}^{-1}$. However, based on model predictions, Bouaziz et al. [52] claimed that the mechanical dissipation should be temperature and composition dependent as the mechan- ical properties (such as yield point) of austenite is deter- mined by them. As shown in experimental results, the length of the stag- nant stage increases with increasing isothermal holding time. This can be explained as follows: in the later stage of isothermal bainite transformation, the interfaces migrate at a very slow velocity, and the Mn or Si spikes can form in front of the interfaces. The width of Mn or Si spikes will extend with increasing isothermal holding time, which would prolong the length of the stagnant stage. As shown in Fig. 4, in a type II experiment there is almost no ferrite formation during the 20 min isothermal holding at $520\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, while, compared with the type IC experiments, the trans- formation kinetics during the following cooling stage in the type II experiment is significantly retarded. This retar- dation is also due to the effect of solute spikes.

## 6. Conclusions

In this work, a series of interrupted cooling experiments were designed to study the mechanism of bainitic ferrite transformation in the Fe-3Mn-0.1C and Fe-3Mn-1.5Si-0.1C alloys. A special "stagnant stage" feature is observed, the length of which is not a constant but increases with increasing holding time. This observation can be taken as firm evidence of diffusional rearrangement at the austen- ite-bainite interface. A so-called Gibbs energy balance approach is proposed to model the bainitic ferrite transfor- mation kinetics during the interrupted cooling experiments, and a kinetics transition from fast growth mode without diffusion of Mn or Si inside the interfaces to slow growth mode with diffusion inside the interfaces is predicted. The model calculations for interrupted cooling experiments for both alloys predict the occurrence of a stagnant stage after a well defined holding time with surprising accuracy. It is found that the stagnant stage is caused by the slow growth mode. The existence of solute spikes in front of interfaces is also effective for retarding the growth of bain- itic ferrite plates.
