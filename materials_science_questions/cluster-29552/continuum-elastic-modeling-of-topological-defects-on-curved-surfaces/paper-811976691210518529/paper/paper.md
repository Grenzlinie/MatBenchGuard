### ENERGETICS OF THE FOCAL CONICS OF SMECTIC PHASES

M. KLÉMAN

Laboratoire de Physique des Solides, Université Paris-Sud, Bât. 510, 91405 Orsay, France

(Reçu le 8 juillet 1977, accepté le 31 août 1977)

Résumé. - Après avoir rappelé les méthodes analytiques nécessaires dans la description géométrique des couches fortement courbées dans les phases smectiques, on les applique au cas des cyclides de Dupin. On calcule en particulier l'énergie des domaines focaux, en insistant sur les différences qui apparaissent entre domaines focaux normaux (bâtis sur une ellipse et une branche d'hyperbole) et domaines focaux paraboliques.

Abstract. - Analytical methods are given which enable one to describe geometrically layers of large curvature in a smectic phase. Dupin cyclides in focal domains are taken as examples. The curvature energy of focal domains is calculated. The difference between normal focal domains (built on an ellipse and an hyperbola), and parabolic focal domains is stressed.

The most evident category of defects present in smectic A liquid crystals, when studied by optical microscopy, are the so-called *focal conics*, or *focal domains*, whose topological and geometrical features were first described by G. Friedel and F. Grandjean [1]. Optically, one observes a pair of focal conics, viz, an ellipse E and one branch of an hyperbola H, around which the smectics layers fold in such a way that each of the layers keeps a constant thickness. Those layers have therefore common normals, forming a two-parameter family (a congruence). All these normals are constructed by joining any point on the ellipse to any point on the branch of the hyperbola. The physical part of the normal is either located between the two points of contact with the conics, or outside. According to the location, one distinguishes focal domains of the first species and of the second species (Bouligand) [2].

Up to now, it appears that, except for a few rare observations, only domains of the first species have been observed (Fig. 1). The surfaces along which the layers fold are called Dupin cyclides.

Although the geometry of these objects is well-known, it is not possible to find in the published works on liquid crystals an analytical description of focal domains $(^{1})$, and no calculation has been made of their energy. It is the purpose of this note to provide the mathematical instruments necessary, which consist of some elements of the theory of surfaces (the best available reference for this topic (in french) is Darboux [3]) and to present some results concerning the energetics of focal domains.

This paper will deal successively with $a)$ definition of local axes on a surface and definition of the principal radii of curvature; $b)$ the fundamental quadratic form for Dupin cyclides; $c)$ specialization to the case of focal parabolae; $d)$ expression of the energy and discussion.

### 1. Local axes on a surface and principal radii of curvatures.
We shall use rectangular curvilinear coordinates $(u, v)$ on a surface $\Sigma$ such that, at each point $M$ of $\Sigma$, the equation of $\Sigma$ reads locally
$$
\mathrm{d} z=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} s_{u}^{2}}{R_{1}}+\frac{\mathrm{d} s_{v}^{2}}{R_{2}}\right)+0(3) \tag{1}
$$
without crossed terms. $\mathrm{d} z$ is measured along the normal to $\Sigma$. $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ are the principal radii of curvature at $M$, and the lines $u=$ const., $v=$ const. are called the lines of curvature. $\mathrm{d} s_{u}$ and $\mathrm{d} s_{v}$ are lengths measured along those lines. $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ are also the radii of curvature of the sections of $\Sigma$ by the planes $\mathrm{d} v=0$ (for $R_{1}$) $\mathrm{d} u=0$ (for $R_{2}$) perpendicular to $\Sigma$ in $M$. $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ are algebraic lengths, whose sign is determined by the orientation chosen on the normal to $\Sigma$ in $M$. If $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ are of the same sign, $\Sigma$ is locally an elliptic paraboloid. If $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ are of opposite signs, $\Sigma$ is locally an hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle-point) (Fig. 2).

Let us introduce the fundamental quadratic form
$$
\mathrm{d} s^{2}=A^{2} \mathrm{~d} u^{2}+B^{2} \mathrm{~d} v^{2}=\mathrm{d} s_{u}^{2}+\mathrm{d} s_{v}^{2}
$$

where $A(u,v)\mathrm{d}u=\mathrm{d}s_{u}$ and $B(u,v)\mathrm{d}v=\mathrm{d}s_{v}$. These quantities are related to $\sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{R_{1}}$ and $\sigma_{2}=\frac{1}{R_{2}}$ as follows $(^{2})$ :

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
AB\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2} & = -\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial B}{\partial v}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left(\frac{1}{B}\frac{\partial A}{\partial u}\right) \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}(\sigma_{2}B) & = \sigma_{1}\frac{\partial B}{\partial u} \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial v}(\sigma_{1}A) & = \sigma_{2}\frac{\partial A}{\partial v}
\end{aligned}
\right\}\cdot(2)
$$

The infinitesimal element of surface on $\Sigma$ is also of importance ; it is given by
$$
\mathrm{d}\Sigma=AB\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v\qquad(3)
$$
and can be expressed with the help of the first of eq. (2) eventually.

2. Dupin cyclides. — Let us now specialize to the case of Dupin cyclides and let $x,y,z$ be coordinates in a frame of reference linked to the focal domain, such that the equation of the ellipse E reads :
$$
z=0,\quad\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}=1\qquad(4)
$$
and that of the hyperbola H :
$$
y=0,\quad\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}-b^{2}}-\frac{z^{2}}{b^{2}}=1.\qquad(5)
$$

Let $M'(x',y')$ a point on E and $M''(x'',y'')$ a point on H. The distance $M'M''$ is found to be
$$
M'M''=\left|\frac{c}{a}x'-\frac{a}{c}x''\right|\qquad(6)
$$

$(^{2})$ Let us consider two neighboring frames of reference on $\Sigma$, in $M(u,v)$ and $M+\mathrm{d}M$. The infinitesimal rotation which brings the frame in $M$ to that one in $M+\mathrm{d}M$ can be easily expressed as a function of $\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},A,B$, and their derivatives. Consider now a closed circuit on $\Sigma$, and follow it by bringing along a frame of reference suffering such infinitesimal rotations. When the circuit is completed from $M$ to $M$, the same frame of reference must be found again in $M$ as that one started off. These conditions of compatibility on the frames of reference yield eq. (2).

where $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ $(^3)$. If $c/a = e$ is the eccentricity of the ellipse, then

$$
M' M'' = | ex' - e^{-1} x'' | .
$$

The two spheres which are respectively centred on the two focal lines

$$
\begin{aligned}
& (X - x')^2 + (Y - y')^2 + Z^2 = (ex' - r)^2 \\
& (X - x'')^2 + Y^2 + (Z - z'')^2 = (e^{-1} x'' - r)^2
\end{aligned} \tag{7}
$$

are clearly tangent to each other in $M$, and the distance $M' M''$ is equal to the sum (or the difference) of their radii. Therefore they envelop the same surface $\Sigma$, fixed by the parameter $r$. The plane tangent to $\Sigma$ in $M$ is perpendicular to $M' M''$. Hence this surface is a Dupin cyclide.

Orienting $M' M''$ fixes a sign to the radii of the two spheres, which are also the principal radii of curvature of the Dupin cyclide in $M$, since a congruence of normals touches its focal surface at the centers of curvature of the parallel surfaces $\Sigma$. Hence we can write

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1 & = \pm \left\{ ex' - r \right\} \\
R_2 & = \pm \left\{ e^{-1} x'' - r \right\} \\
M' M'' & = | R_1 - R_2 |
\end{aligned} \tag{8}
$$

$M' M''$ does not depend on $r$, i.e. all the parallel Dupin cyclides have as expected the same centers of curvature, on E and H. The same sign outside the brackets has to be chosen for $R_1$ and $R_2$.

Let us parameterize the conics in the usual way

for E
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
x' & = a \cos v \\
y' & = b \sin v
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{9}
$$

for H
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
x'' & = c \cosh u \\
z'' & = b \sinh u
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{10}
$$

and calculate the coefficients of the fundamental form. This can be made as follows : consider a cone made of the lines joining a fixed points $M''$ on H to all the points $M'$ on E (Fig. 3). This cone intersects the cyclide along a circular line of curvature ($u =$ const.) at a distance $R_2$ from $M''$. By definition one has

$$
B \mathrm{d}v = R_2 \mathrm{d}\omega \tag{11}
$$

where $\mathrm{d}\omega$ is the angle under which one sees an element of the circle from the apex of the cone. $\mathrm{d}v$ is the corresponding variation of $v$ between $M'$ and $M'''$. Similar considerations apply to $A \mathrm{d}u$. Finally one gets

$$
\begin{aligned}
A & = \pm b\sigma_2/(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) \\
B & = \pm b\sigma_1/(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) .
\end{aligned} \tag{12}
$$

FIG. 3. — Focal domain. Definition of the fundamental quadratic form (see text).

The signs of $A$ and $B$ are arbitrary in the sense that a change of sign corresponds to a change in the orientation chosen for the lines of curvature. However it is reasonable to choose this sign such that $A$ and $B$ are positive quantities which choice makes $\mathrm{d}s_u = A \mathrm{d}u$ and $\mathrm{d}s_v = B \mathrm{d}v$ positive when $\mathrm{d}u$ and $\mathrm{d}v$ are positive.

Let us describe in more details the various cyclides in a focal domain, when $r$ varies.

Physical part of the normal between E and H (first species). — $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ are of opposite signs. Let us orient the normals from their intersections with E to their intersections with H. This choice implies $\sigma_1 < 0, \sigma_2 > 0$, with :

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_1 & = (c \cos v - r)^{-1}, \quad \sigma_2 = (a \cosh u - r)^{-1} \\
A & = b\sigma_2/(\sigma_2 - \sigma_1), \quad B = b\sigma_1/(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)
\end{aligned} \tag{13}
$$

$r$ varies in the range $[- c, + \infty]$ (see Fig. 4).

$r = - c$; the Dupin cyclide is reduced to a point which is that apex $J$ on the ellipse which is opposite to the physical branch of the hyperbola.

$- c < r < c$; the Dupin cyclide has two singular points on the ellipse (obtained for $R_1 = 0$), none on the hyperbola.

$c < r < a$; the Dupin cyclide has no singular points.

$r > a$; singular points on the hyperbola only.

![](./images/811976691210518529_4.jpg)

FIG. 4. — Values of $r$ on the different Dupin cyclide which build the domain. Above : view in the plane containing the hyperbola. Below : view in the plane containing the ellipse.

$(^3)$ In this paper $e$ will always mean the eccentricity of the ellipse and will never mean the well-known transcendental number.

These geometrical properties could lead us to choose as the origin of Dupin cyclides, for the sake of symmetry, that one for which $r^2 = ac$. Letting $\rho = r - \sqrt{ac}$, one gets
$$
R_{1}=c(\cos v-e^{-1 / 2})-\rho, \quad R_{2}=a(\cosh u-e^{1 / 2})-\rho.
$$

However we shall have no reason here to use this choice.

Physical part of the normal outside the segment joining E and H. — $\sigma_{1}$ and $\sigma_{2}$ have the same sign, which never changes (there is no physical point where $\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}$). Orienting as above the normals from E to H, one has, choosing figure $1b$ as an example of the topological situation, we have in mind :
$$
\sigma_{1}<0, \quad \sigma_{2}<0, \quad \sigma_{2}<\sigma_{1}
$$
(focal domain of first species, continuous with the part described by eq. (13)).

Hence
$$
\begin{gathered}
\sigma_{1}=(c \cos v-r)^{-1}, \quad \sigma_{2}=(a \cosh u-r)^{-1} \\
A=\frac{b \sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}} \quad B=\frac{b \sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}}.
\end{gathered} \quad(14)
$$

One gets focal domains of second species by changing all signs in eq. (14) $(\sigma_{1}>0, \sigma_{2}>0, \sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2})$.

— The entire set of values of $r$. Finally, on a given normal, which we can define entirely by the set of parameters $[u, v]$, one can distinguish 3 regions :

Whatever the value of $r$ might be, Dupin cyclides of the second species always have singular points, either on E, or on H.

Reference [2] gives good pictorial representations of focal domains; see also photographs of plaster models in [4]. One will recall the following facts.

— Hyperbolic parts of Dupin cyclides are limited to focal domains of the first species and fill that part of space which is contained between two half-cylinders whose generatrices are parallel to the asymptotes of the hyperbola. Each of these half-cylinder is limited by the ellipse. The remainder of the Dupin cyclides give focal domains of elliptic curvature, with $\sigma_{1}<0, \sigma_{2}<0$ (in our notation), i.e., in the simple case of tori, figure $1c$. Hence focal domains of the first species are continuous in their whole and fill space entirely.

— Focal domains of the second species (with positive curvatures) correspond to other cyclides; they fill space entirely in their whole.

— Focal parabolae (limiting case of the former). Parametrize the two parabolae as follows (see Fig. 5)
$$
\left.\begin{array}{rl}
P_{1} & z_{1}=-\frac{f}{2}+f \alpha_{1}^{2} \\
& x_{1}=2 f \alpha_{1} \\
P_{2} & z_{2}=-f \alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{f}{2} \\
& y_{2}=2 f \alpha_{2}
\end{array}\right\} \quad(15)
$$

![](./images/811976691210518529_5.jpg)

FIG. 5. — Coordinates for a parabolic focal domain.

$f$ is the focal length of the parabola. The distance between two points $M_{1}$ and $M_{2}$, one on $P_{1}$, the other on $P_{2}$, is
$$
\Delta=M_{1} M_{2}=\left|z_{1}-z_{2}+2 f\right|=f\left|\alpha_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}+1\right|.
$$

Proceeding as above, introduce two radii of curvature
$$
\begin{aligned}
& R_{1}=-f \alpha_{1}^{2}-\frac{f}{2}-r \\
& R_{2}=+f \alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{f}{2}-r
\end{aligned} \quad(16)
$$
and a fundamental quadratic form which is there
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{d} s^{2}=4\left(R_{1}^{2} \mathrm{~d} \alpha_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2} \mathrm{~d} \alpha_{2}^{2}\right)= & \\
& =\frac{1}{f^{2}}\left(R_{1}^{2} \mathrm{~d} x_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2} \mathrm{~d} y_{2}^{2}\right). \quad(17)
\end{aligned}
$$

Focal parabolae of the first species.
$$
-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}\right)<r / f<+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}\right).
$$

Whatever $r$ might be, one has always $\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}<0$.

Cyclides obtained by letting $r/f$ vary in the range $[-1/2, +1/2]$ have no singular points. It is interesting to notice that the set of all cyclides fill all space, whereas in the case of focal conics, the cyclides of the first species (with $\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}<0$) are limited. Hence, whereas focal conics of the first species give domains

of finite size (which fact leads to the difficult problem of filling space with focal domains), focal parabolae can give a focal domain of infinite size. This advocates for the existence of such domains.

Focal parabolae of the second species. They correspond to the ranges of variation $-\infty < r/f < -1/2$ or $1/2 < r/f < +\infty$. These two types of focal domains are of course equivalent, by the symmetry $x \to y$, $z \to -z$. Such focal domains fill in space entirely. We have $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 > 0$.

3. Curvature energy of a focal domain. — The energy of curvature of a smectic layer is of the form
$$
\rho f=\frac{1}{2} K_{1}\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\right)^{2} \tag{18}
$$
per unit area of the layer and per unit length measured along the normal. We shall integrate this quantity over a focal domain, which needs calculating the integral
$$
W=\int \rho f \mathrm{~d} V=\frac{1}{2} K_{1} \int\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\right)^{2} A B \mathrm{~d} u \mathrm{~d} v \mathrm{~d} r. \tag{19}
$$

Normal focal domain (not parabolic) of the first species, $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 < 0$. — Eq. (19) reads, using eq. (13)
$$
\begin{aligned}
W & =-\frac{1}{2} K_{1} b^{2} \int \frac{\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)} \sigma_{1} \sigma_{2} \mathrm{~d} u \mathrm{~d} v \mathrm{~d} r \\
& =-\frac{1}{2} K_{1} b^{2} \int \frac{(c \cos v+a \cosh u-2 r)^{2}}{(c \cos v-a \cosh u)^{2}} \frac{\mathrm{d} r \mathrm{~d} u \mathrm{~d} v}{(a \cosh u-r(c \cos v-r))}.
\end{aligned} \tag{20}
$$

Let us first integrate on $r$, in the range $[c \cos v, a \cosh u]$
$$
\begin{aligned}
W & =-\frac{1}{2} K_{1} b^{2} \int\left[\frac{4 r}{(c \cos v-a \cosh u)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(c \cos v-a \cosh u)} \ln \left|\frac{c \cos v-r}{a \cosh u-r}\right|\right]_{r=+c \cos v}^{r=a \cosh u} \mathrm{~d} u \mathrm{~d} v \\
& =K_{1} b^{2} \int \frac{1}{c \cos v-a \cosh u}\left(2-\ln \left|\frac{c \cos v-a \cosh u}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}\right|\right) \mathrm{d} u \mathrm{~d} v
\end{aligned} \tag{21}
$$
where $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ is a core radius which is here taken as constant all along $\mathrm{E}$ and $\mathrm{H}$, and of the order of a layer thickness.

This integral can be evaluated near $e=0$ (i.e. near the situation where $\mathrm{E}$ is a circle) : one gets (see appendix)
$$
W=K_{1} \frac{b^{2}}{a}\left(2 \pi K\left(e^{2}\right)\left(2 \ln \frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}+1\right)+\pi^{2}(2 \ln 2-5)\right) \tag{22}
$$
which takes the (exact) form for $e=0$
$$
W=2 \pi K_{1} a\left(\ln \frac{2 a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}-2\right) \tag{23}
$$
where $a$ is the radius of the circle. $K(e^{2})$ is the complete elliptic function of the first kind ($K(0)=\pi / 2$) and is a very quickly increasing function of $e^{2}$ (which becomes infinite for $e=1$ ($e=1$ corresponds to the parabola, which case requires a special calculation)).

One can separate in $W$ the effect of the size of the focal domain, represented by $a$, from that of the eccentricity $e$ : let us keep in eq. (22) the terms which are definitely larger when the size of the domain is not microscopic. One gets :
$$
W=4 \pi K_{1}\left(1-e^{2}\right) K\left(e^{2}\right) a \ln \frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}} \tag{24}
$$
$f=(1-e^{2}) K(e^{2})$ is a monotonous decreasing function when $e$ increases. Hence the eccentricity tends to be as large as possible. However this equation is valid only for small values of $e$. Let us write the energy in the general case : one gets
$$
W=K_{1}\left(1-e^{2}\right) a\left\{4 \pi K\left(e^{2}\right)\left(\ln \frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}-2\right)+\int \frac{\ln |e \cos v-\cosh u|}{-e \cos v+\cosh u} \mathrm{~d} u \mathrm{~d} v\right\}. \tag{25}
$$

Hand-made numerical evaluations of the integral lead to the same conclusion : for a given long axis $a$, $W$ is a decreasing function of $e$. Hence the situation in which the Dupin cyclides are degenerated into tori is not stable. However the transformation from a torus to a general cyclide requires a change in the nature of the core. In the case of a torus the singularity on the circle into which the ellipse is degenerated is a weak one : the different tori envelop cylindrically the circle but do not reach it. On the contrary a general cyclide has singular conical points on the ellipse ; this makes the ellipse singularity topologically different from the circle singularity, and most probably of a higher energy (see a calculation by de Gennes). Note incidentally that the topological barrier is presumably higher in lyotropic systems than in thermotropic systems. Independently of the topological barrier, which can freeze a low-eccentricity focal domain, it appears in conclusion that, when the core energy is taken into account, the total energy may reach a minimum for a given value of $e$, $a$ being kept constant.

For the limiting value $e = 1$, $W$ tends to zero, although $K(e^{2})$ becomes infinite, because
$$
\left(1-e^{2}\right) K\left(e^{2}\right)_{(e \rightleftharpoons 1)} \frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{2}\right) \ln \frac{1 b}{1-e^{2}}{ }_{(e \rightleftharpoons 1)} 0.\quad (26)
$$

Hence $W$ vanishes for $e \to 1$. In other words, this indicates to us that the limiting value $e = 1$ that we are here considering does not correspond to an ellipse becoming a parabola, but an ellipse flattening to a straight segment of length $2a$ (the minor axis length $b$ vanishing). (Note also that the perimeter $p$ of an ellipse is given by the expression
$$
p = 4\ aK(e^{2})
$$
which correctly tends to $2 \pi a$ when $e \to 0$, but becomes infinite for $e \to 1$.)

The study of the parabolic focal domain requires therefore a separate study.

Normal focal domain of the first species, $\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}>0$.
— Here we have to integrate eq. (20) in the range $-\infty < r < + c \cos v$; no change in the range of integration for $u$ and $v$. It is evident that the integral diverges for $r \to -\infty$, so that we have to introduce a cut off length $R$. The case is quite different from the former, where $W$ is finite whatever $a$ and $e$ might be. The divergence of the energy for $r$ large makes those parts of the Dupin cyclides less probable, and in fact they are hardly observed, as already pointed out.

Normal focal domain of the second species, $\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}>0$.
— The range of integration of $r$ is a $\cosh u < r < + \infty$. The energy diverges and the same remarks apply.

Parabolic focal domain of the first species. — The total energy reads
$$
W=2 K_{1} \int \frac{\left(f \alpha_{1}^{2}-f \alpha_{2}^{2}+2 r\right)^{2}}{\left(f \alpha_{1}^{2}+\frac{f}{2}+r\right)\left(f \alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{f}{2}-r\right)} \mathrm{d} \alpha_{1} \mathrm{~d} \alpha_{2} \mathrm{~d} r
\tag{27}
$$
$$
\text{where}\quad -f\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2}\right)<r<f\left(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}\right).
$$

The integration over $r$ yields :
$$
\begin{aligned}
W & =8 K_{1} f \int\left(1+\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}\right) × \\
& ×\left\{-1+\frac{1}{2} \ln \left(\frac{f}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}\left(1+\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}\right)\right)\right\} \mathrm{d} \alpha_{1} \mathrm{~d} \alpha_{2} \quad(28)
\end{aligned}
$$
where $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ is a core radius.

$W$ diverges by integrating over $\alpha_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2}$, in contradistinction to the case of the normal focal domain. Let us introduce a cut-off radius $R$, which is the size

of the cylinder of axis $z$ in which we shall confine the parabolic focal domain. One obtains, after integration of eq. (28) (in polar coordinates)

$$
\begin{aligned}
W=2 \pi K_{1} f & \left(-\frac{5}{32} \frac{R^{4}}{f^{4}}-\frac{5}{4} \frac{R^{2}}{f^{2}}+\left(\frac{R^{2}}{2 f^{2}}+\frac{R^{4}}{16 f^{4}}\right) ×\right. \\
& \left.× \ln \frac{f}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}+\left(1+\frac{R^{2}}{4 f^{2}}\right)^{2} \ln \left(1+\frac{R^{2}}{4 f^{2}}\right)\right) \quad(29)
\end{aligned}
$$

which practically reduces for $R \gg f$, to

$$
W=\frac{\pi K_{1} f}{8} \frac{R^{4}}{f^{4}} \ln \frac{R^{2}}{4 f r_{\mathrm{c}}}. \quad(30)
$$

Apart the dependence on $R$, which differentiates those domains from the normal one, the dependence on $f$ is also remarkable : $W$ diverges for $f=0$, i.e. for the parabolae reduced to segments along the $x$ axis. This is also at the opposite of normal focal domains, we discussed above. The reason is the follow- ing : Rosenblatt *et al.* [6], in a recent study of parabolic focal domains, noticed that the part of Dupin cyclides corresponding to very large values of $\alpha_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2}$ (i.e. located far from the central region), do not tend to planar layers, but present an undulation, of the form

$$
\Delta z=f \cos 2 \theta \quad(31)
$$

which does not depend on $r$. Hence, however large $\alpha_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2}$ are, there is still a curvature energy. This phenomenon does not exist for normal focal domains, where the Dupin cyclides become flat for large $r$'s. This explains the behaviour in $R$. The behaviour in $f$ is a purely dimensional effect. $W$ must necessarily depend on $(R / f)$. If the ratio $R / f$ is taken constant when $f$ decreases, $W$ vanishes for $f=0$. But this operation consists of decreasing the volume of the cylinder in consideration; in the same manner we took the limit $W(e, a)$ in the case of normal domains by letting the volume occupied by the focal domain decrease. Hence the behaviour is finally the same in both cases.

Parabolic focal domain of the second species. — Let us start from eq. (27) and integrate in the range $-\infty<r<-f\left(1 / 2+\alpha_{1}^{2}\right)$. It appears that we have to introduce a cut-off on $r$, viz. $-Z$; we shall assume $Z \gg R^{2} / f, Z$ is practically the length of the cylindrical zone of radius $R$. One gets, limiting ourselves to the largest term

$$
W=27 \pi K_{1} \frac{Z R^{2}}{f^{2}}. \quad(32)
$$

This quantity is definitely larger than eq. (30). Hence, only parabolic focal domains of the first species are to be expected.

4. Conclusion. — The present paper treated of the energy of focal domains in layered media with the help of the mathematical methods of the analytical theory of surfaces. Their power can be extended without much difficulty to other surfaces than Dupin cyclides. But let us stress the important results we have here obtained for this case.

— As far as we are concerned with bulk curvature energies, the only parts of the focal domains which are favoured are those with negative gaussian curvature (i.e. $\sigma_{1} \sigma_{2}<0$ ). This condition restricts normal (as opposed to parabolic) focal domains to a certain region of space (made of two semi-infinite cylinders) which anyway has a finite bulk energy, of the order of

$$
W_{\mathrm{n}}=\pi K_{1}\left(1-e^{2}\right) p \ln \frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}} \quad(33)
$$

where $p$ is the perimeter of the ellipse and $a$ the half-length of its major axis. Parabolic focal domains of negative gaussian curvature occupy the entire space, but their energy diverges. If one limits a parabolic focal domain to an infinite cylinder of radius $R$, the bulk energy is of the order of

$$
W_{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{\pi K_{1} f}{8} \frac{R^{4}}{f^{4}} \ln \frac{R^{2}}{4 f r_{\mathrm{c}}} \quad(34)
$$

where $f$ is the focal length. This is indeed also a small energy, and it is interesting to compare eq. (33) and eq. (34) for $R=a$ (i.e. both focal domains of different types occupying approximately the same volume). Let us study the condition

$$
W_{\mathrm{p}}<W_{\mathrm{n}} \quad(35)
$$

for $e=0$. This gives :

$$
a^{3} / f^{3}<16 \pi \ln \left(\frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}\right) / \ln \left(\frac{a^{2}}{4 f r_{\mathrm{c}}}\right) \quad(36)
$$

i.e.

$$
R \lesssim 3 f. \quad(37)
$$

This condition can be easily achieved, and this might explain recent results Rosenblatt *et al.* [6], who claim to have observed parabolic focal domains. It is interesting to note that condition (37) does not depend on elasticity constants.

— Of course this discussion does not take into account the elastic energy of the compressed layers (certainly quite small) or the core energy. Hence a problem which remains open and cannot be solved without a discussion of these two terms (especially the last one) is whether the geometrical quantities $e, a$ (for normal focal domains) or $f$ (for parabolic focal domain) reach an optimum value which minimize the total energy.

Appendix. — Let us write $W = K_1\ b^2\ I$, with

$$
a I = \int \frac{1}{e \cos v - \cosh u} [2 - \ln (e \cos v - \cosh u)] \mathrm{d}u \mathrm{d}v - \int \frac{1}{e \cos v - \cosh u} \ln \frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}} \mathrm{d}u \mathrm{d}v \tag{A.1}
$$

The second integral of eq. (A.1) can be evaluated in a closed form (use for example Abramovicz and Stegun [7]). One gets

$$
- \int \frac{\mathrm{d}u \mathrm{d}v}{e \cos v - \cosh u} = 4\ \pi K(e^2) \tag{A.2}
$$

Let us now introduce the integral

$$
M(e, f) = \int \frac{1}{e \cos v - f \cosh u} | 2 - \ln (e \cos v - f \cosh u) | \mathrm{d}u \mathrm{d}v \tag{A.3}
$$

(A.1) therefore reads

$$
I = \frac{4\ \pi}{a} K(e^2) \ln \frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}} + \frac{1}{a} M(e, 1) \tag{A.4}
$$

Straightforward derivations performed on $M(e, f)$ yields :

$$
f \frac{\partial M}{\partial f} + e \frac{\partial M}{\partial e} + M = \frac{4\ \pi}{f} K\left(\frac{e^2}{f}\right) \tag{A.5}
$$

Let us expand $M(e, f)$ in the vicinity of $e = 0, f = 1$. This gives :

$$
M(e, f) = M(0, 1) + e \frac{\partial M}{\partial e} (0, 1) + f \frac{\partial M}{\partial f} (0, 1) - \frac{\partial M}{\partial f} (0, 1) - e \frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial e \partial f}
$$

Introduce this expression in (A.5) :

$$
2\ M(e, 1) = 4\ \pi K(e^2) + M(0, 1) - \frac{\partial M}{\partial f} (0, 1) - e \frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial e \partial f} (0, 1) + 0(e^2)
$$

Straightforward integrations yield

$$
M(e, 1) = 2\ \pi K(e^2) + \pi^2(2 \ln 2 - 5) + \cdots
$$
