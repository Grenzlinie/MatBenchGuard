# Different Cooling Rate Dependences of Different Microstructure Units in Aluminium Glass by Molecular Dynamics Simulation *
LIU Chang-Song(刘长松), ZHU Zhen-Gang(朱震刚), XIA Jun-Chao(夏俊超), SUN De-Yan(孙得彦)

Laboratory of Internal Friction and Defects in Solids, Institute of Solid State Physics,
Chinese Academy of Sciences, Hefei 230031

(Received 21 May 1999)

Constant-pressure molecular dynamics simulation and the pair analysis technique have been performed to study the microstructural evolution of aluminium during rapid solidification. The microstructure characteristics of icosahedral ordering increase with decrease of the cooling rate, whereas the microstructure unit characteristics of hcp crystalline structure decrease. There are two kinds of microstructure units which are similar to those in the fcc crystal containing intersticialcies. These two kinds of microscopic units are nearly independent of the cooling rate. The microscopic structural unit characteristics of fcc crystalline structure do not depend on the cooling rate either. These results may help us understand the microstructure of glass and its stability.

PACS: 61.43. Bn, 61.72. Hh, 81.05. Kf

Detailed knowledge of the atomic structure is essential for understanding the special properties of amorphous materials. There seems to be general agreement that the basic process underlying the glass transition is the arrest of structural relaxations. $^{1}$ But it is not clear to what extent this leads to new structural units. Very different structural models have been proposed, $^{2}$ and some of them assume that the glass structure is essentially different from those of the liquid and the crystal. Recently, the known diaelastic response of crystals containing interstitials has been used to construct an interstitialcy model for condensed states of fcc metals. $^{3}$ In this model, amorphous materials correspond to a fixed concentration $c_{0}$ of frozen-in interstitialcies and the concentration $c_{0}$ depends on the method of preparation of the material. However, as in the noncrystalline state, the lack of atomic periodicity makes the experimental determination of the structure a largely unsolved problem. Therefore, molecular dynamics simulations offer an invaluable complementary tool. In the present work, by using a many-body potential and the pair analysis techniques we investigate how the quenching rate affects the macroscopic and microscopic properties of aluminium glass, in the hope of gaining an understanding of the characteristics of the glass structure and its stability.

In the present work, the glue potential $^{4,5}$ is employed to model the Al atomic interaction in constant-pressure molecular dynamics simulations. The system consisting of 500 atoms is subjected to periodic boundary conditions. The cooling rates investigated are $1.9 \times 10^{15}(\gamma 6)$, $1.9 \times 10^{14}(\gamma 5)$, $1.9 \times 10^{13}(\gamma 4)$, $9.5 \times 10^{12}(\gamma 3)$, $4.7 \times 10^{12}(\gamma 2)$, and $1.9 \times 10^{12}(\gamma 1)$ K/s. The microstructure can be described by the pair analysis techniques, $^{6}$ which are widely used to study the microstructure in the noncrystalline state. $^{7}$ In this technique, pairs of atoms are characterized with four indices. The 1551 pairs are characteristic of icosahedral ordering, whereas, 1421 pairs and 1422 pairs are characteristic of fcc and hcp crystalline structures, respectively. In Fig.1, we show the 1541 pairs and 1431 pairs for two cases, an fcc Al structure containing dumbbell interstitialcy and Al glass.

The microscopic quantity first investigated is the relative number of the 1551 pairs, shown in Fig.2. The relative number of 1551 pairs increases with decreasing temperature. This changing trend with temperature is in agreement with that in Ref.7. The slower the cooling rate, the greater the number of icosahedral ordering clusters become, which accords with Ref.8. This cooling rate effect on the 1551 pairs is similar to that on the enthalpy and the density, but its magnitude is significantly larger than any change we observed for macroscopic quantities.

Since 1541 and 1431 pairs represent short-range ordering in liquid and amorphous states, $^{7}$ we then investigate these two kinds of pairs, shown in Fig.3. At high temperatures, there is a linear increase in the 1541 and 1431 pairs, whereas at the glass transition temperature they reach their maxima; with further decreasing temperature they do not exhibit any significant change. The relative number of 1541 and 1431 pairs is nearly independent of the cooling rate, which is completely different from the case of the 1551 and the 1422 pairs (to be studied later). In Ref.7, because only one cooling rate was used, the relationship between these two kinds of pairs and the cooling rate was absent. For one cooling rate, our present results accord with that in these references. To our knowledge, the physical picture of these two kinds of pairs so far is still unclear, while the 1551, 1421, and 1422 pairs give an apparent physical signal. In fcc metals, an inter- stitialcy will produce 32 pairs of 1541, 16 pairs of 1431,16 pairs of 1311, 4 pairs of 1441, and 2 pairs of 1401, annihilate 56 pairs of 1421, and finally have an incre- ment of 14 pairs in total. From Figs.1(a)-1(d), we see that the 1541 and 1431 pairs in Al glasses are very much similar to those in the interstitialcy model. The1541 pairs are not regarded as having five-fold sym- metry, but the deviation of 1541 pairs from 1551 pairs is smaller than that of other kinds of pairs. According to the interstitialcy model, after the glass transition the ratio of the 1541 and 1431 pairs is almost a con- stant 2. In the range of low temperatures, the ratio of the 1541 pairs to the 1431 pairs is about 0.65, which is much smaller than 2. However two points should be noted. One is that the ratio of the sum of the 1541 and1551 pairs to the 1431 pairs is approximately equal to2 (the result in Ref.9 shows that this ratio for amor- phous Ni at 100K is 1.9, which agrees with our resultand the interstitialcy model), implying that the 1551 pairs come from the 1541 pairs. Using the recently de- veloped activation-relaxation technique, $^{11}$ we observe the transformation between 1541 pairs and 1551 pairs and other transformations among various pairs. How- ever, this direct comparison may be very rough. Based on our results and the interstitialcies model, the con- centration $c_{0}$ of frozen-in interstitialcies in amorphous states may not depend on the cooling rate. This seems reasonable, since the cooling rates used in our present work are very high. Though the 1541 and 1431 pairs do not show a dependence on the cooling rate, they may play an important role in the dependence of otherkinds of pairs on the cooling rate. The role of the 1541 and 1431 pairs is like a bridge which links two differ- ent kinds of pairs showing different dependence on the cooling rate. The results we obtained for the 1541 and1431 pair may be a test of the interstitialcies model for condensed states of fcc metals.

Next, we investigate the 1421 and 1422 pairs, shown in Figs.4(a) and 4(b). The relative number of these two kinds of pairs first increases and then decreases with decreasing temperature. The change in the 1421 pairs is nearly independent of the cooling rate, whereas the relative number of the 1422 pairs decreases with decreasing cooling rate in low temper- atures region. This may come from the fact that the crystal structure is fcc.

Jund et al. have studied the fraction $f_{5}$ of pen tagonal faces and the standard deviation $\sigma_{v}$ of the cell-volume distribution (a large value of $f_{5}$ is a sign of strong icosahedral local order characteristic of a glass phase, and $\sigma_{v}$ describes the local densityfluctuations). $^{10}$ They found that in the glass phase $\sigma_{v}$  increases with increasing quenching rate while $f_{5}$ re mains constant around 0.45 and the crystallization in- creases with increasing cooling rates. They think that the higher the local density fluctuations, the higher the probability of finding crystal germs. Therefore, the faster the quenching rate, the higher the proba- bility of finding local arrangements close to a crys- tal germ which leads to an increase in the crystalliza- tion with increasing cooling rates.
