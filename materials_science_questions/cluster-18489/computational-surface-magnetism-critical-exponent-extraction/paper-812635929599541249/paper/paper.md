# Domain structure near $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ in thick Ising ferromagnetic plates

W A Barker and G A Gehring
Department of Theoretical Physics, University of Oxford, 1 Keble Road, Oxford OX1 3NP, UK

Received 20 February 1985

Abstract. The temperature dependence of the domain structure of an Ising ferromagnetic slab is examined near to the critical temperature. It is found that the values of the domain size and surface magnetisation evaluated as the transition temperature is approached from below agree nicely with the values obtained from a theory of the dominant fluctuations in the paramagnetic phase. The theory is compared with available experimental data.

## 1. Introduction

A slab of Ising ferromagnet magnet has a dipolar energy that is proportional to the value of the square of the surface magnetisation multiplied by the surface domain length as shown by Kittel (1946). Thus there are two ways of reducing this energy. The system can form domains, which cost wall energy, or the value of the magnetisation can fall near the surface; this reduces its ordering energy (in this case too domains have to form because the dipolar interactions cause a non-zero derivative of $M$ in the $\hat{z}$ direction to induce non-zero derivatives in the perpendicular direction).

At low temperatures the ordering energy is large and domains form. In a thin slab these are the stripe domains discussed by Kittel (1946). In thicker slabs, Lifshitz (1944) and Kaczér (1964, 1970) have shown that it is more favourable to have surface or branch domains. These are sketched in figure 1. However, at temperatures close to the transition temperature it becomes more favourable for the system to have a reduced surface magnetisation.

In this paper we show how the domain patterns evolve with temperature close to $T_{\mathrm{c}}$. A theory for the dominant fluctuations in the paramagnetic phase is developed. It is found that the value of the domain size and the reduction of the surface magnetisation tend to the same limiting values from the two sides of the phase transition for both 'thin' and 'thick' slabs. The temperature dependence of the critical thickness for the formation of branch domains is also calculated.

We note that while demagnetising effects are intrinsic to ferromagnets, they do not always occur in ferroelectrics due to the possibility of free charges becoming trapped on the sample surfaces (J Lajzerowicz 1984, private communication).

The reason that we work on Ising (and anisotropic Heisenberg) systems rather than the isotropic (or cubic) Heisenberg model is because of the absence of closure domains and because of the interest of the anisotropic correlations above $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ described by Aharony and Fisher (1973).


![](./images/812635929599541249_1.jpg)

Figure 1. (a) A sketch of the stripe domain pattern showing $D$, the domain width. (b) A sketch of one-branch structure: the width of the branch is $\eta D$ on the surface and the branch extends a distance $l$ into the domain.

The temperature dependence of the domain size for the Kittel stripe structure has been calculated below $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ by Stauffer (1972) using Ising critical exponents and by Kent (1975) and Barker and Gehring (1983) using the classical exponents appropriate for the dipolar system. Gabey and Garel $(1984,1985)$ have investigated the behaviour of the many-branched state, close to and below $T_{\mathrm{c}}$. Above $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ Garel and Doniach (1982) and Barker and Gehring (1983) investigated the form of the dominant fluctuations that lead to the Kittel stripe phase for a thin slab and Wasilewski (1981) has studied the fluctuations for a very thick slab. In this paper we develop a theory that can interpolate between these regimes.

The importance of the dipolar interactions for a given magnet may be characterised by the number, $f$, defined as follows:

$$
f=\mu_{0} M_{0}^{2} / N k T_{\mathrm{c}}. \tag{1.1}
$$

Here $M_{0}$ is the saturation magnetisation at zero temperature and so $f$ represents approximately the fraction of the total ordering energy that comes from magnetic dipolar forces. Classical critical exponents apply for reduced temperature $t(\equiv\left|(T-T_{\mathrm{c}})/T_{\mathrm{c}}\right|) \ll f$ as shown by Aharony and Fisher (1973) (provided $f$ is small; otherwise the range of the exchange forces is also involved as shown by Chalker and Gehring 1979). The interesting region for domain formation is generally in the dipolar regime so classical critical theory is used predominantly in this paper. (The logarithmic corrections are neglected.)

The domain structure arises because of a balance between dipolar and exchange energy generally expressed as a demagnetisation energy and a domain wall energy.

There is a characteristic length in this problem, $\gamma$,

$$
\gamma(T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{w}}(T)/\mu_{0} M^{2}(T) \tag{1.2}
$$

where $\sigma_{\mathrm{w}}(T)$ is the wall energy (per unit area) and $\mu_{0} M^{2}(T)$ is the magnetostatic energy (per unit volume) both defined at a given temperature $T$.

In the low-temperature limit of an Ising magnet the width of the wall is given by the lattice parameter $a_{0}$ and the magnetisation dependence of $\gamma$ cancels out so $\gamma$ is independent of temperature; for nearest-neighbour interactions to $z$ nearest neighbours
$$
\gamma \simeq\left(a_{0} / z\right) / f.\qquad(1.3)
$$

The long-range dipole forces suppress the roughening transition, as shown by Lajzerowicz (1980) and Chalker (1981). Close to the transition where $\xi$, the correlation length, is large a continuum theory is valid. Bulaevskii and Ginzburg (1964) showed that the magnetisation profile in the wall is given by
$$
M(x T)=M(T) \tanh (x / 2 \xi(T))\qquad(1.4)
$$
where
$$
\xi=r_{0} / 2(3 t)^{1 / 2}\qquad(1.5)
$$

$$
r_{0}^{2} \equiv \sum_{n} r_{n}^{2} 2 J\left(r_{n}\right) / \sum_{n} J\left(r_{n}\right)\qquad(1.6)
$$

$$
t=\left|\left(T_{\mathrm{c}}-T\right) / T_{\mathrm{c}}\right|\qquad(1.7)
$$
and that
$$
\gamma(t)=\frac{8}{3}(t / f) \xi=4 r_{0} t^{1 / 2} / 3^{3 / 2} f \simeq 0.77 r_{0} t^{1 / 2} / f.\qquad(1.8a)
$$
(For a nearest-neighbour model $r_{0}$ is equal to the lattice constant $a_{0}$.)

The critical exponent for $\gamma$ in a short-range Ising model theory $(t \geqslant f)$ can be worked out using the temperature dependence of $\sigma_{\mathrm{w}}$ and $M^{2}$ following Stauffer (1972) (see also Jasnow (1984) for a detailed discussion of the critical dependence of $\sigma_{\mathrm{w}}$ ):
$$
\gamma(t)=\gamma_{0} t^{\gamma-v}.\qquad(1.8b)
$$

We shall use the explicit form (1.8a) throughout $\S \S 2,3$ but quote the power-law exponents that follow from relation (1.8b) in the summary of results given in $\S 4$.

However it is often favourable for anisotropic Heisenberg magnets to have Bloch walls at low temperatures. In this case $\gamma$ depends on the ratio of the anisotropy $K_{0}$ to the exchange $J$ and is again independent of temperature:
$$
\gamma \simeq 2 \pi\left(K_{0} / J\right)^{1 / 2} a_{0} / z f.\qquad(1.9)
$$

Bulaevskii and Ginzburg (1964) showed that as the temperature is raised there is a transition to a linear Ising wall (which Lajzerowicz and Niez (1979) have shown can be described as a second-order phase transition with chirality as the order parameter).

The transition occurs for
$$
t \simeq 2 K_{0} / J.\qquad(1.10)
$$

For temperatures below this value $\gamma$ is almost independent of temperature and given by equation (1.9), for smaller reduced temperatures $\gamma$ varies with temperature as given by equations (1.8).

In $\S 2$ we consider what happens to the domain walls as the transition temperature is approached from below and in $\S 3$ we consider the paramagnetic regime. A table summarising our results is given in $\S 4$ and the theory is compared with available experiments.

### 2. Theory in the ordered phase

In the low-temperature regime, where $\gamma$ is a constant, Lifshitz (1944) showed that the stripe domain pattern becomes unstable and a branch pattern occurs for thick samples. The theory of the multi-branched limit was considered by Kaczér (1964, 1970) and also by Privorotskii (1976). The domain patterns are sketched in figures 1 and 2.

![](./images/812635929599541249_2.jpg)

Figure 2. A sketch of Privorotskii's domain scheme up to the third level of branching, $N = 3$; the separation between branches on the surface is $d = D3^{-N}$ (here $d = D/27$). The depths of penetration $l_1, l_2, l_3$ are also shown.

Recently Gabay and Garel (1984) have extended the Privorotskii scheme to high temperatures where $\gamma \propto t^{1/2}$. Our analysis is based mainly on the Kaczér scheme. The critical thickness for a single branch to occur in the configuration shown in figure 1 was calculated by Kaczér (1964) from the following domain free energy:

$$
F = \mu M^2\left\{(D/\Delta)(1 - 4\eta + 6\eta^2) + \gamma L/D + \frac{8}{3}(\gamma D)^{1/2}\left[1 - (1 - \eta)^{3/2}\right]\right\} \tag{2.1}
$$

where

$$
\Delta = \pi^3/4 \times 1.05 \simeq 7.38. \tag{2.2}
$$

Here $M$ is the bulk magnetisation, $D$ is the domain size, $L$ is the slab thickness, $\eta$ is the fraction of a domain covered by a branch and $\gamma = \sigma_{\rm w}/\mu_0 M^2$ was defined in $\S 1$.

The first term in (2.1) is the dipolar energy due to the discontinuity in $M$ at the surface; it has the form derived by Kittel (1946) with a multiplicative factor depending on $\eta$; as one would expect this factor is a minimum for $\eta = \frac{1}{3}$, when $(1 - 4\eta + 6\eta^2)$ is also equal to $\frac{1}{3}$, because the periodicity of $M$ at the surface is now $D/3$. The second term is the contribution from the plane domain walls running across the slab. The third term is the wall energy of the branch domain; this has been found by first evaluating the shape

of the branch (its walls curve slightly) by an Euler-Lagrange minimisation, its depth \( l \) (shown in figure 1) is also chosen to minimise the energy. It is this minimum energy for a branch that is included in equation (2.1).

The size of the domains (in the bulk) is determined by minimising \( F \) with respect to \( D \). It is found that the minimum value of \( F \) corresponds to a non-zero \( \eta \) for \( L \geqslant L_k \) where
$$
L_{k}=\Delta^{3} \gamma \simeq 402 \gamma. \tag{2.3}
$$

This is the result of Kaczér (1964) (expressed in SI) for the stability limit for the stripe phase. At elevated temperatures where the continuum theory is valid we use equation (1.8) to obtain
$$
L_{k}=L_{k}^{0} t^{1 / 2} \tag{2.4a}
$$
where
$$
L_{k}=0.77 \Delta^{3} r_{0} / f=309 r_{0} / f. \tag{2.4b}
$$

Thus the critical thickness drops sharply near to the phase transition. This temperature dependence was also obtained by Gabay and Garel (1984) but with a different numerical coefficient which corresponded to the thickness when one branch with \( \eta=\frac{1}{3} \) is the most stable configuration (calculated in the Privorotskii scheme).

This leads to:
$$
L_{k}^{\prime}=7954 \gamma \tag{2.5}
$$
(their \( \delta=4 \pi \gamma \)).

That this differs so much from equation (2.3) is only partly due to the higher free energy of the Privorotskii model (a rough correction factor gives \( L_{k}^{\prime \prime} \simeq 3000 \gamma \)) but much more due to the fact that the free energy (2.1) takes rather similar values for \( \eta=0 \) (stripe phase) and \( \eta \simeq \frac{1}{3} \) (one branch) over a wide range of \( L \)-values. The value of \( L \) at which the free energy (2.1) for \( \eta=0 \) equals that for \( \eta=\frac{1}{3} \) is estimated from (2.1) to be \( L \simeq 12 \) \( L_k \simeq 5000\gamma \). (This is to be expected since it is known experimentally that a variety of domain structures are metastable.) The two results given by equations (2.3) and (2.5) can be used to define the range of the branch stripe transition.

The domain size is found for \( \eta=0 \):
$$
D(L)=(\Delta L \gamma)^{1 / 2} \cong 2.4\left(r_{0} L / f\right)^{1 / 2} t^{1 / 4}. \tag{2.6}
$$

This is the result for the stripe phase previously obtained by Stauffer (1972) for non-classical exponents for \( \gamma \) and Kent (1975).

The value of \( D \) at \( L_k \) is found by combining equations (2.6) and (2.3):
$$
D\left(L_{k}\right)=\Delta^{2} \gamma \simeq 4.2\left(r_{0} / f\right) t^{1 / 2}. \tag{2.7}
$$

We note that as the theory only makes sense for \( L \) and \( D \) very much bigger than \( r_0 \) this transition is expected to be observable only if \( f \ll 1 \), i.e. in magnets with high Curie temperatures. Barker (1984) has looked at the interaction energy between two walls and found that (as expected) the non-interacting picture is breaking down significantly when the separation between walls, \( D \), is comparable with their width, \( \xi \). This criterion was first used by Stauffer (1972).

\( t_k^* \) and \( L_k^* \) are defined by
$$
D\left(t=t_{k}^{*}, L=L_{k}^{*}\right)=\xi\left(t_{k}^{*}\right) \tag{2.8a}
$$

and
$$
L_{k}^{*}=L_{k}\left(t=t_{k}^{*}\right) \quad L_{k}^{\prime *}=L_{k}^{\prime}\left(t=t_{k}^{*}\right)
\tag{2.8b}
$$
and are easily evaluated from equations (1.5), (1.8a), (2.3), (2.5) and (2.7):
$$
t_{k}^{*} \simeq 7 \times 10^{-3} f
\tag{2.9a}
$$
$$
L_{k}^{*}=\Delta \xi\left(t^{*}\right) \simeq 26 r_{0} / f^{1 / 2}
\tag{2.9b}
$$
$$
L_{k}^{\prime *} \simeq 508 r_{0} / f^{1 / 2}.
\tag{2.9c}
$$

We note that $L_{k}^{*}$ differs from $L_{k}^{0}$ (equation (2.4b)) in depending on $f^{1 / 2}$ instead of $f$ as well as the numerical factor. Hence a large variation in $L_{k}$ with temperature is observable in systems $f \ll 1$ in agreement with our earlier remark that this transition can only be observed in systems with strong exchange interactions. For completeness we quote the results for thin slabs $L<L_{k}^{*}$ such that the stripe phase is stable at all temperatures (these results were derived and discussed in Barker and Gehring 1983). We define $D_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}$ and $t^{*}$ by
$$
D_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}=D_{\mathrm{s}}\left(t^{*}\right)=\xi\left(t^{*}\right)
\tag{2.10a}
$$
and find their values from equation (2.6):
$$
D_{\mathrm{s}}^{*} \simeq 1.18\left(r_{0}^{2} L / f\right)^{1 / 3}
\tag{2.10b}
$$
$$
t_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}=0.022\left(r_{0} f / L\right)^{2 / 3}.
\tag{2.10c}
$$

In thick slabs, $L \gg L_{k}$, there are many nests of branches; the branching occurs in a small fraction of the volume of the slab and hence the contribution from the domain walls associated with the branches tends to a finite limit; however, the dipolar energy tends to zero. Kaczér (1964) suggested that a reasonable form for the free energy is given by
$$
F / \mu_{0} M^{2}=\gamma L / D+\frac{8}{3}(\gamma D)^{1 / 2}.
\tag{2.11}
$$

Minimising this with respect to $D$ leads to an expression for $D$ in the thick-slab many-branch limit:
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{B}} & =\left(\frac{3}{4} L\right)^{2 / 3} \gamma^{1 / 3} \\
& =D_{\mathrm{B}}^{0} t^{1 / 6}
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.12a}
$$
$$
D_{\mathrm{B}}^{0}=\left[\left(3^{1 / 2} / 4\right) L^{2} r_{0} / f\right].
\tag{2.12c}
$$

Gabay and Garel (1984) used the Privorotskii (1976) domain scheme shown in figure 2 where the demagnetising energy is reduced by $3^{-N}$ for $N$ nested branches. They calculated the equilibrium value of $X^{2}\left(\equiv 3^{-N}\right)$ which is the reduction of the demagnetising energy caused by the branching. In our notation their result for large $N$ (small $X$) is as follows:
$$
X^{2} \cong 222(\gamma / L)^{2 / 3} \simeq 171\left(r_{0} / f L\right)^{2 / 3} t^{1 / 3}.
\tag{2.13}
$$

There are now two characteristic domain sizes in the problem: $D$, the domain size in the bulk, and $d=D X^{2}\left(=D 3^{-N}\right)$, the domain spacing at the surface:
$$
d=d_{0} t^{1 / 2}
\tag{2.14a}
$$
$$
d_{0} \simeq 141 r_{0} / f.
\tag{2.14b}
$$

The demagnetising energy is proportional to $M^2 d$ and Kaczér (1964) predicted from physical arguments that it should be independent of $L$ in the large-$L$ limit (it is small and Kaczér dropped it from the free energy as given by equation (2.11)) this is confirmed by equation (2.14b).

Let us define $\bar{d}$ and $\bar{t}$ as follows:

$$
\bar{d} = d(\bar{t}) \tag{2.15a}
$$

$$
\bar{t} \simeq 0.002f \quad \bar{d} = 6.4\ r_0/f^{1/2}. \tag{2.15b}
$$

The separation between branches takes its minimum value, $d$, on the surface. At reduced temperatures less than $\bar{t}$ this domain pattern is blurred out. (The branching in the interior of the slab may persist to smaller values of $t$ as the separations are larger; see figure 2.) The magnetisation is dropping smoothly near the surface. The demagnetisation energy was given in terms of $X$; we conjecture that this can now be reinterpreted in terms of a surface magnetisation, $M_{\text{s}}$:

$$
E_{\text{dm}} = \mu_0 M^2 D X^2/\Delta = \mu_0 M_{\text{s}}^2 D/\Delta \tag{2.16a}
$$

$$
M_{\text{s}} = X M. \tag{2.16b}
$$

We assume that $X$ is given by equation (2.12) and that for $t > \bar{t}$ $X^2(\equiv \frac{1}{3}^N)$ defines the branch index $N$ and for $t < \bar{t}$, $X$ defines the reduction in surface magnetisation above. (This definition of $M_{\text{s}}$ is different from Gabay and Garel (1984) who defined $m_{\text{s}} = X^2 M$.)

Justification for our hypothesis comes from comparing this theory with the results obtained for $T \geqslant T_{\text{c}}$ in which it is seen that $X$ interpreted in this way has meaning throughout the critical regime and also from comparison with the work of Tarasenko *et al* (1976). They looked at a continuum theory of a stripe domain pattern and also found a strong reduction of the surface magnetisation.

$$
M(Z = \pm L/2)/M(Z = 0) \equiv M_{\text{s}}/M \simeq D/L. \tag{2.16c}
$$

This is of the same form but smaller by a numerical factor of 18 than the value of $X$ obtained from equations (2.12a) and (2.13).

The theory finally breaks down at a reduced temperature $t^* (< \bar{t})$ defined by

$$
D_{\text{B}}^* = D_{\text{B}}(t^*) = \xi(t_{\text{B}}^*) \tag{2.17a}
$$

$$
t_{\text{B}}^* = r_0 f^{1/2}/16(2^{1/2})L \simeq 0.044(r_0 f^{1/2}/L \tag{2.17b}
$$

$$
D_{\text{B}}^* = (r_0 L/f^{1/2})^{1/4}[4(2)^{1/2}/3]^{1/2} \simeq 1.37(r_0 L/f^{1/2})^{1/2}. \tag{2.17c}
$$

Gabay and Garel also showed that $D_{\text{B}}^* \propto (r_0 L)^{1/2}$ in the thick-slab limit and evaluation of their numerical coefficient gives 1.10 instead of 1.37 in equation (2.17c) (this confirms the insensitivity of the results to the domain scheme chosen). Tarasenko *et al* (1976) also found a critical value for $D \simeq 1.8(r_0 L)^{1/2} f^{-1/4}$ associated with a stripe pattern but with a strongly reduced value of surface magnetisation (given by equation (2.16c)). However they predicted that the value of $D$ approaches the above value linearly with temperature which is not in agreement with our results.

One also finds the limiting value for the surface magnetisation reduction, $X^*(= M_{\text{s}}^*/M)$, from equations (2.12) and (2.17b):

$$
X^* \equiv X(t_{\text{B}}^*) \simeq 24.8(r_0/L)^{1/2} f^{-1/4}. \tag{2.18}
$$

### 3. Theory for the paramagnetic phase

The aim of this section is to calculate the form of the magnetisation fluctuation that first goes unstable as the temperature is reduced. The temperature at which this occurs is the transition temperature for the finite sample and is reduced below the bulk value because of the demagnetising energy in this geometry.

A theory of surface effects but without full dipole forces was considered by Kaganov and Omel'yanchuk (1972) and Tilley and Žekš (1984). Our theory is similar to that of Wasilewski (1981) except that we do not impose the constraint that the magnetisation vanishes on the surface.

Above the phase transition it is natural to expand the free energy in terms of oscillating functions of the magnetisation expecting that it is the long-wavelength oscillations that will become the critical modes that freeze in at $T_{\mathrm{c}}$.

Barker and Gehring (1983) used the complete set of functions obtained by expanding the $M(x y z)$ in terms of a full-range Fourier series in the $z$ direction $(-L / 2 \leqslant z \leqslant L / 2)$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
M(x y z)=\hat{z} \sum_{q_{x} q_{y}} \sum_{p=-\infty}^{\infty} \exp \left[\mathrm{i}\left(q_{x} x+q_{y} y\right)\right][C(q) \cos (2 p \pi z / L)+Y(q) \sin (2 p \pi z / L)] \\
q_{\perp}^{2}=q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2} \quad q_{z}=2 \pi p / L .
\end{gathered}
$$

The demagnetising field energy which depends on the $C(q)$ is not diagonal in this representation so it is not straightforward to find the values of the coefficients that minimise the free energy. The sine terms are zero on the surface $(z= \pm L / 2)$ but have odd parity. The branched solutions shown in figures 1 and 2 have even parity and a reduced average value of the magnetisation at the surface. This leads us to try a combination of even-parity modes of the form

$$
M(x, z)=M_{0}[\cos \theta+\sin \theta \cos (\pi z / L)] \cos (\pi x / D) .
$$

This is sketched in figure 3. We shall determine the value of $\theta$ such that the fluctuations of magnetisation are those that cost the least free energy and are thus the modes that will first condense at the transition temperature for the slab. For $\theta=0(\cos =1)$ we recover the uniform solution discussed by Garel and Doniach (1982) and Barker and Gehring (1983). For $\theta=\pi / 2(\sin \theta=1)$ we obtain the Wasilewski (1981) solution which we expect to represent the limiting behaviour for very thin and thick slabs respectively.

The contribution to the free energy of the whole slab for magnetisation fluctuations of the form of (3.2) is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\langle F\rangle / \mu_{0} M_{0}^{2}= & \left(A^{*}+D / 4 L \pi\right) \cos ^{2} \theta+\frac{1}{2}\left[A^{*}+(D / L)^{2}+\left(r_{0}^{2} / 6 f\right)(\pi / L)^{2}\right] \sin ^{2} \theta \\
& +(4 / \pi) A^{*} \cos \theta \sin \theta .
\end{aligned}
$$

![](./images/812635929599541249_3.jpg)

Figure 3. A sketch of the form of the fluctuation of the magnetisation assumed for the paramagnetic phase.

Here
$$
A^{*}=t / f+r_{0}^{2} \pi^{2} / 6 f D^{2}. \tag{3.4}
$$

The free energy has been divided by magnetostatic energy hence the presence of the factor $f^{-1}=N k T_{\mathrm{c}} / \mu_{0} M_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}$ in the terms that arise in part from the exchange interaction.

The origin of the terms is now detailed: $t/f$ is the inverse bulk susceptibility; $(r_{0}^{2}/6f)(\pi/D)^{2}$ and $(r_{0}^{2}/6f)(\pi/L)^{2}$ come from $|\partial M/\partial x|^{2}$ and $|\partial M/\partial z|^{2}$ respectively; $D/4L\pi$ is the demagnetising energy, which arises from the discontinuity of $M_{z}$ at the surface; $(D/L)^{2}$ comes from the non-analytic dipolar term $|q_{z}/|q||^{2}$. The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ in front of the $\sin^{2}\theta$ term arises from averaging $\cos^{2}(\pi z/L)$ over the slab. The last term in $\sin\theta$ $\cos\theta$ arises because the two parts of the trial function (3.2) are not orthogonal over the range $-L/2\leqslant z\leqslant L/2$.

We see that there are two terms that force the minimum of $F$ to be at non-zero $q_{x}(\equiv\pi/D)$. The demagnetising energy is reduced if the system breaks into domains: this is the term in $D/4L\pi$. Alternatively the system can eliminate its demagnetising energy by having zero surface magnetisation but then $q_{z}=\pi/L$ and the term in $(q_{z}/|q|)^{2}$ forces $q_{x}$ to be non-zero and larger than $q_{z}$.

In order to find the form of the fluctuations that cost less energy we minimise $F$ with respect to both $\theta$ and $D$ at the temperature corresponding to $A_{\mathrm{c}}^{*}$ at which this minimum energy fluctuation vanishes. This leads to the following equations for $\varphi\equiv\tan\theta$ and $D$:
$$
0=\left(-2 \frac{r_{0}^{2} \pi^{2}}{6 f D^{3}}+\frac{2 D}{L^{2}}\right) \varphi^{2}-\frac{8 r_{0}^{2} \pi}{6 D^{3}} \varphi+\left(-2 \frac{r_{0}^{2} \pi^{2}}{6 f D^{3}}+\frac{1}{4 L \pi}\right) \tag{3.5}
$$

$$
0=\varphi^{4} \frac{D}{\pi L}+\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{D}{2 L}-\frac{1}{8 \pi}\right)+\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{D}{L \pi}-\frac{1}{2 \pi^{2}}\right)+\varphi\left(\frac{D}{2 L}-\frac{1}{8 \pi}\right)-\frac{1}{2 \pi^{2}}=0. \tag{3.6}
$$

We first solve these in the two limiting cases of large and small $L$.

For large $L$, $\cos\theta$ will be small and $D$ approaches the value calculated by Wasilewski (1981):
$$
D \simeq\left(r_{0} L \pi / 6^{1 / 2} f\right)^{1 / 2}\left[1+(2 / \pi) \cos \theta+\mathrm{O}\left(\cos ^{2} \theta\right)\right] \tag{3.7}
$$

$$
\varphi=(L / 8 D)(1-4 D / L)+\mathrm{O}(D / L). \tag{3.8}
$$

We make contact with the branched theory below $T_{\mathrm{c}}$.

The value of $D$ equation (3.7) in the thick-slab limit, $\cos\theta\simeq0$, should be compared with the limiting value $D_{\mathrm{B}}^{*}$ given by equation (2.17c).

The functional dependence is the same and the numerical factors are close: 1.32 (Kaczér free energy), 1.10 (Gabay-Garel) and 1.13 for the high-temperature—Wasilewski—value.

The reduction in surface magnetisation is given from equations (3.2), (3.7) and (3.8):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{M(z=L / 2)}{M(z=0)}=\frac{\cos \theta}{\cos \theta+\sin \theta} & =\frac{1}{1+\varphi} & & (3.9 a) \\
& \simeq 8 D / L & & (3.9 b) \\
& \simeq 9.05\left(r_{0} / L\right)^{1 / 2} f^{-1 / 4}. & & (3.9 c)
\end{aligned}
$$

This should be compared with the results of the previous section. For $X^{*}$ the agreement with equation (2.18) is reasonably good.

In the limit of very small $L$ we find $\sin \theta \simeq 0$ and the demagnetising term is dominant. We find

$$
D=2 \pi\left(L r^{2} / 6 f\right)^{1 / 3}\left[1+(4 / 3 \pi) \sin \theta+\mathrm{O}\left(\sin ^{2} \theta\right) \ldots\right]. \tag{3.10}
$$

The limiting expression $D \propto L^{1 / 3}$ was obtained by Garel and Doniach (1982) and Barker and Gehring (1983) who also showed that it again approached the value of $D_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}$ calculated in the high-temperature limit of the stripe configuration.

The intermediate regime and the calculation of the high-temperature analogue of $L_{k}$ is harder to deal with. The value of $\varphi(=\tan \theta)$ for a thin slab is given by

$$
\varphi=\left(1 / 2 \pi^{2}\right) 1 /(D / 2 L-1 / 8 \pi). \tag{3.11}
$$

We see that this is never zero (for $L \neq 0$ ). This contrasts with the Kaczér condition for calculating $\eta$ at low temperatures (2.3). This is because at low temperatures it costs a finite energy to create another domain wall whereas above $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ it costs a vanishingly small energy to give the magnetisation a (vanishingly) small curvature in the $z$ direction.

The derivation of equations (3.10) and (3.11) involved neglecting terms $\varphi^{2} / 2 \pi^{2}$ in comparison with $\varphi D / 2 L$. This is valid (neglected terms $\leqslant \frac{1}{10}$ included terms) for

$$
L \leqslant L_{\mathrm{c}} \simeq 15 r_{0} / f^{1 / 2}. \tag{3.12}
$$

This should be a reasonable estimate for $L_{k}^{*}$ and actually agrees very well with (2.9b).

In a similar way we can look at the region of validity of the thick-slab theory. One finds that equation (3.7) is valid for

$$
L \geqslant 340 r_{0} / f^{1 / 2}. \tag{3.13}
$$

This is comparable with the result for $L_{k}^{\prime *}$ obtained from Gabay-Garel theory and given by (2.9c).

Another simple estimate can be obtained by solving equations (3.5) and (3.6) for $\varphi=1$. This corresponds to a value of $X^{2}=\frac{1}{4}$ instead of the one-branch result $X^{2}=\frac{1}{3}$. The value of $L_{k}$ obtained this way is

$$
L_{k}=220 r_{0} / f^{1 / 2}. \tag{3.14}
$$

This is also close to the Gabay and Garel (1984) value and represents a value for $L$ such that the system could be assumed to be just in the branched configuration. The phase diagram expected is sketched in figure 4.

The transition temperature in a finite slab is depressed below the bulk value $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ because of the extra energy required for the domain structure. Let the fractional tem-

## 4. Summary and experimental comparison

There is a gigantic literature on the observation of domain structures in uniaxial ferro- magnets at low temperatures, including some beautiful photographs of branched domains; for example, Hubert (1967). However the effects that we have been calculating require very precise temperature control close to the transition and there are not many results in this area. We have calculated the thermodynamically stable domain configuration ignoring the hysteresis effects which are so important at low temperatures.

However, there is some experimental evidence from the work of Szymczak *et al* (1979) and Molho and Porteseil (1983) that hysteresis effects vanish close to $T_{\mathrm{c}}$.

There are two types of prediction that we have made. One is the temperature dependence of various quantities associated with domain structure as the critical temperature is approached from below. The other is the limiting value that these quantities take, which we have argued is also characteristic of the dominant fluctuations that occur above $T_{\mathrm{c}}$. For ease of reference all the main results are summarised in table 1 (numerical coefficients are given to one figure).

The low-temperature theory is valid provided that the domain walls may be described by a continuum Ising theory. Thus $t$ must be sufficiently small that $\xi \gg$ lattice spacing $t \leqslant 10^{-2}$ or for an anisotropic Heisenberg model $t<2 K / J$ as shown by Bulaevskii and Ginzburg (1964) and Lajzerowicz and Niez (1979). The value of $t$ must be bigger than some critical value given in the table. Also our results have used classical exponents which require $t \ll f$; however the table does include a list of the exponents expected in the non-classical regime. (These have been obtained by looking at the $\gamma$-dependence of the various quantities and using (1.8b) instead of (1.8a). This has the effect of replacing $t^{1 / 2}$ by $t^{\gamma-\nu}$.)

Above the phase transition the correlations start to become anisotropic for reduced temperature $t \sim f$ and the dominant fluctuations start to depend on the sample shape for $\xi_{\|} \sim L$. The reduced temperature for which this occurs in the dipolar regime is given by

$$
\xi_{\|}\left(\tilde{t}^{* *}\right)=r_{0} f^{1 / 2} / 6^{1 / 2} \tilde{t}^{* *} \quad \xi_{\|}\left(t^{* *}\right)=L \quad \tilde{t}^{* *} \simeq\left(r_{0} / 6^{1 / 2} L\right) f^{1 / 2}. \tag{4.1}
$$

We note that this is approximately equal in magnitude (but opposite in sign) to $t_{\mathrm{B}}^{*}$ and $\simeq 1 / 6 t^{* *}$ given in equation (3.19). In the temperature range $-t^{* *}<\left(T-T_{\mathrm{c}}\right) / T_{\mathrm{c}}<\tilde{t}^{* *}$ the critical fluctuations are dominated by the dipolar effects and it is in this region that the oscillations develop that lead to domains. (In a system with extremely weak dipolar effects where Ising exponents are still valid when $\xi_{\|} \simeq L$ we find

$$
t_{\mathrm{I}}^{* *} \simeq\left(r_{0} / 6^{1 / 2} L\right)^{1 / \nu} \tag{4.2}
$$

this would require $f<t_{\mathrm{I}}^{* *}$.)

Experimentally the value of $f$ ranges between something of the order of one for low-temperature magnets and $10^{-6}$ for weak ferromagnets; some examples for Ising-like systems are $f \simeq 2$ for $\mathrm{Tb}(\mathrm{OH})_{3}, f \simeq 8 \times 10^{-2}$ for $\mathrm{Gd}, f \simeq 4 \times 10^{-3}$ for barium ferrite and $f \simeq 4 \times 10^{-6}$ for $\mathrm{YFeO}_{3}$.

Experiments to test the stripe phase predictions should be done on samples with $L<L_{k}^{*}$. Some values for quantities of interest are given in table 2 for $f=10^{-5}$ and $f=10^{-1}$, taking $L=L_{k}^{*}$ and $r_{0}=6 \times 10^{-10} \mathrm{~m}$.

We see that a sample with a small value of $f$ would be suitable for observing the temperature dependence of $D$ but not observing that it tends to a finite limit at $t_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}$ because $t_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}$ is too small. The branch phase is more accessible experimentally. The theory predicts that at low temperatures there are two periodicities $D$ and $d$ corresponding to the domain spacing in the bulk and in the surface branching and that these have different temperature dependences. At temperature $t=0.002 f$ the surface branching is washed out and the magnetisation is reduced smoothly near the surface. A sample with $1<f<10^{-1}$ would give accessible values for $\bar{t}$. It is possible that hysteresis effects set in more strongly below $\bar{t}$.

It is interesting to note the depth of the crystal penetrated by the branches. In the Privorotskii (1976) scheme the different levels of branches penetrate different amounts (this is shown in figure 2). Privorotskii shows that the deepest branch penetrates a distance $l \simeq 0.1 \mathrm{~L}$, which is independent of temperature, and the $n$th branch a distance $l_{n}$:
$$
l_{n} \simeq 2 L / 3^{3 n / 2}. \tag{4.3}
$$

The last branch to be formed penetrates a distance
$$
l_{n} \simeq 2 L X^{3} \simeq 36 d. \tag{4.4}
$$
(This is independent of $L$.) The aspect ratio, $d / l_{n}$, of the last branch to be formed is independent of temperature. In the range $\bar{t}>t>t_{\mathrm{B}}^{*}$ the theory predicts that the surface magnetisation is reduced by the factor $X$, which varies as $t^{1 / 6}$, and $D$ also varies as $t^{1 / 6}$ ($X \simeq 18 D / L$). The branches are being continuously blurred out from the surface.

The thickness must be large compared with $L_{k}^{0}$ (given in table 1): $L=K\left(r_{0} / f\right)$ where $K \geqslant 10^{4}$ say. The value of $t_{\mathrm{B}}^{*}$ is given by
$$
t_{\mathrm{B}}^{*}=f^{3 / 2} / 3 K. \tag{4.5}
$$

Hence a value of $f \simeq 1$ would give $t_{\mathrm{B}}^{*} \simeq 10^{-4}$ for a plate thickness $L \simeq 6 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~m}$ which seems possible. Systems with a smaller value of $f$ would have a $t_{\mathrm{B}}^{*}$ that is inaccessibly small.

There are three techniques that have been used near to $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ which are: bulk magnetisation measurements, neutron scattering and light scattering.

Molho and Porteseil (1983) have done some elegant magnetisation experiments on gadolinium. They measured the coercive field, remanent magnetisation and Barkhausen power for a toroidal sample of polycrystalline Gd for reduced temperatures $3 \times 10^{-4}<t<0.1$. They saw a change of behaviour at a value of $t$ close to that predicted for the transition between Ising and Bloch walls ($t_{0} \simeq 7.5 \times 10^{-3}$). They interpreted their results using the theory of Stauffer (1972) for the critical behaviour of stripe walls and got good agreement with the Ising theory. Further such experiments using the slab geometry would be very useful.

Neutron scattering techniques with polarised neutrons would seem the ideal tool for measuring the fluctuation spectrum and showing that the dipole interactions actually cause a transition to a state with $q \neq 0(=\pi / D \hat{x})$. Wasilewski (1984) has recently extended his theory to show that dipolar forces should have a dramatic effect on the neutron scattering linewidth.

Drabkin et al (1976) used polarisation analysis to study the onset of bulk magnetisation and the formation of domains in zero external field. They measured the net rotation and reduction of the polarisation of the neutron beam in nickel, a single crystal

of barium ferrite and polycrystalline yttrium-iron garnet. In all systems they found that they could not detect any domains until a temperature several degrees below that for which the ordered magnetism appears was reached. Rekveldt *et al* (1977) did see evidence of domains in a single-crystal iron disc using polarised neutrons above $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ but deduced that this was induced by an applied field together with a non-uniform demagnetising field.

Optical methods are of two main types. The transmitted beam can be focused and the domain pattern studied directly or the existence of domains inferred from light scattering. Szymczak *et al* (1979) measured the domain spacing directly for yttrium orthoferrite, $\mathrm{YFeO}_{3}$. This material has a small value of $f(\sim 4 \times 10^{-6})$ and should have passed through the stripe-to-branch transition close to $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ in their experiment. They did detect a very rapid reduction of the domain size close to $T_{\mathrm{c}}$.

Further experiments to check our predictions are required. However, we note that care must be taken to try to avoid hysteresis effects to make sure that it is the equilibrium domain configuration that is being measured.
