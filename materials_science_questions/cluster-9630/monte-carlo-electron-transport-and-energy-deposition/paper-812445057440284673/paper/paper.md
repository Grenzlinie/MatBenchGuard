We present measurements of the $\beta$-spectrum of $^{35}$S with silicon detectors using a virtually loss-free method originally developed for free neutron decay. We set stringent limits on the amplitude of heavy neutrino admixtures in the same system that so far had produced the most significant heavy neutrino signals. We found that the energy spectrum of electrons scattered from solid matter has the shape expected for a heavy neutrino kink.

## 1. Introduction

The precise measurement of the shape of nuclear $\beta$-spectra with solid state detectors is a notoriously difficult task. Possible sources of error are (back-) scattering and (self-) absorption processes which occur whenever the fast electrons interact with matter.

During the eighties a method of virtually loss-free $\beta$-spectroscopy had been developed, mainly for studies of the $\beta$-decay of free neutrons [1]. This method allows to suppress to a high degree the loss mechanisms that usually plague $\beta$-spectroscopy. During the past two years we have extended this method to the long-standing problem [2,3] of the existence of a 17 keV massive neutrino branch seen in the decay spectra of $^3$H, $^{14}$C, $^{35}$S, $^{55}$Fe, $^{63}$Ni, and $^{71}$Ge, see refs. [4,5] for references.

Our aim was to study carefully the system that so far had produced the most pronounced 17 keV neutrino admixture effect, namely $^{35}$S $\beta$-decay monitored with silicon detectors. Experiments using a source-aperture-detector configuration lined up within a vacuum chamber had shown a heavy neutrino admixture of amplitude $\sin^2\theta=(8.4\pm0.6\pm0.5)\times10^{-3}$ [3]. On the other hand, experiments using magnetic $\beta$-spectrometers found no heavy neutrino effect on the $1\times10^{-3}$ level [4]. Therefore a further aim of our study was to perform a direct test whether "heavy neutrino" signals can artificially be produced by deliberately switching off some loss-suppression mechanism of our apparatus. A first report of this work was given in ref. [6].

## 2. Experimental method

For our investigation of the 17 keV neutrino problem we used not the full size neutron decay spectrometer PERKEO but a smaller version set up for test purposes (PERKINO). The principle of the apparatus is the following: a small $^{35}$S $\beta$-source (of negligible thickness) is placed in the center of a long solenoidal

magnet. The decay electrons are guided by the magnetic field to either one of two silicon detectors installed near the ends of the solenoid, as shown schematically in fig. 1. Hence the detector solid angle of acceptance is truly $4 \pi$, and no kind of edge effects exists.

About one third of the electrons impinging on one of the detectors are backscattered from the detector and deposit only part of their energy in it. This is shown in the conversion electron spectrum in the middle of fig. 1: backscatter events are evenly distributed over all energies below the K, L, M, ... conversion lines. However, in our apparatus an electron backscattered from one detector will be guided along a magnetic field line to the other detector and will, a few nanoseconds later, deposit its energy there. For some electrons which are backscattered near the glancing angle the fringe field of the solenoid acts as a magnetic mirror and projects the electron back onto the same detector. So all electrons, including the conversion electrons originating from secondary (Auger-, shake-off, knock-on etc.) processes are trapped by the magnetic field between the two detectors and can lose energy only to them. When, for each event, the total signal amplitudes from both detectors are added electronically, then the pure line spectrum shown at the bottom of fig. 1 is recovered, with line width increased by a factor $\sqrt{2}$.

A recent experiment [7], somewhat related to ours, achieved a partial suppression of electron backscattering by solely using the magnetic mirror effect in the fringe field of the solenoid. No heavy neutrino signal was seen by ref. [6], the result for its amplitude being $\sin ^{2} \theta=(-0.4 \pm 0.8 \pm 0.8) \times 10^{-3}$. Meanwhile the authors of ref. [3] have reanalysed their data and find [5] that, after correction for intermediate electron scattering on a baffle, the data are consistent with no heavy neutrino ($\sin ^{2} \theta<3.5 \times 10^{-3}$, $90 \% \mathrm{CL}$, for $m_{\mathrm{v}}=17 \mathrm{keV} / c^{2}$).

### 3. Instrumental details

The 7T superconducting solenoid is uniformly wound, 30 cm long, and 6.2 cm in diameter. The $^{35}$S source, which has a $\beta$ end point energy of 167 keV and a halflife of 87.5 d, is prepared from a carrier-free solution of sodium sulphate (Du Pont). A drop-let of this solution of 0.5 mm diameter and about 1 kBq activity is dried onto the center of the carbon foil. The mass density of the foil is $5\ \mu\mathrm{g/cm^2}$, corresponding to 160 monolayers. The foil is suspended in an aluminium orifice with 15 mm opening placed in the center of the solenoid. The electron energy loss within the source does not surpass several tens of electron volts. This had been checked in earlier measurements [1] on similar sources with the magnetic electron spectrometer BILL of the Institute Laue-Langevin, which has a resolution of 30 eV at 300 keV. The electrons in the 7 T field have typical radii of gyration of 0.2 mm. The time-of-flight distribution of the electrons is limited to a few tens of nanoseconds due to the gradients of the magnetic field, cf. ref. [1].

The silicon detectors are of the surface barrier type, with an active area of $300\ \mathrm{mm^2}$. In the first run of the experiment detectors with a gold window of 170 nm silicon equivalent thickness and an energy resolution of 2 keV were used (Silena), and in the second run with an aluminium window of 50 nm silicon equivalent and 3 keV resolution (Intertechnique). The detectors are cooled to a temperature of 120 K. For the given positions of the detectors in the fringe field of the magnet, the maximum length of the helicoidal paths of the electrons in the dead layer of the detector is twice that seen at normal incidence. For a detailed evaluation of other possible sources of error see ref. [1].

Data taking is done with the usual NIM-CAMAC-PC chain. The analogue signals from both detectors are amplified in an ORTEC 572 module in the pile-up reject mode with a shaping time of $1\ \mu\mathrm{s}$, and summed with an ORTEC 533 module. Both the single and the sum signals then are converted in a Silena 4418 peak sensing gated ADC.

The spectrometer is calibrated with conversion electron sources $^{109}\mathrm{Cd}$ ($E_\gamma=88.03$ keV), $^{139}\mathrm{Ce}$ (165.85 keV), $^{57}\mathrm{Co}$ (14.41, 122.06, and 136.47 keV), and $^{114\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{In}$ (190.27 keV), prepared in the same way as the $^{35}$S source. The sources are installed near the center of the solenoid and are, when not used, hidden behind lead shields, with no direct line of sight to the detectors. For measurement the source in question is moved remotely to the center of the solenoid. This allows calibration measurements at any time during the run under identical conditions. The detector response function is derived from the shapes of the $^{139}\mathrm{Ce}$ and the $^{114\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{In}$ K-conversion lines. These lines are slightly asymmetric due to external bremsstrahlung losses and dead layer losses of the electrons in the detectors, and are modelled by a gaussian with an exponential lower tail. Due to this asymmetry, the central weight of the $^{114\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{In}$ K-conversion line at $E_\mathrm{K}=162.33$ keV is shifted by $-0.9$ keV, and that of the $^{139}\mathrm{Ce}$ line at $E_\mathrm{K}=126.93$ keV is shifted by $-1.3$ keV. Due to the $2\times2\pi$ acceptance of the detectors Auger electrons emitted during the transition are seen either at their true (low) energies (cf. fig. 1), or appear at the sum of the conversion and Auger energies.

For the theoretical $^{35}$S $\beta$-spectrum we took the unscreened Fermi function from Behrens and Bühring [8]. The ratio of unscreened to screened Fermi function was provided by Bühring [9], the radiative corrections and the shape function are from Sirlin [10] and Calaprice and Millener [11], respectively. The electron-antineutrino is assumed to be a linear combination of a massless and a massive branch. For a nonzero amplitude of the massive branch one expects a kink in the allowed $\beta$-spectrum at an energy lower by $m_\nu c^2$ than the endpoint energy.

### 4. Measurements with backscatter-suppression

In our first run we analyzed the measured $^{35}$S $\beta$-spectrum between 130 keV and the endpoint energy, after correction for pile-up events. The shape of the pile-up spectrum was generated by an iterative procedure of convolving the data spectrum with itself [3] and extrapolated to the region below the endpoint energy. Free fit parameters of the theoretical $^{35}$S $\beta$-spectrum are the heavy neutrino mass $m_\nu$, its mixing amplitude $\sin^2\theta$, the endpoint energy $E_\mathrm{max}$, and the overall intensity. The background rate of 1 count/s ($10^{-3}$ of the total rate) and its spectrum was separately measured. A minimum $\chi^2=155.1$ for 161 data points is found for the fit parameters $m_\nu=6\ \mathrm{keV/c^2}$, $\sin^2\theta=1\%$, and $E_\mathrm{max}=167.35$ keV.

Fig. 2 shows a contour plot of $\chi^{2}$ in dependence of $m_{v}$ and $\sin ^{2} \theta$. For each point in the plane of fig. 2 the remaining parameters (endpoint energy and overall intensity) are optimized anew. The position of our $\chi^{2}$ minimum is indicated in fig. 2, as well as that found in ref. [3]. Our result is consistent with the standard prediction $m_{v}=0$, or $\sin ^{2} \theta=0$, which has $\chi^{2}=157.7$. A one-sigma contour in this two dimensional plot re- quires [12] a change in $\chi^{2}$ by $\Delta \chi^{2}=2.30$. An upper limit of $\sin ^{2} \theta<1.5 \times 10^{-3}(90 \% CL)$ is obtained for the amplitude of a heavy neutrino with mass between17 keV and 32 keV. Systematic (one sigma) errors in the theoretical spectrum $(0.3 \times 10^{-3})$ , the detector response function $(0.9 \times 10^{-3})$ and in the extrapola tion of the pile-up spectrum to lower energies(0.3x10-3), when added quadratically, increase this limit to $\sin ^{2} \theta<2.2 \times 10^{-3}(90 \% CL)$ .

In order to improve on this result we did a second, high statistics measurement of about equal duration but with 4.3 times more events. We found, however, that for this second run, due to the higher event rate, the uncertainties in the pile-up extrapolation were so large that the evaluation method used in the first run could not be applied. We therefore followed a differ- ent strategy, which had in similar form already been used by Schreckenbach et al. in an early search for neutrino kinks [13]: no a priori assumption on the shape of the spectrum is made, but a third order po- lynomial is fitted to the data in the interval between the location of the presumed kink and the endpoint energy. The procedure of successive fitting mutually orthogonal polynomials is used, up to the order of the polynomial which still gives a significant decrease of x2. Then this baseline is extrapolated to lower ener- gies, and a kink is added with amplitude $\sin ^{2} \theta$ as the only remaining fit parameter. When no massive neu- trino exists then this extrapolation must smoothly follow the data over a certain energy interval to be determined in the evaluation. Applied to our secondrun this procedure gives $\sin ^{2} \theta<3.5 \times 10^{-3}$ for $m_{v}=17$  $keV$ , with $90 CL$ and $\chi^{2}=0.95 / DOF$ . This limit, de rived from the second, high statistics run, is worse than the limit from the first run and reflects the insta- bilities of the extrapolation method used. The error also includes the error in the definition of the base- line. Residuals from the fit are shown in fig. $3 c$ .

As the data evaluation procedure and hence the er- ror sources are completely different in both runs, we combine both results and find $\sin ^{2} \theta<1.9 \times 10^{-3}$ for a heavy neutrino admixture with mass $m_{v} \sim 17 keV$ , with $90 \%$ confidence limit.

## 5. Measurements with scattered electrons
We next tried to find out what happens to the en- ergy spectrum of electrons that are allowed to scatter on some surrounding material and then reach the de- tector. To this end we separately measured in our de- tector 1 the energy spectrum of the electrons back- scattered from detector 2, the latter serving as the(active) scatter medium. Due to magnetic effects in the fringe field both the angle of incidence of the elec- trons on the scatterer (detector 2) as well as the angleof reemission are limited by some critical angle $\theta_{c}$ (i.e., angles near the glancing angle are excluded). The size of $\theta_{c}$ is determined by the ratio of the magnetic field $B$ at the position of detector 2 to the field $B_{0}$ atthe center of the solenoid: $\sin \theta_{c}=\sqrt{B / B_{0}}$ , with $\theta_{c}=0$  along the axis of the solenoid.

Fig. 3a shows the measured spectra of initial and scattered electrons, with $\theta_{c} \approx 60^{\circ}$ . We see that the spectrum after scattering has an effective endpoint energy well below and clearly separated from $E_{ max }$ , and could be mistaken for a "neutrino kink". A fit of a massive neutrino spectrum to the sum of the initial and the scattered electron spectrum gives a "neutrino mass" of $m_{v}=33 keV$ , with $\chi^{2}=0.81 / DOF$ (see re siduals from a fit in fig. 3d). The appearance of an energy gap in the spectrum of scattered electrons is somewhat surprising. It means that a minimum energy must be deposited before the electrons can leave the scatterer. This can be expected only when two conditions are met: Firstly, when backscattering under glancing angle is excluded or negligible (which is the case in our experiment); secondly, when backscattering is predominantly due to multiple small angle scattering, and not to single large angle scattering. To check this problem we have done a Monte Carlo simulation of this problem. The result, shown in fig. 3b, agrees well with our measurement. The energy gap found in this simulation is 27 keV. In the literature we found only one early measurement that was able to detect this gap, cf. figs. 9 and 10 in ref. [14].

It appears to us that we succeeded in isolating experimentally the very same effect that was proposed in ref. [5] to resolve the " 17 keV conundrum". In ref. [5] intermediate electron scattering from the "flat" surface of a 0.8 mm thick aluminium baffle located between the source and the detector was made responsible for the appearance of a "neutrino kink". There, according to their fig. 1, typical angles of incoming and outgoing electrons were around $65^{\circ}$, measured from the normal of this surface. In our experiment, at first sight, the scattering configuration looks quite different, but it is not, because the way the electrons are transported from the source to the scatterer (our detector 2) and then to the detector (our detector 1) is utterly irrelevant. The angular distribution of electrons impinging onto our scatterer is due to a (magneto-optical) caustic, with a pole at $\theta_{\mathrm{c}}=60^{\circ}$, and therefore has a rather narrow effective width, similar as in ref. [5].

However, we found that the way this scattering leads to a "kink" in the electron spectrum is different from that proposed in ref. [5]. In ref. [5] it was assumed that the spectrum of the electrons after scattering is continuous all the way up to $E_{\max }$, and therefore, "the intermediate scattering contributions cannot produce a "kink" per se", but only give some twist to the data. Our data, on the other hand, show that real sharp kinks are generated whenever electrons are scattered in some restricted geometry.

What must be done to move this kink to $\Delta E=17$


keV? The position of the kink depends on the angle of incidence of the electrons. Our Monte Carlo data indicate that the kink appears at $\Delta E \approx 17$ keV when $\theta_{\mathrm{c}} \approx 70^{\circ}-75^{\circ}$ for our apparatus. Additional measurements show a similar trend. More work is in progress.

## 6. Conclusion

We used a virtually loss-free method of $\beta$-spectroscopy in the same source-detector combination for which the most significant heavy neutrino signals had been reported, and found no evidence for a 17 keV neutrino of amplitude above $1.9 \times 10^{-3}$ (90% CL). Our experiment showed that electrons scattered in a restricted geometry can produce very pronounced heavy neutrino kinks as an artifact. The position of the kink can be changed with the angle of incidence on the scatterer.
