Hitachi Research Laboratory, Hitachi, Ltd., 7-1-1 Omika-cho, Hitachi, Ibaraki 319-12, Japan
¹Instrument Division, Hitachi, Ltd., 882 Ichige, Hitachinaka, Ibaraki 312, Japan

(Received August 29, 1997; accepted for publication November 6, 1997)

By utilizing the focused ion beam (FIB) technique, we evaluated signal intensity ratios of analyzed areas and the matrix for energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy with a transmission electron microscope (TEM-EDX). In the case of conventional FIB samples (H-shape), electrons scattered from the thin film area irradiate the sidewalls of the matrix (Si substrate) under the thin film position and they generate a large number of X-rays. These matrix signals are reduced by making samples as U-shaped and removing the underlying sidewalls. The final matrix (Si) X-ray signal is reduced by 90% compared with the conventional H-shaped samples.

KEYWORDS: FIB, ULSI, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, TEM

### 1. Introduction

As size in semiconductor design rules decreases and integrated circuit density increases, micro-analysis of particular ULSI areas becomes more and more important today. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) with a transmission electron microscope (TEM) is one of the most frequently utilized technique for micro-analysis. There are three advantages: (a) it has spacial resolution better than one nanometer, (b) most elements can be detected except for light ones (C and B), and (c) quantitative analysis is simple and easy. For precise EDX analysis, the specimen must be thinned to less than 200 nm in thickness to decrease electron scattering within it. Recently, the focused ion beam (FIB) technique has been frequently used for TEM sample preparation, because it has the following advantages:¹⁻² (a) it can focus ion beams on exact points and particular areas can be thinned and (b) the sample thickness is uniform and the observation area is large enough for practical use. One serious problem, however, occurs if the specimen is analyzed by the EDX technique; a large number of X-rays come from a matrix (substrate) and they significantly influence quantitative micro-analysis. Yamaguchi *et al.*³ reported that in TEM-EDX spectra both Ga and As signals appeared when the TEM electron probe was focused on an area which did not contain Ga atoms. The Ga X-rays are suspected to come from the substrate material, however, the precise mechanism has not been clarified yet.

We compared EDX signal ratios of X-rays from analyzed areas and the matrix of different FIB samples. We found that optimization of FIB sample shape can improve the precision of EDX qualitative analysis.

### 2. Experimental

The procedure for specimen preparation is schematically drawn in Fig. 1. Test pieces of ULSI devices were cut to about $30\ \mu\text{m}$ in thickness with a dicing saw [Fig. 1(a)] and ion beams were used to cut particular areas into less than $0.2\ \mu\text{m}$ thickness [Fig. 1(b)]. A conventional sample shape is schematically illustrated in Fig. 2 and dimensions are given in Table I ($T$: dicing thickness, $D$ and $W$: depth and width of a removed area, $U$ and $L$: upper and lower heights from the thin film area, $\alpha$: tangential angle between $D$ and $L$). Several samples were prepared with different $\alpha$ values to investigate the effect of side walls. TEM (Hitachi HF-2000) attached to a EDX system (Kevex model 3600) was used at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV (take off angle of X-rays: $68^\circ$). Probe diameter of the electron beam was between 20 and 50 nm. The beam current was measured by a fluorescent screen, and it was controlled between 100 and 400 pA. The spectra were obtained at various tilt angles of the specimen to the detector, from $-10^\circ$ to $+10^\circ$, which correspond to the inclined foil region upward or downward, respectively. The intensity of X-rays was normalized by a probe current.

### 3. Results

Figure 3 shows a scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of a conventional FIB specimen. The original piece is steeply cut from both upper and lower sides, and the center region is left to be uniformly thin. Figure 4 shows a TEM image of an analyzed area, where W and Ti/TiN layers are observed. Figure 5 shows an X-ray spectrum which was acquired when the electron probe was focused on the Ti region. Because the intensity of the Si–K line is comparable to that of the Ti–K line, qualitative analysis of Ti provides a value (54 at.%) much lower than 100 at.%. Figure 6 shows the effect of specimen tilt angles on Si X-ray intensity. The electron beam was focused on either the Ti region (square) or W region (circle). The X-ray intensities of Si increase with increasing tilt angle. The W region causes larger Si X-rays than the Ti region. Because the chemical composition is not changed by the tilting angle, the Si peak is thought to come from the Si substrate. Figure 7 shows Si X-ray intensities of three different specimens. The intensity increases as the angle $\alpha$ (defined in Fig. 1) is increased. When the instrumental background of the X-ray intensity was measured by focusing the TEM electron probe apart from the specimen, only a few counts per 100 s were detected.

### 4. Discussion

In order to explain the different X-ray sources, five mechanisms are schematically illustrated in Fig. 8: (a) forward scattered electrons, (b) back scattered electrons, (c) secondary electrons, (d) characteristic X-rays from the analyzed area (true signal), (e) instrumental background of X-rays. Because the background count was sufficiently small, the background X-rays (e) can be neglected. Next, we evaluate the effect of (a), (b) and (c). If we assume that forward scattered electrons mainly cause the noise of Si X-rays, the intensity dependence upon the specimen tilt angle is qualitatively explained. Figure 9 shows schematically how the scattered electrons irradiate the bulk area of a specimen. By tilting the specimen in the plus (minus) direction as shown in (c) (in (a)), the bulk sidewall area under the analyzed area approaches (departs from) the scattered electrons. When sidewalls are irradiated by more (fewer) electrons, more (fewer) X-rays are generated from the bulk Si. This explains the tilt angle dependency shown in Figs. 6 and 7. Figure 6 also implies that the heavier element, W causes larger Si peaks than Ti. ### 5. Conclusions

By utilizing the FIB technique, we evaluated signal intensity ratios of analyzed areas and the matrix for energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy with TEM. In the case of conventional FIB samples (H-shape), tilting the sample in a particular direction caused more extra Si bulk signals. Electrons scattered from the analyzed thin film area hit underlying sidewalls and generated a large number of X-rays. Most of these noise X-rays could be removed by making FIB samples U-shaped.
