# Simulation Study of Auger Electron Emission Features in Tip–Sample Electric Field Region for Scanning Probe Electron Energy Spectrometer

WenJie Liu, ChunKai Xu*, YongGang Li¹, ZeJun Ding¹, KeZun Xu, and XiangJun Chen

Hefei National Lab for Physical Science at Microscale and Department of Modern Physics,
University of Science and Technology, Hefei 230026, China
¹Hefei National Lab for Physical Sciences at Microscale and Department of Physics,
University of Science and Technology, Hefei 230026, China

Received April 29, 2009; accepted September 7, 2009; published online December 21, 2009

A simulation study of the emission features of the Auger electrons in the electric field region localized within the cylindrical tip shield of a scanning probe electron energy spectrometer (SPEES) are reported. By taking into account synthetically the influence of every detailed factor, the relative intensity distributions for the outgoing Auger electrons are calculated by simulation. The results show that only those Auger electrons emitted from an annulus on sample surface with a width less than its radius can escape from the tip–sample electric field region. This provides a possible way for SPEES to improve the spatial resolution beyond the size of near-field emission electron beam from the tip. The distribution of the emission direction and the vertical height of the outgoing Auger electrons at the edge of the electric field have also been studied, revealing that most of the outgoing Auger electrons are parallel to the sample surface, and their vertical heights are constrained to a small range. These results are significant for improving the detection efficiency, the energy and spatial resolutions of the SPEES, and also provide instructive data for the designing of the tip shield and the electronic optical system in the tip–sample region for an SPEES.

## 1. Introduction

Since the invention of the scanning tunneling microscope (STM),¹ great progress has been achieved in imaging and manipulating single atom and molecule.²⁻⁸ However, the spectroscopic information provided by STM is still limited, and it is difficult for STM to identify a specific atom on solid surface. The combination of the scanning probe technique and the electron energy spectroscopy technique is a promising way to resolve this problem. Great efforts have been made by several groups, such as Miyatake et al.,⁹¹⁰ Tomitori et al.,¹¹⁻¹⁴ Palmer et al.,¹⁵⁻¹⁹ and our group.²⁰⁻²² In these studies, the STM tip is used as a field emission electron source and the elemental identification can be achieved by measuring the energy spectra of the electrons backscattered from the sample surface stimulated by the field emission electrons from the STM tip. However, a strong electric field will be introduced between the STM tip and the sample surface, which will severely suppress the emitted electrons from the sample surface and make them difficult to be detected. The work of both Miyatake et al.⁹¹⁰ and Tomitori et al.¹¹⁻¹⁴ have approved that an unscreened STM tip is inapplicable for the measurement of the Auger electron energy spectra (AES) as the relative low energy of Auger electrons compared to the biasing voltage applied to the tip–sample. A tip shield is thus necessary for these kinds of experiments.

Therefore it is important to study how the backscattered electrons (or Auger electrons) move through the electric field of the tip–sample region. A correlative simulation has been carried out by Mizuno et al.²³ in their development of a low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) apparatus with the STM tip. They have proved that under the suppression of the tip bias, the secondary electrons ultimately emitted paralleled to the sample surface, which has also been confirmed by Palmer et al.¹⁵ in their work of a scanning probe energy-loss spectrometer (SPELS). The simulation of Mizuno et al. has also shown that it is helpful for electrons' outgoing by adding a bias voltage to the tip shield. However, Mizuno et al. only simulated the transmission process of the electrons from the sample surface to the edge of the electric field and the simulation is based on low tip bias and a fixed kinetic energy of the elastically scattered electron from the sample surface. Palmer et al.¹⁶ also processed a kind of simulation which concerned the current intensity of incident beam at sample surface from the STM tip. They found that the width of the current density profile is of the same order as the tip–sample separation. In order to obtain the outgoing current density, Palmer et al. made an assumption that the reflectivity for all electron scattering events from the surface was 1%. And then an annulus around the centre of the tip was presented from which the reflected electrons could be collected. The width of the annulus was 10 nm which was deemed to be a more likely indication of the spatial resolution achievable in SPELS. However in their simulation, only elastic electrons were taken into account and it is surely not applicable for Auger electrons.

No attempt has been made to carry out a complete simulation from the electron emission of the STM tip to the electron outgoing from the sample surface to the edge of the electric field. Besides, the experiments of the four groups above-mentioned are all restricted to poor detection efficiency. It is important to study the whole flying characteristics of Auger electrons, which will be instructive for the operation and modification of the scanning probe electron energy spectrometer (SPEES) apparatus.

In this paper, a complete simulation study from the electron emission of the STM tip to the Auger electron outgoing off the edge of the electric field in the SPEES has been reported. The results are significant for improving the detection efficiency, the energy resolution and the spatial resolution of the SPEES, and also provide instructive information for the designing of the tip shield and the electronic optical system in the tip–sample region.

## 2. Theoretical Simulation

In this work, the outgoing of Au NOO Auger electrons stimulated by field emission electrons from the STM tip biased $-132\ \text{V}$ has been investigated by simulation. Figure 1 shows the sketch of the geometrical configuration of the simulation region, where $r_0$ is the distance between the emission position of the Auger electron on sample surface and the sample center, $\theta'$ is the angle of the incident electron with sample surface, $\theta_0$ is the elevation angle of the outgoing Auger electrons on sample surface, and $\theta$ and $h$ are the elevation angle and the vertical height of the Auger electrons at the edge of the electric field.

To calculate the intensity distribution of the outgoing Auger electrons, all physical processes must be considered, including the field emission of the STM tip, the ionization and deexcitation of the sample (Au) atoms, the transmission of the Auger electrons from inside to surface in the sample and the transmission from the sample surface to the edge of the electric field.

### 2.1 The field emission of the STM tip
The field emission characteristics of a simulated chemically etched tungsten tip are determined by analyzing the intensity distribution of the field emission current density on the sample surface. The field emission of electrons from the tip is governed by the Fowler-Nordheim equation, $^{24-26)}$ and the current intensity of the field emission electrons at tip surface is expressed as

$$
J_{i}=\frac{C_{1} \varepsilon^{2}}{\phi t^{2}(\psi)} \exp \left[\frac{-C_{2} \phi^{3 / 2} v(\psi)}{\varepsilon}\right],
\tag{1}
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the electric field intensity; $\phi$ is the work function of the tip material; $C_1$ and $C_2$ are constants which are $1.54 \times 10^{-6}$ and $6.83 \times 10^{7}$ respectively; $\psi=3.79 \times 10^{-4} \varepsilon^{1 / 2} / \phi$, which is the Schottky lowering of the work-function barrier; $v(\psi)$ and $t(\psi)$ are slowly varying functions which have been computed by Burgess et al. $^{25)}$ and a close approximation can be used over the operating range of most cathodes the following values: $^{26)} t(\psi)=1.1$ and $v(\psi)=0.966-\psi^{2}$. The unit of the current density $J_i$ is $\text{A/cm}^2$.

In the present work, the field emission electrons are driven and accelerated by the tip bias and collide with sample atoms as incident electrons. The current intensity distribution of the incident beam on sample surface $J_S$ is then determined by simulating the electron traces from the tip to the sample surface, while every trace has a weight equal to its corresponding $J_i$. Figure 2 illustrates the normalized distribution of $J_S$ (solid squares) with the distance $r_0$ from the center, while the tip is biased $-132\ \text{V}$ and the tip-sample separation is $50\ \mu\text{m}$. By fitting $J_S$ distribution with Gaussian function (solid line), FWHM was determined to be $15.5\ \mu\text{m}$.

### 2.2 The ionization of the sample atoms
For Auger electron emission, the inner shell of sample atoms should be ionized. The ionization probability $P_i$ is proportional to the ionization cross-section of the inner orbit $Q_{\text{w}}$ in the form of $P_{i}=N d Q_{\text{w}}$, where $N$ is the atomicity density, $d$ is the depth of the ionized atomic layer. $Q_{\text{w}}$ can be expressed in the following empirical equation: $^{27)}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{w}}= & \frac{6.51 \times 10^{-14} b_{\mathrm{w}}}{E_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{U}\right)\left(\frac{U-1}{U+1}\right)^{3 / 2} \\
& \times\left[1+\frac{2}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{2 U}\right) \ln (2.7+\sqrt{U-1})\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
$$

where $b_{\text{w}}$ is the full number of the electrons of the ionized major shell, $E_{\text{w}}$ is the ionization energy of the orbit, and $U$ is the ratio of the incident energy to the ionization energy, i.e., $E_{\text{p}}/E_{\text{w}}$, where $E_{\text{p}}$ is the incident energy. For the Au NOO Auger process simulated in this work, $b_{\text{w}}=32$, $E_{\text{w}}=88\ \text{eV}$, $E_{\text{p}}=132\ \text{eV}$, and $U=1.5$. Therefore the ionization cross-section of Au $N$ orbital is: $Q_{\text{w}}=2.48 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{cm}^2$.

### 2.3 The deexcitation of the sample atoms
The ionized sample atom will deexcite mainly through two different processes: the fluorescence emission and the Auger decay. The fluorescence yield $P_X$ can be calculated through the following semi-empirical equation: $^{28)}$

$$
\left(\frac{P_{X}}{1-P_{X}}\right)^{n}=A+B Z+C Z^{3},
\tag{3}
$$

Then the Auger deexcitation probability $P_{\rm A}$ will be $P_{\rm A}=1-P_{X}=0.048$.

### 2.4 The transmission of the Auger electrons from inside to surface of the sample

Since the incident electron has a penetration depth through the sample surface, most of the Auger electrons are generated beneath surface by several atomic layers. If the inelastic collision happens during the transmission to the surface, the Auger electron will lost its characteristic information. The probability $P_{\rm el}$ of the "elastic" transmission process of the Auger electrons from the inside to the surface of the sample has been calculated with Monte Carlo method, which will be discussed later.

### 2.5 The transmission of the Auger electrons from the sample surface to the edge of the electric field

This process is simulated by SIMION 8.0. The transmission rate of the Auger electrons in this process $P_{\rm S}$ is acquired by simulating the electron traces in the electric field region. A tip-sample model is established as shown in Fig. 3(a), which is a cross-section through the cylindrical axis of the tip and detailed tip parameters are denoted in Fig. 3(b). The diameter and the length of the tip is 420 and $320\,\mu{\rm m}$ respectively, the inner diameter of the tip shield is $620\,\mu{\rm m}$, the length of the tip extending out of the shield is $50\,\mu{\rm m}$ and the distance between the tip and the sample is also $50\,\mu{\rm m}$. The tip model is built according to the chemical etched tungsten tip, $^{17)}$ which is composed of three parts. The top part is a paraboloid with focus $p=100\,\rm nm$, the medium part is a cone with half cone angle $\alpha=10^{\circ}$, and the base part is a parabola of revolution with the focus of parabola $p=40\,\mu{\rm m}$. The lengths of the three parts are 10, 213, and $97\,\mu{\rm m}$ respectively. The tip is shielded by a grounded metal cylinder. Since the tip is very close to the sample surface, the electric field can be approximately treated as being localized within the cylindrical shield. Therefore the simulation region is confined to the edge of the tip shield, and $P_{\rm S}$ is acquired by simulating the electron traces in this region.

During the simulation, the energy of the emitted electrons from the sample surface is set to be 69 eV which is corresponding to the energy of Au NOO Auger electron, and the tip is biased to $-132\,\rm V$. Because of the cylindrical symmetry of the model, the emission position on sample surface is represented by the distance to sample center $r_0$, and the emission angle on sample surface is defined by elevation angle $\theta_0$ (angle with sample surface) and azimuth angle $\varphi_0$ (angle with the cross-section plane). Totally 21 emission positions are selected within the $r_0$ range from 13 to $50\,\mu{\rm m}$, where the interval of $r_0$ is $0.5\,\mu{\rm m}$ from 13 to $20\,\mu{\rm m}$, and $5\,\mu{\rm m}$ from 20 to $50\,\mu{\rm m}$. For each emission position, electrons with energy 69 eV are emitted in all directions (i.e., $\theta_0$ from 0 to $90^{\circ}$ and $\varphi_0$ from 0 to $360^{\circ}$), and their traces are simulated. The position $(x,y,z)$ and velocity $(v_x,v_y,v_z)$ of every emitted electron at the end of its trace as well as the emission direction $(\theta_0,\varphi_0)$ on sample surface are recorded, and the criteria whether this electron can escape the field region and be detected are derived from these data as the following two: firstly, the position $(x,y,z)$ should be out of the electric region, i.e., $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>310\,\mu{\rm m}$, and secondly, the outgoing direction should be departing from the sample, i.e., $v_z>0$. Therefore the outgoing probability $P_{\rm S}$ for this emission position $r_0$ is calculated by counting the number of the outgoing Auger electrons. The detailed data analysis will be discussed in the next section.

Synthetically taking into account the influences of the above factors, the current intensity of the detectable Auger electrons $J_{\rm A}$ can be expressed as
$$
J_{\rm A}=J_{\rm S}P_{i}P_{\rm A}P_{\rm el}P_{\rm S}, \tag{4}
$$
where $P_{i}$ and $P_{\rm A}$ are constant in the present simulation.
Therefore the relative current intensity of the outgoing Auger electrons $J'_{\rm A}$ is expressed as
$$
J'_{\rm A}=J_{\rm S}P_{\rm el}P_{\rm S}. \tag{5}
$$

In this method, $P_{\text{el}}$ can be expressed as $^{29)}$

$$
P_{\text{el}} = \int_{0}^{\infty} dz \, \Phi(E_{\text{p}}, \theta') \int_{\Delta\Omega} \frac{d\Omega}{4\pi} \phi(\theta_0, z), \tag{6}

where $E_{\text{p}}$ is the energy of the incident electron, $\theta'$ and $\theta_0$ are the elevation angle of the incident electron and the outgoing Auger electron on sample surface respectively which are denoted in Fig. 1, $z$ is the depth underneath the sample surface where the Auger electrons originated, $\Phi(E_{\text{p}}, \theta')$ is the excitation depth distribution function (EXDDF), and $\phi(\theta_0, z)$ is the emission depth distribution function (EMDDF). During the MC simulation, the sample is set to be a grounded metal, and the penetration of electric field into the sample surface is neglected. The EXDDF is calculated by counting the ionization events created by electrons along each particular step length and then the EMDDF is obtained by counting the number of Auger electrons that have escaped from sample surface with small energy loss $(<0.1\,\text{eV})$ within a specific range of $\theta_0$. The normalization of the EMDDF is done by dividing the number of escaped Auger electrons at $z=0$.

In Fig. 4 it can be seen that there are no obvious differences for the angular distribution of $P_{\text{el}}$ at different $r_0$, which has also been confirmed by the previous experiment of Hofmann et al. $^{30)}$ This is reasonable because $r_0$ only affects $\theta'$, which contributes little to the EXDDF. It is worthy to point out that every $P_{\text{el}}$ curve exhibits an almost homogeneous distribution for $\theta_0 > 25^\circ$, and drops to 0 sharply at $\theta_0 = 0^\circ$ when $\theta_0 < 25^\circ$.

The transmission rate of the Auger electrons from the surface to the edge of the electric field is obtained in the form of $P_{\text{S}}(r_0, \theta_0, \varphi_0)$. Because of the cylindrical symmetry of the model, what we concern is how many Auger electrons escaping the electric field region along the radial direction. For the Auger electrons emitted at a certain $\theta_0$ from a certain emission position $r_0$ on sample surface, since the distance of the electron detector entrance to sample center is much larger than $r_0$, the number of the outgoing Auger electrons along the radial direction with $\theta_0$ of this position can be estimated by counting the Auger electrons escaped at parallel direction with $\theta_0$ from all emission positions $r_0$, as shown in Fig. 5. Taking into account the $J_\text{S}$ (Fig. 2) and the $P_\text{el}$ (Fig. 4), the relative current intensities of the outgoing Auger electrons $J_\text{A}'$ as a function of $\theta_0$ for different $r_0$ are calculated according to eq. (5) and is shown in Fig. 8.

With $\theta_0$ integrated, $P_\text{S}(r_0)$, $J_\text{S}P_\text{S}(r_0)$, and $J_\text{A}'(r_0)$ are obtained and shown in Fig. 9. It is observed that the radial distribution of $J_\text{A}'$ and $J_\text{S}P_\text{S}$ are consistent with each other, clearly indicating that the contribution of $P_\text{el}$ to $J_\text{A}'$ distribution can be ignored. The profile of $J_\text{A}'$ shows a peak at $r_0 = 18\,\mu\text{m}$, with its FWHM of $13.5\,\mu\text{m}$. Consequently the radial distribution on sample surface for Auger electrons that can escape from the electric region ultimately presents an annulus with the width less than its radius, which indicates that the spatial resolution of SPEES can be improved beyond the incident electron beam diameter. Similar results have been obtained from the simulation of Palmer et al.$^{16)}$ by simply assuming that only one percent of the reflected electrons could escape the electric region after impacting with sample surface elastically since simulating the current density of incident beam at sample surface. In our simulation contrastively, all physical processes of Auger emission have been taken into account quantitatively from the field emission of the STM tip to the transmission of Auger electrons. The results should be more elaborate and creditable.

### 3.2 The relative intensity distribution for the outgoing Auger electrons at the edge of the electric field
The relative intensity distribution $I(\theta, h)$ as a function of the elevation angle $\theta$ and the vertical height $h$ (Fig. 1) for Auger electrons departing the edge of electric field is shown in Fig. 10(a). The corresponding distributions $I(h)$ and $I(\theta)$ are shown in Fig. 10(b). It is obvious that the vertical height $h$ for outgoing Auger electrons distributes from 50 to $100\,\mu\text{m}$ and the outgoing elevation angles are less than $10^\circ$. It indicates that most of the Auger electrons emitted from the electric region paralleled to the sample surface, and the vertical position is also centralized to a small range.

Experimentally, if a state-of-art toroidal energy- and angle-resolved electron spectrometer (TEARES) with de- tecting plane parallel to the surface is employed, such as the one used in our scanning probe electron energy spectrom- eter, $^{22)}$ most of the outgoing Auger electrons could be collected. A simulation of the outgoing Auger electrons flying in our TEARES was carried out by SIMION 8.0. During the simulation, electrons were emitted in the center of the sample surface, with $\theta$ varying from 0 to $10^{\circ}$ at an interval of $0.1^{\circ}$ (Fig. 11). The result indicated that only those with $\theta$ less than $2.2^{\circ}$ could enter into the entrance and be analyzed by TEARES, therefore the collection efficiency of our TEARES for the outgoing Auger electrons $\eta_{C}$ was calculated to be $56.9\%$ by integrating the normalized angular distribution of the emitted Auger electrons $I(\theta)$ [Fig. 10(b)] from 0 to $2.2^{\circ}$:

$$
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{2.2} I(\theta) d \theta}{\int_{0}^{90} I(\theta) d \theta}=56.9\%. \tag{7}
$$

The ratio of the Auger electrons escaping from the electric field region $N_{\mathrm{O}}$ to the total Auger electrons emitted from the sample surface $N_{\mathrm{T}}$ was also calculated: $N_{\mathrm{O}} / N_{\mathrm{T}} \approx 2\%$. Thus the detection efficiency of our TEARES employed in such SPEES experiment was estimated to be about $1.14\%$, which was improved about two orders of magnitude compared with the conventional hemispherical electron energy analyzer.

## 4. Conclusions
We have presented a simulation study of the emission features of the Auger electrons in the electric field region localized within the cylindrical tip shield in a scanning probe electron energy spectrometer (SPEES). The influences of every detailed process have been taken into account synthetically and the relative current intensity distributions for outgoing Auger electrons have been calculated theoret- ically.

The results indicate that the spatial distribution for the Auger electrons on sample surface that can escape from the electric region presents a annulus with a width less than its radius, which suggests that the spatial resolution of SPEES can be improved beyond the size of the incident electron beam. The distributions of the emission angle and the vertical height for the outgoing Auger electrons at the edge of the electric field region reveal that most of the emitted Auger electrons are parallel to the sample surface with a very small vertical height range. The simulation results suggest a toroidal energy- and angle-resolved electron spectrometer (TEARES) with detecting plane perpendicular to its axis is a good choice for SPEES. The simulation results are significant for improving the detection efficiency, the energy resolution and the spatial resolution of the SPEES, and also provide instructive data for the designing of the tip shield and the electronic optical system in the tip-sample region.
