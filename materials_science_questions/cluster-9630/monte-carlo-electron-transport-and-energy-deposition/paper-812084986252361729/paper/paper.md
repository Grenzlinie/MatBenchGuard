## TL RESPONSE FOR CVD DIAMONDS

$^{1}$LPES-CRESA, Laboratoire de Physique Electronique des Solides, Centre de Recherche sur les Solides et leurs Applications, EA1174, Université de Nice-Sophia Antipolis, Parc Valrose, 06108 Nice cedex 2, France

The thermoluminescent (TL) response of CVD diamond is investigated after bombarding a sample with monoenergetic electrons (at 6 and 21 MeV). Irradiations are performed at room temperature with a medical linear electron accelerator PRIMUS (KD2-Siemens). The TL curve shows a peak at $\sim$540 K. The area of this peak as a function of fluence exhibits saturation behaviour at high energies. Moreover, there is no significant difference between the TL response at 21 MeV electrons and that at 6 MeV. In order to explain these experimental results, calculations of the ionising dose have been performed with the code MCNPX (Monte Carlo N-Particle) for various incident energies from 0.1 MeV up to 100 MeV.

## INTRODUCTION
The use of CVD (Chemical Vapor Deposition) diamond samples for dosimetric applications has already been investigated in the past$^{(1,2)}$. These samples have one glow peak at $\sim$540 K and a weak thermal fading at room temperature irradiation$^{(3)}$. They also show good response to various radiations tested such as alpha, protons, gamma and electrons$^{(3,4)}$.

This work focuses on electrons irradiation in order to point out the effect of energies on thermoluminescent (TL) response. The aim is then to state whether CVD diamond samples are likely to be easily used in electron dosimetry applications. If the answer is affirmative, the main advantage of this kind of passive detector compared with others would be that it is very small in size, chemically inert, non-toxic and tissue-equivalent material.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
The studied sample comes from a free-standing polycrystalline high-quality white diamond wafer synthesised at the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL5K251). The wafer was deposited with a growth rate of $1.85\ \mathrm{\mu m\ h^{-1}}$ on a tungsten substrate heated to $750^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ under a working pressure of 15.4 kPa by using microwave plasma-enhanced CVD (MWCVD). The microwave power was 4.5 kW and flows of methane and hydrogen gases were 6.3 and $156\ \mathrm{scm^{3}}$, respectively.

The sample was laser cut from the 50-mm-diameter wafer. Its surface is $5\times5\ \mathrm{mm}$ and its thickness is $260\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

Irradiations were performed at room temperature with a medical linear electron accelerator PRIMUS (KD2-Siemens) at the Centre Antoine Lacassagne in Nice (France). The beam is known to have a field of $20\ \mathrm{cm}\times20\ \mathrm{cm}$ at a source-to-surface distance equal to 100 cm. The monitoring unit (MU) is used as in medical applications and is proportional to the number of primary electrons emitted by the source. The accelerator is adjusted to a flux of $300\ \mathrm{MU\ min^{-1}}$ corresponding, at 100 cm, to a fluence rate of $0.75\ \mathrm{MU\ min^{-1}\ cm^{-2}}$. Electron beams are not strictly monoenergetic but the most probable energies at the surface of the medium are chosen to be either 6 or 21 MeV. For simplicity, in the following, the experimental energies will denote the most probable energy of the beam.

After irradiation, the low energy traps were depleted by pre-heating the sample up to 478 K and then cooling down to room temperature. The TL readout was performed from room temperature to 675 K with a linear heating ramp at a rate of $1\ \mathrm{K\ s^{-1}}$.

The contribution of the noise was estimated by performing the TL readout with the non-irradiated sample and was then removed from the glow curves. The number of trapped charges is characterised by the TL peak area (integration from room temperature up to 675 K). Glow curves have been achieved at 6 and 21 MeV for various fluences. Figure 2 also shows that, surprisingly, the difference between the two curves (21 and 6 MeV) is $<3\%$. Because the experimental uncertainty is typically $\sim 2\%$, it is possible to conclude that there is no significant difference between the TL response of diamond at 6 MeV and that at 21 MeV. In the first approximation yet, one should expect a difference because the available energies are different by a factor of $21\ \text{MeV per } 6\ \text{MeV}=3.5$.

In the second approximation, one should take into account that the primary electron is likely to leave the thin target without depositing its whole energy. The values to consider are then the stopping powers of electrons (see Figure 4) and especially the collision contribution that leads to the generation of electron-hole pairs and is called linear energy transfer (LET). Because the target is thin ($d=260\ \mu\text{m}$), it is possible to approximate the energy released by electrons in the target by the product $d\times\text{LET}$. The ratio of energies released at 21 MeV and at 6 MeV is then simply given by the ratio of the LETs at these energies. In carbon, the LETs are $1.679\ \text{MeV cm}^2\text{g}^{-1}$ at 6 MeV and $1.808\ \text{MeV cm}^2\text{g}^{-1}$ at $21\ \text{MeV}^{(6)}$ (see Figure 4). The expected difference between energy released by ionisation at 21 MeV and that at 6 MeV should then be $7.7\%$.

where $d$ is the thickness of the sample (cm), $\mu$ is the mass per unit of volume ($\text{kg}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$), $\Phi$ is the electron fluence ($\text{cm}^{-2}$) and $\bar{e}(E)$ is the mean energy deposited in the sample by an electron ($J$).

The ionising dose, and then the number of electron-hole pairs generated, is simply proportional to the mean energy deposited in the sample by an electron. To estimate this latest, simulations with MCNPX (Monte Carlo N-Particle) have been performed previously$^{(7)}$. A $260\ \mu$m thick sample composed of carbon with a density of $3.52\ \text{g}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ is irradiated for various incident electron energies from 100 keV up to 100 MeV. For each electron energy, around $10^5$ primary electrons are simulated in the sample and the mean energy deposited is then determined with a calculation uncertainty $<0.08\%$. Figure 5 shows the results of the simulations by plotting the deposited energy as a function of electrons energy. At very low energy ($<0.35$ MeV) the deposited energy follows the incident energy because electrons are stopped within the sample and also because the radiative stopping power is negligible compared with that of collision (see Figure 4): the whole incident energy is used to generate electron-hole pairs. Between 0.35 and 2 MeV, the radiative stopping power is still weak but the primary electrons are able to leave the sample and the ionising energy loss is then governed by the LET. Because the LET is decreasing in this energy range, the deposited energy is also decreasing. Consequently, a maximum of the deposited energy is obtained at $\sim$0.35 MeV. This latest value is governed by the sample thickness and a thicker sample will shift the peak towards higher energy. Above $\sim$2 MeV, the mean energy deposited is slightly increasing only 1.2% when the incident energy increases from 2 to 100 MeV. However, the difference between the deposited energy at 6 MeV and that at 21 MeV is $<0.7\%$, which agrees with the presented experimental results (Figure 2). This non-dependence with electron energy is explained by the effects of two opposite contributions that counterbalance each other: the primary electrons release more energy in the sample at high energy but the radiative process, relative to primary and secondary electrons, is also increasing and contributes to bring energy outside the sample.

CONCLUSION

CVD diamond sample has been bombarded by electrons at 6 and 21 MeV in order to investigate the effect of electron energy on TL response. The peak area at 540 K shows no significant dependence on electron energy that is the number of trapped carriers is the same at both energies. This agrees with MCNPX simulations that show that the deposited energy in the $260\ \mu$m thick sample is quite constant in the 2–100 MeV energy range. This non-dependence of incident energy is attributed to the weak sample thickness and the loss of energy by radiative processes at high energy.

The main conclusion of this work is that a single sample cannot be used for radiation protection applications unless the incident electron energy spectrum is known. This method, which consists of measuring the TL area, will only allow the measurement of the number of electrons traversing the exposed sample. Energy information could be obtained if this concept is extended to a dosemeter that will consist of a select series of different sample thicknesses; each sample stopping a different number of electrons of a given energy range.
