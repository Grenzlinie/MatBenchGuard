# ASSESSMENT OF THE CAPABILITIES OF MONTE CARLO ELECTRON TRANSPORT MODELS USING THE CONTINUOUS SLOWING DOWN APPROXIMATION

Dave W. VANDE PUTTE *

Atomic Energy Board, Private Bag X-256, Pretoria, South Africa

Received 10 December 1981 and in revised form: 5 April 1982

Monte Carlo electron transport models using the continuous slowing down approximation are tested in a variety of experimental situations not previously investigated and compared to more elaborate models that do not use this simple approximation. Monte Carlo aspects such as computing time, sample size and precision of results are brought to light.

## 1. Introduction
Recent years have seen the development of numer- ous computer codes for simulating electron propagation in matter by using Monte Carlo methods [1-5]. Appli- cations of such codes range from the study of radiation detector response to the dosimetry of irradiated biologi- cal systems.

Simulation is done by breaking up electron trajecto- ries into smaller segments, at the end of which new electron co-ordinates in the phase space are calculated by random sampling from probability distributions that take into account the effect of all interactions having occurred within that segment; consideration of the indi- vidual interactions that the incident electron undergoes would require several orders of magnitude more calcula- tion time, and is hence feasible only for comparatively low initial energies, of approximately 1 keV [1,5]. Distri- butions reflecting the effect of all interactions within a certain portion of trajectory either pertain to the angu- lar deflection of the electron with respect to its direction at the beginning of the segment, or describe the energy loss incurred during the element of trajectory.

When examining the distributions which relate to the energy loss, one is led to distinguish between two classes of transport models. Firstly, those schemes where the electron trajectory is subdivided into segments of pre- determined length, and where the electron energy at the end of each segment is pre-calculated [1-3], using the Bethe formula for average slowing down of charged particles in matter. No energy-loss fluctuations are, therefore, allowed. This is known as the continuous slowing down approximation (C.S.D.A.). In the second class of models, the energy loss over the segment is allowed to fluctuate [4,5] and is obtained by random sampling from an appropriate probability distribution, either that of Landau [6] or of Blunck and Westphal [7].

In both types of transport calculation, the angular deflections at the end of each segment, $\phi$, and $\theta$ (see fig. 1) are generated by random sampling from an azimuthal multiple-scattering distribution for $\phi$, and a polar multiple-scattering one for $\theta$. The media under consideration are assumed to be isotropic, therefore $\phi$ will be equally distributed over the interval $[0,2\pi]$. The polar angle is chosen from the distribution of Goudsmit and Saunderson [8,9], or that of Moliere [10].

Work to date using the C.S.D.A. model does not examine the extent of the validity of this approximation when included in a transport calculation, and tests against experimental data are few. On the other hand, proponents of the second type of transport model do not compare the relative merits of the two schemes in the light of the inevitable extra computing time required when energy-loss fluctuations are permitted.

The aim of this paper is to delineate the region of validity of the C.S.D.A. electron transport model (a code was developed by the author), and to check it against a wide variety of experimental results, in materi- als ranging from beryllium to lead. This is done for initial energies from 100 keV to 2 MeV. Situations investigated are backscattering, transmission and energy deposition. A comparison is also made with results obtained by using the more sophisticated class of trans- port models. Furthermore, the choice of the polar multi- ple-scattering distribution is discussed on the basis of the computer time necessary to evaluate it. Sample sizes are discussed for particular applications, and computing times are given in each case.

## 2. Computational model

### 2.1. Electron path length and the Bethe formula: the continuous slowing down approximation

The path length $s$ (cm) described by an electron slowing down from an energy $EI$ to an energy $E$ in matter is its actual trajectory length and is given by
$$
s=\int_{E}^{E I}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d} E^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} s}\right|^{-1} \mathrm{~d} E^{\prime}. \tag{1}
$$

$\mathrm{d} E^{\prime} / \mathrm{d} s$ is the Bethe stopping power [11,12] (MeV $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$), given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
-\frac{\mathrm{d} E^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} s}= & {\left[\frac{0.3014 \rho Z}{W \tau(\tau+2)}\right.} \\
& \times\left\{1 \frac{0.1306 \tau^{2}(\tau+2)}{I^{2}}+f(\tau)\right. \\
& \left.-\delta(\tau, Z)-C(\tau, Z)\right\}] R_{\mathrm{c}}, \tag{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

where the parameters have the following meaning:
| | |
| --- | --- |
| $E$ | electron energy (MeV) |
| $\tau$ | $=E / 0.5110034$ ($0.5110034$ = electron rest mass) |
| $Z$ | atomic number of target material |
| $\rho$ | density of target material ($\mathrm{g} \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$) |
| $W$ | atomic weight of target (g) |
| $I$ | mean ionization potential of target (MeV) |
| $f$ | is the relativistic correction [11,12] |
| $\delta$ | is the density correction [11,12] |
| $C$ | is the shell correction [11,12] |

The expression between square brackets is the stopping power due to excitation and ionization of the medium. $R_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the radiative correction which accounts for Brems- strahlung energy losses. For electron energies up to a few MeV:
$$
R_{\mathrm{c}}=1+\frac{Z \tau}{1600}. \tag{3}
$$

Eqs. (1) and (2) constitute the continuous slowing down approximation: a one-to-one correspondence between energy and path length is established.

### 2.2. Polar multiple-scattering angular distribution of Molière

For the probability $f(\theta) \sin \theta \mathrm{d} \theta$ per unit solid angle that an electron be deflected to an angle between $\theta$ and $\theta+\mathrm{d} \theta$ with respect to its initial direction when slowing down from $EI$ to $E$, Molière gives the following expres- sion, valid for small angles [10,13]:
$$
f(\theta) \theta \mathrm{d} \theta=\omega \mathrm{d} \omega\left[2 \exp \left(-\omega^{2}\right)+\frac{f_{1}(B)}{B}+\frac{f_{2}(B)}{B^{2}}+\cdots\right]
$$

($\theta \leqslant 30^{\circ}$; a further condition is that at least 30 to 40 electron-nucleus interactions occur when slowing down from $EI$ to $E$ [11])
where
$$
\omega=\frac{\theta}{\chi_{\mathrm{c}} \sqrt{B}},
$$

$f_{1}$ and $f_{2}$ are functions whose expression is given in Molière's original paper [10].
$$
\chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}=\int_{E}^{E I} \chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\left(E^{\prime}\right)\left|\frac{\mathrm{d} E^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} s}\right|^{-1} \mathrm{~d} E^{\prime},
$$

$$
\chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}=\frac{4 \pi N Z^{2} e^{4}}{p^{2} v^{2}},
$$

where $N$ is number of electrons per cubic centimetre, $e$ the electron charge, $Z$ the atomic number of the target material, $p$ the electron momentum, $v$ the electron veloc- ity. $B$ is obtained by solving the transcendental equation
$$
B-\ln B=\ln \left[\frac{\chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{1.167 \bar{\chi}_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}\right]+K,
$$

where
$$
\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}=6.8 × 10^{-5} Z^{+2 / 3}\left[1.13+3.76\left(\frac{Z}{137 \beta}\right)^{2}\right] \frac{1-\beta^{2}}{\beta^{2}},
$$

$$
\beta=v / c,
$$

$$
\ln \bar{\chi}_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}} \int_{E}^{E I} \chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\left(E^{\prime}\right) \ln \chi_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\left(E^{\prime}\right)\left|\frac{\mathrm{d} \Gamma^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} s}\right|^{-1} \mathrm{~d} E^{\prime},
$$

$$
K=\frac{1}{Z+1}\left\{\ln \left[0.160 Z^{-2 / 3}\left(1+\frac{3.33 Z}{137 \beta}\right)\right]-C F\right\}.
$$

In the evaluation of $K$, $\beta$ is calculated at an energy half-way between $EI$ and $E$. $CF$ is a material constant. (For a tabulation of all material constants involved here, see ref. 14). Bethe extended the validity of the Molière expression for $f(\theta)$ to all angles by multiplying it by $\sqrt{\theta / \sin \theta}$ [15] (This extension will be applied here). Goudsmit and Saunderson established an expression for $f(\theta)$, valid for all angles but whose numerical evaluation is more complex [9], and more time consuming as will be seen further on.

### 2.3. Initial and intermediate energies

A maximum initial electron energy of 2 MeV was decided upon. Most experimental results where Brems- strahlung and associated electron production play a minor, or no role, in the electron transport process are confined to this region. Furthermore, the form of the radiative correction $R_{c}$ introduced above, is valid only when radiation losses are less than 10 to 20 percent of total losses, which is the case for electron energies up to 2 MeV (eq. (3)). A set of $k+1$ decreasing energies, $E_{n}$, is constructed by using the following scaling law:

$$
E_{n}=E_{n-1} \frac{1}{2^{1 / 16}} ; \quad E_{1}=2 \mathrm{MeV}.
$$

The lowest energy, $E_{123}$ is set equal to 0.01 MeV instead of 0.0101315 MeV. Lower energies are not considered because at energies less than 0.01 MeV, electrons are practically at rest, and of little consequence to the transport problems examined here. This defines a cut-off energy below which no electrons are observable in these calculations. Next, a set of $k$ path-length steps, $\{d_{n}\}$ is obtained by

$$
d_{n}=\int_{E_{n+1}}^{E_{n}}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d} E^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d} s}\right|^{-1} \mathrm{~d} E^{\prime}.
$$

Thus, $d_{n}$ is the path length travelled by an electron slowing down from $E_{n}$ to $E_{n+1}$. In elements of atomic number higher than aluminium, the $d_{n}$ for $n \geqslant 90$ are exceedingly small (see table 1). Therefore, fewer $E_{n}$ and $d_{n}$ are retained, the last one for an electron slowing down from $E_{k}$ to $0.01 \mathrm{MeV}$, with, e.g., $k=112$ in copper, 96 in silver, 94 in gold and 93 for lead. $E_{k+1}$ then becomes 0.01 MeV and the value of $d_{k}$ is changed to

$$
\sum_{i=k}^{123} d_{i}.
$$

### 2.4. Data preparation

For each $E_{n}$ and associated path length step $d_{n}$, a corresponding cumulative probability distribution of the polar multiple-scattering angle is calculated by using the Molière theory; the initial energy being $E_{n}$, and the final energy $E_{n+1}$. Angles chosen are given in table 2 and will be referred to a $\theta_{i}$. The corresponding cumulative distribution values are noted as $F_{n}^{i}$ with

$$
F_{n}^{i}=\operatorname{Prob}\left(\theta \leqslant \theta_{i}\right).
$$

For each material under consideration, the sets $\{E_{n}, d_{n}, F_{n}^{i}\}$ are stored on magnetic disc.

The reason for opting for the Molière formulation instead of that of Goudsmit and Saunderson is that it requires approximately 100 times more time to calculate the latter type of distribution. For 123 distributions in aluminium on an IBM SYST-370/Model 155-III ma- chine, this means 1 minute versus 98 minutes. With the exception of ref. 5, all other codes in the literature use the Goudsmit-Saunderson distribution.

### 2.5. Transport calculation

Suppose a transport calculation has to be performed for electrons of initial energy $E I \leqslant 2$ MeV. The electron is given the energy $E_{i}$, where $E_{i}$ is the $E_{n}$ value closest to $E I$. The trajectory is broken up into a succession of segments of length $d_{n}(n \geqslant i)$ at the end of which the electron energy is given by $E_{n+1}(n \geqslant i)$. At the end of each segment, an azimuthal multiple-scattering angle $\phi$ is sampled by calling a random number $R_{1}$ from a square distribution over the interval $[0,1]$, and multiply- ing it by $2 \pi$. The polar multiple-scattering angle $\theta$ is obtained by calling another such random number, $R_{2}$, and solving:

$$
R_{2}=\operatorname{Prob}\left(\theta^{\prime} \leqslant \theta\right)
$$

### 2.6. Program structure
From the previous sections, it is seen that transport calculations in any material are done in two phases. First, prepare the set of $\{E_{n}, d_{n}, F_{n}^{i}\}$ values for the material of interest and store it. This constitutes the data-preparation program. The second phase is the transport calculation itself. Whatever the initial energy $E I \leqslant 2 MeV$ , the same data set $\{E_{n}, d_{n}, F_{n}^{i}\}$ is used, which is universal for this particular material and need not be recalculated for every initial energy. For initial energies lower than $100 keV$ , it is not recommended to use this method since the number of segments would be too small to reflect the fine structure of the electron trajectory (the number of segments for $100 keV$ elec trons in aluminium is 54). In such cases one could conceive of trajectories made up of greater numbers of shorter steps. However, path-length steps then become so small that one is no longer in the region of multiple- scattering, but in that of plural-scattering [11], where the Moliere and Goudsmit-Saunderson theories are no longer valid (too few electron-nucleus interactions take place). Transport problems in this energy region should rather be solved by using analytical methods such as that developed by Bethe, Rose and Smith [16].

## 3. Results and discussion
Three types of transport problems were investigated for monoelemental plane parallel media: backscattering, transmission and energy deposition. Calculations were done where experimental results are available from the literature. It should be noted that, despite the numerousapplications, experimental data are in fact fairly scarce: much remains to be done in this field. The most widely investigated phenomenon is backscattering, followed by transmission, then energy deposition. By far the most popular element is aluminium, probably because of its technological importance, then come lead, copper, gold, silver and beryllium, in that order. A few others have also been studied on a sporadic basis. Monoenergetic electron sources are considered here; results for sources are merely a combination of several monoenergetic "ex- periments".

### 3.2. Backscattering

#### 3.2.1. Backscattering coefficient

Fig. 3 shows the variation of the backscattering coefficient $A$ with initial electron energy for aluminium, at perpendicular incidence on a foil of semi-infinite thickness. The experimental results are obtained from various authors (see Tabata [17] for a list). The experimental cut-off energies are a few hundred electron volts and experimental errors are generally reported to be $5\%$ of the measured values. For other elements, not shown here, calculated backscattering coefficients also show an overestimation which is of about a factor 1.5 for graphite, decreasing with increasing atomic number. For silver, experimental results and calculations agree. In the case of lead, computed values are lower than the measured ones, by a factor 0.7. For each calculated value, 6000 electron histories were simulated, requiring between 3 and 22 min, depending on the initial energy and material. Fig. 4 shows that the measured and calculated backscattering coefficients for aluminium agree for foils of thickness less than 0.3 times the total electron path length. Patau [4] and Wittig [5], who include energy-loss fluctuations in their calculations, give results for the case of fig. 3.

### 3.3. Transmission

#### 3.3.1. Transmission coefficient
Fig. 8 gives the transmission coefficient $T$ for aluminium and lead respectively, as a function of foil thickness (given in units of total electron path length) for 960, 336, 250 and 159 keV electrons. Seliger [22] does not give the error bounds of his measurements, but all experimental points are within $5\%$ of the indicated curve in the case of aluminium, and $20\%$ in that of lead. Monte Carlo results have a $10\%$ precision (error bars are not indicated for graphical reasons). Calculated transmission is below the experimental curve and the discrepancy increases with foil thickness. One of the reasons for the discrepancy between calculation and experiment could be the fact that the cut-off energy in the computations is 10 keV, as mentioned previously, whereas it is a few hundred eV in the experiment; the thicker the foil, the greater the number of lower energy electrons transmitted. A more sophisticated calculation done by Wittig [5] shows no significant improvement (fig. 8). In the present calculations, the sample size was 6000 particles. Computing time varied between 12 and 20 min, depending on the energy, material and foil thickness.

#### 3.3.2. Transmitted angular and energy distributions
Fig.9 shows the angular distribution of 1.75 MeV incident energy electrons emerging from aluminium and lead foils respectively. Agreement is excellent in the case of aluminium, not quite as good for lead. Sample sizes were of 15000 particles, requiring 3 min of computing. This relatively modest time requirement is due to the fact that for such thin foils $(132\ \mathrm{mg/cm^2}\ \mathrm{Al}, 50\ \mathrm{mg/cm^2}$ Pb) electrons spend a minor fraction of their energy in the medium. Fig. 10 is a doubly differentiated result: it gives the energy spectrum of electrons transmitted through an angle $58^{\circ}\leqslant\tau\leqslant62^{\circ}$, through an aluminium foil. Similar results for $\tau\simeq5^{\circ}$ could not be evaluated because of the low frequency of such events (1/2500). For the same reason, no results are given for lead foils. The sample size in fig. 10 was 25000 particles, the time 50 min. No differentiated results are available from the work of Patau [4] or Wittig [5].

### 3.4. Energy deposition

Fig. 11 shows the energy deposition in plane parallel media. For the experimental and Monte Carlo data, the energy deposited $ED(z)$ at depth $z$ is given relative to its maximum value. Experimental error is $2\%$ in the case of the Aiginger and Gonauser [24] data (normal incidence). Miller et al. [25] claim a $1\%$ experimental error for their values (oblique incidence). Monte Carlo precision varies from 5 to $10\%$. The position of the maximum of energy deposition, is determined by calculation, is within $10\%$ of the measured position of the maximum. With one exception, agreement between the computed and measured curves is 5 to $10\%$ in the vicinity of their maxima. Worst agreement occurs in the case of the lightest element, beryllium. The comparisons between measurement and computations done by Patau [4] and by Wittig [5] show no better agreement, despite the fact that electron energy-loss fluctuations were taken into account in their codes. For each of the present calculations, a sample of 4500 histories was used, requiring 10 to 22 min of computing time.

### 4. Conclusions

A twofold conclusion may be drawn.

(1) To assess the various computational methods, a number of parameters should be considered: sample size, computing time, corresponding standard deviations. Unfortunately this information is lacking in many instances for both types of models. Furthermore, differentiated results are scarce. The few experimental results which allow a comparison between the two types of transport models, reveal no marked superiority of calculations in which energy-loss fluctuations are catered for; this should be viewed in the light of the additional computing time needed to take these fluctuations into account. It also appears that the use of the Molière multiple-scattering distribution as extended by Bethe offers a definite time saving over the Goudsmit- Saunderson formulation.

(2) Judging from the results above, one may say that C.S.D.A. models, although based on relatively un- sophisticated physical assumptions, yield a substantial amount of reliable and detailed information in a useful range of energies and situations. Differentiated results, however, agree with experiments only if expressed in relative units, except for thin foils ($t \leqslant 0.3$ times the total electron path length). One should, nonetheless, beware of differential results in heavier elements or any result pertinent to events that occur seldom. Shortcomings are due not only to neglect of energy-loss fluctuations but also to the relatively high cut-off energy and the fact that secondary-electron production (delta rays) is not taken into consideration. Photoelectron production due to Bremsstrahlung also plays a role, especially in extended media. Furthermore, the Monte Carlo method used here is a crude one, no variance reduction techniques were introduced, so that only an increase in sample size can produce more reliable results for events that occur seldom; this can lead to exorbitant computer time requirements. One should attempt to improve on this aspect, nevertheless bearing in mind the words of Fano, Spencer and Berger [26]: "Most of the refine- ments of the Monte Carlo method also require some additional computing effort per (electron) history, and the overall figure of merit of any scheme depends on the cost of analysing, programming and running the problem on a computer."
