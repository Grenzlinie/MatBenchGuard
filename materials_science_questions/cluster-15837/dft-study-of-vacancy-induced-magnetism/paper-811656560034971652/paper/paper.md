## ABSTRACT

The magnetism induced by D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects in graphene is investigated using first principles calculations. We find that the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects cause remarkable lattice distortion, which significantly alters the electronic structures and magnetic properties of graphene. Moreover, the Lieb's theorem cannot be used because the bipartite symmetry is broken locally by lattice relaxation. If the dangling bonds around vacancies are passivated by the $sp^2$ hydrogen saturation, the lattice distortion is suppressed. Both the Lieb's theorem and tight-binding method can thus be used.

### 1. Introduction

Graphene, a single-atom-layer two-dimensional material, has attracted worldwide attention due to its novel electronic properties and potential device applications [1–4]. Seeking magnetism of carbon materials has always been of particular interest because most magnetic materials are based on $d$ and $f$ elements in current technological applications [5–8]. New carbon-based magnetic materials can greatly extend the limits of technologies relying on magnetism. More promising is the application of such material to design some nanoscale magnetic and spin electronic devices. Naturally, carbon-based magnetism attracts enormous attention in science and technology and becomes a prosperous and challenging research area [9].

It has been known that the pristine graphene is nonmagnetic. In order to tailor the properties of graphene, we can introduce some typical impurities or vacancy defects in graphene to alter its electronic structures. Generally, the vacancy defects in graphene can be created by irradiation of the noble gas ions [10] and by the transmission electron microscope with a focused (down to 1 Å in diameter) electron beam [11]. The magnetism induced by the vacancy defects in graphene has been confirmed by many previous theoretical and experimental investigations [12,13]. The origin of magnetism in graphene has attracted considerable attention because stable magnetism in graphene may lead to large development in graphene-based spintronic devices. However, the mechanism of magnetism in graphene is not fully understood until now. An important result based on the Hubbard model [14–16] is the famous Lieb's theorem derived by Lieb in 1989 [17]. The major idea of Lieb's theorem is that, considering the electron–electron repulsive interaction, the ground state of a bipartite system at half-filling has the total spin,

$$
S=\frac{1}{2}\left|N_{A}-N_{B}\right|, \tag{1}
$$

where $N_A$ and $N_B$ denote the occupation number of the sublattices A and B, respectively. According to Lieb's theorem, the imbalance between the two sublattices is the origin of magnetism in graphene. Moreover, we can see from Eq. (1) that an antiferromagnetic ground state can also exist if $S=0$.

In order to understand the mechanism of magnetism in graphene, lots of work has been done using the tight-binding (TB) method. Since the TB method is usually restricted to the framework of hydrogenated graphene, the important lattice distortion induced by impurities and defects is ignored completely. Meanwhile, the hydrogen saturations eliminate the dangling bonds which may contribute to the magnetism. Hence the TB method is not suitable to investigate the magnetic properties of graphene with *true* vacancy defects. If the lattice distortion is considered, the results from the TB calculations will be modified. Considering the lattice relaxation, some authors have performed first-principles calculations in graphene with single-vacancy defects [13,18–21]. They found that, when the single-vacancy defects occupy the same sublattice, ferromagnetism is preferred. Otherwise, antiferromagnetism is energetically favorable. As a representative of vacancy defects, we pay much attention to D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects in graphene in the present Letter. We find that the physical properties of graphene alters greatly. Generally, the effect of lattice relaxation is prominent and the magnetism is modified remarkably by the lattice distortion. In this case, the vacancy reconstruction results in the formation of 5-membered rings. The bipartite symmetry of the system is broken locally. As a result, the famous Lieb's theorem cannot be used. We prove that lattice relaxation is extremely important and must not be neglected. Furthermore, if the dangling bonds around the vacancy defects are passivated by the $sp^2$ hydrogen saturation, the lattice relaxation will be

## 2. Calculation methods

Our spin-polarized calculations are based on the CASTEP code [22]. The Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof's (PBE) exchange-correlation functionals [23] and ultrasoft pseudopotentials [24] are adopted. The plane-wave energy cutoff is taken to be 400 eV. We construct a supercell composed by $10 \times 5 \times 1$ primitive cells (see Figure 1a), in which 100 atomic sites (corresponding to the ideal graphene) are included. We wish to seek the ground state of this supercell including two D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects and to investigate its magnetic properties. By setting the initial spin states suitably and calculating the total energy of the supercell, we can find whether the ground state is nonmagnetic, ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic. In our following calculations, a Monkhorst-Pack [25] $2 \times 4 \times 1$ $k$-mesh is adopted. Convergence with respect to the plane-wave cutoff energy and $k$-point sampling has been carefully checked. In our supercell geometry, the distance between adjacent equivalent layers is set as $10 \mathring{A}$. All structures are

### 3. Results and discussion

Considering the hexagonal symmetry of graphene, the conventional high symmetry points in the band structures are selected as $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$-$\mathbf{M}$-$\mathbf{K}$-$\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$. In order to investigate the magnetic properties of the ground state, we construct a large supercell with $|\mathbf{a}_1| = 2|\mathbf{a}_2|$ in this Letter. Accordingly, the high symmetry points in the band structures are naturally chosen as $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$-$\mathbf{M}$-$\mathbf{X}$-$\mathbf{K}$-$\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$. In Figure 1a, we show the lattice structure without optimization and the difference between the spin-up ($\alpha$) and spin-down ($\beta$) electron densities. Figure 1a clearly shows that the electron density of $\alpha$ electrons is larger than $\beta$ electrons around the vacancies. Hence the ground state of the structure is ferromagnetic. Moreover, the spin population has a damped oscillation as a function of the nearest-neighbor index. The physical reason can be explained as follows. The defect state, distributed over the sites of the sublattice with the defect, has a power law decay [13,26]. Owing to the exchange spin-polarization effect, a negative spin density appears on the even-nearest-neighbor sites, while a positive spin density is enhanced on the odd-

nearest-neighbor sites [13]. Figure 1b and c further show the energy band and density of states (DOS), respectively. We can see from Figure 1b that the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects induce ten midgap states. Moreover, each midgap state splits into two substates due to spin splitting. There are four substates occupied by $\alpha$ electrons and the others are empty, which is further supported by Figure 1c. This directly leads to the local net magnetic moment of $4\mu_B$.

In order to deeply analyze the physical origin of magnetism shown in Figure 1, we carefully observe Figure 1c and find that, for the $\alpha$ electrons, the four midgap states under the Fermi-level have a $p$-wave character, while the other six are $sp^2$ hybrid waves. For the $\beta$ electrons, both the $p$ wave and $sp^2$ hybrid wave states are above the Fermi-level. The midgap $p$ wave states are quasilocalized states, which contribute to weak itinerant magnetism. The $sp^2$ dangling bond states are localized states, which lead to local magnetic momentum. According to Refs. [13,27], we know that there are two mechanism on the origin of magnetism in graphene with defects. One is weak itinerant magnetism, and the other is local magnetic momentum. Both the $p$ quasilocalized and the $sp^2$ dangling bond states induce new energy levels. The occupied status of electrons at these new energy levels determine the total magnetic momentum of the system. The magnetism can thus emerge if $\alpha$ electrons cannot be canceled by $\beta$ electrons at these energy levels. In the present structure, the dangling bond states do not induce magnetism because the Fermi level lies below them, leaving only the weak itinerant magnetism from four substates occupied by the $\alpha$ electrons.

Generally, the physical properties of the structure can be altered significantly after optimizing the lattice. The optimized graphene structure with the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects is shown in Figure 2a. We can easily observe the obvious difference between Figures 1 and 2. In Figure 2a, we find no any difference between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ electron densities at the same value of isosurface as in Figure 1a. This means that no any electron is localized around the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects in the optimized graphene structure. Figure 2b and c clearly show that there are no any difference between the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ electrons for the band structure and DOS. Furthermore, we obtain $0\mu_B$ magnetic moment for the structure of Figure 2a. Obviously, the previous magnetism (see Figure 1) disappears in the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy graphene structure with the geometry optimization. This result can be understood as follows. As mentioned in the above text, the magnetism in graphene with vacancies comes from the localized states due to the redundant dangling bonds and the weak itinerant states arising from the imbalance between the two sublattices. We can see from Figure 2a that new covalent bonds are formed due to reconstruction of the dangling bonds after lattice relaxation. In this case, the vacancy reconstruction results in the formation of 5-membered rings. Moreover, the shape of the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy approaches a circle, and the bipartite symmetry of the system is broken locally. As a result, the Lieb's theorem cannot be used. The two possible contributions for magnetism are both suppressed for the relaxed structure. This directly leads to disappearing of the magnetism. We thus conclude that lattice relaxation is extremely important and dominates the magnetism, which should be included when investigating the electronic structures of graphene with vacancy defects.

In Figure 3a, we show the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy graphene structure with the $sp^2$ hydrogen saturation after optimization. We find that the lattice relaxation is suppressed, and the displacement of carbon atoms are much less than the case without hydrogen saturation (see Figure 2a). The imbalance between the two sublattices are thus maintained. Our results further indicate that the magnetic moment is $4\mu_B$ for the present structure, which can be understood from the electron structure (see Figure 3b and c). We can see from Figure 3b that there are four $\alpha$ electrons occupying the midgap states. It is the four $\alpha$ electrons that contribute $4\mu_B$ magnetic moment. Figure 3c further shows that the hydrogen saturation can eliminate the previous six localized $sp^2$ dangling bond states (see Figure 1c). The $\alpha$ electrons can not be canceled out by the $\beta$ electrons, which directly leads to magnetism. This result is also in agreement with Lieb's theorem. Because the imbalance between the two sublattices is $3-1=2$ for each D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defect, Lieb's theorem gives a total spin of $S=\frac{1}{2}\times2=1$, which directly leads to a local magnetic moment of $2\mu_B$. Hence the total magnetic moment of the supercell including two D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects equals $4\mu_B$.

Moreover, we would like to point out that, after hydrogen saturation, the magnetic momentum may not equal to the one without atomic relaxation and hydrogen saturation. This is because the hydrogen saturation eliminates the dangling bonds and alters the electronic structures and distribution of the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ electrons. The difference between the occupied numbers of the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ electrons, which determines the magnetism, is also changed. Specially, the magnetic momentums of structures shown in Figures 1 and 3 are equal. It is just because that all the dangling bonds states lie above the Fermi level in Figure 1, so they do not induce any local magnetic momentum. Furthermore, our calculations for the total energy show that the ferromagnetic state for a large supercell with $|\mathbf{a}_1|=2|\mathbf{a}_2|$ (see Figure 3a) is 0.3 (0.2) eV lower than the nonmagnetic (antiferromagnetic) state. We further construct a new large supercell with $|\mathbf{a}_2|=2|\mathbf{a}_1|$ and calculate its total energy and electronic structures. The identical results with Figure 3 are obtained.

These clearly indicate that the ferromagnetic coupling emerges along the $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ directions. Hence the ground state is ferromagnetic. This conclusion is also supported by Ref. [27] for the single-vacancy defects with hydrogen saturation. The distinct energetic difference of 0.3 or 0.2 eV also indicates a relatively high Curie temperature $T_c$ [28,29].

Based on the following two reasons, we can understand that hydrogenation can stabilize the structure of graphene with vacancy defects. Without hydrogen saturation, the new covalent bonds are formed due to reconstruction of the dangling bonds. The vacancy reconstruction results in the formation of 5-membered rings. The bipartite symmetry of the system is thus broken locally (see Figure 2). If considering hydrogen saturation, the reconstruction is suppressed, and the imbalance between the two sublattices is kept, which is similar to Figure 1. On the other hand, we know from Ref. [27] that the vacancies in graphene can migrate and come together to form large holes. However, after the hydrogen saturation, the migration of vacancies is suppressed. The reason is that the migration of vacancies is mediated by C–C bond breaking and reforming. The hydrogen saturation reduces the number of unsaturated carbon atoms which contribute to the migration of the vacancy. When the structure of graphene with vacancy defects is stabilized by the hydrogen saturation, the TB method can be adopted conveniently. Meanwhile, the famous Lieb's theorem can also be used. For the double- or triple-vacancy graphene with the $sp^2$ hydrogen saturation, the structure can also be stabilized based on our calculations and its magnetism can be analyzed using the Lieb's theorem.

## 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, we investigate the electronic structures of graphene with D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects using first-principles calculations in detail. Our results clearly show that lattice relaxation is remarkable in graphene with vacancy defects. The properties of graphene can be modified significantly. The magnetism of graphene with D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy defects without optimization can even disappear due to lattice relaxation. Both the electron band and DOS are also altered notably by the lattice distortion. Additionally, due to the reconstruction near the D3-symmetry tetra-vacancy, the bipartite symmetry is broken locally. Thus the Lieb's theorem cannot be used in this case. The TB method is also not suitable to investigate the magnetic properties of graphene with true vacancy defects. However, the lattice relaxation can be suppressed if the dangling bonds around the vacancies are passivated by the $sp^2$ hydrogen saturation. Then both the Lieb's theorem and tight-binding method can be adopted. The results obtained in this Letter are useful for designing some important graphene-based nanoscale magnetic and spin electronic devices.
