# Effective parameters on physical properties of nanotube SiC (7,7)

Mohammad Yuonesi

Department of Physics, Ayatollah Amoli Branch, Islamic Azad University, Amol, Iran

myuonesi@gmail.com

## Abstract
In this paper, we engineered the physical properties of the isolated single-walled SiC nanotube (NT) such as (7,7). Through bundling the nanotube to tetragonal crystalline structure or creating the inherent point defects of both Si and C, the physical properties of the isolated single-walled SiC nanotube (7,7) will change notably. In use of the data calculated from the density functional theory using the Quantum SPRESSO Software, we studied the physical properties including the lattice constants, stability, band gap and the direction of translation point, charge density, bulk modulus, magnetization, symmetry number, radial buckling, effective mass and the velocity of carriers. All the data were compared with the pure isolated single-walled SiC nanotube.

Keyword: Density functional theory (DFT), SiC nanotube, Bundle, Semiconductor, Density of state, Point defect

## 1. Introduction
One of the most important properties of finding a material is its stability at a certain temperature. Previously, it was thought that materials with only three-dimensional structures were stable and materials with two-dimensional structures cannot be stable, but nowadays the sheet materials such as graphene, SiC[1], BrN[2], AlN[3], BrN[4] and so on, have been discovered. These materials can form single wall or multi walls tubular structures with zigzag, armchair or along a vector in a specific direction called the chiral vector (n,m), where n and m are integers. These walls can be bundled to form a crystalline structure or be introduced point defects either intrinsically or extrinsically. Each of these methods is a suitable way for engineering materials to control their physical properties. These materials have the abundant applications in electronics, spintronics, and optoelectronics for diodes, transistors and sensors [5,6].

Here, we will study the physical properties of the materials similar to graphene having a sheet of silicon carbide been made into tubular, bundled to form the tetragonal crystalline structure and the intrinsic defects such as atomic vacancy of Si or C. For comparison, all the data were compared to the pure isolated single-walled SiC nanotube. Rotation of nanotubes would break the symmetry of unit cell and consequently changes its physical properties that we didn't consider this effect here [7]

## 2. Computational method
Our calculations were performed in two ways. One is to use the commercial programs (packages) and the other is a self-written program. The packages are a) the Quantum SPRESSO Software, which is an integrated suite of computer codes for electronic-structure calculations and materials modeling, based on the density-functional theory, plane waves, and pseudopotentials (norm-

conserving, ultrasoft, and projector-augmented wave) [8] and b) the xcrysden program [9] for the visualization of molecular and/or crystal structures from the specific input data and for the visualization of properties, e.g., charge densities. All the total energy calculations were accomplished in a super cell 1×1×1 periodic repetition of the conventional unit cell of the tetragonal-p lattice. The ultrasoft pseudo potentials were employed for the atomic potential of silicon atom and carbon atom. The number of the electronic valance of the outer shell for atom C and atom Si, in pseudo potentials are 4 and 4 with orbitals $s^{2}$ and $p^{2}$, respectively. The k-space integration in a uniform mesh (1×1×20), (8×8×8) and (1×1×20) were performed for the isolated single-walled SiC-NT (ISW-SiC-NT), the SW-SiC-NT crystalline bundle and inherent defect at the isolated SW-SiC-NT with the super cell of 28, 28 and 27 atoms by Monkhorst-Pack grids, respectively. None of the atom Si and atom C has the magnetic property. The selected kinetic energy cutoff is 350 eV. The total energies for calculation are converged within 10 μeV. The partial occupancies for each wave function are used according to smearing with value of the gaussian spreading of 0.1 eV for the Brillouin-zone integration in materials. The convergence criterion is satisfied when all components of all forces are smaller than the force convergence threshold. Here, it was selected less than 0.001 Ry/au. Although the electronic properties of SWSiCNT crystalline bundle are strongly correlated with the interlayer spacing due to the Van der Waals correction, here no Van der Waals correction has been included in the calculations

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Structural property

#### 3.1.1. Unit cell
Studying of the physical properties of a material depends on its stability at a fixed temperature. Preventing from duplicating figures, we have only considered the atomic line defect C or Si and the bundled structure for ISW-SiC-NT in figures 1 and 5. There are 28 atoms in a unit cell. A line of atoms C or Si were removed to form a line defect with 3.57 percent. In other word, nanotube is opened with breaking up of the molecular bond Si-C to form the C-C bond at the atomic defect Si as shown in figure 1b. It is an interesting phenomenon because the molecular bond C-C is much stronger than the molecular bond Si-C and Si-Si. Figure 5 shows the unit cell of the bundled SW- SiC-NT (BSW-SiC-NT). It illustrates that the nanotubes form chemical bonds in the x-direction but not in the y-direction. With this reason atom C and atom Si don't sit together in this direction, and there is only the Van der Waals force existence in this direction [10, 11]

### 3.1.2. Lattice constants

To determine the stable condition, we minimized the total energy with respect to the volume change of the unit cell. Figure 2 shows changes of the total energy versus the volume changes of unit cell based on the crystalline structures for a) the isolated single-walled SiC nanotube (ISW-SiC-NT), b) the bundled single-walled SiC nanotube (BSW-SiC-NT), and c) the atomic C line defect for the isolated single-walled (ISW -SiC-NT)c and the atomic Si line defect for the isolated single-walled (ISW-SiC-NT)Si after relaxing. The change of total energy (volume) is the subtraction of the total energy (volume) calculated in the unit cell from the minimum of energy (volume) fitted by Murneghan's equations.

![](./images/813113170376785920_3.jpg)

Figure 2: Minimizing the energy changes versus the volume changes.

The calculated lattice constants were listed in Table 1. The lattice constant of ISW-SiC-NT along the axial direction of nanotube is 3.073 Å. For the BSW-SiC-NT tetragonal crystalline structure, the lattice constants are $a = b =14.738$ Å and $c =3.0737$ Å and the wall-to-wall distance is 2.851 Å. The lattice constants for the atomic defects C and Si in ISW-SiC-NT along the axial direction are 3.063 Å and 3.052 Å, respectively. Preventing the interaction between ISW-SiC-NT nanotubes, the lattice parameters in the x- and y-directions (the cross section) were selected to be 30 Å. It can be seen that the calculated lattice constant of tetragonal bundled crystalline structure is 14.738 A° for both the x- and y-directions. This explains the reason why the single-walled nanotube (7,7) can be bundled to form of tetragonal crystalline structure. The lattice constant of ISW-SiC-NT is larger than that in (ISW-SiC-NT)C and (ISW-SiC-NT)Si. The vacancy decreases the lattice constant and this decrease in the atomic C defect is less than that in the atomic Si defect. It is due to the atomic radius of C (70 pm) is smaller than Si (110 pm). The calculated lattice parameters were fitted by Murneghan's equations [12] which have a good agreement with our raw data [13].

The other important parameter is the translational vector. The translational vector of the ISW-NT is **c** and it is **a+b+c** for the tetragonal bundled crystalline structure. The tubular diameters of all unit cells have been calculated after relaxing. The calculation results for the ISW-NT is 11.84 Å but it is 11.887 Å for the bundled crystalline structure. This difference refers to the Van der Waals force that pulls nanotube from four sides in the tetragonal structure. This stretch increases the tubular diameter of tetragonal unit cell. Creation of vacancy in the SW-NT decreases the tubular diameter because the inner radius is pulled by the atoms on nanotube surface as it increases. The bigger the size of vacancy is, the smaller the tubular diameter will be. It is the natural phenomenon that the tubular diameter of the atomic Si defect becomes smaller than that of the atomic C defect.

### 3.1.4. Buckling
Buckling effect, indicated by $\beta = r_C - r_{Si}$, shows the amount of displacement of the different atoms from the perimeters of circles $r_C$ and $r_{Si}$. Based on Table 1, buckling of ISW-SiC-NT before and after relaxation is zero. Buckling in the atomic C defect and the atomic Si defect ISW-SiC-NT crystalline bundle after relaxing are 0.0322, 0.0395 and 0.0458 Å, respectively. Important factor of buckling is the neutralization of the radius forces which move the different atoms from their sites. As seen, the atomic defect and crystalline bundle increase the amount of buckling. The bigger vacancy size grows the buckling because of the atomic radius of the point defect.

### 3.1.5. Bulk modulus
The bulk modulus is the scale of hardness or some indispensable energy for changing the form of a material. The larger it is, the harder crystal or the stronger chemical bond will be. The bulk modulus was obtained by fitting the calculated data with Murnaghan's state equation [3,14]. The calculated bulk modulus has been shown in Table 1. As seen, the bulk modulus of the ISW-SiC-NT along the tube axis is 828 kbar but for the isolated nanotube with intrinsic defects C and Si along the tube axis are 768 kbar and 781 kbar, respectively. For the bundled nanotube along the tube axis and vertical to the tube axis are 3398 and 872 kbar, respectively. Thus, if the nanotube is bundled to the simple cubic form, its hardness along the tube axis will increase. The isolated single-walled nanotube with

the intrinsic vacancy has the less bulk modulus and the decrease is even more for the C defect samples.

### 3.2. Band structures
#### 3.2.1. Bandgap
Figure 3 illustrates the total density of state (TDOS) and the band gap for all samples. The studies show that rolling, bundling and defect introducing change the optical properties (electronic property) such a band gap of the materials significantly. Among these materials, intrinsic Si-defect ISW-NT is not a semiconductor. The transition of other materials are from the gamma point to z-point (figure 1c) that correspond to the indirect semiconductors. The band gap of the ISW-NT, crystalline bundling and intrinsic defect of atom C are 2.312, 1.503 and 0.99 eV, respectively, with transitions from k-points $0.65\frac{\pi}{c} \to 0.95\frac{\pi}{c}$, $0.68\frac{\pi}{c} \to 0.92\frac{\pi}{c}$ and $0.45\frac{\pi}{c} \to 0.60\frac{\pi}{c}$, respectively. In bundled tube, there is a Van der Waals force, which puts tubes together without any chemical bond. This force not only eliminates the degeneracy of band structure but also spreads the band structures to cause energy dispersion. For all samples, wherever the band structures, having the DOS is zero and the Fermi energy smears to a Gaussian value 0.01, are selected as the origins [11]. However, the absolute value of energy is not important but the change of energy is important. The energy change was considered with respect to its Fermi energy $E_f$. For illustrating how close the Fermi energy to the conduction band or the valance band, we calculated the energy differences of the maximum of the valance band energy ($E_f$-$E_{VBM}$) and the minimum of the conduction band energy ($E_{CBM}$ - $E_f$) to the Fermi energy for a semiconductor and listed in Table 2. If the amount of $E_{CBM}$ - $E_f$ becomes smaller, the Fermi energy is closer to the conduction band. Therefore at BSW-NT and (ISW-NT)$_C$, the Fermi energy tend close to the conduction band.

### 3.2.2. Partial density of state (PDOS)
For determining the role of each atomic orbital in the unit cell, partial density of state (PDOS) accompanying with the total density of state (TDOS) has been drawn in figure 4. As seen, orbital s of atoms Si and C for all the samples is almost bound to their atomic nucleus which play the less important role in forming the valance and the conduction bands. On the other hand, the chemical bonds from the orbital p of atoms Si and C for all the samples are farther from their atomic nucleus and play the more important role in forming the valance and the conduction bands.
Because the energy of the orbital s of all atoms are almost lied between -4 eV and -16 eV that are much lower than the valance band and the conduction band energies (orbital p) between -1 eV and +1 eV. For the ISW-NT and the BSW-NT, their valance bands were formed by the orbital p of atom C and the conduction bands were formed by the orbital p of atom Si. However, the role of orbital p of atom C in the (ISW-NT)ₑ is slightly smaller but hybrid with the orbitals s and p of atom Si, although the more hybridization with the orbital s of atom Si is found than that for the orbital p. The Fermi energy of the (ISW-NT)ᵢ, which isn't a semiconductor, was formed by the orbital p of atom C and atom Si and a small hybridization of the orbital s of atom Si.

### 3.3. Charge density
Charge density shows how the valance electrons of an atom are distributed around it. An isolated atom C has two core electrons of the orbital $1s^{2}$ and four valance electrons from the orbital $2s^{2}$ and the orbital $2p^{2}$. An isolated atom Si has also ten core electrons from orbital $1s^{2}$, orbital $2s^{2}$ and orbital $2p^{6}$ and four valance electrons from the orbital $3s^{2}$ and the orbital $3p^{2}$. Used the pseudo potentials are only for the valance electrons, we found the charge densities change when the SiC molecule is formed. Table 3 shows the charge density of the SiC molecule. As seen, the fractions of charge density from the orbital s and the orbital p of atom C is more than that of atom Si because the more Pauling electronegativity of atom C (2.55) than that of atom Si (1.9) [15].

### 3.4. Chemical bond
For determining the type of the chemical bond, the spatial distribution of the charge density has been drawn for the BSW-SiC-NT in figure 5, which is shown in logarithm scale. It illustrates well that firstly, the chemical bond is formed in the nanotube forms along the $x$-direction but not in the $y$-direction. Perhaps this is the reason why the atom C and the atom Si don't sit together along the $y$-direction. Along the $y$-direction, there is only molecular bonding due to the Van der Waals force. Secondly, the type of chemical bonding is a polar

covalent bond because the more gathering of the charge density at the perimeter of atom C due to its larger electronegativity. Thirdly, the charge density is zero at the tube center. Thus, the chemical bonds of molecule SiC in the whole unit cell are the polar covalent bond. Here, there are two types of the molecular bond $\sigma$ and the molecular bond $\pi$. Three polar covalent bonds are the molecular bond $\sigma$ and one chemical bond will be the molecular bond $\pi$.

### 3.5. Magnetic property
Atoms C and Si are nonmagnetic elements with the electronic structures $[He]2s^{2}-2p^{2}$ and $[Ne]3s^{2}-3p^{2}$, respectively. Calculations show that rolling, crystalline bundling and intrinsic defect have no effect on the magnetic property of the SW-SiC-NT.

### 4. Conclusion
Manufacturing of devices with high quality and low consumption requires to engineer the materials. The physical properties of nanotubes can be engineered with changing the atomic defect or the structural configuration. For observing the changes of the physical properties such as atomic configuration, structure of crystal, optical property, hardness of material, charge density, the calculated data must be compared with that of the pure isolated single-walled nanotube. Our *ab initio* study shows a small change can transform a semiconductor to a none semiconductor and increase or decrease band gap, bulk modulus, total charge, effective mass and velocity of carriers at nanotube. These results may be useful for the practical applications of SiCNT such as doping, storage and absorption phenomena in optoelectronics, spintronics and sensors.
