### Defect-related ferromagnetism in ultrathin metal-free g-C₃N₄ nanosheets†

Daqiang Gao, Qiang Xu, Jing Zhang, Zhaolong Yang, Mingsu Si,\* Zhongjie Yan and Desheng Xue\*

Cite this: *Nanoscale*, 2014, 6, 2577

Received 23rd September 2013
Accepted 22nd December 2013

DOI: 10.1039/c3nr04743a

www.rsc.org/nanoscale

Ultrathin metal-free g-C₃N₄ nanosheets with intrinsic room temperature ferromagnetism were synthesized by heating urea in an airtight container at different temperatures. Results indicate that the samples' saturation magnetization increases with the carbon defect concentration, revealing its carbon defect related ferromagnetism. Moreover, we further confirmed the defect induced ferromagnetic nature by *ab initio* calculations. It is believed that this finding highlights a new promising material toward realistic metal-free spintronic application.

Since the discovery of the exotic properties of graphene, two-dimensional (2D) layered materials such as metal chalcogenides,¹,² transition metal oxides,³,⁴ insulator hexagonal boron nitride,⁵ topological insulators of Bi₂Te₃ (Sb₂Se₃, Bi₂Se₃),⁶,⁷ silicon- or germanium-based counterparts of grapheme,⁸,⁹ and other 2D compounds¹⁰⁻¹² have gained renewed interest. The common feature of these layered materials is that the bulk 3D crystals are stacked structures, where they involve van der Waals interactions between adjacent sheets with strong covalent bonding. Results indicated that such materials span the entire range of electronic structures, from insulator to metal, and are important in various applications such as optoelectronics, spintronics, catalysts, chemical and biological sensors, supercapacitors, solar cells, lithium ion batteries and so on.¹³⁻¹⁶

Graphite-like carbon nitride (g-C₃N₄) also belongs to the layered materials, where the 2D g-C₃N₄ nanosheets have drawn much scientific interest due to their potential applications in solar energy conversion, photosynthesis, electrocatalysis, bioimaging application, and photocatalytic performance.¹⁷⁻²⁰ In addition, 2D g-C₃N₄ has the ability of water splitting and organic pollutant degradation under visible light irradiation.²¹ However, in most of these existing pristine properties, no magnetic ordering is expected because it is predicted by first-principles calculation that the perfect g-C₃N₄ is nonmagnetic, limiting its applications in next-generation metal-free spintronics.²² Fortunately, there are various alternative experimental approaches of magnetic modulation to induce magnetism: recently, based on g-C₃N₄, some magnetic structures were predicted; by using first-principles calculations, *Li et al.* showed that when patterned in the form of a kagome lattice, nonmagnetic g-C₃N₄ can not only become ferromagnetic but also its magnetic properties can be further enhanced by applying external strain.²³ In addition, a new type of graphitic carbon nitride (g-C₄N₃) was synthesized experimentally²⁴ and was investigated to possess magnetic properties by theoretical calculations.²⁵ Results indicate that substitution of the N atom with a C atom in nonmagnetic g-C₃N₄ will inject a hole into the graphitic C₃N₄, which may significantly alter its electronic and magnetic properties, that is, g-C₄N₃ shows metal-free ferromagnetism and intrinsic half metallicity. What is more, results indicate that hydrogenation could also cause ferromagnetism in g-C₃N₄ apart from hole doping.²⁶ Such tuneable electron spin-polarization and ferromagnetism in g-C₃N₄ are quite promising for its applications in spintronics.

Though lots of reports about the ferromagnetism of the modified g-C₃N₄ were predicted,²³⁻²⁶ experimental evidence is rare. In this letter, ultrathin metal-free g-C₃N₄ nanosheets were synthesised by a one step thermal decomposition method and the room temperature ferromagnetism of the g-C₃N₄ nanosheets is reported. Results indicate that the observed ferromagnetism is related to the carbon insufficiency in the samples, which is also confirmed by the first-principles calculations. This finding provides a new promising material toward realistic metal-free spintronics.

### Characterization
X-ray diffraction (XRD, X'Pert PRO PHILIPS with Cu Kα radiation) was employed to study the crystal structure. The morphology of the samples was studied by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-4800) and a high resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM, TecnaiTM G2 F30, FEI, USA). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, VG ESCALAB 210) was utilized to determine the bonding characteristics of the samples. The composition was confirmed using an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP, ER/S). An elemental analyzer (Vario EL) was employed to study the C/N rations of the samples. The infrared absorption spectra of the samples were recorded by the KBr pellet method on a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; NEXUS 670, Thermo Nicolet Corp., Madison, WI, USA) in the range of 500 to 3500 cm⁻¹. The magnetic properties were measured using a Quantum Design MPMS magnetometer based on a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID).

### Calculation details
The electronic properties were studied *via* VASP code, which is based on density functional theory with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) for the exchange-correlation (XC) potential within the projector augmented-wave method. The cutoff energy of plane waves is set to be 400 eV, and the vacuum space is at least 15 Å, which is large enough to avoid the interaction between periodic images. A 9 × 9 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack grid is used for the sampling of the Brillouin zone during geometry optimization and a higher 15 × 15 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack grid for self-consistent calculations. All the atoms in the unit cell were allowed to relax, and the convergence of force is set to 0.01 eV Å⁻¹. The model of a 2 × 2 supercell with one carbon atom removed was used to simulate the electronic and magnetic properties.

## Results and discussion
XRD was used to characterize the phase structure of the final products. Only two distinct diffraction peaks can be found for all the samples shown in Fig. 1a, which can be indexed to the hexagonal phase of g-C₃N₄ (JCPDS 87-1526). The peak at 13.02° corresponds to in-plane ordering of tri-s-triazine units, while the strongest peak at 27.38° is due to the stacking of the conjugated aromatic system, which can be indexed to the graphitic materials as the (002) peak and corresponds well to the interlayer d-spacing (0.336 nm) of the g-C₃N₄.²⁷,²⁸ Further observation indicates that the (002) peak shifts toward higher diffraction angles from S450 of 27.2° to S525 of 27.7° with increased heating-temperatures, indicating that the crystal structure of g-C₃N₄ tends to become more stable under high temperature. Formation of g-C₃N₄ with the tri-s-triazine building units was also confirmed by FTIR and the results are shown in Fig. 1b. The intense band at 810.1 cm⁻¹ represented the out-of-plane bending vibration characteristic of heptazine rings, which is the characteristic mode of the triazine units of g-C₃N₄.²⁹ Besides, the intense bands at 1630.5, 1569.4, 1462.1 and 1418.2 cm⁻¹ were assigned to typical stretching vibration modes of heptazine-derived repeating units. The bands at 1323.8 and 1256.1 cm⁻¹ corresponded to the stretching vibration of connected units of C-N(-C)-C (full condensation) or C-NH-C (partial condensation), which was also supported by the stretching vibration modes of the broad weak band around 3300 cm⁻¹ for hydrogen-bonding interactions.³⁰ The microstructure of the samples was also investigated. One can clearly see the homogeneous sheetlike structure of the materials with numerous mesopores of several tens of nanometres in size, introducing more edge states and defects in the samples, which will be discussed later. The above results indicate that pure phase and ultrathin g-C₃N₄ nanosheets were obtained in our case.

The key finding in our case is the experimental observation of room temperature ferromagnetism in ultrathin metal-free g-$C_3N_4$ nanosheets. The magnetization versus magnetic field ($M-H$) curves for the synthesized metal-free g-$C_3N_4$ nanosheets (S450) measured at 10 and 300 K are shown in Fig. 3a. As can be seen from the pristine $M-H$ curves, the g-$C_3N_4$ nanosheets show the ferromagnetism signal in the lower field region mixture with the diamagnetic (DM) signal in the high-field region. After deducting the DM signal, the measured saturation magnetization ($M_s$) and coercive force ($H_c$) of sample S450 are 0.0093 (0.0076) emu g$^{-1}$ and 96 (45) Oe at 10 (300) K, respectively, which are comparable to those obtained for other dopant-free diluted magnetic semiconductors.$^{34}$ The temperature dependent magnetization measurements for g-$C_3N_4$ nanosheets (S450) recorded at 50 Oe are shown in Fig. 3b. Note that the two curves are separated in the whole measured temperature range, revealing that the Curie temperature of the sample is expected to exceed 330 K. In addition, the ZFC curve shows no blocking temperature in the range of 2-330 K, indicating that there are no ferromagnetic clusters in the sample. Besides, ICP results also ruled out the possibility of the existence of metal element in the samples (Table S1 in the ESI$\dagger$), therefore, the ferromagnetism of the ultrathin metal-free g-$C_3N_4$ nanosheets is the intrinsic nature and the observed ferromagnetism in our case is considered to be other factor, such as vacancies like other metal-free systems.$^{35,36}$ TEM results show the porous structure of the nanosheets and the edge states of such pores will lead to lots of possible defects, which may introduce ferromagnetism.$^{37,38}$ Besides, XPS results reveal the obvious carbon insufficiency in the samples, so the observed ferromagnetism is considered to be related to carbon vacancies. The relationship between ferromagnetism and the carbon vacancies of the samples obtained by heating the urea at different temperatures is further studied. As can be seen from Fig. 3c, S-like $M-H$ curves with clear hysteresis for samples S475, S500 and S525 were also observed, indicating their room temperature FM nature. In addition, the $M_s$ of the four samples decreases gradually with the increase of heating temperature. Here, an elemental analyzer was employed to study the C/N ratio of the samples quantitatively. It can be seen from Fig. 3d that sample's $M_s$ decreases with the increase of the C/N ratio, further revealing its carbon defect related ferromagnetism.

To understand the carbon vacancy-related ferromagnetic ordering observed in the experiment, first-principles calculations based on the density functional theory were carried out. For g-$C_3N_4$, there are two non-equivalent carbon atoms exist in the unit cell: one is the nearest neighbour carbon atom from the cantered nitrogen atom and the other is the next nearest neighbour carbon atom. Our first-principles calculations show that only the latter contributes net magnetic moments when one of these carbon atoms is removed (shown in Fig. 4a). Here, we use a $(2 \times 2)$ supercell with this type of carbon vacancy to study the magnetic properties of g-$C_3N_4$. Results indicate that the local structure undergoes a dramatic change accordingly as one carbon atom is removed: the original hexagonal ring is transformed into the pentagonal one as the two nitrogen atoms near the carbon vacancy bond directly. In contrast, the third nitrogen atom is far away from those two bonding nitrogen atoms, where there is no obvious bonding among them, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. This above structural change results in spin polarized states with a net magnetic moment of $2.0\ \mu_B$ (shown in Fig. 4b). The electronic structure results indicate that these spin-polarized states only occur near the Fermi level. In the case of the spin-up state, there is no band crossing the Fermi level, as shown in Fig. 4c. However, for the spin-down case, three flat bands cross the Fermi level as shown in Fig. 4e.

Thus, a semiconductor feature corresponds to the spin-up state while a typical metallic character corresponds to the spin-down state. This is a complete, 100%, spin-polarized material, which can be also seen in Fig. 4d. It implies that g-C₃N₄ with carbon vacancies has potential applications in spintronics.

Based on the above discussion, these magnetic moments correspond to ferromagnetic coupling through the spin-polar- ized conduction electrons at the spin-down branch. The remaining electrons localised on the dangling bonds have two aspects: one contributes the magnetic moments and the other acts as the conduction electron which play a vital role in the formation of ferromagnetic ordering. Thus, the underlying exchange mechanism is very similar to the case of RKKY interactions.³⁹,⁴⁰ This is the direct manifestation of room ferromagnetism observed in experiments.

## Conclusions

In summary, we reported intrinsic carbon defect related room temperature ferromagnetism in ultrathin metal-free g-C₃N₄ nanosheets, which was also confirmed by first-principles calculations. This discovery provides a new system to induce and manipulate magnetic moments in ultrathin metal-free nanosheets, which have potential applications in spintronics.
