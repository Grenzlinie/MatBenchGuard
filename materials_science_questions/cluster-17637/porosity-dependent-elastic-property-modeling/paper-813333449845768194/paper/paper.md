According to the GN model, the effective elastic constants, $c_{ij}$, of a SL made of two alternating layers of arbitrary symmetry can be expressed as a function of the elastic constants of the constituent layers $c_{ij}^{[\zeta]}$ and the thickness fractions $f^{[\zeta]}=d^{[\zeta]}/(d^{[1]}+d^{[2]})$ where $\zeta=1,2$ [4]. For layers of cubic symmetry, each with independent elastic constants $c_{11}^{[\zeta]}, c_{12}^{[\zeta]}$ and $c_{44}^{[\zeta]}$, as is the case here for p-Si films formed from $\mathrm{p}^{+}$ substrates [17, 18], the SL elastic constants tensor $c$ has six independent elastic constants and is of the form [19]

$$
c=\left[\begin{array}{cccccc}
c_{11} & c_{12} & c_{13} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
c_{12} & c_{11} & c_{13} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
c_{13} & c_{13} & c_{33} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & c_{44} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & c_{44} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & c_{66}
\end{array}\right]. \tag{3}
$$

The SL is therefore elastically equivalent to a tetragonal crystal. The effective elastic constants are of the following forms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{11}= & -\frac{f^{[1]} \frac{c_{12}^{[2]}-c_{12}^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}\left(c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}+c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[2]}\right)}{\left(f^{[1]}+f^{[2]}\right)\left(\frac{c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+f^{[2]}\right)} \\
& +\frac{f^{[1]}\left(c_{12}^{[1]}+c_{11}^{[1]}\left(-\frac{c_{12}^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+\frac{c_{12}^{[2]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}\right)+c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[2]}\right.}{f^{[1]}+f^{[2]}},
\end{aligned} \tag{4a}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{12}= & -\frac{f^{[1]} \frac{c_{12}^{[2]}-c_{12}^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}\left(c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}+c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[2]}\right)}{\left(f^{[1]}+f^{[2]}\right)\left(\frac{c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+f^{[2]}\right)} \\
& +\frac{f^{[1]}\left(c_{12}^{[1]}+c_{12}^{[1]}\left(-\frac{c_{12}^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+\frac{c_{12}^{[2]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}\right)+c_{12}^{[2]} f^{[2]}\right.}{f^{[1]}+f^{[2]}}
\end{aligned} \tag{4b}
$$

$$
c_{13}=\frac{\frac{c_{12}^{[1]} c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+c_{12}^{[2]} f^{[2]}}{\frac{c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+f^{[2]}}, \tag{4c}
$$

$$
c_{33}=\frac{c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}+c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[2]}}{\frac{c_{11}^{[2]} f^{[1]}}{c_{11}^{[1]}}+f^{[2]}}, \tag{4d}
$$

$$
c_{44}=\frac{c_{44}^{[2]} f^{[1]}+c_{44}^{[2]} f^{[2]}}{\frac{c_{44}^{[2]} f^{[1]}}{c_{44}^{[1]}}+f^{[2]}}, \tag{4e}
$$

$$
c_{66}=\frac{c_{44}^{[1]} f^{[1]}+c_{44}^{[2]} f^{[2]}}{f^{[1]}+f^{[2]}}. \tag{4f}
$$

For the purposes of the discussion below, the relevant coordinate system is oriented so that the $z$-axis is normal to the SL layers and the $x$- and $y$-axes lie in the plane of the layers. In this case, the velocities of the pure transverse (T) and mixed (QL and QT) acoustic modes propagating at angle $\theta'=\sin ^{-1}(\sin \theta_{\mathrm{i}} / n)$ to the $z$ axis in a material of tetragonal symmetry are given by

$$
v_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\left(c_{44} \cos ^{2} \theta^{\prime}+c_{66} \sin ^{2} \theta^{\prime}\right)^{1 / 2} \tag{5}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{ \pm}= & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \rho}}\left(c_{11} \sin ^{2} \theta^{\prime}+c_{33} \cos ^{2} \theta^{\prime}+c_{44}\right. \\
\pm & {\left[\left(\left(c_{11}-c_{44}\right) \sin ^{2} \theta^{\prime}+\left(c_{44}\right.\right.\right.} \\
& \left.\left.\left.\left.-c_{33}\right) \cos ^{2} \theta^{\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(c_{13}+c_{44}\right)^{2} \sin ^{2}\left(2 \theta^{\prime}\right)\right]^{1 / 2}\right)^{1 / 2},
\end{aligned} \tag{6}
$$

respectively, where $\rho$ is the mass density [19]. Equations (5) and (6) can be reduced to the corresponding equations for cubic systems if $c_{11}=c_{33}, c_{44}=c_{66}$ and $c_{12}=c_{13}$.

### 3. Experimental details

The p-Si samples used in this study were prepared by electrochemical etching of $\sim 500 \mu \mathrm{m}$ thick (100)-oriented B-doped (resistivity $0.005-0.02 \Omega \mathrm{cm}$ ) crystalline silicon (c-Si) wafers in an electrolyte consisting of $149 \%$ HF: 1 $\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{H}_{5} \mathrm{OH}$ by volume. A copper sheet in contact with the back side of the wafer and connected to the positive terminal of a programmable constant current source served as the working electrode. The counter electrode was a platinum wire loop of diameter comparable to that of the opening of the cell $(\sim 13 \mathrm{~mm})$. Etching was done in the dark, at room temperature. Prior to etching each sample underwent a $60 \mathrm{~s}$ dip in $49 \% \mathrm{HF}$ to remove native oxide. After the etch, each sample was rinsed under running water and immersed for $1 \mathrm{~min}$ in pentane.

Calculation of SL elastic properties from the GN model requires knowledge of the constituent layer elastic constants. Several single layer p-Si films of various porosities were therefore first prepared and their elastic properties characterized using Brillouin light scattering. Binary p-Si SLs were then constructed with constituent layers prepared under the same conditions (same etching current density, electrolyte and parent c-Si substrate) as the single layer films.

Etching conditions and resulting single layer film parameters are given in table 1. The etching current, $I$, was fixed at a value between 15 and $300 \mathrm{~mA}$ and the etch times were chosen so that the film thickness was $\sim 5 \mu \mathrm{m}$. Film porosity, $\xi$, was obtained using a gravimetric method [20] and ranged from $33 \%$ to $72 \%$. The relation $\rho_{\text {p-Si }}=\rho_{\text {c-Si }}(1-\xi)$, where $\rho_{\text {c-Si }}$ is the density of c-Si, was used to calculate the effective film density from the porosity. The refractive indices of the films, $n$, were obtained from the two-component Bruggeman effective medium model [21]. Etch rates, $e_{r}$, for each current were determined by measuring the total film thickness by scanning electron microscopy and dividing by the total etch time. Knowledge of these rates provided control of the SL constituent layer thickness via the etch time.

The SLs were made by periodically alternating the current between two of the values ($I^{[1]}$ and $I^{[2]}$) used above for single layer film formation. The etching current $I^{[1]}$, etching time $t^{[1]}$, and hence the porosity and thickness of one of the layers ($\xi^{[1]} = 59\%$, $d^{[1]} = 10$ nm), were kept the same for all SLs. Etching currents $I^{[2]}$ and times $t^{[2]}$ for the other SL constituent layer were chosen to obtain porosities $\xi^{[2]} \neq 59\%$ and layer thicknesses $d^{[2]} \sim 10$–20 nm. The number of current cycles (250) was chosen so that the SL was a few micrometres thick. Scanning electron microscopy measurements of the total SL thickness provided indirect confirmation of the constituent layer thicknesses. The average SL porosity and density were determined from the relations $\xi_{\text{ave}} = f^{[1]}\xi^{[1]} + f^{[2]}\xi^{[2]}$ and $\rho_{\text{ave}} = \rho_{\text{c-Si}}(1 - \xi_{\text{ave}})$, respectively. As for the single layer films, the SL refractive indices were determined from the two-component Bruggeman model [21]. Table 2 summarizes the fabrication conditions for the non-59% porous constituent layers and resulting SL average porosity and refractive index.

BLS experiments were performed using $180^\circ$ backscattering geometry with 532 nm light provided by a single mode $\text{Nd:YVO}_4$ laser. All experiments were carried out in ambient conditions with spectrum collection times up to 24 h ($\sim$125 000 scans). The incident light was polarized in the plane of incidence and had a power of $\sim$50 mW. The penetration depth was estimated to be $\sim 2\ \mu$m [22]. Depending on $\theta_{\text{i}}$, the scattered light was collected using $f/2.8$ or $f/4.0$ optics, focused on a $450\ \mu$m pinhole, and subsequently analysed using a tandem $(3+3)$-pass Fabry–Perot interferometer. After exiting the interferometer, the light passed through a $700\ \mu$m pinhole before being detected by a photomultiplier with a dark count rate of $\leqslant 1\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The external angle of incidence was changed from $\sim 10^\circ$ to $\sim 80^\circ$, which is equivalent to a range of $\sim 5^\circ$ to $\sim 30^\circ$ to the [0 0 1] direction inside a sample. This range was deliberately chosen to be as wide as possible to allow investigation of the directional dependence of the Brillouin peak frequency shifts and consequently that of the bulk phonon velocities.

## 4. Results and discussion

### 4.1. Single layer films

Figure 1 shows representative spectra collected from the single layer p-Si films. The peak labelled R is attributed to the Rayleigh surface mode because its frequency shift is the smallest of all observed peaks and because it increases linearly with $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$. Due to the latter, for small $\theta_{\text{i}}$ this peak is not visible in the spectra either because it is swamped by the central elastic peak or because it lies within the spectral range centred on this elastic peak that is shuttered to prevent saturation of the photomultiplier tube. The peaks labelled QT and QL are attributed to quasi-transverse and quasi-longitudinal bulk phonons, respectively, and are the main focus of this work. This assignment is based on several observations. Firstly, the frequency shifts of these peaks show only a weak dependence on $\theta_{\text{i}}$ and, hence, they cannot be due to surface modes. Secondly, no additional peaks were observed in any of the spectra for frequency shifts larger than $\sim$60 GHz, which is the shift assigned to the QL mode for the sample with the smallest porosity. Interestingly, it is also noted that the intensity of the QT (QL) peak increases (decreases) with increasing $\theta_{\text{i}}$. The frequencies of the R, QT and QL modes were obtained from the spectra by fitting Lorentzian functions to the corresponding Brillouin peaks (see figure 2).

In general, spectra collected from low porosity films ($\xi < 50\%$) are of poorer quality than those collected from films of higher porosities (see figure 1), with the result that for some of the low porosity samples, some expected peaks are not observed. The improved signal-to-noise ratio for the higher porosity films may be due to a decrease in optical extinction coefficient [23] and an associated increase in the elasto-optic contribution to the scattering due to the increased scattering volume [24]. In addition, in all of the spectra apart from those collected from the 33% porous film, a broad shoulder on the Rayleigh mode peak can be seen. It covers the frequency range from $\sim$7 GHz to $\sim$20 GHz for low porosity films and $\sim$5 GHz to $\sim$15 GHz for those of higher porosity. Its intensity and width vary depending on the sample and $\theta_{\text{i}}$. This feature is similar to that observed in Brillouin scattering experiments on GaAs, Cr and polycrystalline metals [25] with the exception that, in some of the spectra of the current work, reasonably well-defined peaks can be distinguished in the shoulder region (denoted $\text{S}_{\text{i}}$, though not labelled in figure 1 to avoid confusion). These peaks could be due to the anisotropic character of the p-Si layer [26]; however, more work is needed to definitively reveal their origin(s).

Figure 3 shows representative Brillouin peak frequency shift versus $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ data for several of the single layer films (figures 3(a)–(d)). Also shown are corresponding plots of the directional dependence of the bulk phonon velocities obtained from equation (1) and the $\nu_{\text{QT}}$ and $\nu_{\text{QL}}$ versus $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ data (figures 3(e)–(h)). The Rayleigh surface phonon velocity for each film (see table 3) was determined by fitting equation (2) to shift versus $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ data for the peak of lowest frequency shift. Typical fits are indicated by the dashed lines in figures 3(a)–(d). It is also noted that the $\text{S}_{\text{i}}$ modes, observed in the spectral region $\nu_{\text{R}} \leqslant \nu \leqslant \nu_{\text{QT}}$, appear to either be independent of $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ or to depend roughly linearly on it over the range studied (see dotted lines of best-fit in figures 3(a)–(d)).

### 4.2. Superlattices

Figure 5 shows sets of spectra collected from three different p-Si SLs. As is the case for the single layer films, better quality spectra are obtained from SLs of higher average porosity than from those of lower average porosity. The mode assignment is based on the same grounds as for the single layers films and is indicated above the topmost spectrum of each set. In addition to the peaks identified, some well-defined, narrow peaks, at frequency shifts between the R and QT modes were observed in some spectra. These peaks show a linear dependence on $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ and resemble features observed in Brillouin spectra of thin metallic $\sim 0.5\,\mu\text{m}$ thick SLs [33]. Similar narrow and sharp peaks were also observed by Carlotti *et al* [34] in spectra collected from a transparent ZnO film of hexagonal symmetry. In both of these reports this peak is associated with the 'leaky' longitudinal mode (in Dutcher's work they are identified as originating due to longitudinal guided modes) with velocities that depend on propagation direction and strongly on $c_{11}$. The problem with direct comparison is that, despite similarities in the lineshape and $\theta_{\text{i}}$ dependence, the p-Si SLs studied here are an order of magnitude thicker than those in [33] and [34] and hence peaks due to individual guided modes are not expected [35]. For simplicity these modes are labelled $S_{\text{i}}$ and that is how they are referred to from here on.

Figure 6 presents plots of the peak frequency shifts versus $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ (6(a)–(c)) and the corresponding directional dependence of the bulk mode velocities (6(d)–(f)) for the p-Si SLs. In general, the $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ dependence of the various modes mimics that seen for the single layer films, with the exception of the S mode observed in single layer film spectra that is virtually independent of $\sin\theta_{\text{i}}$ (see figure 3).

Four of six SL elastic constants ($c_{11}$, $c_{13}$, $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$) were obtained by global fitting of equation (6) to data like that shown in figures 6(d)–(f). As for single layer films, the QT mode was fitted as opposed to the T mode because it gave more precise elastic constants. This, however, may be related to the fact that $v_{-}$ and $v_{+}$ are functions of the same four constants, $c_{11}$, $c_{13}$, $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$, in contrast to $v_{\text{T}}$, which depends only on $c_{44}$ and $c_{66}$. It is noted that for propagation directions near the $z$-axis, the QT and T mode velocities are nearly the same (QT and T are degenerate for $\theta_{\text{i}}' = 0$). Initial guesses (and, likely, very good estimates) of $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$ were obtained by taking the velocities of the QL and QT modes propagating at $\theta_{\text{i}}' \sim 2^\circ$ to be equal to their values at $\theta_{\text{i}}' = 0^\circ$: $v_{+} = \sqrt{c_{33}/\rho}$ and $v_{-} = v_{\text{T}} = \sqrt{c_{44}/\rho}$ (see equation (5) and equation (6)). The values of the $c_{ij}$ were allowed to vary freely, with the only constraints being those imposed by the stability conditions for a tetragonal system: $c_{11} > 0$ and $c_{44} > 0$ [36] (referred to hereafter as the 'free fit'). As $v_{-}$ and $v_{+}$ are not functions of $c_{12}$ and $c_{66}$, these constants were not determined. Finally, it is noted that although a SL created using two layers of cubic symmetry should have tetragonal symmetry [4], as a check, a fit of analogous expressions for the more general case of orthorhombic symmetry [19] was also done but resulted in a larger value of chi squared.

Table 4 shows the SL elastic constants determined from the fitting procedure described above. Also shown, for the purposes of comparison, are complete sets of elastic constants for SLs with constituent layers matching those of the actual SLs calculated from the GN model (via (4b)–(4f)) [4]. The constituent layer elastic constants required for the calculations were obtained from table 3. It can be seen from table 4 that the free-fit results have $c_{11} \leqslant c_{33}$, whereas the model, in general, predicts $c_{11} \geqslant c_{33}$. This fact was used as the rationale for carrying out a second fit (referred to hereafter as the 'constrained fit') with the additional constraint $c_{11} \geqslant c_{33}$ on the fitted values of $c_{ij}$ to see if better agreement could be obtained between model and experiment. Apart from the model prediction, there is otherwise no obvious physical reason why such a relationship should hold. The elastic constants determined from this fit are also given in table 4. For both the free and constrained fits, fixing $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$ to values determined directly from the small-$\theta_{\text{i}}'$ velocities via the relations $v_{+} = \sqrt{c_{33}/\rho}$ and $v_{-} = v_{\text{T}} = \sqrt{c_{44}/\rho}$ resulted in a slight decrease in the uncertainty associated with $c_{11}$ and $c_{13}$ but did not affect their values to any significant extent.

The qualitative similarity of the $S_{\text{i}}$ spectral peaks to those seen in other studies suggests that they could be due to longitudinal guided modes. With this assumption, values of $c_{11}$ were estimated via the relationship $c_{11} \approx \rho v_{\text{S}_{\text{i}}}^2$ [33, 34] and are given in table 4 together with values of $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$ calculated directly from small-$\theta_{\text{i}}'$ velocities (referred to as 'direct' values).

For both $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$, good agreement is obtained between the GN model, both fits, and the values obtained directly from the low $\theta_{i}'$ velocities. For all of the other SLs, imposition of this constraint in the fitting process resulted in the value of $c_{11}$ reaching the lower limit imposed on it (i.e. the value of $c_{33}$). The obtained value of $c_{11}$ does, however, agree within uncertainty with that calculated using GN model, especially for samples with a high porosity non-59% porous layer (hereafter to as the ‘variable layer’). This result suggests decreased anisotropy and the equivalence of all three crystallographic axes, as for cubic material $c_{11}=c_{33}$ ([19]). Unfortunately, the fact that the value of $c_{11}$ reaches the imposed limit decreases the credibility of the result. Without the constraint $c_{11}>c_{33}$, the results for all samples other than the 59–33% SL suggest significant anisotropy in the tetragonal SL, with values of $c_{11}$ smaller than those of $c_{33}$.

In figure 7(b) the values of elastic constant $c_{11}$ are plotted as a function of SL average porosity. The values of $c_{11}$ obtained from the constrained fit show much better agreement with the model results than those for the free fit. On the other hand, the free-fit $c_{11}$ show reasonable agreement (within $\sim$10%) with values of $c_{11}$ calculated directly from the velocity of the $S_{1}$ mode. This degree of agreement is similar to that obtained from analogous calculations done in Brillouin scattering studies of thin metallic SLs [33].

The values of $c_{13}$ obtained from the constrained fit show better agreement with the model values for SLs with higher average porosity than for those of lower average porosity. The lack of agreement between the experimental and model values in SLs with variable layer of lower porosity is, at least partially, a consequence of the difficulties in obtaining the elastic constants for those layers. The elastic constant $c_{13}$ is strongly dependent on $c_{12}^{[\zeta]}$, which was the one determined with the lowest accuracy and, in the case of low porosity layers, has significant associated uncertainty (see table 3). The values of $c_{13}$ obtained in the free fit do not, in general, agree with the corresponding model values.

As the porosity of the variable constituent layer approaches higher values, the effective elastic constants tensor obtained using the GN model for the SLs changes from tetragonal symmetry to cubic with $c_{11}\approx c_{33}$, $c_{12}\approx c_{13}$ and $c_{44}\approx c_{66}$. This is likely related to the decrease of the difference between the elastic constants of the constituent layers. The difference between model values of appropriate pairs of elastic constants ($c_{11}$ and $c_{33}$, $c_{12}$ and $c_{13}$ and $c_{44}$ and $c_{66}$, respectively) goes to zero as the difference between values of the transverse and longitudinal elastic constants of the constituent layers, $c_{44}^{[\zeta]}$ and $c_{11}^{[\zeta]}$, decreases. In the same limit ($\Delta c_{ij}^{[\zeta]}\to0$) the anisotropy ratio $c_{11}/c_{33}$, approaches unity. This result could be explained by the fact that for more elastically similar constituent layers, less stress and strain exists at the interlayer boundaries.

A way of checking whether the lack of agreement between the values of $c_{11}$ and $c_{13}$ obtained from the free fit and the model is associated with their uncertainties is to evaluate the elastic constants of the variable layer from equation (4a). The elastic constants $c_{11}$, $c_{13}$, $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$ obtained from the free fit and the elastic constants $c_{11}^{[1]}$, $c_{12}^{[1]}$ and $c_{44}^{[1]}$ of the layer with $\xi=59\%$ were substituted into the expressions for $c_{11}$, $c_{13}$, $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$ according to equation (4b), equation (4c), equation (4d) and equation (4e). Negative values were obtained for $c_{44}^{[2]}$, violating one of the stability conditions for cubic symmetry ($c_{44}>0$) [27]. Moreover, physically unreasonable values for $f^{[\zeta]}$ were obtained (larger than unity or very close to 0).

The good overall agreement between values of $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$ determined from the model, both fits and those calculated directly from the small-$\theta_{i}'$ velocity data suggests that the model holds promise as a means for accurately predicting the elastic properties of p-Si SLs. There is no obvious reason, however, why the model does not predict values for $c_{11}$ and $c_{13}$ that are similar to those obtained from the free fit. It is conceivable that the model values of $c_{11}$ and $c_{13}$ have not been accurately determined due to inaccuracies in the constituent layer elastic constants $c_{ij}^{[\zeta]}$ or thicknesses (or equivalently, the $f^{[\zeta]}$ that served as input for the model. The problem may also be related to the boundary conditions at the interfaces between the constituent layers, as the system was created by etching a solid crystalline wafer as opposed to deposition of individual layers on a free surface, creating strains and stresses as the structure is created. This process could also result in imperfect interfaces (rough or of finite thickness) between constituent layers whereas the GN model assumes perfect, planar interfaces. ## 5. Conclusions

Based on the results presented in this work the GN model holds promise for predicting the elastic properties of p-Si SLs. While good agreement was found between the model and experimental elastic constants $c_{33}$ and $c_{44}$, only crude agreement exists for $c_{11}$ and $c_{13}$.

These studies show that it is possible to tailor the elastic constants of p-Si SLs, making possible the creation of structures of the same effective density, but possessing different elastic properties.
