# Analysis of the compressive behaviour of the three-dimensional printed porous titanium for dental implants using a modified cellular solid model

Graham Gagg¹, Elaheh Ghassemieh² and Florencia E Wiria³

## Abstract
A set of cylindrical porous titanium test samples were produced using the three-dimensional printing and sintering method with samples sintered at 900 °C, 1000 °C, 1100 °C, 1200 °C or 1300 °C. Following compression testing, it was apparent that the stress–strain curves were similar in shape to the curves that represent cellular solids. This is despite a relative density twice as high as what is considered the threshold for defining a cellular solid. As final sintering temperature increased, the compressive behaviour developed from being elastic–brittle to elastic–plastic and while Young's modulus remained fairly constant in the region of 1.5 GPa, there was a corresponding increase in 0.2% proof stress of approximately 40–80 MPa. The cellular solid model consists of two equations that predict Young's modulus and yield or proof stress. By fitting to experimental data and consideration of porous morphology, appropriate changes to the geometry constants allow modification of the current models to predict with better accuracy the behaviour of porous materials with higher relative densities (lower porosity).

## Keywords
Cellular model, porous titanium implant, micro-computed tomography, morphology, compressive behaviour

## Introduction
Cellular solids are a class of material characterised by a porous or ‘cellular’ morphology with a relative density of 0.3 or less.¹,² In addition to encompassing a wide range of engineered materials, cellular solids are also widely found in nature.³ The materials prove useful as the cellular structure allows modification of the mechanical properties resulting in the ability to customise materials for a wider range of applications.⁴ Modification of the mechanical properties to effectively meet specifications has been enabled in no small part by the extensive work on cellular solids by Gibson and Ashby. Their work systematically covers a definition of cellular solids and the range of structures that can make up cellular morphologies, basic two-dimensional (2D) and advanced three-dimensional (3D) theoretical derivations of cellular solid properties and the resulting material behaviour.¹,⁵⁻⁹ This has allowed the mechanical properties of cellular solids to be more easily predicted, leading to simplification of including the materials in the design process.

When considering porous metals, relative densities rarely go as low as 0.3, that is, a porosity of 70%. While there is a range of literature investigating porous metals, the mechanical properties are reported with little or no attempt to model the behaviour based on the results.¹⁰,¹¹ While work by Wen et al.¹² does apply the cellular solid model to porous titanium, there is no attempt made to modify it to better fit the data. Other modelling of properties is restricted to prediction of Young's modulus using Nielsen's relationship.¹³,¹⁴ The lack of modelling is probably due to a lack of a convenient generic model, such as the cellular solid model,

## Materials and methods

The porous titanium samples are produced using the3D printing and sintering method, using grade 1 com- mercially pure (CP) titanium and polyvinyl alcohol(PVA) as a space-holder/porogen, as described in previ- ous work $^{16}$ with samples having a final sintering tem perature (FST) of $900^{\circ} C, 1000^{\circ} C, 1100^{\circ} C, 1200^{\circ} C$ or1300°C. The produced samples were roughly cylindri-cal with approximate dimensions of $10 ~mm$ length $\times$ 5 mm diameter (Figure 1(a)).

## Micro-computed tomography imaging

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging data gathered using established procedure $^{17}$ and reported in previous work provided the required morphology data.
The scanner (1172; Skyscan, Kontich, Belgium) that has been used is a high-resolution, compact desktop unit with associated control, reconstruction (NRecon, Skyscan) and analysis (CTAn/CTVol, Skyscan) soft- ware. In order to image the denser titanium and cap- ture the correct resolution, the energy of the scan is increased to the maximum $100 kV(\sim 100 \mu A)$ , which results in approximately $20 \%$ X-ray penetration through the body of the sample. The 'medium' camera is used for image capture with a pixel size of $4.3 \mu m$ , standard settings of $590 ~ms$ capture time, $0.7^{\circ}$ step size and three images per step for noise reduction aver- aging. An aluminium and copper $(Al+Cu)$ filter ensures only X-rays of adequate strength are reaching the target.

## Compression testing

Force-displacement raw data is generated via compres- sion testing carried out to the American Society for Metals (ASM) axial compression testing standard $^{18}$ on a universal test rig (Hounsfield, Hounsfield now Tinius Olsen, Surrey, UK) with compressive load cell. Thesamples are loaded axially at a strain rate of $0.5 ~mm /$  min to approximately $75 \%$ strain or until the sample fails. The raw data are then used to calculate (engineer- ing) stress-strain curves for each of the tested samples. The results of the test are shown in Figures 2-4 and isalso used to obtain Young's modulus $E^{*}$ and $0.2 \%$  proof stress $\sigma_{0.2 \%}$ in Table 2.

This is despite the material not strictly being a cellular solid - between $35\%$ and $40\%$, the porosity is too low (relative density of 0.6-0.65). The cellular solid model sets the threshold of relative density at 0.3 due to the nature of cell geometries. Beyond this density, cell size (diameter) and cell wall thickness begin to approach similar values, and the beam bending theory used to model the deformation of cell walls is less applicable as the increasingly squat cell walls fail axially rather than through bending. $^{19}$ However, the shape of the compressive stress-strain curves of the sample material suggests a modified model could be possible for higher relative densities.

With samples sintered at lower FSTs, elastic-brittle behaviour was observed. The lowest FST produced samples that failed following the elastic phase while increasing intermediate FSTs produced progressively less erratic plateau phases and increasing evidence of densification. The highest sintering temperature produced a stress-strain curve that best corresponds to elastic-plastic behaviour. This was supported by observations during the experiments with the samples that failed at the end of the elastic phase fracturing and crumbling away from below the cross head. The degree of fracturing was less pronounced with subsequent increasing FST samples and no observable incidence in the highest FST samples.

In order to provide a consistent measure of a yield point across the stress-strain curves of all samples, the $0.2\%$ proof stress method will be used. The result of this adjustment of the raw data is a refinement of the values of sample Young's moduli in addition to accurate estimations of when the material can be considered to have yielded (Table 2 and Figure 3). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) analysis on the data obtained from analysis of the compression tests for modulus and proof stress indicates statistically significant differences in the range of FSTs considered. However, it should be noted that these changes are not large. The main reason is that sintering stage is used to consolidate the titanium phase and removal of the PVA agent. In the range of the temperatures (900 °C–1300 °C), the consolidation and removal are achieved reasonably. The morphological changes are mainly directed by the size and shape of the PVA and the size of the titanium powder, which have been the same in all cases. Therefore, the differences in the morphology of samples such as pore shape, size, porosity and density have not been large (while statistically significant).

Other than the samples with an FST of 900 °C that underwent brittle failure following the elastic phase and can, therefore, be discounted, all samples display some sort of post-yield behaviour. Relating the plateau phase to observations of the samples during testing, it is apparent that the erratic nature is due to a series of ‘secondary failures’ (Figure 4). These are manifest as small pieces of the sample breaking away from the main body, accompanied by a corresponding drop in recorded stress. The lack of densification is due to the amount of secondary failures that occur – if the number is sufficient, there is not enough left of the sample for the test to continue. At higher FSTs, the plateau becomes more regular although there is still one or two instances of secondary failure with corresponding drops in stress. As the majority of the sample survives, there is sufficient material to allow densification to occur. Relating back to the cellular solid model, it is stated that densification begins at a relative density of approximately $0.5.^{19}$ As the tested samples have densities of approximately 0.6 to begin with, well above this threshold, densification is already occurring during the plateau phase. This is evident from the inclined nature of the post-yield plateau. The model’s assumption of axial failure at higher relative densities is also indicated by the nature of the secondary failures, which occur with parts breaking off the main sample along failure planes orientated at approximately 45° to the direction of applied load. This suggests, as expected, that a combination of beam bending of cell walls and axial failure is active following the elastic phase, and that the titanium colonies that make up the cell walls at higher FSTs are possibly less inclined towards axial failure, indicated by the reduction in the secondary failures.

The transition from plateau phase densification to full densification phase is indicated by a large increase in the gradient of the graph in the region of 70% strain. As relative densities are similar regardless of FST, all samples that show evidence of densification do so at approximately the same strain level, indicating the relationship between relative density and final densification.

## Elastic phase model
Fitting of the recorded data to the model is based on equations derived from the geometry of cellular solid morphology

$$
\frac{E^{*}}{E_{s}} \propto \left(\frac{t}{l}\right)^{4} \tag{1}
$$

$$
\frac{E^{*}}{E_{s}} = C_{1} \left(\frac{\rho^{*}}{\rho_{s}}\right)^{2} \tag{2}
$$

where $E^{*}$ is the modulus of the porous material, $E_{s}$ is the modulus of the solid, $t$ is the pore wall thickness, $l$ is the strut (pore wall) length, $C_{1}$ is a geometry factor, $\rho^{*}$ is the density of the porous material and $\rho_{s}$ is the density of the solid. $^{19}$ For the purpose of this investigation, a value of 110 GPa is used as Young’s modulus of grade 1 CP titanium. Using the cellular solid model, where $C_{1}=1$, over-predicts the value of Young’s modulus by a considerable margin at the relative densities being investigated. It should be noted that in the work by Wen et al., $^{12}$ which produced porous titanium by a powder metallurgy-porogen method, the unmodified

From equation (1), it is clear that as the slenderness of the pore walls (ratio of strut length to thickness) decreases, so does the porous modulus. Equation (2) shows that a decrease in relative density will also result in a reduction in the porous modulus. In basic terms, if a standard pore shape is assumed, it is possible to envi- sage relative density decreasing due to an increase in pore size, an increase in the number of pores with their size remaining constant, a larger increase in the number of pores while their size reduces or a combination of these effects. However, a decrease in relative density combined with a decrease in slenderness would only occur in the case of an increase in pore numbers with a reduction in pore size. Therefore, it is necessary to con- sider the shape of the pores, enabled by the inclusion of the geometry factor term in equation (2). The morpho- logical analysis outputs data on pore size as an average diameter, equating pores to a sphere. If this dimension is considered for a variety of shapes to derive strut lengths, which represent various values of the geometry factor, it is possible to see how much pore shape influ- ences the modulus of the porous material. Slendernessis related to relative density and pore shape by $^{19}$

$$
C_{1}\left(\frac{\rho^{*}}{\rho_{s}}\right)^{2} \propto\left(\frac{t}{l}\right)^{4} \tag{3}
$$

which means a value for the geometry factor can be cal- culated from

$$
C_{1}=\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\rho^{*}}{\rho_{s}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{t}{l}\right)^{4}} \tag{4}
$$

The relative density (porosity) and wall thickness values are output from the morphological analysis. Variation in strut length occurs by considering pores with varying cross-sectional shapes and relating these to the pore diameters $(d)$ output by the morphological analysis. Pore cross section can be assumed to vary from square to decagonal as they become more spheri- cal (Table 3 and Figure 5(a)). The graph indicates that the value of $C_{1}$ that best results in predicted moduli matching measured moduli is derived from a strut length relating to an octagonal cross section (Figure5(b)). This again corresponds well with the model for a true cellular solid. Rather than being spherical, the cells(or pores) of true cellular solids are commonly tetrakai- decahedra $^{6}$ - a 14-faced 3D shape that an 8-sided cross section is a good fit for. The results of the estimated elastic modulus using the octagonal $E_{oc}^{*}$ and decagonal geometries $E_{dc}^{*}$ are reported in Table 2. The results of modulus estimation using various shape cells in cellular model are presented in Figure 5 alongside with the modulus values obtained from compression tests. The comparison shows that an octagonal shape offers clo- sest match to the experimental results. It should be noted that the cellular model assumes that the cellular structure is uniform, indicating a repeating units through the microstructure. It also assumes that the pore has regular shape, wall thickness and pore size. All these assumptions are far from the real morphology of the porous structure, which has range of pore sizes and shapes and wall thicknesses. These assumptions are the causes of the discrepancies between the model and measured results.

## Yield model
In a similar way to how the modulus is modelled dur- ing the elastic phase, modelling of a yield point is also based on equations derived from the cellular solid morphology

$$
\sigma_{p l}^{*}=\sigma_{y s} \cdot C_{5}\left(\frac{\rho^{*}}{\rho_{s}}\right)^{3 / 2} \tag{5}
$$

where $\sigma_{y s}$ is the yield strength (or in this case, the $0.2 \%$  proof stress) of the solid material and $C_{5}$ is the geome try factor. $^{19}$ Taking a value of $220 MPa$ as the $0.2 \%$  proof stress of grade $1 CP$ titanium and a guideline value of $C_{5}=0.3$ , as used in the standard cellular solid model, the predicted and measured values of $\sigma_{p l}^{*}$ can be compared. It can be seen (Table 2 and Figure 6) that this value of $C_{5}$ under-predicts the recorded values by a large margin. Again, a similar thing is seen in the work by Wen et al. $^{12}$ with the cellular solid model under predicting yield stress levels. With increased relative densities, such as that of the tested samples, an increase in the value of $C_{5}$ is needed to bring the model values in-line with the measured values. As the original model is based on a relative density of 0.3 and a $C_{5}$ value of0.3, modelling with $C_{5}$ equal to the relative density was used to produce modified model values. This modified model much more closely approximates the data recorded experimentally. The results of the standard model and modified model predictions and values obtained from the stress strain curves of the compres- sive tests for $\sigma_{0.2 \%}$ are reported in Table 2.
