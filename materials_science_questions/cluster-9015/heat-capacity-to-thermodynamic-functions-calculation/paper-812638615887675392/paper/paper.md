Thermodynamic parameters are reported for gaseous hydroxides and oxyhydroxides of Al, Zr, and Y. The structures and vibrational frequencies are calculated using DFT (Density Functional Theory) with the B3LYP functional. This yields entropy at 298.15K and heat capacities. The enthalpies are calculated from appropriate reactions and the CCSD(T) (Coupled Cluster, Singles -Doubles and perturbative Triples) approach. The hydroxide groups are treated as hindered rotors for all species. The results are compared to the limited experimental and theoretical calculations for these species. Finally, the data are put into a data bank for a free energy minimizer and the vapor pressures for each species are compared.

Introduction

Refractory oxides find a wide range of applications at high temperatures [1, 2], including refractory bricks, coatings for turbine blades, and as electrolytes in fuel cells. The major oxides of interest are alumina, zirconia, and yttria. Many of these applications involve a moist environment. Combustion environments typically have 3-10% water vapor. Typically, the cathode of a fuel cell has a 2% water vapor/air environment [2]. It is well-known that condensed phase oxides react with water vapor to form volatile hydroxides and oxyhydroxides [2, 3]. In this study we focus on the gaseous hydroxides and oxyhydroxides of aluminum, zirconium, and yttrium. There are limited experimental and theoretical studies on the thermochemistry of many of these species [4-12]. In this study, quantum chemical methods are applied to obtain basic thermochemical data (entropy, enthalpy, and heat capacities) for the major gaseous hydroxides and oxyhydroxides of aluminum, zirconium, and yttrium.

The major hydroxide and oxyhydroxide species from aluminum are Al(OH), AlO(OH), Al(OH)₂, and Al(OH)₃. As a note, we do not consider aluminum oxy-hydrides here. The Al(OH) and Al(OH)₃ species are well-studied, as shown in Table I [8-12]. The experimental methods used to study these materials include Knudsen effusion mass spectrometry (KEMS), which works well for the AlOH species formed in reducing atmospheres, flame spectroscopy, and transpiration which works well for

the Al(OH)₃ species formed in oxidizing atmospheres. Thus, the data in Table I can be compared to the output of our calculations. In addition to these studies, Opila and Myers [13] have studied the reaction of water vapor and high purity alumina (sapphire) coupons. While these studies were not conducted under equilibrium conditions, they provide some data for further comparison to theory.

Contrary to alumina, both experimental and theoretical data on the hydroxides and oxyhydroxides of zirconia and the rare earth oxides are very limited. Krikorian [7] has estimated thermochemical data for a range of metal hydroxides and oxyhydroxides. These include Y(OH), Y(OH)₂, Y(OH)₃, YO(OH), Zr(OH)₂, Zr(OH)₃, Zr(OH)₄, ZrO(OH), and ZrO(OH)₂ gaseous species. Treating the –OH group as a pseudo-halogen, he first estimates the M-OH bond energies at 298K from the following derived relations:

$$
\overline{D}(M-OH)=0.83\overline{D}(M-F) \tag{1a}
$$

$$
\overline{D}(M-OH)=1.05\overline{D}(M-Cl) \tag{1b}
$$

Krikorian combines these values, M-O values at 298K, and tabulated enthalpies of sublimation for the metal at 298K, $\Delta_{sub}H_{298}^{o}$, to estimate the enthalpies of formation of the metal hydroxides and oxyhydroxides at 298K. Entropies and Gibbs Energy Functions are calculated from the empirical relation

$$
S_{298}^{o}=108.8+25.1\ln A_{M}+20.9x+41.8y \tag{2}
$$

In the species MOₓ(OH)ᵧ, A_M is the molecular weight and x and y are the subscripts in the formula. Krikorian concludes with calculations of the vapor pressures of various metal hydroxides and oxyhydroxides. His estimates of vapor pressure of species above Y₂O₃ and ZrO₂ are very low, which is consistent with the well-known stability of these oxides in high temperature combustion environments.

Experimental measurements of the reaction of zirconia and the rare earth oxides are very challenging, due to the required high temperatures to obtain measurable signals. The major methods [3, 14] are

transpiration or weight loss from a specimen after diffusion through a well-defined boundary layer. Transpiration involves analysis of downstream deposits and finding a flux which fully [14] dissolves the deposit is a challenge. Weight losses are very small when caused by a low vapor pressure vapor species. Horwath and Jacobson previously exposed $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ coupons to a $50 \%$ water vapor/Ar environment at high temperature [15]. Careful examination of the post-exposure $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ coupon indicated formation of $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{SiO}_{5}$ on the surface after every experiment, despite attempts to remove every source of $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ in the system. Courcot et al. [5,6] did a similar experiment and report vapor pressures for $\mathrm{Y}(\mathrm{OH})_{3}$ and $\mathrm{YOOH}$ comparable to those of $\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{OH})_{4}$. However, it is not clear if $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{SiO}_{5}$ was formed on the surface and if the static boundary layer effect was included in the calculations.

Golden and Opila [16] used a steam jet to examined the reaction of $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{g})$ at $1000^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ and $1200^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ reacted with $\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{OH})_{4}(\mathrm{~g})$ from the $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ steam jet nozzle to form $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{SiO}_{5}$. Parker and Opila [17] have conducted similar studies of $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{g})$ at $1400^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ with a Pt-Rh steam jet nozzle. Only faceting was observed. These studies confirm the propensity of $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ to form $\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{SiO}_{5}$ from $\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{OH})_{4}$ and confirm the low vapor pressures of $\mathrm{Y}-\mathrm{OH}$ species at these temperatures.

In recent years quantum chemistry methods have been extended to many complex systems. Accurate calculations of thermodynamic properties have been reported for the Si-OH [18, 19], Al-OH [10], Cr-OH [20], Ti-OH [21], and Ta-OH [22] systems. Until recently, limited calculations were available on compounds with the heavy transition metals and rare earth metals. However, newer methods have made these calculations more accessible. In this paper we present thermodynamic data from quantum chemical calculations for the Al-OH, Zr-OH, and Y-OH, systems for quantum chemical calculations. The results are compared to past estimates, experiments, and evidence for low volatility.

## Methods

In this work we perform B3LYP hybrid [23, 24] functional and coupled cluster singles and doubles [25] including the effect of connected triples determined using perturbation theory [26], CCSD(T), calculations. All electron treatments and those including effective core-potentials (ECPs) are used.

The basis sets used in this work [27-31] are given in Table II. The all electron calculations include scalar relativistic effect using the Douglas-Kroll-Hess approach [32]. The AE (All Electron) calculations use a triple zeta (TZ) basis set that should be very similar in quality to the TZ basis set used in the ECP calculation. Thus, the difference between the AE and ECP/TZ calculations serves as a test of the ECP. The results for these treatments are given in the Tables. For those systems with a double bond, e.g. MOOH and ZrO(OH)₂, the differences are in the 2-3 kJ/mol range. For systems with only single bonds, the differences are less than 1 kJ/mol. On basis of this test, we conclude that the use of the ECP calculation does not introduce any significant errors in the computed errors in the computed results.

The B3LYP approach is used to optimize the structures, compute the harmonic frequencies, and to compute bending and rotational potentials. The OH bend and rotation are not well described by the harmonic approximation. We correct the bend for anharmonic effects and treat the rotation using the hindered rotor approximation. For the bending potentials, the M-O-H angle is fixed and all other parameters are optimized. In previous work we fixed all parameters when computing the bending potentials. For small distortion angles, there are only small changes, but for larger distortions the optimized approach shows some relaxation that will improve the properties computed at high temperature. The periodicity of the rotation is required for the hindered rotor approximation and we determine this by computing the full rotational potential.

Using the B3LYP geometries, accurate energetics are computed using the CCSD(T) approach. For open-shell calculations the restricted Hartree-Fock (RHF) based RCCSD(T) (Restricted Coupled Cluster, Singles -Doubles and perturbative Triples) approach [33] is used. The CCSD(T) calculations are extrapolated to the complete basis set limit (CBS) using the $X^{-3}$ approach of Helgaker et al. [34].

In the AlOₓHᵧ CCSD(T) calculations all electrons except the Al 1s are correlated. Correlation of the Al 2s and 2p electrons improves the $3s^23p^1\ (^2\text{P})$-$3s^13p^2(^4\text{P})$ separation and therefore increase the

This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved

accuracy for the species where Al forms multiple bonds. For the $YO_xH_y$ and $ZrO_xH_y$ species, the metal 1s-3d and O 1s electrons are not correlated. Note that for the ECP calculations the 1s-3d metal electrons are included in the ECP. Correlation of the Y and Zr 4s and 4p electrons improves the metal $4d^n5s^2$-$4d^{n+1}5s^1$ separation and, since the oxygen 2s and the metal 4s and 4p orbitals are all correlated, any mixing of the O 2s and metal semi-core orbitals does not lead to problems at the correlated levels.

All of the DFT calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 [35], while MOLPRO [36] was used to perform the CCSD(T) calculations. The interactive molecular graphics tool Jmol [37] was used for the visualization of the molecular structures and the vibrational modes.

The -OH groups have a low rotational barrier and therefore need to be treated as hindered rotors for the calculation of thermodynamic properties. The hindered rotor option [38] in Gaussian16 is used to compute this effect using the Pitzer-Gwinn [39] option. The OH bending potential is anharmonic and we account for this by solving for the vibrational levels of the bending potential and computing the thermodynamic properties using the computed levels.

The metals have the following uncertainties in their heat of formation for the gaseous phase: Al, ± 4.2 kJ/mol [40], Zr ± 8.4 kJ/mol [40], and Y ± 3.0 kJ/mol [41]. Combining these experimental uncertainties with our estimated uncertainty in the best reaction energies reported in Tables III-V of about 4 kJ, leads to uncertainty in our heats of formation of +- 8, +-12 and +-7 for Al, Zr, and Y compounds respectively.

The calculated thermodynamic quantities (S, H, Cₚ, G) are listed in the Supplemental Information. As in our other studies of gaseous metal hydroxides [21, 22], we have included contributions from the – OH groups as hindered rotors as well as anharmonicity corrections. These are all listed in the Supplemental Information. The three trihydroxides, Al(OH)₃, Zr(OH)₃, and Y(OH)₃, present interesting differences. As the calculated structures show (Figures 1-3), the Al(OH)₃ molecule has a bent -O-H geometry, as opposed to the linear -O-H geometry in Zr(OH)₃ and Y(OH)₃. Also the metal-oxygen bond in Al(OH)₃ is the shortest. This leads to a computed rotational potential for Al(OH)₃ with the second minima about 7.9 kJ/mol above the first minima and about 5.9 kJ/mol below the barrier between them. For these reasons we assign the periodicity of the hindered rotor contribution in Al(OH)₃ to be one as opposed to two for the other trihydroxides. In the Supplemental Information, we list the calculation results for a periodicity of one and two. However, our preferred results for Al(OH)₃ are with a periodicity of one. All the calculated data are summarized in Table VI in a form for ready entry into any of the common thermodynamic databases (e.g. FactSage [42], ThermoCalc [43], and IVTAN [44]).

Figure 8 gives the results of calculating the reactions products from $1000-2000 \mathrm{~K}$ with $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}, \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$, $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}, \mathrm{Ta}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{5}, \mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}, \mathrm{ZrO}_{2}$ and $50 \% \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}-\mathrm{Ar}$. Reactions between the oxides were not allowed, so effectively we have each oxide in its pure state. Figure 8 shows many vapor species and effectively gives the stability of each solid oxide in a water vapor environment. Note that of these oxides, $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$, $\mathrm{Ta}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{5}$, and $\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ appear the least stable. But, of course, this depends on the operating temperature and the tolerable material loss rate for a particular material.

The material loss rate or the recession rate can be calculated from the vapor pressure and, if needed, the thermodynamics of mixed oxides. This has been discussed in other papers [21, 45, 46]. In most applications, the reaction of water vapor with the oxide occurs in a flowing gas stream. Usually diffusion of the product vapor (e.g. metal hydroxides and/or oxyhydroxides) through a static boundary layer is rate controlling. Analytical expressions or CFD (computational fluid dynamics) can be used to calculate the resultant material loss, based on the equilibrium vapor pressures [45, 46]. Depending on the application, which defines operation time, water vapor levels, temperature, velocity, and acceptable material loss, a maximum acceptable vapor pressure can be specified [46]. For a low molecular weight vapor, a pressure of $10^{-7}$ bar gives a material loss rate of 1 mil $(0.0254 \mathrm{~mm}) / 10,000$ h. Other investigators have selected $10^{-6}$ bar as a limit [47]. Using $10^{-7}$ bar as a limit on Figure 8, we can see that, depending on the temperature, $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}, \mathrm{Ta}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{5}, \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$, and $\mathrm{TiO}_{2}$ may generate a high enough metal hydroxide/oxyhydroxide pressure to be a problem. Note that the Zr and Y hydroxides/oxyhydroxides all have much lower vapor pressures.

As noted, for complex oxides, the calculated vapor pressures must be modified by the thermodynamic activity of the particular oxide component. For example, in a 7YSZ (7 weight percent yttria stabilized

## Conclusions
Thermodynamic parameters for gaseous hydroxides and oxyhydroxides of Al, Zr, and Y are calculated using quantum chemical methods. The structures and vibrational frequencies are calculated using DFT with the B3LYP functional. The enthalpies of formation are calculated from appropriate reactions and the CCSD(T) approach. The hydroxide groups are treated as hindered rotors for all species. This leads to $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} \mathrm{H}^{\circ}(298), \mathrm{S}^{\circ}(298)$, and $\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{p}}$ for the major species in the $\mathrm{AlO}_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{OH})_{\mathrm{y}}, \mathrm{ZrO}_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{OH})_{\mathrm{y}}$, and $\mathrm{YO}_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{OH})_{\mathrm{y}}$ systems. Data in this form are easily used to build a database for computational thermodynamics codes, such as FactSage [42]. The enthalpies of formation at 298.15K and calculated vapor pressures show generally good agreement to the limited experimental and theoretical calculations for these species. A larger FactSage database which includes the $\mathrm{SiO}_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{OH})_{\mathrm{y}}, \mathrm{TiO}_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{OH})_{\mathrm{y}}$, and $\mathrm{TaO}_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathrm{OH})_{\mathrm{y}}$ systems was used to calculate the vapor pressures of the major hydroxide species above $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}, \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}, \mathrm{TiO}_{2}, \mathrm{Ta}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{5}, \mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}, \mathrm{ZrO}_{2}$ and $50 \% \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$-Ar. At 1500 K, the calculated vapor pressures indicate a ranking in regards to material loss of $\mathrm{Ta}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{5}>\mathrm{SiO}_{2}>\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}>\mathrm{ZrO}_{2}>\mathrm{Y}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$.
