**Abstract**—Samples of low- and high-temperature modifications of $LiCoO_2$ were produced and described. The heat capacity was measured by DSC in the temperature ranges of 140-570 and 180-570 K, respectively. The enthalpy and entropy of the cobaltates with respect to temperature were calculated from the data of heat capacity.

**Keywords**: differential scanning calorimetry, lithium cobaltate, thermodynamic characteristics.

DOI: 10.1134/S0036024411030071

## INTRODUCTION

Lithium cobaltate compositions have been widely used as cathode materials in rechargeable power sources since 1991 [1]. Several crystal modifications of $LiCoO_2$ are known [2-7], but only one of them displays good electrochemical performance [2]. This phase has a layered structure of $G-NaFeO_2$ type with space group $R3m$ [3]. As a rule, it is specified in the literature as $O3$ or a high-temperature ($HT$) phase.

The synthesis of functional $HT$ $LiCoO_2$ takes place through an intermediate stage that forms a so-called low-temperature (LT) modification [4, 5] with the structure of spinel ($R3m$) [6]. It should be noted that in reference to the phases of lithium cobaltate, the terms "high-temperature" and "low-temperature" do not characterize their thermodynamic stability, but reflect the conditions of their synthesis. It has been shown experimentally [4] that at $400^\circ$C and above, the $LT$ phase is metastable, its low transformation rate into a stable $HT$ modification being brought about by kinetic factors. On the basis of quantum-mechanical calculations, it has been hypothesized [7] that the $LT$ phase is metastable in the entire range of temperature.

The useful properties of $LiCoO_2$ depend considerably on a number of characteristics (surface area, structure imperfections, and so on), which are in turn determined by the synthesis and operating conditions [8-12]. A large number of research papers that essentially describe how to select empirically the optimum conditions for obtaining $LiCoO_2$ with regard to its functional properties have been released [2]. Using a physiochemical model as the basis for this is more efficient and makes the task easier. At the heart of the model lie the thermodynamic properties of the substances.

The thermodynamics of the high-temperature phase has been studied quite thoroughly [8-12]. Its heat capacity was measured by adiabatic differential calorimetry from liquid-helium temperatures of up to 300 K [8, 9]. No information on the thermodynamic properties of low-temperature modification can be found in the literature. It is obvious that those data are necessary to create a physicochemical model, since $LT$ $LiCoO_2$ is a precursor of the functional $HT$ phase in solid-phase synthesis.

In this work, the heat capacities of $HT$ and $LT$ $LiCoO_2$ were measured in the temperature range of 180-570 and 140-570 K, respectively.

## EXPERIMENTAL

The samples were synthesized by solid-state reactions.

$HT$ $LiCoO_2$. A stoichiometric quantity of $Li_2CO_3$ (special purity) and $Co_3O_4$ (special purity) mixed with an excess of citric acid were fired for 24 h in packets of aluminum foil at $150^\circ$C, after which they were annealed in air at $200^\circ$C for another 24 h to remove the citric acid and decompose formed citrates. The product was ground, pressed, and annealed further in air at $700^\circ$C for 150 h with intermediate homogenization.

$LT$ $LiCoO_2$. A stoichiometric quantity of $Li_2CO_3$ (special purity) and $Co_3O_4$ (special purity) were mixed, pressed into a tablet, and annealed for 700 h at $400^\circ$C. Synthesis was performed in air. The sample obtained by this method is denoted as $LT$-1. A mixture of $LiOH \cdot H_2O$ (chemical purity) and $Co_3O_4$ (special purity) taken in a stoichiometric ratio were pressed and annealed in an oxygen stream at $350^\circ$C for 100 h with intermediate homogenization (sample $LT$-2). Lithium hydroxide was preliminarily dried above $CaCl_2$ in a dessicator. Stoichiometry of the monohydrate was confirmed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA).

The compositions of the samples were investigated by X-ray phase analysis, thermogravimetric analysis TGA, iodimetry, Raman spectroscopy, and temperature programmed reduction (TPR).

XRD. The samples were investigated on a STOE STADI P diffractometer at 298 K. The Rietveld analysis was performed using the TOPAS software [13] as an internal full profile procedure. The structural and scale data were refined, along with the profile half-width, the background factors, and the texture parameters. Structural models from the ICSD database were used [14].

TGA. The samples were analyzed on a NETZSCH TG 209 F1 Thermo-Microbalance in open alundum containers ($V = 85$ mm³, $d = 6.7$ mm). The standard experiments were performed at a heating rate of 10 K/min in an argon (special purity) stream (20 ml/min). In the cases noted in the text, artificial air was used as a static atmosphere: $O_2$ ($20.9 \pm 0.5$ vol %), $N_2$ ($79.1 \pm 0.5$ vol %), $CH_4$, $CO$, $CO_2$ (<0.005 vol %). The weight of a sample was about 13 mg. The measuring system was calibrated by the melting point of standard substances (Ag, Al, Bi, In, and Sn; purity, 99.99%). The systematic error in determining the mass was no more than 0.1% ($CaC_2O_4 \cdot 2H_2O$ standard). The weight loss value was determined according to the ISO/CD 11358 standard and [15].

The oxygen ratio was determined by iodometric titration [16].

Raman spectra were obtained with a resolution of $4$ cm⁻¹ in the geometry of $180^\circ$ on an Equinox 55 IR–Fourier spectrophotometer’s FRA-106 RS add-on unit (Bruder) using a Nd-YAG laser with a power of 350 mW and a wavelength of 1064 nm. An integration time with 5000 spectra accumulations was used.

TPR. Reduction was performed in a flow quartz reactor by linear heating (12 K/min) from 25 to 900°C in a constant stream (23 ml/min) of a gas mixture (5% hydrogen solution in argon). The change in the stream’s hydrogen concentration was continuously monitored by a thermal conductivity detector. Hydrogen consumption was calculated by the peak area on the experimental curve. The system was calibrated against a NiO standard.

The heat capacity of the samples was measured by DSC according to ASTM E 1269 standard (with a synthetic sapphire as a reference substance) on a NETZSCH DSC 204 F1 in an argon stream (10 ml/min) at a heating rate of 10 K/min. Special aluminum containers ($V = 56$ mm³, $d = 6$ mm) furnished with a holed lid were used. The DSC cell was cooled at a rate of 20–30 K/min. The weight of a sample was 20–30 mg. The measure system (gage) was calibrated to ISO/CD 11357-1 against the phase transitions parameters of standard substances ($C_6H_{12}$, Hg, $KNO_3$, In, Sn, Bi, and CsCl; purity, 99.99%). The systematic error of temperature calibration (detected against In) proved to be 0.1 K. At least three independent series of measurements were made for each sample, and the results were treated statistically. To reduce the drift of the baseline, the temperature range was divided into two sections. The heat capacity of the low-temperature phase was measured on the $LT$-2 sample preheated to 250°C in an inert atmosphere. The mass of the product was calculated using TGA data.

The samples were weighed on an R 160P (Sartorius Research) analytical balance with an accuracy of $1 \times 10^{-2}$ mg. The values of the thermodynamic functions were calculated from the experimental dependences of $C_p(T)$. The data from [9] at 210 K were used for the $HT$-phase.

The experimental curves of the heat capacity dependence on temperature were approximated using the Haas–Fisher polynomial [17]:

$$
C_{p}(T)=a_{0}+a_{1} T+a_{2} T^{2}+a_{3} T^{-0.5}+a_{3} T^{-2}. \tag{1}
$$

The quality of the approximation was monitored by the magnitude of the determination coefficient. The model’s adequacy and the admissibility of co-processing the curve parts before and after the aperiodicities were verified using the Fisher test. The statistical significance of the regression coefficients was assessed using the $t$-test.

In low-temperature phases, the population density of cation sites corresponds to the ratio of Li/Co $0.812 \pm 0.046$ ($LT$-1) and $1.008 \pm 0.044(LT$-2). The loss of lithium in the synthesis of $LT$-1 probably occurs during long annealing even at low temperatures.

The samples of $LT LiCoO_2$ lost weight while heated. The shape of the TGA curves (Fig. 2) allows us to distinguish three stages of the process: (1) $T<100^\circ$C, (2) $T=100$–$250^\circ$C, (3) $T>250^\circ$C. Additional investigations by TGA and TPR were conducted to determine the nature of these stages.

The program of TG measurements consisted of the following: the samples were heated to $400^\circ$C, cooled to $40^\circ$C, and held under argon or synthetic air for 1 h. They were then heated to $400^\circ$C once again. When reheated, the samples kept in an inert atmosphere proved to exhibit a slight weight loss above $300^\circ$C. The shape of the TGA curves obtained before and after keeping in air was reproduced in full, but the initial phase ($T<100^\circ$C) at stage four was absent.

The aggregate data suggest that the vaporization of absorbed water occurs up to 100°C, and the next stages of the process seem to be associated with the release of oxygen. The loss of slightly combined oxy- gen takes place at $T < 250^\circ\text{C}$, which proceeds to the process of $\text{LiCoO}_2$ reduction at higher temperatures. XRD did not show formation of any foreign phases during TGA experiment, but the lattice parameters of the sample had changed to some extent (Table 1), which could indicate the formation of O-deficient $\text{LiCoO}_{2-\delta}$. The appearance of oxygen vacancies was found after annealing the $HT$ phase at much higher temperatures [19]. The content of oxygen in the initial samples of $LT$ $\text{LiCoO}_2$ also proved to be considerably lower, the value $\delta$ for $LT$-2 being $0.037 \pm 0.008$. This is likely to lead to a discrepancy in the calculated lattice parameters from those reported in the literature, where the oxygen index was not determined.

The steps on the TGA curves are not separated by horizontal plateaus, making it difficult to calculate the value of the weight loss. The water contents in the samples were determined by the results of isothermic annealing for 30 min at 100°C (under these condi- tions, the DTG was equal to zero). They were $0.38 \pm$ $0.02$ wt $\%$ ($LT$-1) and $0.13 \pm 0.02$ wt $\%$ ($LT$-2).

The weight loss value (0.42%) at the second stage (100–250°C) was calculated using Marsh’s method in [15]. The amount of oxygen, which is equivalent to the amount of hydrogen absorbed at this stage, was also equal to 0.42 wt %.

The experimental results of heat measurements are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 4; the polynomial coef- ficients (1) are represented in Table 3.

S-shaped bends were observed on the specific heat curves obtained by DSC in the range of 25°C. A sharp drop in the rate of spin–lattice relaxation was recorded at a temperature close to this in [20, 21], which was interpreted as the result of the migration of $\text{Li}^+$ defects. The aperiodicities on the DSC curves are likely to be of the same nature. Indirect confirmation of this hypothesis is that the inflection on the curve of the $LT$-1 sample ($\text{Li/Co} = 0.812$) is much more pronounced than that of $LT$-2, the degree of cation nonstoichiom- etry of the latter lying beyond the sensitivity of XRD. The best indicator of low concentrations of vacant lithium sites is the appearance of a spectrum band in the range of $680\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ in the Raman spectra [22], found in $LT$-2 phase (Fig. 5).

On the other hand, the observed specific heat ape- riodicities could occur as a result of the magnetic dis- ordering of divalent cobalt impurities (the Curie tem- perature of CoO is 271 K [23]) present in oxygen-defi- cient cobaltates [19, 24]. The adiabatic curves display no sharp jump in thermal conductivity; this is likely due to the much lower value of the ratio of the rate of a sample’s temperature change to its relaxation time, as compared to the DSC experiments.
