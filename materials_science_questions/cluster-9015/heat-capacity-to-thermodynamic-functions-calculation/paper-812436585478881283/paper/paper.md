# Low-temperature heat capacity of $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ glasses: thermophysical and structural implications

${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$ Laboratoire de Physique des Géomatériaux, UMR CNRS 7046, Institut de Physique du Globe, 4 place Jussieu,

## Abstract

The heat capacities of rapidly quenched $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ glasses have been measured by adiabatic calorimetry from less than 10 to 350 K. The residual entropies at 0 K obtained from these new results and ancillary data are $11.2 \pm 0.8$ and $6.6 \pm 1.1 \mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{mol} \mathrm{K}$ for $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ glasses with fictive temperatures of 980 and 543 K, respectively. On a g atom basis, both of these entropies are similar to the low value found previously for $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ glass. For $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$, this is likely due to the small contribution of boroxol groups to the topological entropy of the glass. The importance of the Boson peak increases in the order $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}, \mathrm{GeO}_{2}, \mathrm{~B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ which corresponds to the order of decreasing glass transition temperatures. Some implications about short- and medium-range order are finally drawn from these results and the differences between the glass and crystal phases of the three oxides.

## 1. Introduction

The small family made up by oxides that vitrify readily on cooling from the molten state includes $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$. As both of these substances also vitrify when mixed with other oxides, and particularly with the archetypal glass former $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$, they constitute on their own the starting point of other glass families. Knowledge of their physical properties is thus important for understanding not only the peculiarities of the vitreous state, but also the properties of technologically important germanate, borate, germanosilicate and borosilicate glasses.

Whereas the thermodynamic properties of amorphous $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ are accurately known above room temperature [1,2], the low-temperature heat capacities and entropies of the glasses have not been measured consistently from less than 10 K up to room temperature. Such measurements are required to determine the vibrational entropies of $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ glasses, and also to derive their configurational entropies which are the thermodynamic measures of disorder. In addition, $C_{p}$ measurements at low temperatures represent a sensitive probe of interatomic forces and vibrational densities of states, particularly at low frequencies where the so-called Boson peak originates.

In this paper we report adiabatic $C_p$ measurements for $GeO_2$ and $B_2O_3$ glasses from less than 10 to 350 K. With these and ancillary data for the liquid and crystalline phases of these substances, we calculate the residual entropy of the glasses. As a further test of the long-standing analogy made between germanates and silicates, we then examine the low-temperature heat capacities of the crystal and glass phases of $SiO_2$ and $GeO_2$ with reference to short-range order around Si and Ge atoms, which are tetrahedrally coordinated by oxygen, and compare these results with those of $B_2O_3$ in which B atoms are in a markedly different 3-fold coordination. We also discuss the heat capacity of these glasses near 0 K in terms of the vibrational density of states at low frequencies as observed by Raman spectroscopy or inelastic neutron scattering.

In passing, we stress at once that $C_p$ calculations made from available vibrational densities of states (e.g., [3,4]) do not reproduce well all observations made for $GeO_2$ and $B_2O_3$ glasses from the vicinity of 0 K to room temperature. The present results should thus prove useful to improve quantitative links between the structural and thermophysical properties of these simple yet important glasses.

## 2. Experimental methods

The heat capacities were measured with the Mark XIII cryostat [5]. This intermittently heated, adiabatic system for measurements in the range 5–350 K has a gold-plated copper calorimeter with an axial entrant well for the heater/thermometer assembly and a gold gasketted screw type closure. A capsule-type, platinum resistance thermometer calibrated at the National Bureau of Standards was used to determine the sample temperature against the NBS 1955 scale below 90.18 K and against the IPTS-48 scale at higher temperatures. About $10^{-4}$ mole of helium was introduced to speed up thermal equilibrium in the calorimeter whereas grease was used to enhance thermal contact between the calorimeter and the heater/thermometer assembly. The measurements on $GeO_2$ have been made recently with a computer operated system which was not in use at the time of the measurements on $B_2O_3$, which have long remained unpublished.

The $GeO_2$ sample was prepared from the same 99.999% pure $GeO_2$ material, investigated previously [1]. We heated this material to about 1500 K for 1 h in a platinum crucible in an electrical muffle furnace and then quenched it by dipping the bottom of the crucible into water. As a result of this procedure, the fictive temperature of the resulting glass was likely a little higher at the edge than at the center of the crucible. No density differences were found on chips taken from various parts of the sample, however, with the average value of 3.622 (2) $g/cm^3$. Experiments were made on a 30 g sample. The $B_2O_3$ glass sample was kindly provided by Barber who prepared it at the Owens–Illinois Technical Center. The weight of the sample was about 150 g.

For both samples, the heat capacity of the empty calorimeter was determined in separate series of experiments in which nearly identical amounts of grease and helium gas were used as in the measurements on glasses. Heat capacities were obtained after subtraction of the heat capacity of the empty calorimeter, appropriate adjustment for the small differences in the amounts of grease and helium, and standard curvature corrections. The heat capacity of the sample increased from about 50% at 70 K to 70% at 350 K of the total heat capacity of the calorimeter with the thermometer/heater assembly. Typically, the standard deviations of the reported data are lower than 10% below 15 K, 0.4% between 25 and 50 K, and 0.2% up to 350 K.

Near room temperature good agreement is also found with the heat capacities determined from drop-calorimetry experiments [1]. For the interval 273–970 K, we obtained from both sets of observations the following equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{p}= & 94.790+4.7724 \times 10^{-3} T-1.5271 \times 10^{5} T^{-2} \\
& -718.70 T^{-0.5} \quad(\mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{mol} \mathrm{K}),
\end{aligned}\qquad(1)
$$

which reproduces the $C_p$ data of Table 1 above 273 K and the relative enthalpies of Richet [1] with average absolute deviations of 0.14% and 0.08%, respectively. This equation also reproduces the relative enthalpies measured by Kelley and Christensen [14] between 395 and 900 K to within their error margins, the average absolute deviation being 0.7%.

For ${\rm B_2O_3}$, measurements at very low temperatures have shown that the heat capacity is the highest for samples with the highest fictive temperature [15]. The differences reach about 10% near 5 K but they vanish above 15 K so that the effect on the entropy at 298 K can be neglected.

### 3.2. Residual and configurational entropies

The residual entropy of a glass, $S_{\mathrm{g}}(0)$, is the entropy frozen in at the glass transition, $S^{\mathrm{conf}}(T_{\mathrm{g}})$. It can be calculated from an entropy cycle that begins with the third-law entropy of the crystal, goes up to the entropy of the liquid at the congruent melting temperature of the crystal, and ends at $0 \mathrm{~K}$ at the glass entropy (Fig. 3). Analytically, we write

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\mathrm{g}}(0)= & S^{\mathrm{conf}}\left(T_{\mathrm{g}}\right)=\int_{0}^{T_{\mathrm{f}}} \frac{C_{p \mathrm{c}}}{T} \mathrm{~d} T+\Delta S_{\mathrm{f}}+\int_{T_{\mathrm{f}}}^{T_{\mathrm{g}}} \frac{C_{p \mathrm{l}}}{T} \mathrm{~d} T \\
& +\int_{T_{\mathrm{g}}}^{0} \frac{C_{p \mathrm{~g}}}{T} \mathrm{~d} T,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $C_{p \mathrm{c}}, C_{p \mathrm{l}}$ and $C_{p \mathrm{~g}}$ are the heat capacities of the crystal, liquid and glass, $\Delta S_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the enthalpy of fusion at the equilibrium melting point $T_{\mathrm{f}}$, and $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ is the temperature at which the glass transition took place, i.e., the fictive temperature of the glass. Because the low-temperature $C_{p}$ measurements have been made on quenched glasses, we have selected for $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ the glass transition temperatures of $980 \mathrm{~K}$ for $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $543 \mathrm{~K}$ for $\mathrm{B}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ determined in calorimetry studies [1,2]. In view of the slight influence of thermal history on the relative entropy of glasses above room temperature, note that, if needed, residual entropies could be readily calculated for any other fictive temperature from the data sets mentioned below.

Along with Eqs. (1) and (2) and the low-temperature data of Tables 3 and 4 for the glasses, we used available high-temperature $C_{p}$ and $\Delta S_{\mathrm{f}}$ data

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. $GeO_{2}$ vs. $SiO_{2}$: glasses and crystals

The thermophysical and structural analogies between the glassy and crystalline forms of $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ have long been pointed out (e.g., [1,3,21]). Along the lines followed previously to relate the structure of glasses to their low-temperature heat capacities [22,23], we draw in this section some implications about short- and medium-range order from the newly measured heat capacities of $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ glass. That the low-temperature heat capacity is a sensitive structural probe is illustrated by the $C_{p}$ differences $\left(\Delta C_{p}=C_{p g}-C_{p c}\right)$ between the glassy and crystalline forms of $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ (Fig. 4). For both oxides, data are available for the hexagonal and tetragonal polymorphs in which the coordination of Ge and Si atoms by oxygen is tetrahedral and octahedral, respectively. As apparent in Fig. 4, the heat capacities of the glasses are close to those of the hexagonal forms and much higher than those of the tetragonal polymorphs. For $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ glass, these features are thus consistent with the existence of a three-dimensional network of $\mathrm{GeO}_{4}$ tetrahedra deduced from X-ray diffraction and absorption studies [21,26].

One also observes that $\Delta C_{p}$ is slightly greater for $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ than for $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$, a fact that does not correlate with the density differences between the crystalline and amorphous forms of these oxides (Table 5). For $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$, however, a better match for the glass heat capacity at the lowest temperature is provided by the measurements for cristobalite, the less dense high-temperature polymorph (Fig. 4). As for the residual entropy of a glass, it is the entropy frozen in at the glass transition. It thus constitutes the thermodynamic measure of disor- der. In GeO₂ and SiO₂ glasses, disorder is essen- tially topological since neither germanium nor silicon can substitute for oxygen. The residual entropies of $6.6\pm 1$ and $5.1\pm 1.1$ J/mol K found for the former and the latter [24], respectively, thus suggest that, if different, the distributions func- tions for bond angles and distances should be slightly wider for GeO₂ than for SiO₂. ### 4.2. Heat capacity and Boson peak

It has long been known that the heat capacities of glasses markedly deviate from Debye's laws at very low temperature. At the roots of these devi- ations are excess numbers of low-frequency vib- rational modes with respect to Debye's model (or Boson peaks, for short), which are frequently as- sumed to be a characteristic feature of disordered phases. Comparisons between Figs. 1, 2 and 5 show that, on a g atom basis, the amplitude of the anomaly decreases in the order B₂O₃, GeO₂ and SiO₂ for the glasses, with maximum $C_{p}/T^{3}$ val- ues of $2.2\times 10^{-4}$, $1.7\times 10^{-4}$ and $0.8\times 10^{-4}$ J/ g atom K⁴, respectively. In contrast, the tempera- tures of the Boson peaks slightly increase in this order, which corresponds to that of the glass transition temperatures of 540, 980 and 1480 K, respectively, as determined for usual laboratory cooling rates [1,2,24]. Hence, there exist simple qualitative correlations between the Boson peak and the average bond strengths within the glasses.

But crystals can exhibit similar anomalies. This is clearly exemplified by the available measure- ments on the hexagonal form of GeO₂ (Fig. 1) and on the low-pressure SiO₂ polymorphs (Fig. 5). The data for SiO₂ polymorphs are especially interesting because they show a significant anomaly only for those polymorphs in which silicon is tetrahedrally coordinated by oxygen to form an open three- dimensional network. Furthermore, this anomaly strongly increases when the density decreases to the point that it becomes greater for the less dense polymorph cristobalite than for SiO₂ glass. These effects parallel those observed for GeO₂, B₂O₃ or silicate glasses in which the excess $C_{p}$ is greater for samples with higher fictive temperatures and thus lower densities [8,15,32]. In contrast, no clear Bo- son peak is observed for the dense tetragonal SiO₂ (stishovite) and GeO₂ polymorphs, which have a rutile structure and in which silicon is octahedrally coordinated by oxygen.

A great many general interpretations have been proposed for the Boson peak. For SiO₂ glass, Buchenau et al. [33] concluded from neutron scattering and heat capacity measurements that it results from coupled librations of the corner-shared SiO₄ tetrahedra (at 0.3–4 THz frequencies), which coexist with sound waves. They also pointed out that relative rotations of SiO₄ tetrahedra were borne out by calculations of the phonon density of states of quartz and cristobalite. The aforementioned observations are consistent with these conclusions. They suggest that the interpretation of Buchenau et al. can be extended not only to the hexagonal and vitreous phases of GeO₂, but also to other crystals having a three-dimensional open network. This should particularly hold true for the aluminosilicates minerals anorthite (CaAl₂Si₂O₈) and cordierite (Mg₂Al₄Si₅O₁₈) whose excess low-temperature heat capacities differ little from those of the glasses of the same composition [23]. In view of their very low density, one can surmise in the same way that zeolites should also show particularly strong Boson peaks.

As for the lack of an anomaly for the tetragonal forms of SiO₂ and GeO₂, it can be qualitatively explained in the same way as the \(C_p\) decreases that result from the change of Si and Ge from 4- to 6-fold coordination [34]. Although lengthening of Si–O (and Ge–O) bonds leads to lower frequencies for the modes of vibration that involve Si–O stretching, this effect is overwhelmed by the increases in the frequencies of ‘lattice’ modes which result from the much more compact arrangement achieved in the tetragonal symmetry. The net effect is thus a decrease of the heat capacity and a disappearance of the Boson peak.

### 4.3. \(B_2O_3\) glass and crystal

The uniqueness of boron oxide remains to be discussed. Crystalline \(B_2O_3\) is made up of an infinite chain of BO₃ triangles interconnected in such a way that each oxygen has four O neighbors at distances ranging from 2.3 to 2.43 Å [35]. Although boron is also in planar 3-fold coordination in the glass [36], a specific feature is the existence of the so-called boroxol (\(B_3O_{4.5}\)) groups (i.e., planar rings made up of three BO₃ triangles) which is now well established [37–39]. With \(S_{\text{g}}(0)=11.2\pm0.8\) J/mol K, the residual entropy of vitreous \(B_2O_3\) is in fact similar to the low value of GeO₂ or SiO₂ glasses since an entropy of about 2 J/g atom K is derived in all three cases. The reason is likely that boroxol rings have a regular shape and accommodate a majority of the boron and oxygen atoms [37]. Their contribution to the topological entropy should thus be rather small and the configurational entropy of the glass should then arise from the remaining BO₃ units and their mixing with boroxol rings. Consistent with the fact that fictive temperature effects vanish neat 15 K [15], it has recently been concluded from inelastic light scattering experiments that differences in the Boson peak of \(B_2O_3\) with different thermal histories are essentially due to changes in medium-range order [40]. In this respect, boron oxide thus behaves like silica and silicates [22,23].

As to contrasts between \(B_2O_3\) glass and crystal, one of the most striking feature concerns density (Table 5). That the much higher molar volume of the glass should be associated with a higher vibrational heat capacity and entropy is to be expected. The unusual feature clearly revealed by comparisons with GeO₂ and SiO₂ glasses (Fig. 6) is, however, that the \(C_p\) difference between \(B_2O_3\)
