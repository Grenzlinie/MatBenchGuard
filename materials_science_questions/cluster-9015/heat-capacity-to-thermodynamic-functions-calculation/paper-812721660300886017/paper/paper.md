## ABSTRACT
The reported high thermal conductivity of boron arsenide (BAs) has prompted scientists to characterize the various properties of this material. Thermodynamic data for the enthalpy (H), entropy (S), and Gibbs free energy (G) of BAs were previously predicted from theory. Here, its thermodynamic properties were determined from experimental measurements of its heat capacity $(C_{P})$ over the temperature range of 298 to 1150 K and compared with the predicted data. The thermodynamic properties of BAs at 800 K were calculated to be $H=-8.6kJmol^{-1}$, $S=81.0Jmol^{-1}K^{-1}$, and $G=-73.4kJmol^{-1}$, and we found that its formation reaction becomes endothermic at 984 K. We also analyzed the experimental findings from growing a BAs single crystal (SC) by the chemical vapor transport (CVT) method. Thermodynamic concepts were applied to show that iodine is the most suitable transport agent among the halogens for obtaining BAs SC by CVT. Additionally, three different combinations of precursors were employed during BAs SC growth by CVT. The three approaches are described in terms of differences in partial pressure of a gaseous species, and their results are compared for possible optimization of the BAs SC growth process by CVT.

## I. INTRODUCTION
High thermal conductivity materials are important for the thermal management of high-power-density electronic devices and have a broad range of applications, such as in microprocessors,smart phones, telecommunication systems, and data centers. $^{1,2}$ Diamond has the highest room-temperature thermal conductivity of $2200Wm^{-1}K^{-1}$ and is used as a heat dissipator in electronic devices. However, the production of diamond is very expensive,and it has not been widely used in industry. $^{3,4}$

Based on the conventional theory, only crystals with light constituent elements like carbon and with strong interatomic covalent bonding should exhibit high lattice thermal conductivity, but recent calculations predicted high thermal conductivity in some cubic boron-V compounds at room temperature, which is an exception from the conventional theory. $^{5}$ Using these calculations, and by assuming only three-phonon scattering, boron arsenide (BAs) was initially predicted to have a room-temperature value of $2200Wm^{-1}K^{-1}$, similar to that of diamond. $^{5,7}$ Such a high thermal conductivity is remarkable, although boron (B) is a light element, arsenic (As) is a heavy element. However, a more recent examination considering four-phonon processes predicted a somewhat lower thermal conductivity of $1400Wm^{-1}K^{-1}$ at room temperature for BAs. $^{8,9}$ Thermal conductivity measurements of early BAs single crystals (SCs) at room temperature were as high as $350Wm^{-1}K^{-1},^{10-13}$ but more recent samples have exhibited room-temperature values of $1000-1300Wm^{-1}K^{-1}$ even though there are still lots of defects inside the crystals, close to the four-phonon theoretical predictions. $^{5,14,15}$

Further research on p-type conductivity of the BAs SC has shown that contaminant elements such as silicon and carbon are prime candidates for reducing the experimental value from the prediction. $^{16}$ Therefore, eliminating these contaminants could be a good strategy for increasing thermal conductivity. However, even the current reported thermal conductivity value of BAs is higher than that of many common high thermal conductivity materials like copper, gold, aluminum, etc. Therefore, the high thermal conductivity of BAs, along with its better mechanical performance than the most used material,silicon, makes it a good candidate for future electronic devices. $^{17}$


Chemical vapor transport (CVT) has been the only method reported to grow BAs SCs thus far. Although there has been a significant development in growing BAs SCs using this method, challenges remain for the field, including improving the quality and size of the crystals and commercializing the BAs SC growth process.¹⁸ Studying the thermodynamic principles and theory underlying BAs SC growth using the CVT method are primary steps needed to address these challenges. To describe the transport reaction of this method quantitatively, we first need to quantify the thermodynamic properties of BAs so that the model calculations of the transport reaction can be carried out.

Previously, the thermodynamic properties of BAs have not been experimentally measured,¹⁹,²⁰ and thermodynamic data at high temperatures have only been available based on prediction and the trend of variation of these properties in comparison with similar components.²⁰,²¹ Here, we report the heat capacity ($C_\text{P}$) measurement of BAs at high temperatures and the subsequent calculation of the enthalpy ($H$), entropy ($S$), and Gibbs free energy ($G$), as well as the comparison of our results with the predicted data even though similar data have only been reported up to room temperature.¹¹,¹⁵

Furthermore, by using the calculated thermodynamic data of BAs, we have studied the theoretical concepts underlying the different approaches to BAs SC growth by the CVT method and have compared the theoretical results with the experimental findings.

## II. HEAT CAPACITY MEASUREMENT

In order to calculate $H$, $S$, and $G$ of BAs, a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 404 C) was first used to measure its $C_\text{P}$. Here, the system was programmed to increase the temperature of the BAs sample at $20\ \text{K}\ \text{min}^{-1}$. We used a 77 mg BAs single crystal (Fig. 1) grown by CVT in a sealed quartz tube as our sample for the $C_\text{P}$ measurement. Details of the growth procedure were reported before.⁵

The $C_\text{P}$ of the BAs single crystal was measured from 323 to 1123 K. The maximum temperature was set at 1123 K since BAs is expected to decompose at 1193 K.¹¹ Sapphire was used as the reference for this experiment since it has a well-defined $C_\text{P}$ over this temperature range.

The measured $C_\text{P}$ and its fitted curve are shown in Fig. 2. Additionally, the theoretical $C_\text{P}$ of BAs at higher temperatures, which was calculated based on the classical limit predicted by the Dulong-Petit law for the Debye temperature of 700 K, is shown for comparison.⁷,¹⁴,²²,²³ The $C_\text{P}$ gradually increases with increasing temperature and converges with the constant number 3NK from the Dulong-Petit law at high temperatures. Generally, our measurement deviates from the calculated $C_\text{P}$ by around 4% on average, which is within the 5% experimental error for the $C_\text{P}$ measurement. However, there was a sharp peak of unknown origin at 1087 K. Based on our current knowledge, such a peak should not exist at this temperature for pure BAs. Therefore, to ensure it is not due to malfunctioning equipment, $C_\text{P}$ was measured three times on the same sample, and the peak was observed for all measurements, as shown in Fig. 2. Moreover, the peak was also present in the measurements of other crystals from different batches at exactly the same temperature. The change of enthalpy of the peak in Fig. 2 is $\Delta_r H\ (peak) \approx 0.9\ \text{J}\ \text{g}^{-1}$, but it generally varies in the range of 0.9 to $2.2\ \text{J}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ for different samples. This peak might be due to an impurity phase, for which we are carrying out further studies.

## III. THERMODYNAMIC PROPERTIES CALCULATION

Next, the measured $C_\text{P}$ with respect to temperature was fit to a polynomial equation,²⁴
$$
C_\text{P} = aT^2 + bT + cT^{-0.5} + d.
$$

The resulting coefficients are $a=2.65 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-3}$, $b=-7.24 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-2}$, $c=-1.59 \times 10^3\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-0.5}$, and $d=1.43 \times 10^2\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$. Additionally, the RMSE²⁵ of the fitted curve is 0.7077, and the R-square²⁶ value is 0.9536, both of which indicate a good fit. Taking into account the discussion by Dumont and Monteil,²⁰,²¹,²⁷ we accept that the enthalpy of formation and the entropy of BAs at room temperature are $H_f(298\ \text{K})=-30\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $S_f(298\ \text{K})=40\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, respectively.

$$

The measured entropy is in good agreement with the prediction, but there is a difference for the Gibbs energy and a significant disparity for the enthalpy for which the reasons are unknown yet.

## IV. BAs SINGLE CRYSTAL GROWTH

To the best of our knowledge, chemical vapor transport (CVT) has been the only method reported to successfully grow BAs SCs to date. We studied three different CVT approaches for BAs SC growth by choosing different combinations of precursor sources. In this section, we describe each approach and analyze the underlying theory of each separately.

### A. BAs powder as precursor (1st approach)

In earlier studies of BAs SC growth, BAs powder was initially prepared in a tube from a solid-state reaction of pure arsenic (As) and boron (B) at 1073 K.¹¹ The stoichiometric BAs powder was then used as the precursor in another tube with a halogen as the transport agent to produce BAs SCs using the CVT method. In this approach, the transport equation is defined as follows:

$$\mathrm{BAs}(s) + \frac{3}{2}X_{2}(g) \leftrightharpoons \mathrm{BX}_{3}(g) + \frac{1}{4}\ \mathrm{As}_{4}(g)\quad (\mathrm{X = I,\ Br,\ or\ Cl}).$$

We have analyzed the transport equation with three common halogens, iodine, bromine, and chlorine. To compare these reactions, the enthalpy of the reaction $(\Delta_{r}H)$ at room temperature and the equilibrium constant $(K_{\text{P}})$ at 900 K were calculated using equation

$$\ln K_{\text{P}} = -\frac{\Delta_{r}G}{RT},$$

$$\begin{gathered}
\mathrm{BAs}(s) + \frac{3}{2}\mathrm{I}_{2}(g) \leftrightharpoons \mathrm{BI}_{3}(g) + \frac{1}{4}\ \mathrm{As}_{4}(g), \\
\Delta_{r}H(298\ \mathrm{K}) = 46.1\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}} \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \mathrm{K}) = 2.2 \times 10^{-3},
\end{gathered}\tag{1}$$

$$\begin{gathered}
\mathrm{BAs}(s) + \frac{3}{2}\mathrm{Br}_{2}(g) \leftrightharpoons \mathrm{BBr}_{3}(g) + \frac{1}{4}\mathrm{As}_{4}(g), \\
\Delta_{r}H(298\ \mathrm{K}) = -182.2\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}} \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \mathrm{K}) = 2.7 \times 10^{10},
\end{gathered}\tag{2}$$

$$\begin{gathered}
\mathrm{BAs}(s) + \frac{3}{2}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(g) \leftrightharpoons \mathrm{BCl}_{3}(g) + \frac{1}{4}\ \mathrm{As}_{4}(g), \\
\Delta_{r}H(298\ \mathrm{K}) = -334.6\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}} \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \mathrm{K}) = 1.6 \times 10^{19}.
\end{gathered}\tag{3}$$

The enthalpy values of reactions (2) and (3) are negative, and thus, the reaction of BAs using chlorine or bromine is exothermic and any possible transfer would be from the cold end to the hot end. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 4, since the equilibrium constant values of these two reactions are extremely high $(K_{\text{P}} \gg 10^{4})$, CVT is not possible and these halogens are not suitable agents for BAs SC growth by CVT. No successful CVT experiments using either of these two halogens have yet been reported.

On the other hand, the reaction of BAs with iodine, reaction (1), is endothermic, and the transfer direction is from the hot end to the cold end. It also has an equilibrium constant that lies within the acceptable range $(10^{-4} < K_{\text{P}} < 10^{4})$. Thus, iodine is the only suitable agent among those studied for obtaining BAs SCs using the CVT method.¹⁹ We, therefore, examined reaction (1) in more detail by plotting the partial pressure of the gaseous species. Figure 5 shows the partial pressure of all transport gaseous species in reaction (1). As can be seen in Fig. 5, at lower temperatures, the

### B. B + As and excess As as precursor (2nd approach)
In another approach to BAs SC growth by CVT that is more commonly employed currently, B and As are used as precursors and are placed directly in the tube, rather than preparing BAs precursor separately (as described in Sec. IV A), along with iodine as the agent and extra As. In this case, since a more complex gas phase is formed, the chemical transport of BAs cannot be explained by just one reaction; several equilibrium reactions are important. This is a type of congruent transport, and the number of equilibrium reactions ($r_{\text{u}}$) is derived by the equation below:³⁰

$$
r_{\text{u}} = s - k + 1,
$$

where $s$ is the number of possible gas species and $k$ is the number of elements. The gas species $\text{BI}_3$, $\text{AsI}_3$, $\text{As}_4^{*}$, $\text{As}_4$, $\text{As}_2$, $\text{I}_2$, and I might occur in the sealed tube, so this leads to $r_{\text{u}} = 5$. $\text{As}_4^{*}$ indicates arsenic that originates exclusively from the dissolution of BAs, as in reactions (5) and (6).

Equation (4) is one of the preliminary reactions that occurs in the tube, but since its product is solid-state, it cannot work as a transport equation. However, the BAs polycrystal produced by this reaction is used by reactions (5) and (6) for transportation to the other side of the tube and to create BAs SCs,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{B}(s) + \frac{1}{4}\text{As}_4(g) \leftrightarrows \text{BAs}(s), \\
&\Delta_{r}H(298\ \text{K}) = -68.3\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \text{K}) = 35.7.
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
$$

Below are five equilibrium reactions that were used to determine the partial pressure of all possible gaseous species mentioned above, along with their reaction enthalpy values at room temperature and their equilibrium constant values at 900 K:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{BAs}(s) + \text{AsI}_3(g) \leftrightarrows \text{BI}_3(g) + \frac{1}{2}\text{As}_4^{*}(g), \\
&\Delta_{r}H(298\ \text{K}) = 113.6\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \text{K}) = 2.7 \times 10^{-6},
\end{aligned} \tag{5}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{BAs}(s) + \frac{3}{2}\text{I}_2(g) \leftrightarrows \text{BI}_3(g) + \frac{1}{4}\text{As}_4^{*}(g), \\
&\Delta_{r}H(298\ \text{K}) = 46.1\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \text{K}) = 2.2 \times 10^{-3},
\end{aligned} \tag{6}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{As}_4(g) + 6\text{I}_2(g) \leftrightarrows 4\text{AsI}_3(g), \\
&\Delta_{r}H(298\ \text{K}) = -270\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \text{K}) = 4.0 \times 10^{11},
\end{aligned} \tag{7}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{As}_4(g) \leftrightarrows 2\text{As}_2(g), \\
&\Delta_{r}H(298\ \text{K}) = 228.3\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \text{K}) = 4.3 \times 10^{-6},
\end{aligned} \tag{8}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{I}_2(g) \leftrightarrows 2\text{I}(g), \\
&\Delta_{r}H(298\ \text{K}) = 151.3\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \quad K_{\text{P}}\ (900\ \text{K}) = 4.1 \times 10^{-4}.
\end{aligned} \tag{9}
$$

Equations (5) and (6) can contribute to the transport of BAs using the transport-effective species $\text{BI}_3$, but the homogenous equilibrium of the dissociation of arsenic and iodine plays no role in the mass transfer. ### C. BAs SCs with excess As as precursor (3rd approach)

In the third investigated approach, small BAs SCs obtained using one of the previously described approaches are used as the precursor and are placed in a sealed tube along with excess arsenic and iodine as agents. This approach uses small BAs SCs as a pure source to obtain BAs SCs of larger size and higher quality, as discussed in Ref. 12, although we used a different transport agent $\left(\mathrm{I}_{2}\right)$ than that used in the previous study $\left(\mathrm{TeI}_{4}\right)$. All of the reactions discussed for the second approach (Sec. IV B) are applicable to this approach except reaction (4). Since reaction (4) is not considered a transport equation and was not directly involved in our calculation, our analysis here is the same as for the second approach, except that in this case, BAs is ready to transfer from the beginning of the process, so faster transport is observed in this approach. As explained in Sec. IV B, the product of reaction (4), BAs, is used by Eqs. (5) and (6) for transportation, and since this reaction is slow, we expect that replacing B powders with previously obtained BAs SCs would make the transportation faster. Experiments in Ref. 12 confirm this expectation; additionally, more and thicker crystals

form at the cold end when using the BAs precursor, which shows that the transport rate of this approach is faster than that of the other two.

Optimization: As in the second approach (Sec. IV B), the transport rate of this approach can be increased by using additional excess arsenic, and the optimizations mentioned in Sec. IV A can be applied to this approach as well.

## V. DISCUSSION AND COMPARISON
Three approaches to BAs SC growth by CVT using different combinations of precursors have been analyzed, and each has advantages and disadvantages, which are discussed below.

The first approach (Sec. IV A) has a slow transport rate, resulting in slow SC growth, but not requiring excess arsenic in the tube is a potential advantage. Excess arsenic builds up internal pressure, so the lack of excess arsenic makes the first approach more flexible for changing the tube dimensions to optimize this approach without being concerned about reaching the maximum working pressure and causing the tube to explode. With the flexibility provided by this approach, our analysis shows that choosing tubes with shorter lengths would efficiently increase the transport rate.

The best quality BAs SC obtained thus far has been made by using the second approach (Sec. IV B).$^{5,14,15}$ Crystals made employing this approach have shown near-calculated thermal conductivity values, which is an indication of having fewer defects. However, research has shown that boron powder, which is not 100% pure, is a source of impurities in the crystals.$^{16,33}$ Thus, using boron powder directly in tube is a weakness of the second approach and a source of contamination. The third approach (Sec. IV C) has the advantage of using preselected pure BAs SCs with relatively higher thermal conductivity as the source, which can be considered as a source-purifying step.

Additionally, in both the second and third approaches, excess arsenic has an important role in controlling the transport rate. We have shown that the third approach has a higher transport rate and results in more crystals at the cold end. Although the crystals include defects, imperfections, and holes, these issues can be explained by the lack of good nucleation techniques and of adequate space for crystal growth, which forces the crystals either to merge or to grow on top of one another. However, the third approach, with its faster transport rate and its use of a purer material as the growth source, has good potential to be superior to the other two approaches in terms of the size and quality of crystals obtained.

## VI. CONCLUSION
We measured the heat capacity of a BAs sample with respect to temperature and compared our experimental results to the previous theoretical data. A small amount of arsenic was observed to separate from the BAs sample during baking at 973 K for 1 h. We calculated the enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs free energy for BAs in the range of 298 to 1150 K and compared our results with the previously predicted values. We found that the BAs formation reaction becomes endothermic at 984 K. Finally, BAs SC growth using the chemical vapor transport method was further analyzed using thermodynamic concepts. Three approaches to growth using different combinations of precursors were explained theoretically, and these analyses were compared with experimental results. By considering the advantages and disadvantages of each approach, we concluded that using a precursor combination of small BAs SCs with excess arsenic has the fastest transport rate and has good potential for growing better BAs single crystals.
