# Thermodynamic properties of silicalite $SiO_{2}^{a}$

G. K. JOHNSON, I. R. TASKER, D. A. HOWELL, $^{b}$
Argonne National Laboratory, Argonne, Illinois 60439, U.S.A.
and J. V. SMITH
Department of Geophysical Sciences, The University of Chicago,
Chicago, Illinois 60637, U.S.A.

( Received 9 September 1986; in final form 3 November 1986)

Silicalite, a polymorph of $SiO_{2}$ , is a new and interesting molecular-sieve material. Low temperature (5 to 350 K) heat-capacity, flourine-combustion, high-temperature (350 to 1500 K) drop-calorimetric, and reaction-calorimetric with HF(aq) measurements have beenperformed on this material. At $T'=298.15 K, \Delta_{f} H_{m}^{\circ}(T')=-(905.20 \pm 0.84) kJ \cdot mol^{-1}$ ; $C_{p, m}^{\circ}(T')=(46.34 \pm 0.23) J \cdot K^{-1} \cdot mol^{-1} ;{\Delta_{0}^{T} S_{m}^{\circ}(T')}=(46.29 \pm 0.23) J \cdot K^{-1} \cdot mol^{-1} ;$ and $\Delta_{f} G_{m}^{\circ}(T')=-(852.23 \pm 0.84) kJ \cdot mol^{-1}$ . The enthalpies of reaction with 24.4 mass per cent hydrofluoric acid of silicalite at mass concentrations of 0.5,1,2, and $4 g \cdot dm^{-3}$ were determined to be $-(144.91 \pm 0.23), -(144.80 \pm 0.10), -(145.07 \pm 0.12)$ , and $-(145.06 \pm 0.14) kJ \cdot mol^{-1}$ , respectively. The enthalpies of reaction of silicalite with 20.1 and15.0 mass per cent hydrofluoric acid at a silicalite mass concentration of $1 g \cdot dm^{-3}$ were determined to be $-(143.85 \pm 0.19)$ and $-(142.68 \pm 0.08) kJ \cdot mol^{-1}$ , respectively. Because silicalite reacts rapidly with HF(aq) at 298.15 K, it has potential as a reference material for thermochemical measurements on inorganic silicates and silicate minerals. Silicalite is less stable than quartz, cristobalite, or tridymite, but more stable than $SiO_{2}$ (glass) or the high pressure forms coesite or stishovite.

## 1. Introduction

Silicalite, a polymorth of $SiO_{2}$ , is a new and interesting molecular sieve whose synthesis and crystal structure were reported by Flanigen et al. $^{(1)}$ The crystal structure of silicalite is topologically related to that $^{(2)}$ of the ZSM-5 series of zeolite molecular sieves, and there is apparently a continuous compositional change as the Al content of the ZSM-5 series is reduced to zero. An attractive property of silicalite from a calorimetric point of view is its ease of solution in HF(aq). Traditionally, the dissolution of $\alpha$-SiO₂ in HF(aq) has been used as a reference reaction in silicate calorimetry. However, because of the low reactivity of this material, even in concentrated hydrofluoric acid, it has been necessary to make calorimetric measurements at elevated temperatures (323 to 353 K). Despite many studies (see references 3 and 4 for a summary), there still exists⁽⁴·⁵⁾ much uncertainty about the temperature, acid concentration, and $\alpha$-quartz concentration dependencies of the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz with HF(aq). Because of the greater reactivity of silicalite, calorimetric measurements with it can be done more readily than with $\alpha$-quartz and thus some of the uncertainties involved in the thermochemistry of H₂SiF₆(aq) can be removed. Hence in the present investigation we have undertaken two goals, determination of thermodynamic properties of silicalite and clarification of some of the thermodynamic properties of reaction of SiO₂ with hydrofluoric acid.

Four types of calorimetric measurement on silicalite are reported. Low-temperature (5 to 350 K) adiabatic calorimetry gave the molar heat capacity $C_{p,\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}$ and the standard molar entropy $\Delta_{0}^{T}S_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(T)$; fluorine-bomb calorimetry was used to measure the energy of combustion of silicalite in fluorine from which $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}}H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(298.15\ \mathrm{K})$ was derived (throughout this text we use $T' = 298.15\ \mathrm{K}$); high-temperature drop calorimetry gave the molar enthalpy increments $\Delta_{T'}^{T}H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(T)$, from which the heat capacity and entropy were deduced; and reaction calorimetry in HF(aq) gave the enthalpies of reaction $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(T')$ as a function of silicalite and acid concentrations.

## 2. Experimental

### MATERIALS
The silicalite sample was prepared at Union Carbide Corporation, Tarrytown, NY, by procedures similar to those described previously.⁽¹⁾ A 30 g sample, 5008-3-1-1, was specially prepared by R. L. Patton and R. Gajek (Linde Division, Union Carbide Corporation) from Cab-o-Sil silica, tetrapropylammonium hydroxide, and water since earlier preparations from the same materials contained only $\approx 50$ to $90 \times 10^{-6}$ mass fractions of Na₂O and Al₂O₃. The mean diameter of the barrel-shaped crystals is $\approx 4\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. Upon receipt of this material at Argonne, it was calcined at 873 K overnight and transferred, while hot, into a helium-atmosphere glovebox. The sample was not again exposed to air during any of the measurements described herein. Chemical analysis showed mass fractions of hydrogen and carbon of $31 \times 10^{-6}$ and $93 \times 10^{-6}$, respectively. Spectrochemical analysis showed the presence of metallic impurities (in mass fractions $\times 10^{-6}$): Al, 50; Fe, 100; Mg, 20; Ni, 30; Na, 50; and Ti, 100.

Purified fluorine (99.99 moles per cent) was prepared by distillation of commercial fluorine in a low-temperature still.⁽⁶⁾ Hydrofluoric acid was Baker "Analyzed Reagent" diluted to the appropriate concentration with distilled water.

# LOW-TEMPERATURE CALORIMETRIC PROCEDURES

The calorimeter was loaded and unloaded in a glovebox filled with dry helium. A 1.83179 g sample of silicalite was loaded into the calorimeter (laboratory designation 6-GS-1) with gentle tamping. The calorimeter, constructed from oxygen-free high-thermal-conductivity copper, had an internal volume of $5.976\ \mathrm{cm}^3$ at $T'$ and was sealed by means of a gold gasket. The calorimeter was evacuated, and a small amount of He(g) was introduced to act as an exchange gas and promote rapid thermal equilibrium. For buoyancy and volume calculations, the density of silicalite was taken to be $1.76\ \mathrm{g\cdot cm^{-3}}$. Descriptions of the cryostat, peripheral equipment, and calorimetric procedure along with other relevant details are available in the literature.$^{(7-9)}$

Briefly, the temperatures of the adiabatic shields surrounding the calorimeter were automatically controlled to within $\pm 0.001\ \mathrm{K}$ of the calorimeter temperature at all times. Experimental temperatures were measured using a calibrated platinum resistance thermometer,$^{(8)}$ a calibrated double potentiometer and a photo-galvanometer. The temperature scale used agrees with the thermodynamic scale to $\pm 0.1\ \mathrm{K}$ from 5 to 14 K, to $\pm 0.03\ \mathrm{K}$ from 14 to 90 K, and to $\pm 0.05\ \mathrm{K}$ from 90 to 350 K. Temperature differences are reliable to $\pm 0.01\ \mathrm{K}$ at 4.2 K, to $\pm 0.0008\ \mathrm{K}$ at 14 K, to $\pm 0.0001\ \mathrm{K}$ from 25 to 58 K, and to $\pm 0.001\ \mathrm{K}$ above 58 K. The temperature increment $\Delta T$ was approximately 2 K for each experiment below 20 K. From 20 to 100 K, $\Delta T \approx 0.1\langle T\rangle$ (where $\langle T\rangle$ is the mean temperature across the increment) and, above 100 K, $\Delta T \approx 10\ \mathrm{K}$. Time and potentiometer readings were relayed to an on-line computer, which in turn printed a record of temperature as a function of time. From this, corrections for small deviations from truly adiabatic behavior were made using the steady-state temperature drifts before and after the heating period. The current through and the potential drop across the calorimeter heater during a heating period were automatically recorded at predetermined intervals. A preset timer calibrated against NBS station WWV was used to fix the heating time with an uncertainty less than $\pm 0.01$ per cent. All standards of mass, resistance, and potential difference are traceable to the National Bureau of Standards. The only difference between the equipment used in this work and that described in the literature is that the thermocouple linking the calorimeter with the middle adiabatic shield is now made of Chromel P-to-(gold + mass fraction 0.07 of iron).

# ENERGY-OF-COMBUSTION MEASUREMENTS

This part of the investigation was concerned with measurements of the energy of combustion of silicalite in fluorine according to the reaction:

$$\mathrm{SiO_2(cr,\ silicalite) + 2F_2(g) = SiF_4(g) + O_2(g).} \tag{1}$$

Preliminary experiments indicated that the silicalite sample was spontaneously combustible in fluorine. Subsequent experiments, however, revealed that this was not always so and in later experiments (nos. 6 to 11, table 3) a small amount of powdered silicon was used as an ignition aid. The preliminary experiments also

established that a thin nickel crucible (mass $\approx 11$ g) made a satisfactory combustion support but that the reaction did not go to completion. Even when high-pressure fluorine was used, a small mass (4 to 8 mg) of white powder remained on the crucible following the combustion. This powder had not melted and had an X-ray diffraction pattern identical to that of silicalite.

The calorimetric system, laboratory designation ANL-R-2, was of the rotating-bomb type similar to that described by Hubbard, Katz, and Waddington.${}^{(10)}$ Because silicalite reacts spontaneously with fluorine, a nickel storage vessel similar to that described by Nuttall *et al.*${}^{(11)}$ was used to isolate the reactants from one another until such time as ignition was desired. Calorimetric temperatures were measured with a quartz-crystal thermometer (Hewlett-Packard Model 2804-A) and stored and processed by minicomputer. Five combustions in oxygen of National Bureau of Standards benzoic acid (sample 39i) whose certified specific energy of combustion is $-(26434\pm 3)\ \text{J}\cdot\text{g}^{-1}$ were carried out for calibration purposes. A value of $(14076.02\pm 0.71)\ \text{J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$ was found for the energy equivalent $\varepsilon(\text{calor})$ of the calorimetric system and its standard deviation. Upon completion of the calibrations, the reaction vessel was dried and fluorinated by means of non-calorimetric combustions of (silicalite + fluorine). Subsequently, this part of the apparatus was opened only in a dry-helium-filled glovebox.

The procedures used in the combustions were as follows. In the glovebox, the silicalite sample was placed in a tared nickel crucible and the (crucible + silicalite) was weighed. A small amount of powdered silicon (approximately 5 mg) was then placed on top of the silicalite (but only in some experiments as explained earlier) and the crucible and contents was reweighed. The bomb was assembled, and the storage tank, which had been charged to 2170 kPa pressure of fluorine, was attached. The assembled reaction was removed from the glovebox, evacuated, placed in the calorimeter, and the combustion performed.

Upon completion of the experiment the reaction vessel was evacuated, taken into the glovebox, and opened. The amount of unreacted silicalite was determined by weighing the (crucible + silicalite), gently brushing out the silicalite, and reweighing the crucible. The crucible itself did not undergo any change in mass during the combustions.

# HIGH-TEMPERATURE ENTHALPY MEASUREMENTS

The drop-calorimetric system${}^{(12)}$ used for the enthalpy measurements consisted of a resistance-heated molybdenum-core furnace and a copper-block calorimeter. Furnace-temperature measurements were made with platinum-to-(platinum + mass fraction 0.1 of rhodium) thermocouples. Calorimetric temperatures were determined with a quartz-crystal thermometer (Hewlett-Packard Model 2804-A). The energy equivalent $\varepsilon(\text{calor})$ of the empty copper-block calorimeter was determined by electrical calibration. The calibrations were performed with various corrected temperature rises $\Delta\theta_{\text{c}}$ corresponding to those of the silicalite experiments with the final temperature of the system very near $T'$ in each experiment. Values of the energy equivalent varied slightly with $\Delta\theta_{\text{c}}$, from $4376.46\ \text{J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$ for $\Delta\theta_{\text{c}}=0.5\ \text{K}$ to

$4374.79 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{K}^{-1}$ for $\Delta \theta_{\mathrm{c}}=2.0 \mathrm{~K}$. Seven calibration experiments with $\Delta \theta_{\mathrm{c}}=1 \mathrm{~K}$ yielded a standard deviation of the mean of $\pm 0.35 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{K}^{-1}$ for $\varepsilon$ (calor). In this study, experiments were conducted over the temperature range from 350 to $1500 \mathrm{~K}$. Experiments in which the furnace temperature was $850 \mathrm{~K}$ or lower were conducted with $1.33 \mathrm{kPa}$ pressure of helium in the furnace to accelerate temperature equilibration. At higher temperatures, the furnace was evacuated and heat transfer occurred primarily by radiation. Following the capsule drop in these experiments, the gate was closed and $1.33 \mathrm{kPa}$ pressure of helium was admitted to the calorimeter to facilitate heat transfer. The performance of the calorimetric system had been checked previously with synthetic sapphire (National Bureau of Standards SRM 740), both with helium in the furnace $^{(13)}$ and with an evacuated furnace, $^{(14)}$ and the average differences of the experimental results from the certificate values were 0.13 per cent and 0.17 per cent, respectively.

Preliminary experiments indicated that silicalite was compatible with platinum capsules up to $1500 \mathrm{~K}$. Hence, the silicalite (mass $2.67467 \mathrm{~g}$ ) was welded in a platinum capsule (mass $15.06539 \mathrm{~g}$ ) which also contained $2.06 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~mol}$ of $\mathrm{He}(\mathrm{g})$. Enthalpy increments of an empty platinum capsule of similar shape and mass were determined over the same temperature range and with the same furnace conditions as were used in the silicalite experiments. In our experiments the sample contributed approximately 55 per cent of the total measured enthalpy change.

## ENTHALPY-OF-REACTION MEASUREMENTS

An LKB-8700 precision calorimetric system was used for the enthalpy of reaction measurements according to

$$\mathrm{SiO}_{2}(\mathrm{cr}, \text { silicalite })+6 \mathrm{HF}(\mathrm{aq})=\left(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SiF}_{6}+2 \mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}\right)(\mathrm{aq}). \tag{2}$$

The $100 \mathrm{~cm}^{3}$ reaction vessel and stirrer were both constructed of 18-carat gold. The reaction vessel was modified for quartz-crystal thermometry by the addition of a re-entry well, in which was placed the sensor of a quartz-crystal thermometer (HewlettPackard Model 2804-A). The samples were loaded into Teflon ampoules $^{(15)}$ in the glovebox.

The energy equivalent of the calorimetric system was based on electrical calibrations performed before and after each reaction experiment. The calibrations covered the same temperature range as the corresponding enthalpy of reaction measurements, and the mean temperature of all such experiments was $(298.15 \pm 0.01) \mathrm{K}$.

Enthalpies of reaction of silicalite with 24.4 mass per cent hydrofluoric acid were measured at silicalite mass concentrations of $0.5,1$, and $2 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$. To derive results for a silicalite mass concentration of $4 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$, measurements were made of the enthalpy of reaction of $0.2 \mathrm{~g}$ of silicalite with $100 \mathrm{~cm}^{3}$ of an acid solution already containing $2 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$ of silicalite. The latter arrangement was necessary because the ampoules would not hold more than approximately $0.2 \mathrm{~g}$ of silicalite. Measurements were also made at a silicalite mass concentration of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$ in 20.1 and 15.0 mass per cent of HF. All reactions were very fast requiring less than 5 min after opening the ampoule for the calorimeter to achieve its final drift rate.

### 3. Results

#### LOW-TEMPERATURE HEAT CAPACITY

Experimental values of $C_{p, \mathrm{m}}^{\circ} / R$ are given in table 1. They were calculated as the difference between the results obtained with the loaded calorimeter and those previously obtained for the empty calorimeter. Minor corrections were made for differences in the amounts of helium, Apiezon-T grease, copper, and gold between the two sets of experiments. No correction for helium desorption was necessary. Past experience has indicated that the thermal effects associated with this process are plainly manifested in the curve of $C_{p}$ against $T$ for the region below $T=30 \mathrm{~K}$. The absence of any such anomalous behavior was taken as an indication that any adsorption of helium was not affecting the results. A small curvature correction equal to $-\left(\mathrm{d}^{2} C_{p, \mathrm{m}}^{\circ} / \mathrm{d} T^{2}\right)(\Delta T)^{2} / 24$ was applied to each heat-capacity determination. At $5 \mathrm{~K}$ the heat capacity of the sample was about 10 per cent of the total; at $20 \mathrm{~K}$, 15 per cent; at $100 \mathrm{~K}, 6$ per cent; and from 200 to $350 \mathrm{~K}$, approximately 10 per cent. These values are significantly lower than normal due to the low packing density and density of silicalite powder. Consequently, the $C_{p, \mathrm{m}}^{\circ} / R$ values given in table 1 are estimated to be somewhat more uncertain than usual: by $\pm 5$ per cent near $5 \mathrm{~K}$, about $\pm 1$ per cent near $14 \mathrm{~K}$, and about $\pm 0.5$ per cent at higher temperatures. Because Hay et al. ${ }^{(16)}$ had reported a monoclinic-to-orthorhombic transition in the region between 318 and $333 \mathrm{~K}$ we made several runs through this region. The values for the forms other than silicalite were taken from references 27, 29, and 30. It has been assumed in these comparisons that $S_{m}^{\circ}(T) = 0$ for $T \to 0$ for all the silica polymorphs except glass, for which the value $5.11\ \text{J·K}^{-1}·\text{mol}^{-1}$ is taken.$^{(29)}$ Examination of the table shows that silicalite is less stable than quartz, cristobalite, or tridymite, but more stable than $\text{SiO}_{2}$ (glass) or the high-pressure forms coesite or stishovite. However, it is important to note that there is overlap of some of the values if the errors are independent and not correlated; furthermore, there is evidence for a systematic bias for coesite which is related to surface energy of small crystals in an impure sample.

These results provide an important target for theorists attempting to explain the physical properties of inorganic materials in terms of chemical bonding. The relative instability at $101.325$ kPa of coesite and stishovite with respect to the other polymorphs of silica can be explained in terms of a $p\Delta V$ term which is dependent on the degree of close-packing allowed by the topological features of the chemical bonding. Whereas this term favors coesite and stishovite respectively at pressures greater than $2.5\ \text{GPa}^{(30,31,32)}$ and $\approx 9\ \text{GPa},^{(30,32)}$ it works against them at lower pressures. Probably the important feature of the chemical bonding which accounts for most of this term in coesite is the repulsion between oxygen atoms not bonded to the same Si atom; however, this has not yet been tested by thorough calculations. For the microporous varieties of silica like silicalite, it will be interesting to see whether $\Delta_{f}G_{m}^{\circ}$ is related to the percentage of void volume, or whether other factors such as the topologic constraints on the flexibility of the O-Si-O and Si-O-Si angles and of the O-O distances must be considered. Further calorimetric studies of other microporous varieties of silica should be made as tests of lattice-energy calculations (see, for example, reference 33).

A reversible monoclinic-to-orthorhombic transition in calcined water-free silicalite has been placed between 318 and 333 K by X-ray powder diffraction and between 328 and 333 K by $^{29}\text{Si}$ nuclear magnetic resonance.$^{(16)}$ The transition is gradual, and subtle changes in the n.m.r. spectra of intermediate states require further study.$^{(34)}$ Although the present study does not show unequivocal evidence for the

transition, a plot of $C_{p, \mathrm{~m}}^{\circ}$ against $T$ (figure 1) gives a hint of an anomaly between 320 and 340 K. Because the silicalite structure is rather rigid, it is suggested that the amount of bond-bending at the transition is less than that for the pronounced $\alpha$-to-$\beta$ transition in quartz, and that the thermochemical anomaly would be small. Detailed crystal-structure studies are needed to test this suggestion.

The results reported herein for the enthalpies of reaction of various amounts of silicalite with 24.4 mass per cent of HF(aq) substantiate the generally accepted belief that for $\alpha-\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ the enthalpy of reaction is insensitive to the amount reacted. The results suggest that a minimum in the enthalpy of reaction exists in the vicinity of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$ which then increases toward more dilute or concentrated solutions.

The results obtained from the measurements of the enthalpies of reaction of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$ of silicalite with 24.4, 20.1, and 15.0 mass per cent of HF(aq) at $T^{\prime}$ divided by the mass percentage of HF can be fitted to a straight line (correlation quoteint 0.99994 ) with a slope of $-225.8 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. This differs significantly from that measured by Kilday and Prosen, ${ }^{(3)}-80 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ and Hummel and Schwiete, ${ }^{(35)}-350 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. Kilday and Prosen made measurements at $353.15 \mathrm{~K}$ of the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz (NBS Standard Reference Material No. 1654) with HF whose mass percentage ranged from 18 to 30. Hummel and Schwiete made their measurements at $299.6 \mathrm{~K}$ on very finely divided $\alpha$-quartz with HF mass percentages ranging from 9.8 to 39.0. We do not understand why there should be such a large variation in the acid mass-percentage effect, but it may be due to the slow reaction of $\alpha-\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$. However, because of the rapidity of the silicalite reaction, even in dilute acid, the result obtained by us should be more reliable.

Finally, we address the question of the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz with hydrofluoric acid at $T^{\prime}$. Kilday and Prosen made measurements of the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz $\left(5 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}\right)$ with 24.4 mass per cent of HF at temperatures between $T^{\prime}$ and $358 \mathrm{~K}$. They found that the enthalpy of reaction was linear with $T$ and at $T^{\prime}$ they report $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}} H_{\mathrm{m}}=-136.69 \mathrm{~kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. Converting their result to a mass concentration of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$, using our results, we derive $-136.95 \mathrm{~kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. Hemingway and Robie ${ }^{(4)}$ report measurements of the enthalpies of reaction of NBS-SRM $1654 \alpha$-quartz $\left(1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}\right)$ with 20.1 mass per cent of HF. They obtained enthalpies of reaction of $-(140.21 \pm 0.21) \mathrm{kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ at $348 \mathrm{~K}$ and $-(137.74 \pm 0.21) \mathrm{kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ at $333 \mathrm{~K}$. The former value is in excellent agreement with that obtained by Kilday and Prosen whereas the latter is significantly less negative. If we make a straight-line extrapolation of these two values to $T^{\prime}$ we derive $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}} H_{\mathrm{m}}=-132.00 \mathrm{~kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. Converting to an acid mass percentage of 24.4, again using results reported in this paper, this value is changed to $-132.95 \mathrm{~kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. More recently, Liou and Donahoe ${ }^{(5)}$ used the same calorimeter as Hemingway and Robie, with some modifications, to measure the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz (NBS-SRM 1654, sieved to between 44 and $55 \mu \mathrm{m}$ ) with 24.4 mass per cent of $\mathrm{HF}$ at 308 and $323 \mathrm{~K}$. Their results, -137.35 and $-142.30 \mathrm{~kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$, respectively, were extrapolated linearly to $T^{\prime}$ to obtain $\Delta_{\mathrm{r}} H_{\mathrm{m}}=-(134.09 \pm 0.40) \mathrm{kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. The measurements at $308 \mathrm{~K}$ were for $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ mass concentrations of 0.5 and $2.0 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$. We shall assume that the derived result applies to a $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ mass concentration of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$ as well. In this case, the result at $308 \mathrm{~K}$ is in excellent agreement with

Kilday and Prosen's but the value at 323 K is significantly more negative. It is not possible to reconcile $\Delta_{r} H_{\mathrm{m}}(333 \mathrm{~K})$ from Hemingway and Robie with the $\Delta_{r} H_{\mathrm{m}}(323 \mathrm{~K})$ from Liou and Donahoe; both results were presumably obtained with the same calorimetric systems and $\alpha$-quartz samples.

At this laboratory we had previously measured $^{(9)}$ the enthalpy of reaction at $T^{\prime}$ of $\alpha$-quartz (NBS-SRM 1654) with 24.4 mass per cent of $\mathrm{HF}$ at a $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ mass concentration of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$. The reported $\Delta_{r} H_{\mathrm{m}}$ was $-(135.59 \pm 0.18) \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$. In an attempt to shorten the reaction time (which was still 3 to $4 \mathrm{~h}$ ) the SRM 1654 material was sieved and only the smaller particles, 37 to $44 \mu \mathrm{m}$, were used. Kilday and Prosen had previously shown that the $\Delta_{r} H_{\mathrm{m}}$ of SRM 1654 did not vary with sample size and hence we do not consider this a reason for differences in $\Delta_{r} H_{\mathrm{m}}$.

References 3 and 4 summarize much work does prior to the introduction of SRM 1654. Many of these studies, especially those done near room temperature, involved the use of extremely finely divided $\alpha-\mathrm{SiO}_{2} 2$ to $5 \mu \mathrm{m}$ in size, in order to increase the rate of reaction. Such finely divided samples introduce problems associated with adsorbed water, possible conversion to an amorphous phase, $^{(36)}$ and possible surface energy effects. Hemingway and Robie $^{(4)}$ have reported that material of this size has a $\Delta_{r} H_{\mathrm{m}}$ greater by 1200 to $1400 \mathrm{~J} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ than SRM 1654 . Hence, results obtained with the finely divided material are subject to interpretation and uncertainty.

In summary, it seems that the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz with $\mathrm{HF}(\mathrm{aq})$ is fairly well defined at 348 to $353 \mathrm{~K}$, but has not been independently confirmed at $T^{\prime}$. We can use the results presented in this study to derive the enthalpy of reaction of $\alpha$-quartz with $\mathrm{HF}(\mathrm{aq})$ at $T^{\prime}$. If we combine the enthalpies of reactions (1), $-(710.58 \pm 0.70) \mathrm{kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$, and (2), $-(144.80 \pm 0.10) \mathrm{kJ} \cdot \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$, with $\Delta_{\mathrm{c}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}$ of the reaction:

This result, for the particular mass concentration of $1 \mathrm{~g} \cdot \mathrm{dm}^{-3}$ of $\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ and mass percentage 24.4 of $\mathrm{HF}$, is significantly more negative than any of the values listed above, although within the combined uncertainties it agrees with that reported by Kilday and Prosen. $^{(3)}$ We believe the result for reaction (6) derived herein is more reliable than those previously reported. However, due to the method of derivation, the uncertainty is larger than would be expected from direct measurements of the enthalpy of reaction and, therefore, is not a good value on which to base the thermochemistry of silicates. On the other hand, because the enthalpy of formation of silicalite was determined by combustion in fluorine and is independent of $\Delta_{\mathrm{f}} H_{\mathrm{m}}^{\circ}(\alpha$-quartz), and because it reacts readily with hydrofluoric acid at $T^{\prime}$, it is our suggestion that silicalite be used as a reference substance on which to base the thermochemistry of silicates.

Because there is evidence of structural differences between calcined samples of

silicalite synthesized by different techniques from various starting materials, it is essential that one particular sample be prepared in sufficient quantity to be used as a universal standard.
