# The thermodynamics and competitive kinetics of metastable $\tau$ phase development in MnAl-base alloys

Y. J. Kim and J. H. Perepezko

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Wisconsin-Madison, 1509 University Avenue, Madison, WI 53706 (USA)

(Received October 13, 1992; in revised form November 24, 1992)

## Abstract
The solidification of a metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase in undercooled Mn-Al-C alloys is examined in terms of the relative thermodynamic phase stability and competitive kinetics. The heat of transformation $\Delta H_{f}^{\tau \to \varepsilon}$ of the metastable $\tau$ phase to the equilibrium h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase and the heat of fusion $\Delta H_{f}^{\varepsilon \to l}$ of the h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase in a $\mathrm{Mn}_{0.55} \mathrm{Al}_{0.433} \mathrm{C}_{0.017}$ alloy were measured to determine the Gibbs free energy differences between the metastable and stable phases as a function of temperature. The results indicate that a minimum amount of undercooling of $\Delta T=87 \mathrm{~K}$ is required to form the metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase over the equilibrium phases in a $\mathrm{Mn}_{0.55} \mathrm{Al}_{0.433} \mathrm{C}_{0.017}$ alloy. The thermodynamic evaluation was applied further to develop a complete temperature-time-transformation (TTT) diagram of the $\mathrm{Mn}_{0.55} \mathrm{Al}_{0.433} \mathrm{C}_{0.017}$ alloy based on a solid-state transformation kinetics analysis of the $\varepsilon \to \tau$ reaction during isothermal treatments. This analysis reveals the critical cooling rate of the order of $20 \mathrm{~K} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ to bypass the metastable $\tau$ phase nucleation during solid state transformation in agreement with empirical observations. The combined thermodynamic and competitive kinetics analysis provide a unified guide for the processing of near equiatomic Mn-Al alloys.

## 1. Introduction
One of the highlights in the development of novel microstructures by advanced solidification processing methods is the common occurrence of various metastable structural states. Whether these structures represent ultrafine grain sizes, supersaturated compositions of equilibrium phases or non-equilibrium phase structures, their development has expanded significantly the range of microstructural options that are available for the synthesis of new materials and the attainment of new levels of performance.

Although a given novel microstructure may be synthesized through a variety of seemingly different processing routes, there is a common underlying thermodynamic criterion that is always applicable. Through the various stages of a processing operation the initial state of the reacting material must be energized to an elevated free energy configuration and then retained in a kinetically arrested configuration which represents a partially relaxed state. In the case of solidification processing operations, the energized state is achieved by cooling the liquid into the undercooled domain. While this viewpoint is widely acknowledged and frequently used to account for metastable structure synthesis, the application is usually developed in a qualitative manner. For the most part this is owing to the difficulty in accessing the undercooled state in a manner which allows for the measurement of the relevant thermodynamic properties. In fact, most of the thermodynamic measurements pertaining to metastable solidification reactions have been obtained from droplet samples which can exhibit a deep undercooling during slow cooling [1-4]. The commonly used rapid quench methods do not permit reliable thermodynamic measurements in general.

In a recent study, Kim and Perepezko [5] have reported the formation of a metastable $\tau$ phase with an $\mathrm{L1}_{0}$ structure directly from the melt in an Mn-Al alloy with a small addition of carbon by a containerless levitation melting and rapid quenching process. Since the $\tau$ phase is ferromagnetic, it can be distinguished readily from the non-ferromagnetic equilibrium phases, by the response to an external magnetic field. All previous rapid solidification studies of Mn-Al alloys have reported only the retention of a high temperature $\varepsilon$ phase with an h.c.p. structure, but these studies did not include containerless melt treatment in the processing method [6,7]. In order to evaluate the potential influence of containerless melt treatment on the undercooling response and solidification path for the development of the $\tau$ phase, it is necessary to obtain some information on the thermal history during processing. ## 2. Experimental procedure

High purity aluminum (99.999%), manganese (99.9%, electrolytic) and graphite (Grade UF4S) were used to prepare a $\mathrm{Mn}_{0.55} \mathrm{Al}_{0.433} \mathrm{C}_{0.017}$ alloy. Alloy ingots of approximately 10 g in total weight were prepared from the cleaned pure metals with a high frequency induction furnace. Graphite powders $(<54 \mu \mathrm{m}$ in diameter) were placed at the bottom of an alumina susceptor and the pieces of Mn and Al elements were overlaid on the graphite powders to ensure the complete wetting between graphite powders and the other alloy elements during the melting process. The ingots were heated to $1400{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ under an Ar gas environment and held for about 4 h to ensure that the graphite powder was in solution. Following induction melting, the ingots were encapsulated in fused silica tubes under vacuum and were homogenized at $1100{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 20 h, followed by ice-water quenching.

A Perkin Elmer 1700 High-Temperature Thermal Analysis system was used to monitor melting and crystallization at a controlled heating rate of 10 K $\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ and a cooling rate of $20 \mathrm{~K} \mathrm{~min}^{-1}$ using $\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$ as the reference material. For quantitative measurements of peak areas which are directly proportional to the enthalpy change [8], the thermal analysis data station (TADS) with the 1700 system was applied in the heat flux differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) mode with known sample weight. In this manner, the heat absorbed or released during the thermal treatment may be evaluated to provide information on the heat of transformation as well as the melting and crystallization temperatures for the sample material. In order to evaluate the proportionality constant of the peak area vs. enthalpy change [9], the apparatus is calibrated by measuring the peak area produced by a process which is accompanied by an accurately known enthalpy change. High purity silver (99.999% purity) was selected to carry out the calibration with $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}=11087$ $\mathrm{J} \mathrm{mole}^{-1}$ at the melting temperature, $T_{\mathrm{m}}=1234 \mathrm{~K}$ [10]. The homogenized ingots and DSC treated samples were analyzed with X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Siemens X-ray diffractometer operating with $\mathrm{Cu} \mathrm{K} \alpha$ radiation in the reflection (Bragg-Brentano) mode to identify the phases present.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Thermodynamic analysis

X-ray analysis of the alloy ingot after homogenization at $1100{ }^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 20 h followed by water quenching indicated a single phase $\varepsilon$ structure as shown in Fig. 1 (trace A). After the sample was cycled past the liquidus temperature (cooling rate $=20 \mathrm{~K} \mathrm{~min}^{-1}$ ) in the DSC treatment, the X-ray diffraction pattern of this thermally treated sample indicated only the presence of the ferromagnetic metastable $\tau$ phase as shown in Fig. 1 (trace B) because the transformation of $\varepsilon$ phase to $\tau$ phase was completed during cooling. DSC thermograms of the $\mathrm{Mn}_{0.55} \mathrm{Al}_{0.433} \mathrm{C}_{0.017}$ alloy exhibited evidence of phase transformations of a metastable $\tau$ phase to h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase and h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase to liquid with occurrence of endothermic reactions upon heating with peak onsets at $1068( \pm 2) \mathrm{K}$ and $1485( \pm 2) \mathrm{K}$ respectively as shown in Fig. 2. It should be noted that the $\varepsilon \leftrightarrows \tau$ reaction occurs at constant composition without solute partitioning. Moreover, minor additions (i.e. <2 at.%) of carbon to near equiatomic Mn-Al alloys have been reported to stabilize the metastable $\tau$ phase against decomposition [11] without significant influence on the phase equilibria. The stabilization effect was used to advantage in allowing for the thermodynamic measurements. Owing to the low carbon content, however, the analysis of the measurements can be treated in terms of the binary equilibria.

In order to optimize the accuracy of reaction temperature determination and peak area measurement, high purity silver (99.999% purity) with a known sharp melting temperature at 1234 K and enthalpy change, $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}=11087$ J mole⁻¹ [10] was used as a standard sample for calibration. From this calibration, the conversion factor of measured enthalpy change and known enthalpy change of silver was determined to be 1.05. The heat of transformation $\Delta H_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau\rightarrow\varepsilon}$ of $\tau$ phase to $\varepsilon$ phase at constant composition with an onset temperature, $T_{\mathrm{t}}=1068$ K, was measured with an accuracy of $\pm5\%$ at a heating rate of 10 K min⁻¹ to be 1992 J mol⁻¹ with a corresponding entropy of transformation $\Delta S_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau\rightarrow\varepsilon}$ of 1.8 J mole⁻¹ K⁻¹, where $\Delta S_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau\rightarrow\varepsilon}=\Delta H^{\tau\rightarrow\varepsilon}/T_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau/\varepsilon}$. A typical DCS thermogram is shown in Fig. 3 indicating the onset temperature and peak area which represents the total amount of enthalpy change during a transformation (i.e. $\tau\rightarrow\varepsilon$). In a similar manner, the heat of fusion $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\varepsilon\rightarrow l}$ of $\varepsilon$ phase was obtained as 10 500 J mole⁻¹ with an accuracy of $\pm5\%$. It should be mentioned that since the $T_{0}$ temperature is a thermodynamic temperature at which the molar free energies of the liquid and solid phase are equal for the given composition, i.e. the $T_{0}$ temperature lies between the liquidus and solidus [12], the $T_{0}$ temperature has been selected as the transformation temperature. Thus, the $T_{0}^{\varepsilon/l}$ was estimated as 1490 K approximately from the midpoint in the relatively narrow range between the onset temperature (1485 K) and temperature (1495 K) of minimum endothermic reaction and used to obtain $\Delta S_{\mathrm{f}}^{\varepsilon\rightarrow l}=7.0$ J mole⁻¹ K⁻¹.

The Gibbs free energy difference between crystalline solid phase, $s$, and liquid, $\Delta G^{s\rightarrow l}$, depends upon the difference in heat capacity of the two phases, $\Delta C_{\mathrm{p}}=C_{\mathrm{p}}^{l}-C_{\mathrm{p}}^{s}$, and can be written by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^{s \rightarrow l}= & \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{s \rightarrow l}+\int_{T_{0}^{s / l}}^{T} \Delta c_{\mathrm{p}} \mathrm{d} T^{\prime}-T \\
& \times\left[\Delta S_{\mathrm{f}}^{s / l}+\int_{T_{0}^{s / l}}^{T}\left(\Delta c_{\mathrm{p}} / T^{\prime}\right) \mathrm{d} T^{\prime}\right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

in which $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}$ and $\Delta S_{\mathrm{f}}$ are the enthalpy and entropy of fusion respectively at the $T_{0}^{s/l}$ temperature.

![](./images/812401611967037440_3.jpg)

Fig. 3. Typical DSC heating trace of the $Mn_{0.55}Al_{0.433}C_{0.017}$ alloy showing the extrapolated onset temperature and peak area which is indicated by the shaded area and is directly related to the enthalpy change for a phase transformation of the $\tau$ phase to the $\varepsilon$ phase (i.e. $\Delta H_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau\rightarrow\varepsilon}$).

The Gibbs free-energy difference between metastable liquid and crystalline solid $s$, $\Delta G^{s\rightarrow l}$, and its dependence on temperature are important parameters in the analysis of the kinetics of crystallization at high undercooling. If there are available liquid heat capacity data for a material in the undercooled state (i.e. below $T_{0}^{s/l}$), $\Delta G^{s\rightarrow l}$ can be calculated directly using eqn. (1). In most alloy systems, however, such data are not available. Instead, most often, the temperature dependence of $\Delta G^{s\rightarrow l}$ has been approximated by assuming various forms for the $\Delta C_{\mathrm{p}}$ in eqn. (1) [13-18]. In the simplest analysis [13] it is assumed that $\Delta C_{\mathrm{p}}$ is zero for pure metals or single phase alloy solutions which results in the linear relation as

$$
\Delta G^{s \rightarrow l}=\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{s \rightarrow l} \Delta T}{T_{0}^{s / l}}
\tag{2}
$$

where
$$\Delta T=T_{0}^{s/l}-T$$

The DSC thermogram of the $Mn_{0.55}Al_{0.433}C_{0.017}$ alloy is shown in Fig. 2 indicating two endothermic peaks during heating (heating rate $=10$ K min⁻¹), corresponding to the transformation of a metastable

ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase to a high-temperature $\varepsilon$ phase and $\varepsilon$ phase to liquid respectively (see Fig. 3). Thus, the Gibbs free energy difference between metastable $\tau$ phase and liquid can be written by

$$
\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow l}=\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}+\Delta G^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l} \tag{3}
$$

Therefore, by combining eqn. (2) with eqn. (3) at $T=T_{0}^{\tau / l}$ (i.e. $\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow l}=0$ at $T=T_{0}^{\tau / l}$), the following equation is obtained as

$$
T_{0}^{\tau / l}=\frac{\left(T_{0}^{\varepsilon / l}\right)^{2}(1+K)}{T_{0}^{\varepsilon / l}+K T_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau / \varepsilon}} \tag{4}
$$

where
$$
K=\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l} / \Delta H_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}
$$

Since all the values of the parameters in eqn. (4) such as $T_{0}^{\varepsilon / l}, T_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau / \varepsilon}, \Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l}$, and $\Delta H_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}$ were determined, the $T_{0}^{\tau / l}$ temperature was obtained as 1403 K. Based on the above thermodynamic analysis the thermodynamic properties of the undercooled liquid L, crystalline solid $\varepsilon$ (h.c.p.), and $\tau$ phase below the melting temperature are shown in Fig. 4 as a function of temperature for the case of composition invariant reactions. In order to assess the relative phase stability in this alloy, calculations of free-energy differences between the alloy phases as a function of temperature based on the enthalpy measurements were carried out. In other words, the $\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow l}$ term for a transformation of $\tau$ phase to liquid can be evaluated by the following equation as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow l} & =\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\tau \rightarrow l} \Delta T / T_{0}^{\tau / l} \\
& =-\left(\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\tau \rightarrow l} / T_{0}^{\tau / l}\right) T+\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\tau \rightarrow l}
\end{aligned} \tag{5}
$$

where
$$
\Delta T=T_{0}^{\tau / l}-T
$$

Similar expressions for $\Delta G^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l}$ and $\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}$ can be obtained. The heat of fusion $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\tau \rightarrow l}$ of $\tau$ phase was determined from $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\tau \rightarrow l}=\Delta H_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}+\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l}$ to be $12492 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mole}^{-1}$ based on the measurements of $\Delta H_{\mathrm{t}}^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}$ and $\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}}^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l}$. Therefore, the $\Delta G$ terms for composition invariant transformations of metastable $\tau$ phase to liquid, $\varepsilon$ phase to liquid, and metastable $\tau$ phase to $\varepsilon$ phase are given by

$$
\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow l}=-8.9 T+12492 \quad \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mole}^{-1} \mathrm{~K}^{-1} \tag{6}
$$

$$
\Delta G^{\varepsilon \rightarrow l}=-7.0 T+10500 \quad \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mole}^{-1} \mathrm{~K}^{-1} \tag{7}
$$

$$
\Delta G^{\tau \rightarrow \varepsilon}=-1.8 T+1992 \quad \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mole}^{-1} \mathrm{~K}^{-1} \tag{8}
$$

The results of these determinations are plotted in Fig. 5 and provide important information about the relative phase stability. Based on the phase stability analysis summarized in Fig. 5, a minimum amount of undercooling of $\Delta T=87 \mathrm{~K}$, where $\Delta T=T_{0}^{\varepsilon / l}-T_{0}^{\tau / l}$, is required to form the metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase directly from the liquid in the $\mathrm{Mn}_{0.55} \mathrm{Al}_{0.433} \mathrm{C}_{0.017}$ alloy. At high temperatures $(1075 \mathrm{~K}<T<1490 \mathrm{~K})$, the h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase is indicated to be more stable than the ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase so that the metastable $\tau$ phase could transform to the $\varepsilon$ phase even if the $\tau$ phase did nucleate from the liquid. However, at low temperatures $(T<1075 \mathrm{~K})$ the ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase is more stable than the h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase at constant composition. Of course, the full equilibrium is given by a two phase mixture of $\gamma$ and $\beta$ phase and the $\Delta G^{\gamma+\beta \rightarrow \varepsilon}$ is shown in Fig. 5. based on the reported $\Delta H^{\gamma+\beta \rightarrow \varepsilon}$ data [19]. From the thermodynamic analysis it is now clear that even though the $\tau$ phase is metastable with respect to a two phase mixture of $\gamma+\beta$ it is more stable than $\varepsilon$ phase below 1075 K. For temperatures up to 1075 K, therefore, $\tau$ phase formation represents an intermediate step in the decomposition of the $\varepsilon$ phase.

### 3.2. Transformation kinetics
Since the magnetic properties of Mn-Al-C alloys are very attractive for permanent magnet applications even with the defect structures that are reported to result from conventional heat treatments [20], the solid-state $\varepsilon \to \tau$ transformation kinetics have been investigated using isothermal treatments at various temperatures [21, 22, 23]. In particular, Dreizler and Menth [21] have reported a partial temperature-time-transformation (TTT) diagram of the $\varepsilon$ phase to $\tau$ phase reaction in a $Mn_{0.538}Al_{0.446}C_{0.016}$ alloy based on quantitative metallographic techniques. In fact, the "nose" temperature has not been determined in the $\tau$-start curve owing to the difficulties in measuring the volume fraction of $\tau$ phase transformed from the $\varepsilon$ phase at very short annealing time above 750 K [21, 22, 23] and the competition from the eutectoid formation of $(\gamma+\beta)$. Thus, based on the thermodynamic analysis determined in this investigation (i.e. $\Delta G^{\varepsilon \to \tau}$), it is useful to consider the development of the complete transformation diagram for the $\varepsilon \to \tau$ reaction.

In order to develop an analysis model for the $\varepsilon \to \tau$ reaction, it is necessary to recognize some of the details of the transformation mechanism. According to the detailed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis reported by van den Broek *et al.* [24], the $\varepsilon$ phase cannot be retained completely following a rapid quench in the solid state from high temperature. Instead, the as-quenched $\varepsilon$ phase contains a distribution of islands of a B19 phase ranging from 4 to 10 nm in size which are not detected in X-ray diffraction examination. Upon heating the as-quenched $\varepsilon$ phase to $450\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ the B19 islands coarsen to 40 nm with a density of about $1 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and the degree of B19 ordering increases. Within the ordered B19 islands, stacking faults develop and with continued shearing result in the development of the $L1_{0}$ $\tau$ phase. While the full description of all of the atomistic details of the $\varepsilon \to \tau$ reaction is not complete, it does appear that the reaction involves a thermally activated diffusion component and some lattice shear. At the same time, Dreizler and Menth [21] reported transformation kinetics in a sample with an $\varepsilon$ phase grain size of about $50\ \mu\text{m}$. With such a grain size there is a high density $(>10^{10}\ \text{m}^{-3})$ of B19 ordered islands that can serve as formation sites for the $\tau$ phase. With this partial description of the transformation in mind it appears to be useful to treat the reported kinetics observations for the onset of $\tau$ phase formation in a phenomenological model based upon heterogeneous nucleation at sites (i.e. B19 islands) distributed within the volume of the $\varepsilon$ grains. It is recognized that for very fine $\varepsilon$ grain sizes, grain boundary nucleation sites may become important in controlling the reaction kinetics and perhaps modifying the transformation process as indicated by the recent work of Mulyukov and Khaphizov [23].

Based upon the transformation model outline and classical nucleation theory [25] for heterogeneous nucleation in condensed systems, the steady-state nucleation rate of $\tau$ phase per unit volume, $J\ (\text{m}^{-3}\text{s}^{-1})$ can be represented as

$$
J=A_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{Q}{kT}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G^{*}}{kT}\right) \tag{9}
$$

where $A_{1}$ is a pre-exponential term $(\text{m}^{-3}\text{s}^{-1})$, $Q$ is the activation energy for the diffusional atom attachment to the subcritical size cluster, $\Delta G^{*}$ is the activation barrier for nucleus formation, and $kT$ has the usual meaning. The activation barrier $\Delta G^{*}$ is given by

$$
\Delta G^{*}=\frac{b\sigma^{3}f(\theta)}{\Delta G_{\text{v}}^{2}} \tag{10}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the solid-solid interfacial energy, $\Delta G_{\text{v}}$ is the driving free energy for nucleation per unit volume of product phase, $b=16\pi/3$ is a geometrical factor, and $f(\theta)$ a function of the contact angle $\theta$ where, for example, $f(\theta)=0.25(2-3\cos\theta+\cos^{3}\theta)$ for spherical nuclei.

The thermally activated growth rate $G$ (i.e. growth rate $G$ without compositional change) is related to the net flux of atoms between two phases and thus $G$ $(\text{m s}^{-1})$ can be written as [26]

$$
G=A_{2}\exp\left(-\frac{Q}{kT}\right)\left\{1-\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta G}{kT}\right)\right\} \tag{11}
$$

where $A_{2}$ is a constant $(\text{m s}^{-1})$ and $\Delta G$ is the driving free energy difference between phases.

Since the transformation rate during nucleation and growth reactions in condensed phases is dependent on both reaction rates, the volume fraction transformed at early time $t$, $I$, is given by [26]

$$
I=\frac{V_{\mathrm{f}}}{V}=\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) J G^{3} t^{4} \tag{12}
$$

with the assumption of a constant nucleation and an isotropic growth rate $G$ for spherical transformed regions where $V_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the total volume transformed at time $t$ and $V$ total volume.

In practice, the condition to represent a typical TTT diagram for the start of the transformation during isothermal treatment holding over a time $t$ is usually 1% volume fraction of transformed phase. Therefore, from eqn. (12), a holding time $t$ required to transform to a 1% volume fraction of product phase $I$ associated with heterogeneous nucleation and growth for the steady-state conditions during isothermal treatments can be expressed as the following equation as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln t= & -\ln A_{3}+\frac{Q}{k T}+\frac{b \sigma^{3} f(\theta)}{4 k T \Delta G_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}} \\
& -\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) \ln \left\{1-\exp \left(-\frac{\Delta G}{k T}\right)\right\}
\end{aligned} \tag{13}
$$

where
$A_{3}=(\pi A_{1} A_{2}^{3}/0.03)^{1/4}$

By fitting eqn. (13) with the experimental data [21] as for the $\tau$-start transformation (i.e. 1% volume fraction transformation of $\varepsilon \to \tau$) and $\Delta G^{\varepsilon \to \tau}$ as well as $\Delta G_{\mathrm{V}}^{\varepsilon \to \tau}$ (i.e. $\Delta G_{\mathrm{V}}^{\varepsilon \to \tau}=-119.4+0.11T$ J cm$^{-3}$) determined with lattice parameters of the $\tau$ phase such as $a_{0}=0.2769$ nm and $c_{0}=0.3618$ nm [21] in this investigation, it was possible to determine the prefactor term $A_{3}$, the activation energy term for the diffusion, $Q/k$, and the interfacial energy related term, $b\sigma^{3}f(\theta)/4k$.

In eqn. (13), it is important to point out that the volume fraction transformation at time $t$ is determined by the kinetic factors such as $Q$, $\Delta G$, and $\Delta G_{\mathrm{V}}$ as a function of temperature. At low temperature (i.e. below the "nose" temperature in TTT curve), the driving free energy is very large; then, $b\sigma^{3}f(\theta)/4kT\Delta G_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}\to0$ and $\exp(-\Delta G/kT)\to0$. In other words, even though the barrier for critically sized nucleus formation decreases rapidly as the temperature is lowered and the probability of nucleation thus increases, the formation of the critical nucleus is only part of the thermally activated reaction which includes the diffusion kinetics as well. Therefore, eqn. (13) may be written for the low temperature range as

$$
\ln t=-\ln A_{3}+\frac{Q}{k T} \tag{14}
$$

By using the reported partial TTT diagram [21] for the low temperature range of $625$ K $< T <700$ K to represent the $Q$ dominated range where eqn. (14) can be applied with good accuracy, a value of $\ln A_{3}=38$ s$^{-1}$ and a slope of $3.28\times10^{4}$ K for $Q/k$ was obtained by a least-square fit as shown in Fig. 6. If the interfacial energy term, $b\sigma^{3}f(\theta)/4k$, is treated as approximately constant with temperature [27], the complete isothermal transformation diagram that is described by eqn. (13) can be developed from a nonlinear regression analysis [28] evaluated on the reported results. The interfacial energy term, $b\sigma^{3}f(\theta)/4k$, was then obtained as $1.43\times10^{18}$ K J$^{2}$ m$^{-6}$. It is interesting to point out that the calculated interfacial energy $\sigma$ ranges from $1.6\times10^{-2}$ J m$^{-2}$ to $3.6\times10^{-2}$ J m$^{-2}$ with $b=16\pi/3$ and $0.1\leqslant f(\theta)\leqslant0.9$. Therefore, based on the eqn. (13) the complete temperature-time-transformation (TTT) curve for the $\tau$-start from the h.c.p. $\varepsilon$ phase was developed as shown in Fig. 7 indicating the "nose" of the TTT curve at 7 s and 915 K.

While the kinetic calculations are approximate due to estimates of some of the parameters, the linear approximation for $\Delta G$ and limited temperature range of the experimental data, the results do offer an interesting viewpoint on the kinetics. For example, according to Fig. 7, a critical cooling rate in excess of 20 K s$^{-1}$ is required to bypass metastable $\tau$ phase nucleation during solid-state transformation (i.e. $\varepsilon \to \tau$) while the critical cooling rate was obtained experimentally as about 15 K s$^{-1}$ by Kono [19]. In other words, the calculated transformation diagram is consistent with the observation that high solid-state quench rates do not favor the development of $\tau$ phase

## 4. Summary
Based upon the results of the current studies including a thermodynamic analysis as well as a solid-state transformation kinetic analysis of a MnAl-based alloy, a clear and more complete understanding of the metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase formation has been developed. Since the previous studies, even though they were quite detailed, focused on the attractive magnetic properties of the metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase produced only by conventional solid-state heat treatments (i.e. $\varepsilon \to \tau$), the study of the transformation thermodynamics has not been possible in past work. The examination of thermal analysis performed in this investigation based upon the unique features of $\tau$ phase formation and solid-state transformation behavior in MnAl-based alloys has provided the necessary thermodynamic and kinetics basis to judge metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase formation either directly from the melt as a solidification product or from solid-state reactions (i.e. $\varepsilon \to \tau$) for various heat treatment processes. In order to produce the metastable ferromagnetic $\tau$ phase directly from the melt the minimum amount of undercooling $\Delta T=87\ \text{K}$ is required based on thermodynamic analysis. The complete TTT diagram established for the ferromagnetic metastable $\tau$ phase formation can serve as a guideline for optimum solid state processing conditions.
