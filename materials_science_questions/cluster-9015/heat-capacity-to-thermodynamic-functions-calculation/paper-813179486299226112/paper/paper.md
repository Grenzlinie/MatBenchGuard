# Application of the Akinfiev-Diamond equation of state to neutral hydroxides of metalloids (B(OH)₃, Si(OH)₄, As(OH)₃) at infinite dilution in water over a wide range of the state parameters, including steam conditions

Nikolay N. Akinfiev $^{a, *}$, Andrey V. Plyasunov $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Institute of Geology of Ore Deposits, Petrology, Mineralogy and Geochemistry (IGEM), Russian Academy of Sciences, Staromonetnyi per., 35, 109017 Moscow, Russia
$^{b}$ Institute of Experimental Mineralogy (IEM), Russian Academy of Sciences, Chernogolovka, Moscow Region 142432, Russia

Received 5 July 2013; accepted in revised form 12 November 2013; available online 23 November 2013

## Abstract

The Akinfiev and Diamond (2003) equation of state (EoS) for aqueous nonelectrolytes was employed to describe hydroxides of metalloids (B(OH)₃, Si(OH)₄, As(OH)₃) over a wide temperature and pressure ranges, including steam conditions. The EoS is based on the accurate knowledge of solvent (H₂O) properties and requires only three empirical parameters to be fitted to experimental data, and these are independent of temperature and pressure. For nonvolatile components thermodynamic properties of species in the ideal gas state were evaluated using quantum chemical computations. The proposed approach has been tested to predict the whole set of thermodynamic properties of solutes (the chemical potential, entropy, molar volume, and molar heat capacity) over a wide range of temperatures (273-1200 K) and pressures (0.1-1000 MPa), including the near-critical region and both low and high density regions of the solvent. Thus it can be used for modeling various geochemical processes over a whole range of solvent densities, including processes in boiling fluids and a vapor phase as well.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

An accurate description of the thermodynamic properties of neutral (uncharged) aqueous species over a wide pressure - temperature range is an important task with many practical applications in geochemistry and technology. Nevertheless, presently available thermodynamic descriptions of non-electrolytes are far less accurate than those of ionic species. For instance, the well known Helgeson-Kirkham-Flowers (HKF) equation of state (EoS) (Tanger and Helgeson, 1988) provides excellent predictions of thermodynamic properties of aqueous ions over a wide range of conditions (273-873 K, 0.1-500 MPa).

However, when extended to neutral aqueous species (Shock et al., 1989; Schulte et al., 2001), the HKF model does not predict correctly the behavior of non-electrolyte solutes in the near-critical and supercritical regions of water (Plyasunov, 1991; O'Connell et al., 1996; Lin and Wood, 1996), and in the low density region of the solvent $(\rho_{1}^{*}<0.35\ \mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}})$ as well (Plyasunov and Shock, 2001; Akinfiev and Diamond, 2003). This general deficiency is one of the reasons for the recent work to develop alternative EoS for aqueous non-electrolytes (Harvey, 1996; O'Connell et al., 1996; Plyasunov et al., 2000a,b; Sedlbauer et al., 2000). Recently a simple semiemprical virial-like EoS (Akinfiev and Diamond, 2003) was proposed for describing the thermodynamic properties of aqueous volatile nonelectrolytes at infinite dilution. It is based on the accurate EoS for a solvent (H₂O) and requires only three empirical parameters to be fitted to experimental data, and these

Knowledge of the thermodynamic properties of a solute in the ideal gas state, together with these three parameters, enables the prediction of the whole set thermodynamic properties of the solute at infinite dilution (the partial molar chemical potential, entropy, volume, and heat capacity) over a wide range of temperatures (273-773 K) and pressures (0.1-200 MPa), including the near-critical and low density region of the solvent. Here we extend this approach for description of low and non-volatile hydroxides of metalloids $\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{OH})_{3}, \mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{OH})_{4}$, and $\mathrm{As}(\mathrm{OH})_{3}$. These species occur ubiquitously in natural hydrothermal fluids and show a marked partition into the vapor phase upon boiling (Styrikovich et al., 1960; Kukuljan et al., 1999; Pokrovski et al., 2002). Their presence in high-temperature steam may create considerable corrosion problems in water-steam cycles in power plants' installations (Bellows and Harvey, 1999). Thus the thermodynamic description of these species in a wide range of state parameters is relevant in geochemical and technological applications.

## 2. METHODS

The standard state convention adopted in this work is the one defined by IUPAC (Mills et al., 1993): for a gaseous substance it is the hypothetical state of the pure substance in the gaseous state at the standard pressure $P=P^{\otimes}$ and exhibiting ideal gas behavior $(P^{\otimes}=0.1 \mathrm{MPa})$. The standard state for solid phases is the state of the pure substance in the solid state at the standard pressure, the standard state of solutes in the liquid phase of water is the state at the standard molality $m=1$, at a given pressure $P$, exhibiting infinitely diluted solution behavior. Following the recommendations of IUPAC, the superscripts $*$ and $\infty$ are used to designate the properties of a pure substance and of a solute at infinite dilution, respectively. The subscripts 1 and 2 designate water and a solute.

The detailed derivation of the proposed EoS is given in the previous paper (Akinfiev and Diamond, 2003). Ultimately, the chemical potential of an aqueous nonelectrolyte species at infinite dilution at a given temperature $T$ (in $\mathrm{K}$ ) and pressure $P$ (in MPa) is expressed as

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(P, T)= & g_{2, g}^{\mathrm{o}}(T)-R T \ln N_{w}+(1-\xi) R T \ln \frac{f_{1}^{*}}{P^{\otimes}} \\
& +R T \xi \ln \left(\frac{R T}{P^{\otimes} M_{w}} \rho_{1}^{*}\right) \\
& +R T \rho_{1}^{*}\left[a+b\left(\frac{10^{3}}{T}\right)^{0.5}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here the pure water density $\rho_{1}^{*}$ is expressed in $\mathrm{g} \mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, the pure water fugacity $f_{1}^{*}$ - in $\mathrm{MPa}, R=8.31446 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1} \mathrm{~K}^{-1}$, the molar weight of water $M_{w}=18.0152 \mathrm{~g} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$, and the number of moles of water in $1 \mathrm{~kg}$ of water $N_{w}=1000 /$ $M_{w} \approx 55.508, P^{\otimes}=0.1 \mathrm{MPa}$ is the standard pressure. Symbols $g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(T, P)$ and $g_{2, g}^{\mathrm{o}}(T)$ stand for the chemical potential of a solute in the state of a standard aqueous solution and in the ideal gas state, respectively. The $\xi, a$, and $b$ are the empirical parameters of the EoS that are independent of pressure and temperature: $\xi$ is a "scaling" factor proposed by Plyasunov et al. (2000a) that reflects a relative dimension of the solute in comparison to solvent molecule, and two others ( $a$ and $b$ ) account for the temperature effects of hydration of the solute. Eq. (1) expresses the Gibbs energy of hydration, i.e. a transfer of the solute molecule from the ideal gas state at temperature $T$ to the water environment at the same $T$ and a pressure $P$, so it can be easily modified to express the Henry constant of the solute, $k_{\mathrm{H}}(T, P)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln \left(k_{\mathrm{H}}\right)= & (1-\xi) \ln \frac{f_{1}^{*}}{P^{\otimes}}+\xi \ln \left(\frac{R T}{P^{\otimes} M_{w}} \rho_{1}^{*}\right) \\
& +\rho_{1}^{*}\left[a+b\left(\frac{10^{3}}{T}\right)^{0.5}\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

and its distribution coefficient, $K_{\mathrm{D}}\left(T, P_{\text {sat. }}\right)$, between coexisting solvent's vapor and liquid phases

$$
\ln K_{D}=-\xi \ln \frac{\rho_{1, v a p}^{*}}{\rho_{1, l i q}^{*}}-\left[a+b\left(\frac{10^{3}}{T}\right)^{0.5}\right]\left(\rho_{1, v a p}^{*}-\rho_{1, l i q}^{*}\right) .
$$

Derivation of the expressions for $k_{\mathrm{H}}, K_{D}$ and derivatives of Eq. (1) corresponding to the partial molar volume and heat capacity of the solute are given in the Appendix. There are also expressions for the fugacity coefficient of a solute at infinite dilution in water, $\varphi_{2}^{\infty}$, and for the second cross virial coefficient water-solute, $B_{12}$, an important property, reflecting deviations from ideality of water-solute mixtures at low water densities (Prausnitz et al., 1999). At near-critical conditions the partial molar properties of a solute at infinite dilution in water are governed by the Krichevskii parameter, $A_{K r}$ (see Levelt Sengers, 1991), and the corresponding analytical expression for $A_{K r}$ is also given in the Appendix.

To compute $g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(P, T)$, additionally to $\xi, a$, and $b$, one needs to know thermodynamic properties of the molecule in the ideal gas state, $g_{2, g}^{\mathrm{o}}(T)$, and to use the solvent's EoS to predict $f_{1}^{*}$ and $\rho_{1}^{*}$ at the specified $P, T$ point. As it was applied in the original Akinfiev and Diamond (2003) paper we have employed the multiparameter Hill's EoS for $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ (Hill, 1990) that is valid from triple point pressure and temperature to $1000 \mathrm{MPa}$ (or the melting line) and $1273 \mathrm{~K}$ and provides an accurate representation of existing thermodynamic data of $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ in that range. Note that the differences between the Hill (1990) EoS for water and the overall previously used Haar-Gallagher-Kell model (Kestin et al., 1984) or more recent EoS due to Wagner and Pruß (2002) in the $T-P$ range used in this work are very small (see below). At higher state parameters, exceeding the range of applicability of the Hill (1990) equation of state, the Wagner and Pruß (2002) formulation has been used.

As it has been stated in Akinfiev and Diamond (2003), the use of Eq. (1) for volatile solutes, when properties of corresponding gaseous species are well known, brings to a fair description of their thermodynamic properties over a wide range of temperatures (273-773 K) and pressures (0.1-200 MPa), including the near-critical region of the solvent (see Fig. 1EA of Electronic Annex). The EoS also yields the correct low pressure limit for all thermodynamic properties of the solute, thus it can also be employed in the low density region of the solvent.

For a nonvolatile species lacking the thermodynamic properties in the ideal gas state the quantum chemical computations were employed. Quantum chemical computations were performed using the Gaussian 09W, Revision C.01 suite of programs (Frisch et al., 2009). For the molecules under consideration the 6-311G+(d,p) basis set was used. Electron correlation effects were included by the use of density functional theory (DFT) with the hybrid functional B3LYP. A structure optimization procedure preceded thermochemical calculations. All molecular structures were processed and analyzed for imaginary frequencies using the GaussView 5 program. The temperature dependence of thermochemical properties was computed using standard Gaussian procedure (the Readisotopes option).

If the temperature dependence of the heat capacity of a gaseous molecule, $C_{p2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T)$, is determined, then the $g_{2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T)$ can be expressed in a traditional way

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T) =& g_{2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T_r) - S_{2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T_r) \cdot (T - T_r) \\
&+ \int_{T_r}^{T} C_{p2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T)dT - T \int_{T_r}^{T} C_{p2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T)d(\ln T),
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
$$

where $T_r$ stands for standard temperature of 298.15 K. Combining (1) and (4) one can see that $g_{2,aq}^\infty(T,P)$ is linearly dependent on $g_{2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T_r), S_{2,g}^\mathrm{o}(T_r),$ and the parameters of the EoS, $\xi, a$, and $b$. Thus to determine any set of these values a linear regression of available experimental data (solubility data for solids with the known thermodynamic data that give $g_{2,aq}^\infty(T,P)$, and/or $k_\mathrm{H}(T,P)$, $K_\mathrm{D}(T,P_{sat})$ can be employed. Details of any specific regression procedure are given below.

## 3. RESULTS

### 3.1. B(OH)₃

Raman spectroscopic studies of boron-containing solutions (Schmidt et al., 2005) demonstrates that the planar three-coordinated $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$ species is the dominant form of B in hydrothermal fluids from acidic to slightly alkaline conditions, over wide temperature and pressure ranges (at least up to 900 K and 2 GPa).

The determination of the EoS parameters for $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$ was done as follows. First, we note that the expressions for $K_D$ and $V_2^\infty$ use only the empirical parameters of the EoS without the properties of the molecule in the ideal gas state, so these experimental data are convenient to directly estimate parameters $\xi$, $a$, and $b$ for $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$.

The densities of boric acid solutions up to elevated temperatures and pressures were measured in a number of works (Alekhin et al., 1993; Hnedkovsky et al., 1995; Ganopolsky et al., 1996; Abdulagatov and Azizov, 2004), among which the study of Hnedkovsky et al. (1995), where the vibrating-tube densimeter was employed, is by far the most precise and accurate one and covers the largest temperature range. Experimentally measured molar volume of aqueous boric acid at 298 K given (Hnedkovsky et al., 1995) at 0.1 MPa $V_2^\infty = 39.6\ \mathrm{cm^3\ mol^{-1}}$ was used to fit the scale factor of $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$, $\xi$.

The vapor-liquid distribution of boric acid in water at high temperatures was measured in two studies (Styrikovich et al., 1960; Kukuljan et al., 1999). Styrikovich et al. (1960) determined $K_D$ at temperatures $T=373.2$–637.3 K by the vaporization method with colorimetric analysis of the condensate. The distribution constants were found to be independent of $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$ concentrations $(3 \times 10^{-3}$–0.35 m). In a later work, Kukuljan et al. (1999) employed a method of static vapor-liquid equilibration of dilute (0.02–0.10 m) solutions of boric acid with separate samplings of vapor and liquid coexisting phases and spectrophotometric analysis of concentrations. As seen in Fig. 1, these two sets are in satisfactory (within 30%) agreement at 520–640 K, but diverge at lower temperatures. Thus the $a$ and $b$ values of the EoS were determined by the linear regression employing these data for temperatures $T>520$ K only. Two older detailed studies (Jaulmes and Gontard, 1937; Stackelberg et al., 1937) are devoted to the vapor-liquid distribution of boric acid in water at atmospheric pressure, and the resulting value of $\ln K_D(373\ \mathrm{K})=-5.63\pm0.08$ is retrieved in (Plyasunov, 2011a). This value is considered to be the most reliable at this temperature and it is also used to fit the EoS parameters. Additional values of $K_D$ are reported by Nikolaeva and Bychkov (2007), based on sampling of the coexisting vapor and liquid phases in the wells of the Mutnovskaya geothermal plant (Kamchatka, Russia) at very low concentrations of salts (less than $0.005\ \mathrm{mol\ kg^{-1}}$ of solution). As these data do not refer to a strictly defined, laboratory controlled, chemical system, they were not used in the data treatment.

Evaluated values of $\xi$, $a$, and $b$ are given in Table 1. The properties of $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$ in the ideal gas state were adopted from the recent work of Plyasunov (2011a), and are also given in Table 1. The whole set of $\mathrm{B(OH)_3}$ thermodynamic parameters is now sufficient to predict thermodynamic properties of this species in water at various conditions. Results are presented in Figs. 1 and 2.

It can be seen that the Akinfiev–Diamond (2003) EoS provides an excellent description of the available experimental data in the near critical region of H<sub>2</sub>O. The value of $\Delta_f G^\infty$ is close to the CODATA (Cox et al., 1989) recommended result of $\Delta_f G^\infty=-969.27\pm0.82$ kJ mol<sup>−1</sup>, while the value of $S_2^\circ$ deviates significantly from the CODATA recommendation $S_2^\circ=162.4\pm0.6$ J K<sup>−1</sup> mol<sup>−1</sup>.

To test the applicability of the model to dense aqueous fluids, the set of values of the chemical potential of aqueous boric acid, $g_2^\infty$ (B(OH)<sub>3</sub>(aq)), was generated using available most reliable thermodynamic data of B(OH)<sub>3</sub>(aq) along the isobar of 28 MPa at 298–648 K (see Electronic Annex for details). The accuracy of these data is about 0.8 kJ mol<sup>−1</sup> at standard temperature and about 1 kJ mol<sup>−1</sup> at the highest temperature of 648 K. It was found that deviation of the current model does not exceed 2.2 kJ that corresponds to the deviation of the natural logarithm of a thermodynamic constant, $\ln K<0.4$. However, the agreement may be considerably improved adopting corrected values for thermodynamic properties of gaseous species at standard state conditions, $\Delta_f G^\circ_{298}=-931.15$ kJ mol<sup>−1</sup>, and $S_2^\circ$=274.89 J mol<sup>−1</sup> K<sup>−1</sup>. In this case deviations do not exceed the estimated accuracy in the whole temperature range. In spite of a noticeable difference between $S_2^\circ=135.27$ J mol<sup>−1</sup> K<sup>−1</sup> of the model for the aqueous species and the CODATA recommendation, $S_2^\circ=162.4\pm0.6$ J K<sup>−1</sup> mol<sup>−1</sup>, a good agreement for the temperature dependence of $g_2^\infty$ takes place: presumably an underestimated value of $S^\infty_{298}$(aq) is compensated by the overestimated value of $C_{p2}^\infty$.

Thermodynamic properties of Si(OH)₄ in the ideal gas state were recently determined by the analysis of the relevant experimental data in Plyasunov (2011b). The adopted $C_p^o$ ($T=$ 300–1500 K) values for gaseous Si(OH)₄ were approximated by a function and used in the treatment of data, see Table 1. The fitting procedure to evaluate the parameters of the Akinfiev–Diamond model also included the values of $\Delta_f G^o$ and $S^o$ of Si(OH)₄ in ideal gas state at standard state conditions.

First, to fit the EoS parameters, the dataset for $g_2^\infty$ (Si(OH)₄(aq)) has been generated using accepted experimental data on quartz solubility in water according to the reaction SiO₂(quartz) + 2H₂O = Si(OH)₄(aq), as

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{2}^{\infty}(\mathrm{Si}(\mathrm{OH})_{4}(\mathrm{aq}))(T, P) &=g(\mathrm{quartz})(T, P)+2 g(\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O})(T, P) \\
&-R T \ln m_{\mathrm{Si}},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $m_{\text{Si}}$ corresponds to the molality of silica in the solution. Thermodynamic properties of quartz were adopted from SUPCRT database (Johnson et al., 1992), while $g(\text{H}_2\text{O})(T,P)$ was computed using the Hill (1990) and/or Wagner and Pruß (2002) formulations.

The fitting procedure was organized as described below. It is known that partial molar properties of dilute solutes close to the critical point of water are governed by the Krichevskii parameter, $A_{Kr}$ (Levelt Sengers, 1991). Plyasunov (2012) recommended for Si(OH)₄ $A_{Kr}=-190\pm10$ MPa evaluated from the available relevant data. We have used this value as an anchor while fitting. So, the fitting procedure was iterative. After any initial approximation for $\xi$, values of the $a$, $b$ parameters of the EoS together with $\Delta_f G^o_{298}$(g) and $S^o_{298}$(g) of Si(OH)₄ were determined by a linear regression of the available $g_2^\infty$ (Si(OH)₄(aq)) experimental data. Then the $\xi$ parameter was modified in compliance with the adopted $A_{Kr}$ value (Eq. A7), and the fitting cycle was repeated until $\xi$ ceased changing.

The finally retrieved values for gaseous Si(OH)₄ are $\Delta_f G^o_{298}=-1239.66\pm1.7$ kJ mol⁻¹, $S^o_{298}=346.37\pm3.5$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, and the EoS parameters are $\xi=-1.8933$; $a=0.9285\pm1.1$ cm³ g⁻¹; $b=-0.9409\pm0.97$ cm³ K⁰·⁵ g⁻¹ (2$\sigma$ confidence) (Table 1). Evaluated in this work values of $\Delta_f G^o_{298}$ and $S^o_{298}$ for Si(OH)₄ in the ideal gas state are very close to the data given in Plyasunov (2011b) on the basis of the analysis of the solubility amorphous silica and quartz in low-density steam: $-1238.51\pm3.5$ kJ mol⁻¹, and $S^o_{298}=$ 347.78 $\pm$ 6.2 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, correspondingly.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

This work analyzes the application of the Akinfiev– Diamond (2003) EoS to hydroxides of metalloids ($\mathrm{B(OH)_{3}}$, $\mathrm{Si(OH)_{4}}$, $\mathrm{As(OH)_{3}}$) over a wide temperature and pressure ranges, including steam conditions.

First, we note that this application is successful. Indeed, accurate data on the solubility of quartz in water at 294–1173 K and pressures up to 1000 MPa at concentra- tions of quartz below 1 m, in total 664 points, are described with an accuracy comparable to that of the Manning (1994) equation, which is the current standard for correlating the solubility of quartz in water. In the high-temperature high- pressure region, where the concentration of dissolved silica exceed $1\ m$ ($\sim$0.02 in mole fractions), the model underesti- mates solubilities, which evidently could be accounted for the formation of polymeric species of silica at these condi- tions. The description of the distribution of silica (as well as boric acid and $\mathrm{As(OH)_{3}}$) between the vapor and liquid phases of water is highly accurate. The model predicts steam solubilities of quartz in a very good agreement with avail- able experimental data, even when they were not included in the regression procedure, while the Manning (1994) equa- tion gives much lower solubilities at these conditions.

A good agreement between experimental and model solu- bilities is due to a fair prediction of the chemical potential of the dissolved species, $g_{2}^{\infty}$, in a wide range of state parameters including the low density (vapor) and high density (liquid) re- gions of the solvent ($\mathrm{H_{2}O}$). Temperature and pressure deriv- atives of the master equation for $g_{2}^{\infty}$ allow calculations of $V_{2}^{\infty}$ and $C_{p2}^{\infty}$ of the solute. While the approach provides a good prediction for partial molar volumes and heat capacities near the critical region of water, it overestimates $C_{p2}^{\infty}$ especially at low temperatures. Obviously, it is very difficult to achieve a “perfect” description with an equation, containing only 3 empirical parameters. The models of Sedlbauer et al. (2000) and Plyasunov et al. (2000a,b), recognizing this problem, use the auxiliary “additional” heat capacity function to bring the calculated and experimental heat capacities into a forced agreement. The cost of this decision is the significant loss of the predictive capabilities of models. Therefore, we believe that there should be a formulation with a minimum number of parameters to correlate and predict data for poorly studied solutes, even if it is applicable mainly for chemical potentials (equilibrium constants).

For hydroxides of metalloids (and metals) an additional issue is the thermodynamic properties of species in the ideal gas state. The application of the quantum chemical calcula- tions (the “Gaussian” package in this work) significantly facilitates the solution of this problem, providing reliable values of the heat capacity at various temperatures and rea- sonable values of the entropy of gaseous species.

The proposed EoS is based on the accurate knowledge of the solvent's properties, i.e. its fugacity, $f_{1}^{*}$, and density, $\rho_{1}^{*}$, at given state parameters. In this study we, as a rule, have used Hill’s (1990) EoS, while for the very high $PT$ parameters ($T>1000$ K, $P>800$ MPa) the Wagner and Pruß (2002) (WP) formulation was employed. However, the worldwide geochemical packages, such as EQ3/6 (Wolery, 1992), HCh (Shvarov and Bastrakov, 1999; Shvarov, 2008) employ the SUPCRT (Johnson et al., 1992) format for the thermodynamic database for aqueous species that is originally based on the Haar-Gallagher-Kell (Kestin et al., 1984) (HGK) model. If one incorporates the proposed EoS into these packages it is necessary to know possible errors that arise because of use of different EoS for the solvent. Fig. 8 shows these deviations in reference to the most recent and accurate Wagner and Pruß (2002)

formulation. It can be seen that differences between WP and Hill EoS predictions do not exceed 1% both for density and fugacity of H₂O. As to the HGK model, deviations arise only at very high values of state parameters ($T>1000$ K, $P>800$ MPa), but also do not exceed 2% for density and 3% for the H₂O fugacity. However, the HGK model should be used with care at these extra conditions.

The proposed EoS predicts effects of hydration of sol- utes in water environment. The impact of the effect can be measured by the fugacity coefficient of a solute at infinite dilution, $\varphi_{2}^{\infty}$. The corresponding expression for this property is given in the Appendix (Eq. A8), while its pressure (and H₂O density) dependence at constant temperature of 673 K is given in Fig. 9. It is interesting to note that volatile components (H₂, CO₂, H₂S) demonstrate positive $\ln \varphi_{2}^{\infty}$ values that corresponds to a hydrophobic type of solute- solvent interaction, while for the hydroxides under consideration (B(OH)₃, As(OH)₃, Si(OH)₄) the opposite – a hydrophilic interaction is typical. The strongest one is for silica, that is consistent with Si(OH)₄ being a least volatile component.

An advantage of the proposed EoS is that it works both in low and high density regions of the solvent. Thus it can be used for modeling various geochemical processes in a whole range of solvent densities, including processes in boiling fluids and vapor phase as well. An illustration of the EoS’ capabilities for predicting minerals’ solubilities with the formation of aqueous B(OH)₃, Si(OH)₄, and As(OH)₃ in a wide range of solvent densities are presented in the Electronic Annex.

The definition of the Henry constant, used in this work, is as follows:
$$
k_{\mathrm{H}}(T, P)=\lim _{x_{2} \rightarrow 0} \frac{f_{2}(T, P)}{x_{2}}, \quad \text { (A.1) }
$$
i.e. Henry's constant may be defined at any $T$ and $P$. In the equation above $f_{2}(T, P)$ is the fugacity of a solute. The analytical expression for Henry's constant (in MPa) in the framework of the Akinfiev and Diamond (2003) EoS is given below:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln \left(k_{\mathrm{H}}\right) \equiv & \frac{g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(T, P, x)-g_{2, g}^{o}(T)}{R T} \\
= & (1-\xi) \ln \frac{f_{1}^{*}}{P^{\otimes}}+\xi \ln \left(\frac{R T}{P^{\otimes} M_{\mathrm{w}}} \rho_{1}^{*}\right) \\
& +\rho_{1}^{*}\left[a+b\left(\frac{10^{3}}{T}\right)^{0.5}\right],
\end{aligned}
$$
where $g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(T, P, x)$ stands for the chemical potential of a solute in the state of a standard aqueous solution in the mole fraction concentration scale, and the relation between $g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(T, P, x)$ and $g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(P, T)$ in Eq. (1) as follows:
$$
g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(T, P, x)=g_{2, a q}^{\infty}(T, P)+R T \ln N_{w}. \quad \text { (A.3) }
$$

= & -\xi \ln \frac{\rho_{1, v a p}^{*}}{\rho_{1, l i q}^{*}} \\
& -\left[a+b\left(\frac{10^{3}}{T}\right)^{0.5}\right]\left(\rho_{1, v a p}^{*}-\rho_{1, l i q}^{*}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
