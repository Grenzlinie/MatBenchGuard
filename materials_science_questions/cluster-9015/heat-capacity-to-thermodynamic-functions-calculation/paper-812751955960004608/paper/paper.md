# Statistical thermodynamics of electrons and holes in semiconductors

N. A. Gokcen
Albany Research Center, Bureau of Mines, U. S. Department of the Interior, Albany, Oregon 97321

Received 11 February 1985; accepted 25 April 1985

A statistical thermodynamic treatment is presented for the formation of electrons and holes in nondegenerate semiconductors, and for the accompanying standard changes in the Gibbs energy $\Delta G^{\circ}$, entropy $\Delta S^{\circ}$, heat capacity $\Delta C^{\circ}$, and enthalpy $\Delta H^{\circ}$. The treatment leads to the derivation of a new form for the variation of $\Delta G^{\circ}$ with temperature, i.e., $\Delta G^{\circ}-\Delta G^{\circ}$ (at $0 \mathrm{~K}) \equiv E_{g}-E_{g}$ (at 0 $\mathrm{K})=A T-B T \ln T$, where $E_{g}$ is the forbidden band gap energy, and $A$ and $B$ are theoretically derivable constants, requiring relatively small adjustments for each semiconductor. Reliable data for $\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}, \mathrm{GaAs}$, and $\mathrm{GaP}$ show that this equation and the derived properties $\Delta S^{\circ}, \Delta C^{\circ}$, and $\Delta H^{\circ}$ are well represented as functions of temperature. The foregoing equation is also useful in predicting the temperature dependence of $E_{g}$ from two values of $E_{g}$ at two different temperatures. The resulting interpretation of $\Delta G^{\circ}$ indicates a distinct possibility that $E_{g}$ is constant below a characteristic temperature close to $0 \mathrm{~K}$.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Precise measurements of forbidden band gap energies $E_{g}$ (hereafter band gap energy for brevity) have been critically evaluated and summarized for $\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}, \mathrm{GaAs}$, and $\mathrm{GaP}$ by Thurmond. $^{1}$ The values of $E_{g}$ cover sufficiently wide ranges of temperature to be cited as excellent examples for our purposes. The variation of $E_{g}$ with temperature is frequently expressed $^{1-3}$ by an empirical equation proposed by Varshni $^{3}$; i.e.,
$$E_{g}-E_{g}(0 \mathrm{~K}) \equiv \Delta E_{g}=-\frac{\alpha(T / 100)^{2}}{T+\beta},\qquad(1)$$
where $E_{g}(0 \mathrm{~K})$ is the band gap energy at zero Kelvin, the identity sign $\equiv$ defines $\Delta E_{g}, T$ is the temperature in $\mathrm{K}$, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are empirical constants. The values of $\beta$ were postulated by Varshni as being close to the Debye temperatures of semiconductors near $0 \mathrm{~K}$, but there is no rigorous theoretical justification for this, because for the direct and indirect gaps of the same semiconductor different values of $\beta$ are necessary for accurate representation of data. These values, obtained by appropriate graphical manipulations by Thurmond, $^{1}$ are listed in Table I with the values of $E_{g}(0 \mathrm{~K})$ at zero Kelvin. A modified form of this equation has been suggested by Ravindra and Srivastava. $^{4}$ Their equation, as well as Eq. (1), usually represent the data within $5 \%$ of $\Delta E_{g}$ except at temperatures below $200 \mathrm{~K}$, and above approximately $800 \mathrm{~K}$. Theoretical justification for Eq. (1) by Varshni is based on electron-phonon interactions, which require that $E_{g}$ vary quadratically with temperature for $T \ll \beta$ and linearly with temperature for $T \gg \beta$. This conclusion is controversial as summarized by Panish and Casey, $^{2}$ but Eq. (1) is capable of representing experimental data satisfactorily with only two empirical constants. However, the changes in the entropy and the heat capacity, as obtained from this equation, lead to incorrect statistical thermodynamic interpretations.

The objectives of this communication are therefore to establish improved interpretations of statistical thermodynamic properties of charge carriers in nondegenerate semiconductors, and to derive appropriate equations for temperature dependence of band gaps.

## II. STATISTICAL THERMODYNAMIC TREATMENT

The band gap of a semiconductor is the quantum of energy $h v$ required to excite an electron on the valence band edge to the conduction band, and to generate a hole in the valence band. The resulting physicochemical reaction is
$$e_{\circ}(\text { in valence band })=e^{-}+e^{+},\qquad(2)$$
where $e_{\circ}$ is the electron on top of the valence band edge that generates $e^{-}$(or electron) in the conduction band, and $e^{+}$(or hole) in the valence band. It may be noted that $e_{\circ}$ acts in the same way as a dilute aqueous 1-1 electrolyte, which forms two oppositely charged ions. In fact, reaction (2) for silicon could be written as $\mathrm{Si} \rightarrow \mathrm{Si}^{+}+e^{-}$, but $\mathrm{Si}^{+}$does not act as a hole. The electron $e_{\circ}$ will always be referred to as the electron in the valence band to avoid confusion with $e^{-}$.

The number of quanta of energy may be kept to a minimum during the measurements so that the concentrations of $e^{-}$and $e^{+}$at thermal equilibrium are not significantly disturbed. The energy thus involved is therefore directly related to the formation of the reaction products from one reactant; hence $\Delta G=E_{g}$ (cf. Ref. 5). Another convincing argument that $E_{g}$ is a chemical potential or a Gibbs energy per particle is presented by Thurmond, paraphrased by the following equation:
$$E_{g}=\left(\frac{\partial h v N_{\circ}^{\circ}}{\partial n_{-}}\right)_{S, v, x}=h v=\Delta \overline{G},\qquad(3)$$
where $N_{\circ}^{\circ}$ is the number of energy quanta $h v$ and $n_{-}$is the number of electrons $e^{-}$generated at constant entropy $(S)$,

volume $(V)$, and composition $x$ of $e^{-}$; $\Delta \overline{G}$ is therefore a chemical potential per particle. $^{5}$ Since each quantum of energy among $N_{\circ}^{\circ}$ generates one electron $e^{-}$ it is evident that $\partial N_{\circ}^{\circ} / \partial n_{-}$ is unity. This argument regarding Eq. (3) is correct because an infinitesimal amount of energy is absorbed adiabatically without changing the volume and the carrier concentration, hence $E_{g}$ must represent a Gibbs energy per particle. The equality of $E_{g}$ to the standard Gibbs energy change $\Delta G^{\circ}$, however, requires the equilibrium in reaction (2) as will be seen later.

The concentration of electrons in the conduction band, $n_{-}$, and that of holes in the valence band, $n_{+}$ are given $^{6}$ by
$$
n_{-}=N_{-} \exp \left(E_{f}-E_{g}\right) / k T, \tag{4}
$$
$$
n_{+}=N_{+} \exp \left(-E_{f} / k T\right), \tag{5}
$$
provided that the ratios $n_{-} / N_{-}$ and $n_{+} / N_{+}$ are small compared with unity. $^{6}$ Here, $E_{f}$ is the Fermi energy, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $N_{-}$ and $N_{+}$ are the effective densities of states for electrons and holes, respectively. $^{6-8}$ It is well known from statistical mechanics that the Fermi energy itself is also a Gibbs energy per particle. The equations for $N_{-}$ and $N_{+}$ are
$$
N_{-}=2\left(2 \pi m_{-} k T / h^{2}\right)^{1.5} \equiv q_{-} / V, \tag{6}
$$
$$
N_{+}=2\left(2 \pi m_{+} k T / h^{2}\right)^{1.5} \equiv q_{+} / V, \tag{7}
$$
where $m_{-}$ and $m_{+}$ are the effective masses of electrons and holes, respectively, $q_{-}$ and $q_{+}$ are the partition functions, and $V$ is the volume of semiconductor. The concentrations of electrons and holes may be expressed $^{1}$ by $n_{-} / N_{-}$ and $n_{+} /$ $N_{+}$ which are dimensionless properties similar to mole fractions. The particle Gibbs energies $G_{-}$ and $G_{+}$ as functions of these concentrations are
$$
G_{-}=G_{-}^{\circ}+k T \ln \left(n_{-} / N_{-}\right), \tag{8}
$$
$$
G_{+}=G_{+}^{\circ}+k T \ln \left(n_{+} / N_{+}\right), \tag{9}
$$
where $G_{-}^{\circ}$ and $G_{+}^{\circ}$ are the standard Gibbs energies on the Henrian scale for dilute solutions. $^{5}$ It should be noted that $n /$ $N$ for electrons or for holes are considerably smaller than unity for intrinsic and lightly doped semiconductors so that Henry's law is obeyed, or the activity coefficients are unity. The present treatment is not concerned with heavily doped semiconductors for which the activity coefficients differ from unity, and modified statistical thermodynamic treatments become necessary (see, for example, Refs. 9-12). The standard state is the hypothetically existing state for which $n / N$ is unity and $G=G^{\circ}$ for $-$ and $+$ particles. It is important to stress here that in these hypothetical standard states both charge carriers are free particles, or boltzons, obeying classical statistics because $e^{-}$ is above the Fermi level and $e^{+}$ is below the valence band edge. Such hypothetically existing standard states are common for dilute solutions, e.g., dissolved gases in metals; thus, for hydrogen in liquid aluminum, the Henrian standard state is the pure liquid monatomic hydrogen at 1 atm pressure, a state that is impossible to obtain, but useful in thermodynamic treatments.

It is necessary to assume here that the electrons participating in reaction (2) are those on top of the valence band edge, and their Gibbs energy is $G_{\circ}=G_{\circ}^{*}+k T \ln x_{\circ}$ where $G_{\circ}^{*}$ is a standard Gibbs energy and $x_{\circ}$ is assumed to be a very small fraction of all valence electrons, without proof but heuristically justified by the success of the resulting equations. If $x_{\circ}$ is of the order of $10^{-4}$, then a small fraction of $x_{\circ}$, such as $10^{-5} x_{\circ} \approx 10^{-9}$ ending up in the conduction band in an intrinsic semiconductor cannot change the value of $x_{\circ}$ significantly. Therefore, the term $k T \ln x_{\circ}$ can be added to $G_{\circ}^{*}$ to write $G_{\circ}=G_{\circ}^{\circ}$ for the electrons in reaction (2). Attempts will be made to justify these assumptions after the derivation of approximate equations. It may be emphasized that $x_{\circ}$ is very small, and therefore $e_{\circ}$ cannot contribute measurably to the electronic heat capacity of a semiconductor.

The equilibrium in reaction (2) requires that
$$
G_{\circ}^{\circ}=G_{-}+G_{+}. \tag{10}
$$

Substitution of Eqs. (8) and (9) in Eq. (10) yields
$$
G_{-}^{\circ}+G_{+}^{\circ}-G_{\circ}^{\circ} \equiv \Delta G^{\circ}=-k T \ln \frac{n_{-} n_{+}}{N_{-} N_{+}}. \tag{11}
$$

Equations (4) and (5) can be used to obtain the logarithmic term in Eq. (11), eliminate $E_{f}$, and thus show that
$$
\Delta G^{\circ}=E_{g}. \tag{12}
$$

This equation confirms that $\Delta \overline{G}$ of Eq. (3) is the same as $\Delta G^{\circ}$ of Eq. (11) for reaction (2). Consequently, equilibrium in reaction (2) is necessary to prove Eq. (12).

The terms $G_{-}^{\circ}$ and $G_{+}^{\circ}$, or briefly $G_{ \pm}^{\circ}$, are the values of $G_{ \pm}$ in Eqs. (8) and (9) for $n_{ \pm}=N_{ \pm}$; hence their partition functions are given by Eqs. (6) and (7), so that
$$
G_{ \pm}^{\circ}-G_{ \pm}^{\circ} \text { (at } 0 \mathrm{~K})=-k T \ln \left(q_{ \pm} / N_{A}\right), \tag{13}
$$
where $\pm$ is for "either + or - " but not both, and $N_{A}$ is Avogadro's number. Substitution of $q_{ \pm}$ from Eqs. (6) and (7) with the numerical values of $\pi, k$, and $h$ yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
& G_{-}^{\circ}+G_{+}^{\circ}-G_{-}^{\circ}(0 \mathrm{~K})-G_{+}^{\circ}(0 \mathrm{~K}) \\
& \quad=-2 k T(74.753+1.5 \ln \langle m\rangle+1.5 \ln T+\ln V), \tag{14}
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\langle m\rangle=\left(m_{-} \cdot m_{+}\right)^{0.5}$ is the geometric mean of the carrier masses, which usually changes very slowly with temperature. It will be assumed here that the temperature dependence of $\langle m\rangle$ is negligible, in fair agreement with the summary presented by Thurmond. $^{1}$

It is assumed here, again without proof, that the valence band edge electrons move in a highly restricted volume bound by the atomic core on one side and the electron cloud of the neighboring atoms on the other side. The partition function of this restricted cloud of particles is difficult to formulate but it is tentatively proposed here that
$$
q_{v}=2 V_{v}\left(2 \pi m_{\circ} k T / h^{2}\right)^{1.5} \quad\left(V_{v}=\lambda V\right), \tag{15}
$$
where $V_{v}$ is the restricted volume available for electrons $e_{\circ}$, which is assumed to be a very small fraction $\lambda$ of the semiconductor volume $V$. Further, $V_{v}$ may contain other corrections such as electron spin effects, and those due to the splitting of bands in the semiconductor, and interactions of electrons within each band. It can be shown that the contributions of rotation of a bound electron about an atomic core and its vibration with the core are quite negligible because the moment of inertia of an electron and the distance

between the electron and the shielded nucleus are both very small. Thus $\lambda V$ consists of very thin peripheral ribbon-like volumes joined to each other in such a way that the motion of electrons $e_{\circ}$ is very much limited and may involve occasional exchange of positions with the neighboring peripheral volumes. The corresponding Gibbs energy for $e_{\circ}$ from Eq. (15) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
G^{\circ}-G^{\circ}(\text { at } 0 \mathrm{~K})= & -k T(74.753+1.5 \ln m_{\circ} \\
& +1.5 \ln T+\ln \lambda V). \quad(16)
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtraction of this equation from Eq. (14) as required by the left side of Eq. (11) and then substitution of the numerical value of $m_{\circ}$ as the electron rest mass yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G^{\circ}-\Delta G^{\circ}(0 \mathrm{~K}) & \equiv E_{g}-E_{g}(0 \mathrm{~K}) \\
& =k T\left(18.642-3 \ln \frac{\langle m\rangle}{m_{\circ}}+\ln \frac{\lambda}{V}-1.5 \ln T\right). \quad(17)
\end{aligned}
$$

It is important to remember that $\Delta G^{\circ}$ refers to reaction (2) at constant pressure and constant volume, which is generally the condition for the reactions that take place in solids at ambient pressures.

The fraction of volume occupied by the electrons near the top of the valence band is difficult to estimate. For silicon, it is justified by the subsequent data fitting that $\lambda=1.5 \times 10^{-4}$, which is not unreasonable since $e_{\circ}$ is highly constrained. The numerical value of $\lambda$ is not crucial for the succeeding equations, and it can also be considered as an empirically adjustable parameter. However, without a partition function of the form given by Eq. (15) it is impossible to obtain a heat capacity value equal to $1.5 k$ as will be seen shortly. Substitution of this value as well as $\langle m\rangle / m_{\circ}=1.2$ from the summary of data for silicon by Thurmond, and $V=12.05 \mathrm{~cm}^{3} / \mathrm{mol}$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E_{g}= & k T(6.801-1.5 \ln T)=5.860 \times 10^{-4} T \\
& -1.293 \times 10^{-4} T \ln T ;\left(\Delta C^{\circ}=1.5 k\right), \quad(18)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $E_{g}$ is in electron volts and $\Delta C^{\circ}=1.5 k$ $=1.293 \times 10^{-4}$ is the standard change in heat capacities for reaction (2). The values of $\Delta E_{g}$ calculated from this equation is within $6 \%$ of the values from a smooth curve representing the experimental values for silicon from 200 to $420 \mathrm{~K}$. Precise values are not available above $420 \mathrm{~K}$.

Equation (18) is based on one adjusted parameter $\lambda$, and it is quite likely that the errors involved in $\langle m\rangle, m_{\circ}$, and their temperature dependence as well as $V, V_{v}$, and their temperature dependence, could introduce corrections for the terms in Eqs. (17) and (18). Therefore, it is assumed that the coefficients in Eq. (18) are adjustable within reason if the accuracy of band gap measurements are indeed as accurate as correctly stated by Thurmond $^{1}$; consequently,

$$
\Delta G^{\circ}-\Delta G^{\circ}(0 \mathrm{~K})=\Delta E_{g}=A T-B T \ln T, \quad(19)
$$

where $A$ and $B$ are adjustable parameters within the magnitudes prescribed in Eq. (18). The success of this correlation with the best fit curve for the experimental data for each semiconductor is given in the next section.

## III. CORRELATION OF DATA

The data obtained by various investigators on $\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}$, $\mathrm{GeAs}$, and $\mathrm{GaP}$, and summarized by Thurmond, will be used to illustrate the utility of Eq. (19) and its superiority to Eq. (1). Two or more sets of independent investigations for the same semiconductor agree so well that a curve passing through or between the points on a plot of temperature vs $\Delta E_{g}$ represents the data remarkably well for each semiconductor. The set of data for GaP has slightly larger deviations above $800 \mathrm{~K}$. Other sets of data for diamond, $\mathrm{SiC}, \mathrm{InAs}, \mathrm{InP}$, etc., are not sufficiently accurate to test Eq. (19).

Si and Ge. The experimental data for silicon and germanium were obtained from near $0 \mathrm{~K}$ to approximately $420 \mathrm{~K}$ and the analytical correlation obtained in this paper is expected to be valid up to possibly $500 \mathrm{~K}$. ## IV. DERIVED THERMODYNAMIC PROPERTIES

Differentiation of Eq. (19) with respect to temperature leads to
$$
-\frac{\partial \Delta G^{\circ}}{\partial T}=\Delta S^{\circ}=B-A+B \ln T, \tag{24}
$$
where $\Delta S^{\circ}$ is the standard entropy change for reaction (2).
The results for the foregoing semiconductors are
$$
\Delta S^{\circ}(\mathrm{Si})=-3.486 × 10^{-4}+1.060 × 10^{-4} \ln T, \quad(25)
$$

$$
\Delta S^{\circ}(\mathrm{Ge})=-3.013 × 10^{-4}+1.205 × 10^{-4} \ln T, \quad(26)
$$

$$
\Delta S^{\circ}(\mathrm{GaAs})=-1.987 × 10^{-4}+1.099 × 10^{-4} \ln T,
$$
(27)

$$
\Delta S^{\circ}(\mathrm{GaP})=-4.781 × 10^{-4}+1.537 × 10^{-4} \ln T.(28)
$$

### V. CONCLUSIONS

Equation (19) represents statistical thermodynamic interpretation of the variation of $E_{g}$ with temperature. This representation can be reconciled with other interpretations of temperature dependence of $E_{g}$, based on various models and mechanistic interactions $^{1,14,15}$ but not the derived properties such as $\Delta\Delta H^{\circ}$, $\Delta S^{\circ}$, and $\Delta C^{\circ}$. The main thesis of Eq. (19) is its basis on statistical thermodynamic arguments. It is also gratifying that Eq. (19) is able to represent the data for four well-characterized semiconductors remarkably well, supporting the basis of its derivation. Furthermore, Eq. (19) strongly suggests that there is a characteristic temperature below which $E_{g}$ becomes independent of temperature.

The arguments leading to Eqs. (15) and (16) regarding the properties of valence band edge electrons may justly be criticized as tenuous. However, the experimental data support the fact that $\Delta C^{\circ}$ for reaction (2) must be 1.5 $k$, but the sum of $C_{-}^{\circ}$ and $G_{+}^{\circ}$ for and $e^{+}$ is $3k$ as required by the fact that these carriers are free particles; therefore, it is necessary that $C_{\circ}^{\circ}$ for $e_{\circ}$ be $1.5k$ to make $\Delta C^{\circ}=C_{-}^{\circ}+C_{+}^{\circ}-C_{\circ}^{\circ}=1.5k$ for reaction (2). This necessity might be considered as sufficient to justify Eq. (15); however, it is possible to ignore Eq. (15) and still retain $C_{\circ}^{\circ}=1.5$ as one must and subtract the term $-C_{\circ}^{\circ}T\ln T$ from Eq. (14) to account for the term $-\Delta C^{\circ}$ $T\ln T=-1.5T\ln T$ in Eq. (17) for reaction (2). The remaining terms inside the parentheses in Eq. (17) must then be regarded as empirical constants. It is essential to reemphasize here that a term $(C_{-}^{\circ}+C_{+}^{\circ})T\ln T$ must be present in the derivation of any equation representing $\Delta G^{\circ}$ or $E_{g}$, because $e^{-}$ and $e^{+}$ are free particles, but $C_{-}^{\circ}+C_{+}^{\circ}=3k$ by itself is not compatible with experimental data. Therefore,

$$0(\text{zero})=e^{-}+e^{+}\qquad(30)$$

often used in describing the reaction between electrons and holes, $^{16}$ cannot yield the proper value of $\Delta C^{\circ}$, compatible with experimental results.

The number of electrons $e_{\circ}$ participating as the reactant in reaction (2) is assumed to be very small in concentration, because they are the electrons right on the valence band edge. This assumption eliminates the possibility of a small contribution by $e_{\circ}$ to the heat capacity of semiconductor itself.

It is possible to reach a relationship similar to Eq. (17) by rewriting reaction (2) for a semiconductor such as Si as follows:

$$\mathrm{Si} \rightarrow \mathrm{Si}^{+}+e^{-},\qquad(31)$$

where $e^{-}$ is the free electron in the conduction band, and $\text{Si}^{+}$ is the silicon atom with one missing electron. The activity of $\text{Si}^{+}$ is equal to its mole fraction as in dilute electrolytic solutions. The heat capacities of $\text{Si}$ and $\text{Si}^{+}$ are expected to be comparable; hence, for reaction (30), $\Delta C^{\circ}=1.5k$, which is equal to the heat capacity of the free electron. The value of $G^{\circ}(\text{Si}^{+})-G^{\circ}(\text{Si})$ is expected to be small, and it can be expressed by $\Delta\mathrm{h}-T\Delta\mathrm{s}$ in terms of very small coefficients $\Delta\mathrm{h}$ and $\Delta\mathrm{s}$. To this expression $G_{-}^{\circ}=-kT\ln(q_{-}/N_{A})$ must be added to obtain $\Delta G^{\circ}$ for reaction (31). This procedure would basically justify Eq. (19), and lead to no difficulty in thermodynamics, but it does present physical difficulties in that $\text{Si}^{+}$ does not represent the electrical properties of a hole, as mentioned earlier.

It is feasible to estimate closely the unknown values of the band gap energy $E_{g}$ of a semiconductor at various temperatures from two values of $E_{g}$ at two temperatures, i.e., one value of $\Delta E_{g}$, by using Eq. (18). Further, one more accurate value of $E_{g}$ at another temperature would permit the calculation of $A$ in Eq. (19) while retaining $B=1.5k$. The adjustment required for the refinement of $B$ necessitates at least one more well spaced reliable value of $E_{g}$.
