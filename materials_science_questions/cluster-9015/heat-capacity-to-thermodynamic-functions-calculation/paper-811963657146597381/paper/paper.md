and Thermodynamic Properties of InSe

$^{a}$ Kurnakov Institute of General and Inorganic Chemistry, Russian Academy of Sciences,

Abstract—The heat capacity of InSe has been measured at temperatures from 11.14 to 325.26 K using an adiabatic calorimeter. The results differ significantly from earlier data (by ~2.2% at 200 K). The smoothed heat capacity data have been used to evaluate temperature-dependent thermodynamic functions (entropy, enthalpy increment, and reduced Gibbs energy) of indium selenide. Its thermodynamic properties under standard conditions are $C_{p}^{0}(298.15\ \text{K}) = 49.43 \pm 0.10\ \text{J/(K mol)}$, $S^{0}(298.15\ \text{K}) = 82.20 \pm 0.16\ \text{J/(K mol)}$, $H^{0}(298.15\ \text{K}) - H^{0}(0) = 10.94 \pm 0.02\ \text{kJ/mol}$, and $\Phi^{0}(298.15\ \text{K}) = 45.50 \pm 0.09\ \text{J/(K mol)}$. The Debye characteristic temperature of InSe evaluated from the heat capacity data is $275 \pm 15\ \text{K}$.

DOI: 10.1134/S0020168507090014

## INTRODUCTION

Indium chalcogenide compounds offer a number of interesting physical properties and have a special chemical structure. Indium selenide, InSe, is of considerable technological interest because it is an attractive material for nonlinear optics, solar energy conversion, high-sensitivity infrared detectors, solid-state power sources, and high-sensitivity strain gages.

The heat capacity of InSe was determined earlier by adiabatic calorimetry [1, 2]. Mamedov et al. [1], however, performed measurements starting only at 55 K and, moreover, presented smoothed data, in both graphical and tabular forms, rather than the experimentally determined values. Dmitriev et al. [2] determined the heat capacity of InSe in a wider temperature range, from 5 to 300 K, but their experimental data scatter widely and are presented only in graphical form. Note that, judging from the lattice parameters reported by Mamedov et al. [1], the sample preparation procedure they used (melting of a stoichiometric elemental mixture in evacuated silica tubes, without subsequent annealing) led to the formation of $\beta$-InSe, whereas Dmitriev et al. [2] measured the heat capacity of $\gamma$-InSe, the most common polymorph in Bridgman growth.

The objective of this work was to accurately determine the low-temperature thermodynamic functions of InSe and to analyze the data in a fractal approach [3] in order to assess the feasibility of determining the detailed structure of InSe from heat capacity data.

## EXPERIMENTAL

The starting reagents used were high-purity indium (99.9999%) and selenium (OSCh 17-3). Synthesis was performed in silica tubes sealed off under a vacuum of 0.65 Pa, by melting an elemental mixture at a temperature 20–40 K above the melting point of InSe, followed by prolonged (250 h) annealing at a temperature 100 K below its melting point. X-ray diffraction analysis (DRON-3 diffractometer, $\text{Cu}K_{\alpha}$ radiation, data processing with the Express program) confirmed that the material was single-phase. The lattice parameters of InSe (hexagonal structure) were determined to be $a = 4.009 \pm 0.001\ \mathring{A}$ and $c = 16.625 \pm 0.007\ \mathring{A}$, in agreement with earlier data for $\beta$-InSe [4].

Prior to heat capacity measurements, the sample was ground to a particle size of 1–3 mm in order to reduce the equilibration time during steplike heating in the calorimeter. The weight of the InSe sample loaded into the caloric cell was 4.3455 g. After evacuation, the cell was filled with a heat-exchange gas (helium) to a pressure of $10^{4}\ \text{Pa}$ and sealed. The molecular weight of InSe was taken to be 193.778 [5]. Low-temperature heat capacity data were obtained in an adiabatic calorimeter equipped with a TSPN-2V platinum resistance thermometer.$^{1}$

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The heat capacity of InSe was measured at 128 temperatures (Table 1). In the temperature range studied, no anomalies in heat capacity were detected.

The $C_{p}(T)$ data were smoothed by spline fitting using an application program provided by the IVTANTERMO

Database [7]. The scatter of the experimental heat capacity data about the smoothed values is illustrated in Fig. 1. For most of the data points, the deviation is within 0.2%. At liquid-helium temperatures, the scatter attains 0.5%.

The thermodynamic functions evaluated from spline coefficients are presented in Table 2. The standard entropy, $S^0(298.15\ \text{K}) = 82.20 \pm 0.16\ \text{J/(K mol)}$, is 5.5% higher than that reported by Abbasov [8], $S^0(298.15\ \text{K}) = 77.9 \pm 2.9\ \text{J/(K mol)}$. In addition, Fig. 1 compares the present $C_p(T)$ data with the results reported by Mamedov et al. [1]. Their data are in reasonable agreement with ours but scatter widely, with a systematic deviation which reaches ~2.2% at 200 K.

As shown by Shebershneva [3], the Debye characteristic temperature $\Theta$ can be evaluated rather accurately from $C_p(T)$ data using a fractal model for inorganic compounds (including III–VI semiconductors). This can be done by plotting a series of temperature dependences of the fracton dimension $D$ at various $\Theta$ values and determining the characteristic temperature at which the fracton dimension curve has a maximum between 2 and 3 (since this range corresponds to layered structures).

According to Dmitriev et al. [2], the value of $D$ evaluated by extrapolating the temperature-dependent fracton dimension to absolute zero characterizes the dimensionality of the structure. Figure 2 shows the temperature dependences of the fracton dimension of InSe calculated for $\Theta = 250, 260, 270$, and 280 K. It can be seen from Fig. 2 that, near absolute zero, all of the curves approach the same fracton dimension, slightly above 2, independent of the Debye temperature used in the calculations, which confirms that InSe has a layered structure. This result correlates well with structural data, which attest to the presence of layers in InSe.
