# THE CHEMICAL THERMODYNAMICS OF NUCLEAR MATERIALS
## IV. The high-temperature enthalpies of plutonium monocarbide and plutonium sesquicarbide

Franklin L. OETTING

Rockwell International, Energy Systems Group, Rocky Flats Plant, Golden, Colorado 80401, USA

Received 23 March 1979

The high-temperature enthalpies of plutonium monocarbide and plutonium sesquicarbide have been determined with a copper-block calorimeter of the isoperibol type. The experimental enthalpy data, which were measured relative to 298 K, covered the temperature range from 400 to 1500 K. The calculation of the temperature rise of the calorimeter takes into account the added heat evolution from the radioactive decay of the plutonium samples. These enthalpy results, combined with the heat capacity and entropy of the respective carbide at 298 K available from the literature, have made it possible to generate tables of thermodynamic functions for the plutonium carbides. The behaviour of the heat capacities of both of the plutonium carbides, i.e., a relatively steep increase in the heat capacity as the temperature increases, may be attributed to the formation of vacancies within the crystal lattice although a theoretical treatment of this phenomenon is not given.

## 1. Introduction

Because of the potential use of plutonium carbides as well as uranium carbides as advanced nuclear fuels, there has been considerable interest in the thermo- dynamic behaviour of these materials in the past few years. Nonetheless, the complete thermodynamic analysis of the plutonium carbide system has been somewhat limited due to the lack of high-temperature heat capacity (or enthalpy) data. Interestingly enough, there exists only one published report [1] on the heat capacity of plutonium monocarbide above room tem- perature and no published reports on similar data for plutonium sesquicarbide.

Experimental results on the high-temperature ent- halpy from 298-1500 K are presented here on well characterized samples of plutonium monocarbide and plutonium sesquicarbide. These high-temperature data are certainly essential for the accurate definition of Gibbs' energy functions of the plutonium carbides at the higher temperatures which in turn are necessary for the prediction of the chemical behaviour of the plutonium carbides in nuclear reactor systems.

## 2. Experimental

The calorimeter used for the high-temperature enthalpy measurements on the plutonium carbides is a copper-block drop calorimeter of the isoperibol type originally described by Southard [2]. This partic- ular calorimeter system used for enthalpy measure- ments on plutonium compounds, described in detail earlier [3], was designed for ease of operation within the confines of a glovebox. Since plutonium exhibits both radioactivity and extreme toxicity, the sample was not only double encapsulated but the complete calorimeter was enclosed in a glovebox as a safety precaution. The glovebox, however, was maintained in a radioactive "cold" condition since there would be no contamination of the glovebox under normal operating conditions unless there was an accidental leakage of the sample material from the containing capsule.

The calorimetric system consists of a resistance furnace with platinum windings, the calorimeter proper, which was immersed in a constant-temper- ature oil bath, and the sample. The plutonium carbide

samples were sealed within tantalum capsules which in turn were sealed within capsules of platinum. The purpose of the tantalum containers was to prevent any possible reaction between the carbide sample and platinum whereas the platinum containers would prevent any oxidation of the tantalum by the air environment in the furnace.

The temperature of the platinum capsule in its suspended position within the furnace was obtained by using a platinum-to-platinum-to-(platinum + 10 mass% rhodium) thermocouple whose junction was suspended 1 cm above the top of the suspended capsule. The thermocouple junction, as well as the platinum capsule, was located within a 13 cm isothermal zone within the furnace which differed less than ±0.5 K from the average zone temperature. This isothermal condition, achieved by the adjustment of external furnace shunts for the entire temperature range, was determined from previous experience with the furnace.

The thermocouple was calibrated before and after the plutonium carbide experiments in situ by comparison with standard metal (Cu, Pb, Zn, Sn) melting temperatures traceable to the National Bureau of Standards (USA). The temperature of the carbide samples would of course be slightly higher than that indicated by the thermocouple due to the self-heating of the plutonium sample. A crude mathematical analysis involving the specific power of the plutonium carbide sample, the thermal conductivity and a mathematical model of the cylindrical arrangement enables one to estimate that the temperature of the plutonium carbide was between 0.3 and 1.2 K higher than that indicated by the thermocouple. The spread in temperature is due to the uncertainty in the thermal conductivity and the thermal resistance at the point of contact of the sample material with the enclosure materials. As a first approximation to this mathematical analysis, the temperature of the carbide sample was assumed to be 0.5 K higher due to the plutonium self-heating than that indicated by the thermocouple. This 0.5 K increase in the sample temperature is well within the error estimate of 1 K of the thermodynamic temperature. (All temperatures are based on IPTS-68.)

During a drop experiment, the encapsulated sample was suspended in the furnace at the desired temperature for a minimum of 2 h to attain temperature equilibrium. Previous experience with this apparatus had indicated that a period of 2 h was ample time for the sample to reach the desired temperature. The capsule was then dropped into the copper-block calorimeter which was placed within a brass isothermal jacket. The jacket in turn was immersed in a constant-temperature oil bath whose temperature was controlled to ±0.001 K at 298.15 K. To minimize convection and radiation heat losses, the copper-block was placed on bakelite knife-edged supports and spaced approximately 1 cm from the jacket. After the drop of the hot capsule the temperature of the calorimeter was followed by means of a Maier bridge for a period 3 h so as to accurately define the temperature rise of the calorimeter.

The calculation of the temperature rise for this calorimeter for enthalpy measurements on self-heating radioactive materials has been described in detail previously [3]. This calculation determines the temperature rise from information received from the drop-period and the after-period: a necessary thermodynamic condition as the radioactive sample is an integral part of the calorimeter during the drop- and after-periods but not an integral part of the calorimeter during the fore-period.

The calorimeter had previously been calibrated with the radioactive sample and capsule in the calorimeter proper to determine the energy equivalent of the calorimeter, $2579.3 \pm 3.4\ \text{J}\ \text{K}^{-1}$. When the energy equivalent is multiplied by the temperature rise of the calorimeter, the enthalpy increment of the capsule from 298.15 K (which is the initial temperature of the calorimeter, not the convergence temperature) to the temperature of the capsule, while it was suspended in the furnace, is obtained. An estimate of the overall accuracy of the enthalpy measurements has previously been reported [3] on enthalpy measurements obtained with this calorimeter on the calorimetric standard synthetic sapphire obtained from the National Bureau of Standards (USA), bearing the label: "Calorimetry Conference Standard Aluminum Oxide". The enthalpies obtained on this standard did not deviate by more than ±0.3% from the results on aluminum oxide reported by the National Bureau of Standards (USA) [4]. To confirm the reliability of the calorimeter, enthalpy measurements were made periodically on the same sample of aluminum oxide throughout the time period involved in the enthalpy measurements on the plutonium carbides. ### 2.1. Plutonium carbide samples

The plutonium carbide samples were prepared by arc-melting high purity plutonium metal with spectroscopic grade graphite rod in the proper proportions in a tungsten electrode. Table 1 lists the isotopic composition of the starting plutonium metal for both the monocarbide and the sesquicarbide which were prepared in different experiments using different plutonium metal.

Microscopic examination of the plutonium monocarbide sample indicated the final button to be a single-phase PuC with far less than 1 v/o Pu₂C₃ inclusions and no impurity phases in the grain boundaries at 1000 × magnification. The lattice parameter was found to be 4.9589 ± 0.0003 Å. Chemical analyses showed this material to be 96.0 wt% plutonium and 4.0 wt% carbon. Spectroscopic analyses data are given in table 2. All other impurities were below the analytical detection limit.

From the elemental chemical analyses for this material along with the average atomic weight of 239.126 g mol⁻¹ for plutonium derived from the isotopic results in table 1, the carbide sample composition is calculated to be PuC₀.₈₂ with a gram-formula weight of 248.975 g mol⁻¹. Even though the formula for this plutonium monocarbide (PuC₀.₈₂) places this material near the lower C/Pu atom ratio phase boundary, micrographic examination as mentioned above indicated this material to be single-phase "PuC".

Similar microscopic examination of the plutonium sesquicarbide sample indicated the material to be single-phase with no detectable second-phase. The lattice parameter was found to be 8.1371 ± 0.001 Å. Chemical analyses indicated this material to have 92.88 wt% plutonium and 6.94 wt% carbon. All other impurities which were above their respective detection limit are listed in table 2. With the elemental chemical analyses for this carbide material along with the atomic weight of 239.117 g mol⁻¹ for the plutonium metal, as calculated from the isotopic results in table 1, the composition of this carbide sample is calculated to be PuC₁.₄₉ (Pu₂C₂.₉₈) with a gram formula weight of 514.027 g mol⁻¹ of "Pu₂C₃".

### 3.2. Plutonium monocarbide $(\mathrm{PuC}_{0.82})$

A total of twelve experimental enthalpy determinations were taken on the sample of plutonium monocarbide in the temperature range of approximately 400-1500 K. The PuC sample weighed 21.8777 g (0.08787 mol when the molecular weight of the carbide is taken as $248.975 \mathrm{~g} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$). The platinum capsule weighed 15.4817 g and the tantalum capsule weighed 11.6299 g accounting for approximately 26 and $20 \%$ of the total enthalpy, respectively. No enthalpy corrections were made for the carbide sample impurities as they were insignificant.

Table 3 depicts the temperature of the enthalpy experiment, the order of the experiment, the experimental molar enthalpy value, the smoothed enthalpy value and the percent difference between the experimental and smoothed enthalpy values. The smoothed enthalpy values for $\mathrm{PuC}_{0.82}$ were taken from the equation
$$
\begin{aligned}
& H^{\circ}(T)-H^{\circ}(298)=-20737+65.938 T \\
& \quad-\left(2.423 \times 10^{-2}\right) T^{2}+\left(1.522 \times 10^{-5}\right) T^{3} \\
& \quad+\left(8.433 \times 10^{5}\right) T^{-1} \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}. \quad \text { (1) }
\end{aligned}
$$

The boundary conditions at 298 K for this equation were taken from the low-temperature heat capacity measurements on $\mathrm{PuC}_{0.80}$ reported by Hall et al. [7,8] (see section 4).

The heat capacity equation, which is valid over the temperature range of 298-1500 K and derived from the above enthalpy equation, is given as
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{p}} & =65.938-\left(4.846 \times 10^{-2}\right) T+\left(4.566 \times 10^{-5}\right) T^{2} \\
& -\left(8.433 \times 10^{5}\right) T^{-2} \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~K}^{-1} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}. \quad \text { (2) }
\end{aligned}
$$

The estimated error in these measurements if $\pm 3 \%$ from evaluation of the precision of the measurements $(2.1 \%)$, the analysis of the sample $(0.3 \%)$ and the possible error $(0.6 \%)$ involved in the calculation of the temperature rise of the calorimeter.

### 3.3. Plutonium sesquicarbide $(\mathrm{Pu}_{2} \mathrm{C}_{2.98})$

Thirteen experimental enthalpy determinations were taken on the sample of plutonium sesquicarbide in the temperature range of approximately 400-1500 K. The $\mathrm{Pu}_{2} \mathrm{C}_{3}$ sample weighed 24.3499 g (0.04737 mol, when the molecular weight of plutonium sesquicarbide is taken as $514.027 \mathrm{~g} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ ). The platinum capsule weighed 15.5347 g and the tantalum capsule weighed 13.1745 g accounting for approximately 22 and $19 \%$ of the total enthalpy, respectively. No enthalpy corrections were made for the sesquicarbide impurities as they were insignificant.

Table 4 depicts the temperature of the enthalpy experiment, the order of the experiment, the experimental molar enthalpy value, the smoothed enthalpy value and the percent difference between the experimental and smoothed enthalpy values. The smoothed enthalpy values for $\mathrm{Pu}_{2} \mathrm{C}_{2.98}$ were taken from the equation
$$
\begin{aligned}
& H^{\circ}(T)-H^{\circ}(298)=-50885.1+156.0 T \\
& \quad-\left(3.989 \times 10^{-2}\right) T^{2}+\left(2.348 \times 10^{-5}\right) T^{3} \\
& \quad+\left(2.176 \times 10^{6}\right) T^{-1} \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}. \quad \text { (3) }
\end{aligned}
$$

The boundary conditions at 298 K for this equation were taken from the low-temperature measurements on $\mathrm{Pu}_{2} \mathrm{C}_{3}$ reported by Hall et al. [7,8] (see section 4).

The heat capacity equation, which is valid over the temperature range of 298-1500 K and derived from the above enthalpy equation, is given as
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{p}}^{\circ} & =156.0=\left(7.978 \times 10^{-2}\right) T+\left(7.044 \times 10^{-5}\right) T^{2} \\
& -\left(2.176 \times 10^{6}\right) T^{-2} \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~K}^{-1} \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}. \quad \text { (4) }
\end{aligned}
$$

The estimated error in these measurements is $\pm 3 \%$ from evaluation of the precision of the measurements $(2.1 \%)$, the analysis of the sample $(0.3 \%)$ and the possible error $(0.6 \%)$ involved in the calculation of the temperature rise of the calorimeter.

### 4. Discussion

Because of the comparatively wide range of stoichiometry that exists for plutonium monocarbide, it is somewhat questionable to truly compare this ent-

halpy data on $\text{PuC}_{0.82}$ with data on a plutonium monocarbide with higher carbon content. However, Kruger and Savage [1] have reported high-temperature enthalpy data on $\text{PuC}_{0.87}$ to a temperature of 1300 K. The agreement between these two sets of data is within the overall experimental accuracy however.

Nevertheless with the use of these enthalpy data presented here for $\text{PuC}_{0.82}$, it is possible to generate a table of thermal functions to 1500 K for plutonium monocarbide with a composition of $\text{PuC}_{0.82}$ (table 5). As stated previously, the boundary constraints taken for the enthalpy fit of $\text{PuC}_{0.82}$ were taken from the low-temperature heat capacity measurements reported by Hall et al. [7,8]. Their reported values, $C_{\text{p}}(\text{PuC}_{0.80}, c, 298) = 26.06\ \text{J K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$, $\text{d}C_{\text{p}}/\text{d}T(\text{PuC}_{0.80}, c, 298) = 0.0426\ \text{J mol}^{-1}$ and $S^{\circ}(\text{PuC}_{0.80}, c, 298) = 69.33\ \text{J K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ agree well with the earlier work of Sandenaw and Gibney [9] who reported $C_{\text{p}}(\text{PuC}_{0.81}, c, 298) = 46.19\ \text{J K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $S^{\circ}(\text{PuC}_{0.81}, c, 298) = 70.46\ \text{J K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.

It is interesting to compare the high-temperature enthalpy and heat capacity of PuC, obtained in this report (actually $\text{PuC}_{0.82}$) with that of UC which has been reported earlier from this same laboratory [10] (figs. 1 and 2). It is immediately evident that the enthalpy of PuC, and thus the heat capacity, rises considerably faster with increasing temperature than does that of UC. This significant characteristic of PuC is most probably caused by the formation of vacancies within the PuC crystal lattice although a comprehensive analysis of the vacancy formation is not possible from the data presented here.

As no other enthalpy data for $Pu_2C_3$ have been reported in the literature, no comparison with these data is possible. As stated previously, the boundary constraints taken for the enthalpy fit of $Pu_2C_3$ were taken from the low-temperature heat capacity measurements reported by Hall et al. [7,8]. They reported values of $C_{\text{p}}(Pu_2C_3, c, 298)=114.0\ \text{J}\ \text{K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $\text{d}C_{\text{p}}/\text{d}T(Pu_2C_3, c, 298)=0.1268\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. The value for the standard entropy, $S^{\circ}(Pu_2C_3, c, 298)=150.0\ \text{J}\ \text{K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ [7], along with these enthalpy data was used to generate the thermal functions for $Pu_2C_3$ to 1500 K (table 6). Danan [12] had reported an entropy value for $Pu_2C_3$ of $(128 \pm 8)\ \text{J}\ \text{K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ which is considerably lower than the value by Hall et al. [7].
