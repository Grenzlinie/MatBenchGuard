# THE THERMAL PROPERTIES OF SLAGS BASED ON $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{CaF}_{2}}$

K. C. MILLS, J. S. POWELL, J. W. BRYANT and B. J. KEENE

Division of Chemical Standards, National Physical Laboratory, Teddington, Middlesex, U.K.

(Received 24 June 1980)

**Abstract**—Thermal property data for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$-based slags have been measured to provide information needed for the mathematical modelling of the electroslag remelting process. The total normal emissivity, thermal expansion, thermal conductivity, thermal diffusivity and enthalpy of these slags have been determined for both solid and liquid phases: the results are discussed.

**Résumé**—Certaines propriétés thermiques des scories à base de $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$, ont été mesurées afin de fournir l'information nécessaire à la modélisation mathématique du procédé de refusion sous laitier électroconducteur. L'émissivité totale normale, l'expansion thermique, la conductivité thermique, la diffusivité thermique ainsi que l'enthalpie de ces scories ont été déterminées pour les phases solides et liquide. Une discussion des résultats s'ensuit.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Slags with a high $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ content are extensively used in welding and in the production of high-quality steels by the electroslag remelting (ESR) process. It has been shown [1,2] that the quality of the steel produced by this process is dependent upon various physical and chemical properties of the molten slag layer such as the electrical conductivity, viscosity, interfacial tension, density and chemical activities. There have been several attempts to produce mathematical models of the ESR process in recent years [3–6]. Although some success has been recorded with these theoretical models of the ESR process, their success has been limited by the dearth of thermal property data for the slag layer.

If we consider the ESR process, various modes of heat loss from the molten slag layer can be identified; these include: (a) energy losses due to radiation from the liquid slag layer; (b) thermal transfer through the metal layer; (c) thermal transfer through the solid slag layer formed adjacent to the water-cooled mould; and (d) thermal transfer through any air gap which may be formed by thermal contraction of the metal and slag. It is apparent that a full description of these various modes of energy transfer would require a knowledge of the total normal emissivity, thermal conduction and enthalpy of both solid and liquid slags and the thermal expansion of solid slags. It was the aim of this study to provide these data.

## 2. PREPARATION OF SLAGS

The slags used in this investigation were prepared from “extra-pure” $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$; Analar $\mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}$, and $\mathrm{CaO}$ “low in $\mathrm{Cl}$ and $\mathrm{S}$”. The $\mathrm{CaO}$ was dried by heating at $1000\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and cooling in a desiccator. Weighed amounts of the compounds were intimately mixed, placed in a molybdenum crucible and heated to $1870\ \mathrm{K}$ for 20 min under an atmosphere of dry argon. After being cooled to room temperature, the sample was ground then reheated for a further 20 min at $1870\ \mathrm{K}$; after that it was re-ground in a glove box and stored in a vacuum desiccator. Samples for thermal diffusivity and thermal expansion measurements were made by pressing discs and bars, respectively, and sintering these for 2 h at $20\ \mathrm{K}$ below the solidus temperature for the slag (for most slags a temperature of $1320\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ was employed).

### 2.1. Total normal emissivity ($\varepsilon$)

#### 2.1.1. Theory of method.
Solid $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ is known to be transparent to infra-red (IR) radiation and it would not be surprizing if slags based on $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ were also partially transparent to IR radiation. It is thus necessary to develop a method suitable for semi-transparent materials. Keene and Quinn [7] have shown that the energy emitted from the surface of a semi-transparent slag, $E_{s}$ would be given by equation (1)

$$
E_{s}=\varepsilon_{s} E_{B} \frac{\left(1-r_{b} \mathrm{e}^{-2 K d}\right)}{\left(1-r_{s} r_{b} \mathrm{e}^{-2 K d}\right)} \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{B}$ is the energy emitted by a black body, $\varepsilon_{s}$ is the total normal emissivity of the slag, $r_{s}$ and $r_{b}$ are the reflectivities of the slag and base, respectively, $K$ is the absorption coefficient and $d$ is the depth of the slag above the base. The equation is simplified to $E_{s}=\varepsilon_{s} E_{B}$ by rendering $r_{b}=0$, which can be accomplished by monitoring the radiation emitted by a black body submerged beneath the surface of the slag. Hence the emissivity of the slag can be determined by comparison of the energy emitted from this submerged black body with that of a black body proud of the surface. It can be seen from equation (1) that if the medium is semi-transparent to IR radiation, the introduction of polished inserts of known reflectivity at different depths below the surface would alter the energy emitted; thus, the absorption coefficient could be calculated by monitoring the energy emitted from the surface above these inserts.

#### 2.1.2. Method.
The apparatus used for measuring the emissivity has been described in detail elsewhere [7]; scattered radiation from the heating element was eliminated by using a cone-shaped shield situated above the crucible. The spectral range, $0.5–20\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ was defined by using $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{KBr}$ windows and the emitted energy was monitored by a Hilger–Watt thermopile. The cell containing the slag was fabricated entirely from molybdenum; the black bodies and polished inserts were

installed as "push-fit joints" to avoid welded joints which frequently fail in use.

### 2.2. Thermal expansion of solid slags ($\alpha$)
Linear thermal expansion values of solid slags were determined with a Linseis dilatometer; the measurement apparatus was constructed of either silica or alumina. Thermal expansion values were computed from heating and cooling curves. The dilatometer was calibrated with reference samples of platinum, sapphire and silica.

### 2.3. Thermal diffusivity of solid slags (a)
Thermal diffusivities of solid slags (a) were measured by Dr Roy Taylor (UMIST, Manchester) using the laser-pulse method [8]. The samples, sintered discs of 6.25mm diameter and 0.7-1.2 mm height, were coated with chromium by ion bombardment. Measurements were made over the temperature range, $200-1200\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$.

### 2.4. Thermal conductivity of liquid slags (k)
Numerous problems are encountered in the measurement of thermal conductivity, $k$, of liquids at high temperatures, some of which have been outlined by Elliott [9]. In order to minimize these problems we decided to use a transient technique and selected the line-source method. The apparatus used for these measurements is shown in Fig. 1. A constant current was applied to a filament constructed of $0.125\ \text{mm}\ (\text{Pt}+10\%\text{Rh})$ wire, producing an increase in the filament temperature. The temperature of the filament was then monitored continuously by measuring the resistance potentiometrically. The duration of the entire experiment was about 1s. The signal was amplified, converted to digital output and held in a buffer-store as 1024 data points.

The temperature rise, $\Delta T$, is given by equation (2), where $q$ is the heat flux per unit length

$$
\Delta T=\frac{q}{4 \pi k} \ln \frac{4 k \tau}{r^{2} \rho c_{p} c^{*}} \tag{2}
$$

$\tau$ is the time, $\rho, c_{p}$ and $r$ are the density, heat capacity and radius of the wire, respectively and $c^{*}$ is a constant equal to $\ln \gamma, \gamma$ being Euler's constant.

A plot of $\Delta T$ against $\ln \tau$ contains a linear region, the slope of which is proportional to $(1 / k)$. The proportionality constant was determined from calibration experiments using water, ethanol, methanol, dimethyl phthalate and glycerol before and after the measurements on the molten slag. Corrections were made for the change in slope of the electrical resistivity-temperature curve for $\text{Pt}+10\%\text{Rh}$ wire.

### 2.5. Enthalpy and heat capacity
Specific heat capacity, $c_{p}$, for the temperature range 298-1000 K was measured with a Perkin-Elmer differential scanning calorimeter (dsc) model. The output signal from the dsc was measured with a digital voltmeter and recorded on paper tape. The experiment was run with the calorimeter (a) empty; (b) filled with Calorimetry Conference sapphire; and (c) filled with slag. The treatment of the data to yield $c_{p}$ values has been described by Mills and Richardson [10]. All temperatures were based on IPTS-68.

Measurements of the enthalpy relative to that at 298 K, $H_{T}-H_{298}$, of these slags were made over the temperature range 1000-1900 K by means of the drop calorimeter used by Andon, Martin and Mills [11]. The slags were held in sealed crucibles fabricated from $\text{Pt}+10\%\text{Rh}$ and $\text{Pt}+30\%\text{Rh}$. Unfortunately, the molten slags attacked the platinum-alloy crucibles and restricted the amount of data which could be obtained for the liquid regions of the slags.

### 3.2. Linear thermal expansion of solid slags

Thermal expansion values for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ and for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}+15 \% \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}+15 \% \mathrm{CaO}$ are given in Fig. 4. The values for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ are in good agreement with those reported by Whittemore and Ault (12). The thermal expansion curves for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}+15 \% \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}+15 \% \mathrm{CaO}$ presented in Fig. 4 show a marked hysteresis. The cooling curves, which are quite reproducible, always exhibit the "wobbles" denoted by M and $\mathrm{N}$; these presumably correspond to the formation of $\mathrm{C}_{11} \mathrm{~A}_{7} \mathrm{Fl} \dagger$ from a high temperature phase. The slag contains $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$, $\mathrm{C}_{11} \mathrm{~A}_{7} \mathrm{Fl}$ and probably some $\mathrm{CaO}$; the mean coefficients of thermal expansion for these compounds are believed to be in the ratio of $6: 1: 3$. When a pellet is being prepared by sintering at high temperatures, the differences between the contraction of the three constituent compounds on cooling from the sintering temperatures result in the formation of microcracks. (It would be expected that microcracks would form in the slag cap produced during ESR by a similar mechanism.) When the sample is reheated in the dilatometer the presence of these microcracks results in an apparently low thermal expansion and the observed hysteresis effect is produced by a degree of re-healing of microcracks at higher temperatures.

Calcium fluoride is known to be semi-transparent to IR radiation and the small samples used in this investigation could be "optically thin". The thicknesses of the samples used in this investigation were varied by a factor of two but no differences in the measured diffusivity values were observed. Values of thermal conductivity $k$, were calculated from the relationship, $k=a_{t} c_{p} \rho$, where $\rho$ is the density (calculated from mean value of $\rho$ at $25^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ together with the mean volume coefficient of expansion) and using the $c_{p}$ values obtained in this study. The values, shown in Fig. 6, are some $20 \%$ higher than values reported by Charvat and Kingery [13] at lower temperatures but converge at higher temperatures. Mean values of $k$ obtained by Mitchell and Joshi [14] and by Narita et al. [15] are also shown.

The thermal diffusivity was found to decrease when samples were indifferently stored for several months (Fig. 5); this decrease in thermal diffusivity is believed to be associated with the absorption of water by the sample.

Thermal diffusivity values were also obtained for three samples of $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}+15 \% \mathrm{Al}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{3}+15 \% \mathrm{CaO}$; these are given in Fig. 7. The results show that the thermal diffusivity: (a) is lower than that of pure $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$; (b) is lower for specimens in vacuo than in air; (c) varies appreciably from sample to sample; (d) shows marked hysteresis (sample 3) when measurements were carried out for a heating and cooling cycle; and (e) showed no appreciable change for a specimen measured in air after vacuum annealing, indicating no structural change had occurred during the annealing process. The hysteresis is due to the formation of microcracks on cooling, resulting from the large differences in thermal contraction of the $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}, \mathrm{C}_{11} \mathrm{~A}_{7} \mathrm{Fl}$ and $\mathrm{CaO}$ phases, as discussed in Section 3.2. The presence of microcracks reduces the thermal diffusivity; as these cracks tend to close with increased temperature the thermal diffusivity increases with temperature, and these enhanced values are maintained on cooling until the formation of microcracks starts below $700^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. The variability of the results is also attributable to the presence of these microcracks, as the measured thermal diffusivity is obviously dependent upon the size, shape and distribution of the microcracks. The nature of the gas present in the microcracks is also important, for the thermal diffusivity in air is $\sim 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$ $\mathrm{cm}^{2} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ greater than that in vacuo. The observed thermal conductivity of the sample, $k_{\mathrm{obs}}$, can be considered to be made up of two contributions, $k_{\mathrm{s}}$ the intrinsic thermal conductivity of the sample and, $k_{\text {gas }}$, the gaseous conductivity, thus $k_{\mathrm{obs}}=k_{\mathrm{s}}+k_{\text {gas }}$. In vacuo, $k_{\text {gas }}$ will be zero and thus $k_{\text {obs }}$ will be lower. The identical results for $k_{\text {obs }}$ obtained for $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ in air and in vacuo indicates that few microcracks are formed in a homogeneous solid.

### 3.4. Thermal conductivity of molten slags

The performance of the method was checked by measurement of the thermal conductivity of glycerol up to $150^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$; the results lay within $3 \%$ of the most reliable values reported in the literature [16]. Further checks of the performance at higher temperatures were carried out with $\mathrm{KNO}_{3}(\mathrm{l})$ and $\mathrm{KI}(\mathrm{l})$. The results obtained for $\mathrm{KNO}_{3}(\mathrm{l})$ were $\sim 20 \%$ lower than values listed by earlier workers $[17,21]$ and $10 \%$ below values recently reported by Ogino, Nishiwaki and Yamamoto [22]. The effective total thermal conductivity, $k_{\text {eff }}$ is given by the relationship, $k_{\text {eff }}=k_{c}+k_{R}$, where $k_{c}$ and $k_{R}$ are the phonon and radiation thermal conductivities, respectively. Previous workers assumed $\mathrm{KNO}_{3}(\mathrm{l})$ was opaque to IR radiation and thus $k_{R}=0$, but spectroscopic evidence $[23,24]$ suggests that $\mathrm{KNO}_{3}$ is partially transparent to IR radiation and thus $k_{R} \neq 0$. Furthermore, it has been shown [25] that for the line-source method, $k_{R}$ is dependent upon time; the duration of our experiments is short (1 s) compared with the duration of experiments by other workers $[20,21]$ viz. $50 \mathrm{~s}$; thus we would expect $k_{R}$ to be smaller in our investigation. This could be the reason for the discrepancy between our results and those of previous investigators. It is well known that $\mathrm{KI}(\mathrm{l})$ is transparent to IR radiation and for the reason given above, it is not surprising that our value, $k_{\text {eff }}=166 \mathrm{~mW} \mathrm{~m}^{-1} \mathrm{~K}^{-1}$, is some $30 \%$ below that of the previous investigation [26].

We have measured the thermal conductivity of $CaF_{2}$ (l) with the results shown in Fig. 6. The $k_{\text{eff}}$ values calculated from thermal diffusivity values for the solid phase have been plotted and extrapolated from $1250\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ to the melting point. The value $k_{\text{eff}} = 3050\ \text{mW}\ \text{m}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ due to Ogino, Nishiwaki and Yamamoto [22] is also shown; this value is higher than $k_{\text{eff}}$ values obtained here for the solid phase. Inspection of thermal conductivity data for salts shows that the thermal conductivity approximately halves on transforming from a solid to a liquid. However in Fig. 6 the thermal conductivity of $CaF_{2}$ apparently decreases to about $5\%$ of its value for the solid state. Such is the extent of this decrease that we must question whether the results obtained here with the line-source technique are affected by some systematic errors.

One possible source of error in the line-source technique arises if the melt has an appreciable electrical conductivity; thus a portion of the electrical energy supplied to the filament flows through the melt so that the filament-temperature-increase is less than anticipated. Calculations have shown that the voltage used in this experiment (0.4 V) lies well below the decomposition voltage of $CaF_{2}$ at $1500\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. However a recent communication [27] has suggested that the line-source method when used with molten slags is affected by this source of error. To check this we constructed the apparatus shown schematically in Fig. 8. If the shunting of the electrolytic conductivity in molten $CaF_{2}$ is appreciable then it will affect the apparent value of the thermal conductivity obtained for dimethyl phthalate. The results obtained showed that the polarity of the connections was of vital importance; if the connections were as shown, the value of $k$ was within $10\%$ of the true value, but if the connections denoted $a$ and $b$ were reversed, the apparent value was $20\%$ of its true value. However, if the electrical conductivity of the melt was affecting the measurements it would produce an erroneously high value for the thermal conductivity; from the reasonable values of $k$ for dimethyl phthalate obtained with one polarity and the somewhat low values of $k$ obtained with the other polarity, we conclude that the electrical conductivity of the melt is not responsible for any large systematic error in the measurements. The only explanation we can offer for the observed effect of polarity is that earth loops are formed at high temperatures when the electrical conductivity of alumina (used in the furnace tube and pedestal) becomes appreciable.

![](./images/813168649165602816_14.jpg)

It is necessary therefore to look to some other explanation for the dramatic decrease in $k$ of $CaF_{2}$ (l) at its melting point. For semi-transparent media, like $CaF_{2}$ or glasses, we would expect $k_{R}$ to increase at temperatures above $800\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, and at $1400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ we would expect $k_{R} \gg k_{c}$. However, we have seen that the relative absorption coefficient of $CaF_{2}$ increases very rapidly at the melting point (Fig. 3). Thus, if molten $CaF_{2}$ absorbs IR radiation strongly, then $k_{R}$ will become virtually zero and hence $k_{\text{eff}} \simeq k_{c}$ and this would produce a dramatic decrease in $k_{\text{eff}}$. It was not possible to determine the relative absorption coefficient of solid $CaF_{2}$-based slags owing to the scattering of radiation caused by the crystallinity of the surface of the slag, but it is reasonable to assume that a similar mechanism obtains for slags containing $\sim 90\% CaF_{2}$.

Another possible explanation for the apparent drop in thermal conductivity through the melting region is that the short duration of our experiments (1 s) results in a low value for $k_{R}$, and hence $k_{\text{eff}}$ in our experiments is not strictly comparable with $k_{\text{eff}}$ values obtained by methods which measure an appreciably larger $k_{R}$, such as those recorded by Ogino, Nishiwaki and Yamamoto [22].

Although we feel that these explanations satisfactorily account for the decrease in thermal conductivity at the melting point, we suggest that our experimental measurements for the thermal conductivity of $CaF_{2}$ (l) should be considered as preliminary values at this stage until the possible presence of systematic errors has been more fully investigated.

## 4. SUMMARY

We have provided values of total normal emissivity, thermal expansion, thermal diffusivity, thermal conductivity, and enthalpy for the solid and liquid phases of $\mathrm{CaF_{2}}$ and $\mathrm{CaF_{2}+15\% Al_{2}O_{3}+15\% CaO}$. These data should assist in the development of a reliable mathematical model for the ESR process.
