Yi Wang${}^{\mathrm{a)}}$

(Received 16 July 2001; accepted 10 September 2002)

Based on *ab initio* calculated 0 K results for the assumed face- or body-centered-cubic ground state, the thermal volume expansion of the principal Hugoniot relative to the 300 K isotherm is calculated using the recently developed mean-field potential approach for Cu, Ta, Mo, Pt, and Au. Neglecting shock melting and phase dependence of the high-temperature equation of state, shock-reduced 300 K isotherms at pressures up to 1 TPa (10 Mbar) are derived for these metals by substracting the calculated thermal volume expansion from the experimental shock-wave data. This approach does not invoke any empirical assumptions regarding the Grüneisen parameters or heat capacities. The excellent agreement between such shock-reduced data and the normal standards or empirical reductions by other authors shows that the reduced results can be used as pressure standards for the widely employed static diamond-anvil-cell experiments. © 2002 *American Institute of Physics.*
[DOI: 10.1063/1.1518781]

## I. INTRODUCTION

The development of an accurate equation-of-state (EOS) standard is of considerable scientific importance. The current pressure frontier lies in the pressure range 100–1000 GPa. This range opens up exciting possibilities to study matter under controlled conditions at superextreme compressions. Experimentally, the EOS of condensed matter at megabar pressures can now be probed in both static, low-temperature diamond-anvil-cell (DAC) experiments${}^{1–6}$ and in dynamic, high-temperature shock-wave experiments.${}^{7–12}$ The DAC technique is suitable for the ordinary laboratory in which samples can be studied under controlled conditions. Using laser heating and refinements in instrumentation, temperatures of about 3000 K can be reached up to pressure of the order of 200–300 GPa, while at room temperature Ruoff, Xia, and Xia${}^{2}$ reported an ultrahigh DAC pressure of 560 GPa. Even higher temperatures and higher pressure conditions can be achieved by shock-wave methods. Accordingly, there is an urgent need to develop methods that can be used to facilitate the interpretation of the data at extreme experimental conditions.

When analyzing and interpreting both DAC and shock-wave EOS experiments, an important distinction has to be made between relative and absolute measurements. In the static DAC experiments, only the volume is measured from the x-ray line positions, whereas the pressure is inferred indirectly from the diffraction line shifts of a pressure marker or reference material, which is mixed with the sample and whose $P-V$ relation is well known. In the dynamic shock-wave experiments, where the deduced $P-V$ curve is called Hugoniot, the shock velocity ($D$) and the particle velocity ($u$) are the measured quantities. Through the Rankine–Hugoniot relations,${}^{7}$ these data define a compression curve [volume ($V_{H}$) versus pressure ($P_{H}$)] as a function of the known Hugoniot energy ($E_{H}$)

$$
\frac{1}{2}P_{H}(V_{0}-V_{H})=E_{H}-E_{0}, \tag{1}
$$

where $V_{0}$ and $E_{0}$ refer to the atomic volume and energy under ambient conditions, respectively. Further, $V_{H}/V_{0}$ $=(D-u)/D$ and $P_{H}=\rho_{0}Du$, where $\rho_{0}$ is the initial density corresponding to $V_{0}$.

In this article, we introduce a reduction scheme to convert the measured shock-wave data${}^{7–12}$ to equivalent isothermal DAC results for the five reference metals Cu, Ta, Mo, Pt, and Au. Using the *ab initio* calculated 0 K results as the only input, the thermal volume expansion of the principal Hugoniot relative to the 300 K isotherm is calculated utilizing a recently proposed classical mean-field potential (MFP) approach.${}^{13}$ This method takes advantage of the accuracy of state-of-the-art first-principles electronic structure methods. We suggest that the present shock-reduced 300 K isotherms can serve as pressure markers, i.e., as a high-pressure standard, for future static DAC experiments.

## II. METHOD OF REDUCTION

### A. Reduction procedure

If, for a substance, the Helmholtz free energy is known as an explicit function of volume and temperature, we can calculate all the other thermodynamic parameters. Considering a system with a given atomic volume $V$ and temperature $T$, the Helmholtz free-energy $F(V,T)$ per ion can be written as

$$
F(V,T)=E_{c}(V)+F_{\text{ion}}(V,T)+F_{\text{el}}(V,T), \tag{2}
$$

where $E_{c}$ represents the 0 K total energy, $F_{\text{ion}}$ the vibrational free energy of the lattice ions, and $F_{\text{el}}$ the free energy due to the thermal excitation of electrons. To calculate the 0 K total energy $E_{c}(V)$ in Eq. (2), the full-potential linearized augmented plane-wave (LAPW) method$^{14}$ is employed.$^{15}$ On the mean-field potential$^{13}$ $g(r,V)$ needed to calculate $F_{\text{ion}}$, we use

$$
g(r,V)=\frac{1}{2}\left[E_{c}(R+r)+E_{c}(R-r)-2E_{c}(R)\right]. \tag{3}
$$

It is known that the experimental shock-wave data can be well described by the following $D$--$u$ relation:$^{8}$

$$
D=a+bu+cu^{2}. \tag{4}
$$

The fitted parameters $a$, $b$, and $c$ for the five reference materials are listed in Table I, where for Cu, Ta, Mo, Pt, and Au, the available upper pressures of the shock-wave data are 976, 1097, 1020, 660, and 513 GPa, respectively.

Generally speaking, it is certainly not trivial to calculate the equilibrium atomic volumes within the experimental error bars by means of first-principles methods. Therefore, when dealing with the pressure dependence of the volume it is an advantage to use the reduced atomic volume $X$ $=V/V_{0}$, where $V_{0}$ is the theoretical equilibrium volume, and then consider $P=P(X)$. The experimental shock-wave data $X_{\text{sw}}^{\text{ex}}$ can be expressed as

$$
X_{\text{sw}}^{\text{ex}}(P)=X_{300}^{\text{ex}}(P)+X_{\text{ht}}^{\text{ex}}(P), \tag{5}
$$

where $X_{300}^{\text{ex}}$ represents the experimental 300 K static EOS, and $X_{\text{ht}}^{\text{ex}}$ the volume expansion of the principal Hugoniot relative to the 300 K isotherm. We can also write the calculated results of the MFP approach in the equivalent form:

$$
X_{\text{sw}}^{\text{MFP}}(P)=X_{300}^{\text{MFP}}(P)+X_{\text{ht}}^{\text{MFP}}(P). \tag{6}
$$

In fact, the terms of $X_{\text{sw}}^{\text{MFP}}$ and $X_{300}^{\text{MFP}}$ in Eq. (6) can be easily calculated with the MFP approach. If we replace $X_{\text{ht}}^{\text{ex}}$ in Eq. (5) with $X_{\text{ht}}^{\text{MFP}}$ in Eq. (6), then we can obtain the reduced EOS $X_{300}^{\text{rd}}\simeq X_{300}^{\text{ex}}$ from shock-wave data by

$$
X_{300}^{\text{rd}}(P)=X_{\text{sw}}^{\text{ex}}(P)-\left[X_{\text{sw}}^{\text{MFP}}(P)-X_{300}^{\text{MFP}}(P)\right]. \tag{7}
$$

### B. Applicability of the reduction method

Apart from theoretically calculated quantities, no empirical parameters or ad hoc assumptions are made in the present reduction scheme. This is in contrast to previous empirical studies,$^{11,16,17}$ where additional assumptions about the Grüneisen parameters and the heat capacities have to be made and the thermal electronic contributions have been neglected. Also, the present approach differs from those of Moriarty and co-workers,$^{10,18,19}$ which are actually theoretical calculations rather than reduction of experimental data, as pointed out by Moriarty.$^{18}$ Unlike all previous attempts to deduce so-called "cold" pressure-volume data from the experimental Hugoniot relation, all the required physical quantities are now computed directly from the same theoretical basis employing the well-defined first-principles method.

In our reduction procedure, we have neglected the shock melting and phase dependence of the high-temperature equation of state. However, this does not persist as a severe approximation. Mitchell et al.$^{20}$ found that, for the reference metals, the measured curves of the shock-wave pressure against the volume were rather smooth and the effects of shock melting on the pressure-volume curve were too week to be observed.

While the measured pressure-volume Hugoniot usually shows no sharp discontinuity across the shock melting point (unlike the pressure-temperature Hugoniot), its curvature can definitely be affected in the liquid, as indeed the Grüneisen parameter and electronic density of states are strongly phase dependent in general. Neglecting the actual liquid behavior may or may not be a severe approximation in a given case. However, for the five reference metals of Cu, Ta, Mo, Pt, and Au, the effects due to melting are already included in the uncertainties of the shock-wave data. We must note that in this article our aim is to give the pressure-volume curve rather than its curvature.

In the present reductions, the lattice thermal contribution to the Helmholtz energy is calculated using the recently developed mean-field potential approach.$^{13}$ In appearance, the MFP approach is a simple, very approximate scheme. However, the calculated high-temperature and high-pressure EOS using the MFP approach are rather accurate. For the refrac-

tory metals, it was found that the calculated Hugoniot and 300 K isotherm were almost uniformly positioned at the same side of the measured data if certain deviations between the calculations and the experiments existed. And, this is the main inspiration of the present work. Physically, further sup- port of the MFP approach lies in the fact that the commonly used expressions for the Grüneisen parameter due to Dugdale and MacDonald, $^{21}$ which has been widely adopted in the realistic applications, can be explicitly deduced if we take the second-order approximation to Eq. (3).

### C. Accuracy of the present shock-reduced 300 K isotherm

The accuracy of the presently reduced results are mainly affected by two factors: (1) the uncertainties of the experi- mental shock-wave data and (2) the uncertainties of the MFP approach.

On the shock-wave data, the analysis by Al'tshuler et al. $^{8}$ show that, for Cu and Mo, the accuracies are $\pm 1.2 \%$ at 400 GPa and are $\pm 2.4 \%$ at 1000 GPa. For Ta, Al'tshuler et al. $^{8}$ did not explicitly give the uncertainty, but we can expect that the uncertainties are similar to Cu and Mo. For Pt, the shock-wave data by Holmes et al. $^{10}$ had an uncer tainty of $\pm 1.8 \%$ at pressures up to 660 GPa. For Au, Al'tshuler, Krupnikov, and Brazhnik $^{12}$ did not mention the uncertainty.

On the MFP approach, note that with a moderate modification $^{22}$ to Eq. (3) the three commonly used expres sions for the Grüneisen parameter, i.e., that due to Slater, $^{23}$ that due to Dugdale and MacDonald, that for the free-volume theory, $^{24}$ can all be explicitly deduced under the second-order approximation. If we take the MFP deduced out the Slater's expression for the Grüneisen parameter as the upper limit and the MFP deduced out the free-volume theory expression for the Grüneisen parameter as the lower limit, the maximum uncertainty of the MFP approach is $3.7 \%$ at 400 GPa and $5.9 \%$ at 1000 GPa.

All together, the accuracy of the present reduction should be higher than $5.5 \%$ (for Cu, Ta, Mo, and Pt) at 400 GPa and $8.3 \%$ (for Cu, Ta, and Mo) at 1000 GPa.

It should be mentioned that in our reductions we have not considered possible phase transitions under static com- pressions. In case the pressure has traversed a point where a first-order phase transition occurs, the present results should be used with caution.

## IV. SUMMARY

In conclusion, we have reported 300 K shock-reduced isotherms for Cu, Ta, Mo, Pt, and Au using the first-principles mean-field potential approach coupled with accurate first-principles LAPW electronic structure calculations.

![](./images/812439438511570944_6.jpg)

The five metals treated are all experimental high-pressure standards. Judging from the excellent agreement between the present 300 K shock-reduced isotherms and the used standards or empirical reductions by other authors, we estimate that the accuracy of the pressure in the present 300 K shock-reduced isotherms should be well below 10% for the five metals up to the pressure where first-order solid–solid phase transitions occur. We expect that the present reduced data can serve as the pressure standard for future static diamond-anvil-cell experiments.
