# Dislocations as a source of micropipe development in the growth of silicon carbide

D. I. Cherednichenko${}^{\text{a)}}$
Department of Electrical Engineering, University of South Carolina, Carolina, South Carolina 29208

Y. I. Khlebnikov
Band Gap Technologies Inc., Columbia, South Carolina 29208

I. I. Khlebnikov, R. V. Drachev, and T. S. Sudarshan
Department of Electrical Engineering, University of South Carolina, Carolina, South Carolina 29208

(Received 15 June 2000; accepted for publication 18 October 2000)

Micropipes are the primary macroscopic defects in silicon carbide single crystals. It is shown that a stable hollow core of dislocation can act as an initial site of micropipe development. The specific strain energy necessary to estimate the thermodynamic stability of a hollow dislocation core is expressed in terms of the macroscopic parameters of the material Young's modulus and of the Gruneisen constant. $\copyright$ 2001 American Institute of Physics. [DOI: 10.1063/1.1332422]

## I. INTRODUCTION

Micropipes are the most stable defects in bulk grown single crystal SiC boules. According to Frank's model, micropipes appear when the energy from a high strained region of the material, around a dislocation line, is released creating an additional cylindrical shaped free surface. $^{1}$ In Ref. 2, the general thermodynamic conditions for the hollow core stability have been formulated, taking into account that the material behavior is different from that predicted by Hooke's law at the core of the dislocation. It was shown that a hollow core can be spontaneously created in the center of a dislocation as soon as the free enthalpy of the dislocation becomes equal to or less than zero

$$
\frac{d G}{d r}=2 \pi \gamma\left(\frac{r}{r_{C}}-\frac{U(r) r}{\gamma}+1\right). \tag{1}
$$

Here, $r_{C}=\Omega \gamma / \Delta \mu$ is the critical radius of a nucleation site on a nonstressed surface, and $\Delta \mu$ is the difference in the thermodynamic potential of a particle of $\mathrm{Si}_{x} \mathrm{C}_{y}$ undergoing transition from vapor into condensed phase; $\Omega=M / N_{A} \rho$ is the particle volume in the solid state; $M$ is molecular weight; $\rho$ is the material density, and $N_{A}$ is Avogadro's number; $\gamma$ $\cong 10^{-16} Y / 4 a_{0}$ is the specific surface energy, $^{3} Y$ is Young's modulus, and $a_{0} \cong(\Omega)^{1 / 3}$ is the lattice constant at equilibrium; $U(r)$ is the specific strain energy of the dislocation core.

To estimate the specific strain energy of the dislocation core $U(r)=(1 / 2 \pi r)[d E(r) / d r]$, the linear energy density of a dislocation was approximated by the function $^{2} E(r)$ $=\mu b^{2} \ln \left[1+\left(r^{2} / r_{h}^{2}\right)\right] / 8 \pi$. In this formula the Hooke's radius $r_{h}=\left(\mu b^{2} / 8 \pi^{2} U_{0}\right)^{1 / 2}$ accounts for the effect of plastic deformation (the deviation from Hooke's law) and also excludes the singularity at the center of dislocation. The value $U_{0}$ constitutes the specific strain energy at the center of dislocation (where $\mu$ is the shear modulus, and $b$ is the Burgers vector).

Most common estimation of the specific strain energy at the dislocation center, $U_{0}$, is assumed not to exceed the latent heat of melting; i.e., $U_{0} \leqslant \Delta H_{M}$. Since silicon carbide does not have molten phase, the value of $U_{0}$ for SiC is uncertain. Therefore, in order to estimate Hooke's radius for SiC it is assumed that $U_{0}(\mathrm{SiC}) \cong 1.5 \Delta \mathrm{H}_{M}(\mathrm{Si})=80 \mathrm{~kJ} / \mathrm{mol}$ [where $\Delta H(\mathrm{Si})$ is the latent heat of the melting of silicon]. $^{4}$

We believe that the specific energy within the dislocation core region, where Hooke's law is not valid, can be represented by the energy necessary to initiate intensive breaking of chemical bonds. In this case, the maximum pressure associated with the atomic bonds $P_{M}$ (molecular pressure) can be used to estimate the specific energy in the dislocation core.

## II. SPECIFIC ENERGY OF THE DISLOCATION CORE

In the case of a perfect lattice structure, the specific energy can be expressed as a function of the molecular pressure, as follows:

$$
U_{0}=P_{M} \Lambda n, \tag{2}
$$

where $\Lambda$ is the lattice volume corresponding to the activation of bond rupture; $n$ is the atom concentration in solid.

An analytical expression for the molecular pressure within the crystal lattice, that determines the equilibrium in a solid, can be derived from the series expansion of the quasi-elastic force of atomic interaction $^{5}$

$$
\Delta F=-\beta_{0} \xi+g_{0} \xi^{2}, \tag{3}
$$

where $\beta_{0}$ is the elastic force constant and $g_{0}$ is the anharmonic constant of atomic oscillation; $\xi\left(a-a_{0}\right)$ represents the amplitude of atom displacement. The first term in Eq. (3) describes the reaction force of atomic bonds due to atomic displacement from the equilibrium position. Since the surface atom density is equal to $1 / a_{0}^{2}$, the total pressure created

by the force $\Delta F_{1}=-\beta_{0} \xi$ is defined as $P_{M}=\xi \beta_{0} / a_{0}^{2}$. The first multiplier $\beta_{0} / a_{0}$ in the earlier formula can be consid ered as Young' s modulus of the material $Y$, and the second multiplier $\xi / a_{0}$ represents the relative lattice deformation. The maximum atom displacement from the equilibrium po- sition prior to plastic deformation of the crystal lattice can be determined from Eq. (3) as

$$
\Delta a=\frac{\beta_{0}}{2 g_{0}}. \quad(4)
$$

Then, inserting $\Delta a$ and $Y$ for the molecular pressure ex pression, we will have

$$
P_{M}=\frac{\beta_{0}}{2 g_{0}} \frac{Y}{a_{0}}. \quad(5)
$$

The second term in Eq. (3), $\Delta F_{2}=g_{0} \xi^{2}$, stands for the additional force caused by the asymmetry of thermal atomic oscillations. This force determines the phonon pressure op- posing the pressure of molecular force and tends to expand the crystal lattice from inside. By determination of the aver- age value of $\overline{\Delta F}_{2}=g_{0} \overline{\xi^{2}}$, it is possible to show that the sur face pressure created by this force will be equal to $P_{f}=\left(g_{0} / a_{0}^{2}\right) \overline{\xi^{2}}$. On the other hand, the average potential en ergy of the oscillating atom is proportional to the tempera- ture of the solid; $\Delta \bar{U}=\beta_{0} \overline{\xi^{2}}=k T / 2$. Combining $\Delta \bar{U}$ and $P_{f}$ for the phonon pressure, we will have

$$
P_{f}=\frac{g_{0}}{2 \beta_{0}} \frac{k T}{a_{o}^{2}}. \quad(6)
$$

For purposes of numerical estimations, the phonon and molecular pressures, have to be expressed in terms of mate- rial macroscopic parameters, which can be obtained based on the relationship between the force parameters of lattice os- cillations $\beta_{0}$ and $g_{0}$ and Gruneisen coefficient $^{6}$

$$
\eta=-\frac{V d \beta}{2 \beta d V}, \quad(7)
$$

where $\beta=\beta_{0}-g_{0} \Delta a$ is the force parameter when an atom is in the displaced position; $d V / V$ is the relative volume defor mation.

Assuming an isotropic deformation of the lattice, $d V / V$ $=3 d a / a$, and taking into account that $\beta$ is a function of $a$, $\beta=\beta(a)$, we have

$$
\eta=\frac{a_{0} g_{0}}{6 \beta} \cong \frac{a_{0} g_{0}}{6 \beta_{0}}. \quad(8)
$$

Substituting this expression into (3) we can find that the maximum atomic displacement $\Delta a \cong a_{0} / 12 \eta$ and the final expressions for the molecular and phonon pressures can also be presented in the form

$$
P_{M}=\frac{Y}{6 \eta} \quad(9)
$$

and

$$
P_{f}=3 \eta k T n. \quad(10)
$$

Taking $\Lambda \cong\left(a_{0}+\Delta a\right)^{3}$ for the specific energy at the site of maximum deformation in the dislocation, we have

$$
U_{0}=P_{M} \cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{12 \eta}\right)^{3}. \quad(11)
$$

### III. THE EQUILIBRIUM RADIUS OF THE HOLLOW CORE

Substituting the specific characteristic parameters of SiC into the derived expressions $(\rho=3.2 \times 10^{3} \mathrm{~kg} / \mathrm{m}^{3}, Y=4.4$ $\times 10^{11} \mathrm{~Pa}, \eta=2$, and $M=41)^{7}$ it is possible to show that $P_{M} \cong 3.7 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{~Pa}$ and $U_{0} \cong 531.4 \mathrm{~kJ} / \mathrm{mol}$. This value is practically equal to the evaporation energy of $\mathrm{SiC}^{8}$ $W \cong 564.1 \mathrm{~kJ} / \mathrm{mol}$. Similar estimations for silicon $(Y=4.0$ $\times 10^{10} \mathrm{~Pa}, \eta=2, \rho=2.3 \times 10^{3} \mathrm{~kg} / \mathrm{m}^{3}$, and $\left.M=28.06\right)^{9}$ yield the value of $P_{M} \cong 3.3 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{~Pa}$ and $U_{0} \cong 46.0 \mathrm{~kJ} / \mathrm{mol}$. It has the same order of magnitude as the latent heat of silicon melting $\Delta \mathrm{H}(\mathrm{Si}) \cong 53.3 \mathrm{~kJ} / \mathrm{mol}$.

To estimate the thermodynamic stability of a hollow core in $6 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{SiC}$ with the specific strain energy $U_{0}$, we took the Burger's vector $b_{0}=15.2 \times 10^{-10} \mathrm{~m}$ and $b=n b_{0}$ where $n \geqslant 4$ for a superdislocation. $^{10}$ The calculated surface energy is $\gamma \cong 3.98 \mathrm{~J} / \mathrm{m}^{2}$ and the shear modulus $\mu=1.9 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{~Pa}$. If the saturation ratio in vapor during the bulk growth process is $\alpha_{0} \cong 1.1$ [for the evaluation of the difference in the ther modynamic potential $\Delta \mu=k T \ln \left(\alpha_{0}\right)$ ], then the estimations show that the screw dislocations with a Burger's vector of $b_{0}=15.2 \AA$ do not have a visible open core. On the other hand, for $b=9 b_{0}$, the equilibrium radius of the hollow core of a dislocation (micropipe) is $\cong 4 \times 10^{-6} \mathrm{~cm}\left(r_{P} \cong 26 b_{0}\right)$. Consequently, dislocations with a Burger's vector of $b_{0}$ $=15.2 \AA$, initially present in the substrate, can propagate into the SiC bulk during the growth process as a single disloca- tion. Thus, it can be stated that only superdislocations can produce hollow pipes. But even for superdislocations, the equilibrium radius of the hollow core is actually much smaller than that for any observed micropipes. $^{11}$ Hence, a thermodynamically stable hollow core of dislocation can be considered only as an initial stage of micropipe formation. Additional phenomena in the growth process would be in- volved in the formation of micropipes, observed in bulk crystals. For example, as it is mentioned in Refs. 2 and 12 the thermal etching and capillary actions can occur inside of a hollow core of dislocation.

### IV. CONCLUSIONS

The specific strain energy of the dislocation core in SiC can be expressed in terms of the macroscopic parameters of the material such as Young's modulus and the Gruneisen constant. The evaluated magnitude of the specific strain en- ergy of the dislocation core in SiC is practically equal to the evaporation energy of the SiC crystal. The thermodynami- cally stable hollow core of dislocation in the SiC crystal can be considered only as an initial stage of micropipe formation. Additional phenomena in the growth process, such as the thermal etching and capillary actions inside of a hollow dislocation core would be involved in the formation of micropipes.
