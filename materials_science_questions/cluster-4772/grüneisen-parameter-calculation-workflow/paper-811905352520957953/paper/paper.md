## Abstract
Numerous theoretical and experimental investigations are being carried out on Cu-based alloys due to their technologically important shape memory properties and pseudo-elasticity, which are intimately associated with the martensitic transformation. The austenite phase in Cu-based shape memory alloys is known for its high elastic anisotropy. The complete set of second-order elastic constants (SOECs) and third-order elastic constants (TOECs) of Cu-Al-Ni in the $\beta$ phase has been obtained. A combination of the deformation theory and finite strain elasticity theory has been employed to get the expressions for the strain energy density of the lattice. The theory is based on the Keating's approach by taking into account the two-body as well as three-body interactions up to second nearest neighbors in the Cu-Al-Ni lattice. The elastic constants are compared with the reported experimental values. The relevant role of the comparatively large TOECs in describing the instability of the bcc structure towards the martensitic transformation is also discussed. The aggregate elastic properties, such as the adiabatic bulk modulus, shear modulus, Cauchy pressure and anisotropy factor, are calculated. The mode Gruneisen parameters of the acoustic waves are determined based on the quasi-harmonic approximation method. The low-temperature limit of the lattice thermal expansion and the Anderson-Gruneisen parameter of Cu-Al-Ni are also obtained.

## 1. Introduction
The fast-changing scenario of technological advance in high-pressure research is mainly supported by the development of new materials. Shape memory alloys (SMAs), which form an important part in the category of 'smart materials', are materials that exhibit unique properties, namely pseudo-elasticity and shape memory effect (Delaey 1991, Otsuka and Wayman 1998). These unusual properties are being employed to a wide variety of applications in a number of different fields (Wang *et al* 2004, Planes and Manosa 2001). The transition between the two phases, martensite to austenite, is of continuing interest (Humbeeck and Kustov 2005). It is a first-order phase transition, diffusionless, which can be basically described by the shear mechanism (Delaey 1991). The shape memory effect, along with the mechanical properties, is currently being implemented in the fabrication of space shuttles, thermostats, aerodynamics, solar panels and cryogenic valves. The superelastic properties, along with biocompatibility, are used in surgical tools and orthodontic arches by the medical industry. Much smaller electrical connectors, antennae and electrical switches are being manufactured in the computer industry. These alloys are still expensive to manufacture and have poor fatigue properties (Wang *et al* 2004).

Cu-Al-Ni is an efficient SMA because of its possible economic use at comparatively high temperatures ranging up to 473 K (Delaey 1991). The lattice dynamics of Cu-based SMA systems has been analyzed by means of ultrasonic and

The austenite phase of Cu-Al-Ni has a bcc structure with an $Fm3m$ space group (Pérez-Landazábal *et al* 2002). The total number of atoms per cell is 16 and each atom has eight first-nearest neighbors and six second-nearest neighbors. Hydrostatic strain is accompanied by elastic infinitesimal strains. The hydrostatic strain affects the response of the medium to the infinitesimal additional strain, giving rise to the effect of pressure on elasticity. Such a strain is of fundamental interest in many problems concerning the development of smart materials.

The concurrent knowledge of the second-order elastic constants (SOECs) and third-order elastic constants (TOECs) enables a better understanding of the nonlinear elasticity exhibited by these alloys. The purpose of the present paper is to obtain the elastic anisotropy and related properties of Cu-Al-Ni. In this paper, we determine the second-order and third-order potential parameters, the pressure derivatives of second-order elastic constants and the complete set of second-order and third-order elastic constants of Cu-Al-Ni. We have taken into account two-body interactions up to second neighbors and three-body interactions of the Keating type (Keating 1966). The bcc system is found to have three independent SOECs and six non-vanishing TOECs (Bhagavantam 1966). The values obtained are compared and are in agreement with the experimental results. We have obtained the mode Gruneisen parameters (GPs) of Cu-Al-Ni for the acoustic waves propagating in different orientations in the crystal lattice along with the Anderson-Gruneisen parameter.

### 2. Second-order and third-order elastic constants

We have used a model based on deformation theory and Keating's potential scheme to obtain the SOECs and TOECs of Cu-Al-Ni. Elastic constants of all orders can be obtained as algebraic functions of potential parameters. Higher-order elastic constants will depend on higher-order coordinate derivatives of the potential. The terms in the potential energy equation involving the displacements to the third order have to be considered in order to calculate the TOECs of the lattice. The second-order coupling parameter is the second derivative of the potential energy with respect to the atomic displacements while the third-order coupling parameter is the third derivative of the potential energy with respect to the displacements. Considering Keating's approach (Keating 1966), the potential energy of the Cu-Al-Ni crystal is written as the sum of two-body, three-body, etc interactions.

$$
\phi=\phi^{(2)}+\phi^{(3)}+\phi^{(4)}+\cdots. \tag{1}
$$

Let $\vec{R}_{i}(L\mu, L'\mu')$ be the vector distance between the atom $\mu$ in the cell $L$ and the atom $\mu'$ in the cell $L'$ in the unstrained state and $\vec{R}_{i}'(L\mu, L'\mu')$ be the corresponding vector component in the deformed state. Then the two-body potential between them in the deformed state is written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi^{\prime(2)} &=\phi^{(2)}+\frac{1}{2} \sum_{L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}}\left\{\frac{1}{2} \alpha\left[\vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right)\right.\right. \\
&\left.-\vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right)\right]^{2} \\
&+\frac{1}{6} \beta\left[\vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right)\right. \\
&\left.\left.-\vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right)\right]^{3}\right\}.
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$

Here $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the two-body potential parameters. Similarly the three-body potential between the triplet of atoms $\mu$ in the cell $L$, $\mu'$ in the cell $L'$ and $\mu''$ in the cell $L''$ in the strained state is written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi^{\prime(3)} &=\phi^{(3)} \\
&+\frac{1}{2} \sum_{L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}}\left\{\frac{1}{2} \lambda\left[\vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right)\right.\right. \\
&\left.-\vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right)\right]^{2} \\
&+\frac{1}{6} \zeta\left[\vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right)\right. \\
&\left.\left.-\vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right)\right]^{3}\right\}
\end{aligned} \tag{3}
$$

where $\lambda$ and $\zeta$ are the three-body potential parameters. In a homogeneous deformation (Born and Huang 1962) the components of the interatomic vectors are altered as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{R}_{i}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) &=\vec{R}_{i}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right)+\sum_{j} \varepsilon_{i j} \vec{R}_{j}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \\
&+W_{i}\left(1-\delta_{\mu \mu^{\prime}}\right)
\end{aligned} \tag{4}
$$

where $W_{i}$ are the components of the internal displacements of the sublattices, which are found to vanish. $\varepsilon_{i j}$ are deformation parameters related to the macroscopic Lagrangian strains $\eta_{i j}$ by

$$
\eta_{i j}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\varepsilon_{i j}+\varepsilon_{j i}+\sum_{k} \varepsilon_{i k} \varepsilon_{j k}\right\}. \tag{5}
$$

Therefore, the scalar product in (2) and (3) can be written using (4) and (5) as

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}^{\prime}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right) \\
&=\vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot \vec{R}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right) \\
&+2 \sum_{a b} \eta_{i j} R_{i}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime} \mu^{\prime}\right) \cdot R_{j}\left(L \mu, L^{\prime \prime} \mu^{\prime \prime}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{6}
$$

Substituting for the change in scalar product in (2) and (3) from (6) and summing all the contributions to the potential energy, we obtain the strain energy per unit volume of Cu-Al-Ni as

$$
\Delta \phi=\left(\phi^{\prime}-\phi\right) / V_{z} \tag{7}
$$

where $V_{z}$ is the volume of the unit cell.

In the present work two-body interactions up to the second-nearest neighbors and three-body interactions between the nearest neighbors of each atom in the unit cell are considered to obtain the strain energy density of Cu-Al-Ni. Also, fourth and higher powers of the interatomic displacements are neglected.

Comparing $\Delta \phi$ thus obtained with the strain energy from the continuum model approximation (Murnaghan 1951)

$$
U=\frac{1}{2!} \sum_{i j k l} C_{i j, k l} \eta_{i j} \eta_{k l}+\frac{1}{3!} \sum_{i j k l m n} C_{i j, k l, m n} \eta_{i j} \eta_{k l} \eta_{m n}+\cdots
\tag{8}
$$

(where $C_{i j, k l}$ and $C_{i j, k l, m n}$ are the second-order and third-order elastic constant tensors evaluated at constant entropy) we get the expressions for the second-order and third-order elastic constants of Cu-Al-Ni system in Voigt's notation as

$$
C_{11}=(4 \alpha+3 \lambda+8 \sigma)\left(a^{4} / V_{z}\right) \tag{9a}
$$

$$
C_{12}=(4 \alpha-\lambda)\left(a^{4} / V_{z}\right) \tag{9b}
$$

$$C_{44}=(4\alpha+\lambda)(a^{4}/V_{z}) \tag{9c}$$

$$C_{111}=(\beta+\zeta+16v)(4a^{6}/V_{z}) \tag{9d}$$

$$C_{112}=(\beta+\zeta)(4a^{6}/V_{z}) \tag{9e}$$

$$C_{123}=(\beta-3\zeta)(4a^{6}/V_{z}) \tag{9f}$$

$$C_{144}=(\beta-\zeta)(4a^{6}/V_{z}) \tag{9g}$$

$$C_{155}=(\beta+\zeta)(4a^{6}/V_{z}) \tag{9h}$$

$$C_{456}=\beta(4a^{6}/V_{z}) \tag{9i}$$

where $a$ is the lattice parameter of the unit cell. $C_{ij}$ and $C_{ijk}$ are SOECs and TOECs of Cu-Al-Ni represented by Voigt's notation. Here $\sigma$ and $v$ are the second-order and third-order potential parameters of two-body interactions in connection with the second-nearest neighbour respectively. In the above equations, the numerical prefactors are entirely derived from the crystal structure. The values of the potential parameters are estimated by best fitting with the reported experimental value of elastic constants.

### 3. Pressure derivatives of the second-order elastic constants

We have used finite strain theory (Murnaghan 1951) to obtain the effective second-order elastic constants of a strained cubic crystal in terms of the second-order and third-order elastic constants. Let a cubic crystal be subjected to a hydrostatic pressure $p$. The coordinates of the material particles in the initial state change after applying the pressure. The Jacobian of the transformation is
$$\begin{vmatrix}
1-\kappa & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1-\kappa & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1-\kappa
\end{vmatrix} \tag{10}$$
where every line element is reduced in length by a factor $(1-\kappa)$. In terms of the Lagrangean strain component $\eta$
$$(1-\kappa)^{2}=1+2\eta. \tag{11}$$

The stress tensor in the final state is given by
$$\tau_{ij}=\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}\sum_{p,q=1}^{3}\left[\frac{\partial x_{i}}{\partial a_{p}}\right]\left[\frac{\delta\varphi}{\delta\eta_{pq}}\right]\left[\frac{\delta x_{i}}{\delta a_{q}}\right] \tag{12}$$
where $\rho_{0}$ is the density of the unstrained state and $\rho$ is that of the deformed state, respectively. $x_{i}$ are the coordinates of the atoms in a homogeneously deformed state and $a_{i}$ are the position coordinates of the atoms in the unstrained state of the cubic crystal. Comparing this with the expression
$$\tau_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\sum_{kl}C_{ijkl}'\beta_{kl} \tag{13}$$
where $\beta_{kl}$ are the infinitesimal strain parameters, which arise out of the superimposition of an infinitesimal strain on the deformed state, the expression for the pressure derivatives of second-order elastic constants Cu-Al-Ni are obtained to the first order in $\eta$ as
$$\frac{\partial C_{11}}{\partial p}=\frac{-1}{C_{11}+2C_{12}}[C_{111}+2C_{112}+2C_{11}+2C_{12}] \tag{14a}$$

$$\frac{\partial C_{12}}{\partial p}=\frac{-1}{C_{11}+2C_{12}}[C_{123}+2C_{112}-C_{11}-C_{12}] \tag{14b}$$

$$\frac{\partial C_{44}}{\partial p}=\frac{-1}{C_{11}+2C_{12}}[C_{144}+2C_{155}+C_{11}+2C_{12}+C_{44}]. \tag{14c}$$

The pressure derivatives $\frac{\partial C_{ij}}{\partial p}$ are obtained by substituting the values in (14). For an isotropic substance, the elasticity tensor scheme yields $C_{11}-C_{12}=2C_{44}$. We have calculated $A$, the anisotropy factor for Cu-Al-Ni, along with the shear modulus $C'[=(C_{11}-C_{12})/2]$ and the Cauchy pressure $P(=C_{12}-C_{44})$.

### 4. Gruneisen and Anderson-Gruneisen parameters

At higher temperatures, the vibrations become more and more vigorous but the mean position of the atom remains unchanged. Thus, in a harmonic solid, there can be no thermal expansion. Therefore, the Gruneisen gamma $(\gamma)$ is zero for a harmonic solid. Hence, deviations of $\gamma$ from zero can be interpreted as a direct measure of anharmonicity. The thermal expansion of a solid, therefore, is a property arising strictly due to the anharmonicity of the lattice. A simple method of taking the anharmonicity of the lattice vibrations into account is the quasi-harmonic approximation. Here, the vibrations are assumed to be harmonic even in a strained lattice but the interatomic forces and hence the lattice frequencies are assumed to be functions of the strain components in the lattice.
The Gruneisen gamma of a crystal is defined as
$$\bar{\gamma}(T)=\frac{\beta V}{\chi C_{v}}=\frac{\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}C_{v_{i}}}{\sum_{i}C_{v_{i}}} \tag{15}$$
where $\beta$ is the volume coefficient of expansion, $V$ the molar volume, $\chi$ the isothermal compressibility and $C_{v}$ the specific heat at constant volume. The right-hand side of (15) gives the microscopic significance of $\bar{\gamma}(T)$ in the quasi-harmonic approximation. The thermal energy of a simple lattice is treated as the energy of a spectrum of elastic waves, having any temperature at a certain maximum frequency of vibration. The Gruneisen's ratio thus depends upon the volume and the frequency $\omega$. The GPs $\gamma_{j}(\theta,\phi)$ for the $j$th acoustic mode propagating in the direction $(\theta,\phi)$ can be defined as
$$\gamma_{j}(\theta,\phi)=-\frac{1}{v_{j}(\theta,\phi)}\frac{\partial v_{j}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\varepsilon} \tag{16}$$
where $v_{j}(\theta,\phi)$ is the natural velocity of the $j$th acoustic mode propagating in the direction $(\theta,\phi)$, when the lattice is homogeneously strained by a uniform longitudinal strain $\varepsilon$ (Jayachandran and Menon 2002). The evaluated second-order and third-order elastic constants of Cu-Al-Ni are used to obtain the Gruneisen functions $\gamma_{j}(\theta,\phi)$ of the acoustic modes using (16). At very low temperatures only acoustic modes of long wavelength are predominant. In this $T^{3}$ region, one can apply the Debye theory, and the number of normal modes excited in the $j$th acoustic branch is then proportional to $v_{j}^{-3}(\theta,\phi)$. ## 5. Results and discussion

Higher-order elastic constants have basic cohesive properties related inextricably to the cohesive energy and volume. Knowledge of the complete set of SOECs and TOECs of a particular solid enables a correlation to be made between a number of physical properties generally classified as anharmonic. In this work we have calculated the second-order and third-order potential parameters of Cu-Al-Ni with composition Cu-14.3% Al-4.1% Ni (wt%) at room temperature; these are given in table 1. The values of the second-order and third-order potential parameters are estimated by best fitting with the experimental values of second-order elastic constants reported by Sedlak *et al* (2005) and third-order elastic constants reported by Landa *et al* (2004) respectively. It is to be noted that $\alpha$, $\sigma$, $\beta$ and $\nu$ are related to the stretching of bonds, while $\lambda$ and $\zeta$ are linked with the distortion of the angle between two adjacent bonds in the lattice.

The SOECs and related properties of Cu-Al-Ni are collected in table 2. The elastic anisotropy factor $A$ is found to be large, and comparable to the reported values. It is to be noted that the alloy composition used in all experiments other than that by Landa *et al* (2004) are slightly different. The calculated values of $C_{L}$ $(=(C_{11}+C_{12}+2 C_{44})/2)$, bulk modulus $K$ and shear modulus $C'$ $(=(C_{11}-C_{12})/2)$ are also presented in table 2. The Cauchy pressure $P$ $(=C_{12}-C_{44})$ is also determined, which can be related to the brittle or ductile properties of materials as suggested by Chen *et al* (2003). It is found to be positive, which is typical for ductile materials.

The complete set of six non-vanishing TOECs are presented in table 3. The TOEC $C_{144}$ representing the anisotropy of the shear mode has a smaller value than $C_{111}$. Hence, the effect of pressure is much greater on the longitudinal wave velocity than on the shear wave velocity in Cu-Al-Ni, which is consistent with the literature data. It is remarkable that, like Cu-Al-Zn, all the third-order elastic constants of this alloy are also negative, indicating an increase of the vibrational frequencies under stress, giving rise to an increase in the strain-free energy. The absolute values of the TOECs are very large. This means that the bcc phase observed is considerably anharmonic. Such characteristics have also been observed in the bcc phases of Cu-based shape memory alloys. Consequently we can presume that the anharmonic effect will play an important role in the martensitic transformation in the $\beta$-phase alloys (Nagasawa *et al* 1982, Planes and Manosa 2001).
