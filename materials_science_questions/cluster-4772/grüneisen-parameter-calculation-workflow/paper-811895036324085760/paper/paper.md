# A DISCUSSION OF THE THERMAL EXPANSION AND CONDUCTIVITY IN LIQUID HELIUM II

By H. JONES, PH.D., Trinity College

[Received 5 February, read 7 March 1938]

Some years ago Simon (1) remarked that liquid helium in the neighbourhood of zero temperature should have, in agreement with Nernst's theorem, some kind of ordered structure. Later calculations by F. London (2) indicate that the structure is one of low co-ordination number, and he found the best agreement with the observed energy and volume by assuming that the lattice is of the diamond type (co-ordination number 4). Following this, Fröhlich (3) has emphasized the analogy between the specific heat of He II and that of $\beta$ brass in the neighbourhood of the critical temperature of the latter, from which he concludes, tentatively, that in the ordered state He II has a diamond lattice, and that disorder arises by atoms jumping into the vacant points of the underlying body-centred cubic lattice. (A body-centred cubic lattice may be regarded as composed of two inter-penetrating diamond lattices.) It is the first purpose of this note to show that the negative coefficient of thermal expansion of He II can readily be understood in terms of this disordering process. A similar situation arises in ferromagnetics at temperatures in the neighbourhood of the Curie point, where the anomalous thermal expansion has been shown to be a consequence of the variation of the integral representing the exchange energy with volume*. This integral plays the part of an ordering energy in what is a typical co-operative phenomenon.

In ordinary monatomic substances, for example metals, the thermal expansion is due to the change in atomic vibration frequencies with change of volume. Grüneisen (4) obtained the following well-known relation:

$$
-\frac{d \log \Theta}{d \log v}=\frac{\alpha v}{\chi C_{v}}=\gamma, \tag{1}
$$

where $\Theta$ is the Debye characteristic temperature, $v$ the specific volume, $\alpha$ the coefficient of thermal expansion, $\chi$ the compressibility, and $C_{v}$ the specific heat at constant volume. This formula depends on the assumption that the entropy $S$, which in general depends on two independent variables, say $T$ and $v$, can be expressed as a function of the single variable $T/\Theta$. According to a very general formula, given by Einstein, the variation of the vibration frequency $\nu$ with volume is given by

$$
\nu \propto \chi^{-\frac{1}{3}} \rho^{-\frac{1}{3}}, \tag{2}
$$

where $\rho$ is the density. From this we deduce

$$
\gamma=\frac{1}{2} \frac{d \log \chi}{d \log v}-\frac{1}{6}. \tag{3}
$$

The density of He II has been measured as a function of pressure up to pressures of 30 atmospheres, from which the compressibility and the variation of compressibility with volume can be deduced. The value of $\gamma$ from equation (3) can accordingly be determined and it is found to be positive. Since $\alpha$ is found experimentally to be negative we conclude that equation (1) is not applicable, i.e. the entropy cannot be expressed as a function of $T / \Theta$, where $\Theta$ represents a vibration frequency. In other words, the thermal expansion of He II is due to quite a different mechanism from that which governs thermal expansion in other monatomic substances.

In the theory of the order-disorder transformation in alloys the entropy, arising from the disordering process alone, can be expressed as a function of a single variable. Thus $S=S(T / V_{0})$, where $V_{0}$ represents the ordering energy. The behaviour of the specific heat of He II at different temperatures suggests that the thermal energy is mainly energy of disorder. As a first approximation we assume that the entropy may be expressed as $S(T / V_{0})$ and determine the variation of the ordering energy with volume from the observed thermal expansion data. This could be done by using Grüneisen's formula, merely replacing $\Theta$ by $V_{0}$. It is more convenient, however, to recast formula (1). We have

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial v}\right)_{T}=-\frac{T}{V_{0}} \frac{d V_{0}}{d v}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{v}. \tag{4}
$$

Using the thermodynamic relations

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial v}\right)_{T}=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_{v} \tag{5}
$$

and
$$
C_{v}=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_{v},
$$

we obtain
$$
\frac{d \log V_{0}}{d \log v}=-\frac{v}{C_{v}}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_{v}. \tag{6}
$$

We use the notation $\beta=d \log V_{0} / d \log v$. Equation (6) is, of course, equivalent to the corresponding Grüneisen formula $\beta=-\alpha v /(\chi C_{v})$.

The value of $\beta$ can be obtained from independent measurements, namely the change of the critical temperature with volume along the $\lambda \lambda^{\prime}$ line (7). Along the $\lambda \lambda^{\prime}$ line the curves of the entropy against temperature for given volumes have a discontinuous change of slope (8). Regarding the entropy as a function of the single variable $T / V_{0}$, we see that along the $\lambda \lambda^{\prime}$ line we should have the relation

$$
\frac{T_{c}}{V_{0}\left(v_{c}\right)}=\mathrm{constant},
$$

where $T_{c}$ and $v_{c}$ are the critical temperatures and volumes respectively. In the theories of disorder so far considered this constant ratio depends only on the co-ordination number of the lattice (9). Thus we have

$$
\frac{d \log T_{c}}{d \log v_{c}}=\left(\frac{d \log V_{0}}{d \log v}\right)_{\lambda}=\beta. \tag{7}
$$

The data from which these values were deduced are taken from the work of W. H. Keesom and A. P. Keesom (5). The value of $\beta$ for $\rho=0 \cdot 1684$ is $1 \cdot 35$. This is rather higher than the value deduced from the specific heats and the thermal expansion for temperatures well below the $\lambda$ point. It has, however, the same sign and is near enough in magnitude to show that the thermal expansion is mainly determined by the disordering process. The lack of complete agreement means that the thermal energy of He II is not wholly potential energy of disorder but is also partly energy of vibration. A more precise form for the entropy would be, therefore,

$$
S=S_{1}\left(\frac{T}{V_{0}}\right)+S_{2}\left(\frac{T}{\Theta}\right), \tag{8}
$$

where $S_2$ is always much smaller than $S_1$. With this form for the entropy, equation (6) no longer holds. However, still regarding $\beta$ as defined by equation (7), i.e. determined by the critical temperatures and volumes, and defining a quantity $\gamma$ by the equation
$$
\gamma=-\frac{d \log \Theta}{d \log v}, \tag{9}
$$
where $\gamma$ is then positive, we obtain
$$
\beta+\frac{v}{C_{v}}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_{v}=(\beta+\gamma) \frac{C_{v}^{(l)}}{C_{v}}, \tag{10}
$$
where $C_{v}^{(l)}$ is the contribution to the specific heat arising from the entropy term $S_2$, and $C_v$ is the total specific heat at constant volume.

To find a numerical value of $\gamma$ we can use equation (2), which gives the dependence of $\Theta$ on $\rho$ and $\chi$. We then obtain, by (3),
$$
\gamma=\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{2} \rho \frac{d^{2} \rho}{d p^{2}} /\left(\frac{d \rho}{d p}\right)^{2}. \tag{11}
$$

The data given by W. H. Keesom and A. P. Keesom (5) for the density of He II at $1.25^\circ$ K. under different pressures may be quite accurately expressed by the following formula for pressures up to 25 atmospheres:
$$
10^{4} \rho=1442+20 \cdot 865 p-0 \cdot 8873 p^{2}+0 \cdot 02208 p^{3}, \tag{12}
$$
where $p$ is in atmospheres. The derivatives of $\rho$ with respect to $p$ would not, of course, be accurately given for high pressures.

From equation (12) we then obtain $\gamma=0.63$ at $p=0$. $\gamma$ varies with the density, but it is not possible with the data available to find its value for $\rho=0 \cdot 1684$, since at these high pressures equation (12) would not give $d^2\rho/dp^2$ accurately.

According to the figures we have quoted, the left-hand side of equation (10) is equal to $0 \cdot 41$, and $\beta=1 \cdot 35$. Thus if we take $\gamma=0 \cdot 63$ (the value for zero pressure) we obtain $C_{v}^{(l)}/C_{v}=0 \cdot 2$. At the critical temperature, for the density considered, the specific heat jumps from 1.95 calories per gram per degree to 0.4 calorie per gram per degree, the ratio being again approximately 0.2. We conclude, therefore, from the thermal expansion, as well as directly from the specific heats, that in He II most of the thermal energy is energy of disorder. This is a situation peculiar to He II.

We have seen that the ordering energy $V_0$ decreases as the volume decreases. The significance of this is made clearer by the following considerations. The energy of disorder at the critical point relative to the ordered state is given by
$$
\int_{0}^{T_{c}} T d S=V_{0} \int_{0}^{\zeta_{c}} \zeta \frac{d S}{d \zeta} d \zeta, \tag{13}
$$

where $\zeta = T/V_0$, and $\zeta_c$ is a constant independent of both $T$ and $v$. ## THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY
Since the thermal energy of HeII is mainly energy of disorder we are led to consider the process of heat conduction as a process of flow of disorder*. It has been suggested by Fröhlich that the ordered state has a diamond lattice, while the disorder occurs by atoms jumping to the vacant points of the underlying body-centred cubic lattice. If this body-centred cubic structure is supposed to be rigid, the disordered energy curve would lie very much higher than the observed one shown in (b), Fig. 1. We must suppose, therefore, that there is no under- lying lattice but that the disordered state consists of regions of higher co- ordination number. For instance an atom may go to a "hole" in the diamond lattice, the surrounding atoms expanding, while the atoms in the neighbourhood of the point from which this atom came contract, so that the densities of the two regions remain approximately the same.

Any form of conduction consists of the balancing of two distinct processes: (a) a flow of energy set up and maintained by a temperature gradient and (b) a relaxation process by which a substance whose energy is distributed in a way different from that of thermal equilibrium returns to the equilibrium condition. The conductivity is determined by equating the rate at which equilibrium is disturbed by the temperature gradient to the rate at which it is restored by the relaxation process.

We have then first to consider a flow of disorder, i.e. of atoms which lie in the "holes" of the diamond lattice. The density of such atoms, which, following the usual custom, we call wrong atoms, decreases in the direction of flow; the flow itself is, of course, constant. Secondly, we have to consider the process by which a region where there is an excess of wrong atoms flowing from right to left and a deficiency flowing from left to right relaxes to a state of the same energy, i.e. with the same total number of wrong atoms but with no resultant flow. This cannot happen merely by collisions between the wrong atoms since momentum would be conserved; it can only occur by a wrong atom disappearing and a resulting thermal vibration being set up, and by the reverse process of absorption of a lattice wave and the production of a wrong atom. which thermal equilibrium could be restored would be by collisions of wrong atoms with the walls of the tube in which the helium is contained and down which the heat flows.

We can easily give a formal theory of the conductivity. Let $v$ be the mean velocity with which the wrong atoms move amongst their possible sites, and let $n_{\theta} d \omega / 4 \pi$ be the number of wrong atoms per $\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ whose velocities lie in a direction making an angle $\theta$ with the direction of flow, which we take as the direction of the $x$-axis, and in the solid angle $d \omega$. In equilibrium $n_{\theta}$ is independent of $\theta$ and is equal to $\bar{n}$, where $\bar{n}=\frac{1}{2}(1-s) \Omega, s$ is the degree of order, as defined for example by Bragg and Williams (10), and $\Omega$ is the atomic volume. We have then

$$
\frac{d n_{\theta}}{d t}=\frac{\partial n_{\theta}}{\partial T} \frac{d T}{d x} v \cos \theta. \tag{14}
$$

A time of relaxation $\tau$ may be defined by the equation
$$
n_{\theta}-\bar{n}=\text { constant } e^{-t / \tau},
$$
i.e. by
$$
\frac{d n_{\theta}}{d t}=-\frac{n_{\theta}-\bar{n}}{\tau}. \tag{15}
$$

Thus we find, by equating (14) and (15),
$$
n_{\theta}=\bar{n}-\tau \frac{\partial n_{\theta}}{\partial T} \frac{d T}{d x} v \cos \theta. \tag{16}
$$

Since deviations of $n_{\theta}$ from $\bar{n}$ are small, we replace $n_{\theta}$ by $\bar{n}$ in the second term of (16) and obtain for the flow of wrong atoms per second

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{4 \pi} \int n_{\theta} v \cos \theta d \omega & =\frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \tau \frac{\partial \bar{n}}{\partial T} \frac{d T}{d x} v^{2} \cos ^{2} \theta \sin \theta d \theta \\
& =\frac{1}{3} v^{2} \tau \frac{\partial \bar{n}}{d T} \frac{\partial T}{d x}. \tag{17}
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the flow of energy we multiply by $d E / d \bar{n}$, where $E$ is the energy of disorder per unit volume, and so obtain for the conductivity $\kappa$ Debye's formula
$$
\kappa=\frac{1}{3} v^{2} \tau C_{v}, \tag{18}
$$
where $C_{v}$ is the specific heat at constant volume. This is quite a general formula for heat conduction. The process we have considered is not, however, at all similar to heat conduction in a gas. It is more like heat conduction by lattice waves, but with the important difference that, while the "wave vector" is in general not conserved (11) in the mutual scattering of lattice waves (to which fact alone the finite heat conductivity is due), the momentum of wrong atoms is con- served during collisions.

We can make no attempt here to calculate $v$ or $\tau$, but there are some interesting points to be noticed in connexion with them. With regard to $v$ we may remark

that the zero-point energy of 30 calories per gram atom of He II corresponds to a velocity of a helium atom of $2 \cdot 5 \cdot 10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}$. per sec., while the velocity corresponding to the thermal energy at $1^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$. is $0 \cdot 6 \cdot 10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}$. per sec. Since a diamond lattice is a very open structure, the potential hill which a wrong atom has to surmount in passing from one "wrong" site to the next is small. Thus we may anticipate that $v$ in equation (18) is of the order of magnitude of $10^{4} \mathrm{~cm}$. per sec., and that it is not very sensitive to changes of temperature. Concerning $\tau$ there are two points to be mentioned. First, in heat conduction by lattice waves the corresponding $\tau$ increases as the temperature decreases. This is because the relaxation process is due to the mutual scattering of elastic waves, and the fewer the waves the slower is the relaxation. For low temperatures $\tau$ increases very rapidly as the temperature falls, because for long waves the wave vector is conserved in the scattering process (11). For heat conduction by a flow of disorder the mutual scattering of wrong atoms plays no part because momentum is always conserved. We should not therefore expect $\tau$ to vary with temperature in the same way as it does for conduction by lattice waves. The second point concerns the magnitude of $\tau$, i.e. the probability of interchange between energy of disorder and energy of vibration. He II exists up to a temperature of $2 \cdot 19^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$., while its characteristic temperature is greater than $14^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$. This can be seen from the fact that at $2^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$. the specific heat of the vibrations is certainly less than $0.4 \mathrm{cal}$. per gram per degree, from which it follows, by using Debye's formula for the specific heat at low temperatures, that $\Theta>14$. Now experiments with ordering processes in alloys show that the rate of ordering or disordering becomes exceedingly small for temperatures well below the characteristic temperature. The reason evidently is that only vibrations of short wave length have an appreciable chance of producing an atomic interchange. We may expect therefore that the rate of interchange between disorder and lattice waves is exceptionally small in He II, and therefore that $\tau$ is large.

The hypothesis that the heat conduction in He II is by a flow of disorder appears therefore not to be incompatible with the high value of the thermal conductivity and points to a variation of conductivity with temperature running approximately parallel to the variation of the specific heat with temperature. That is, it should show a very sharp increase with temperature up to the $\lambda$ point.

## SUMMARY

The negative coefficient of thermal expansion of He II is shown to be a consequence of the disordering process which takes place as the temperature rises. The conclusion is reached that the thermal energy of He II is mainly energy of disorder; the vibrational energy of the sound waves amounts only to a few per cent of the whole energy. A theory of heat conduction considered as a flow of disorder is put forward and is shown to be not incompatible with the experimental data at present available.
