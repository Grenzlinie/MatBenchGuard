Letter to the Editor

# PHONON VISCOSITY AND THE HYPERSONIC ATTENUATION OF VITREOUS SILICA AT HIGH TEMPERATURES

R. NAVA

Centro de Fisica, Instituto Venezolano de Investigaciones Cientificas (IVIC),
Apartado 1827, Caracas 1010A, Venezuela

Received 13 July 1984
Revised manuscript received 10 June 1985

Calculations based on the ultrasonic Grüneisen parameter for an anisotropic continuum show that the high temperature attenuation of hypersonic waves in fused silica in excess of structural relaxational losses cannot be explained by anharmonic interactions with acoustic thermal phonons. Rough qualitative agreement with experiments is found above room temperature by including three-phonon interactions with a band of infra-red optical modes of relaxation time inversely proportional to the temperature.

Numerous studies of the ultrasonic absorption of vitreous silica show that the absorption coefficient is greatly enhanced compared to the crystalline phase [1]. A generally observed feature is a broad absorption peak around liquid nitrogen temperatures attributed to a distribution of structural relaxa- tion of some molecular units of the glass network [2,3]. Experimental results at frequencies up to 1 GHz have been successfully explained by Hunklinger [4] by the use of a Gaussian distribution of activation energies truncated at low temperatures but this procedure fails to account for the large high temperature absorption observed at hypersonic frequencies (greater than 10 GHz) deduced from the measured Brillouin linewidths [5-8]. Experimental studies in Suprasil by Pine [5] at 27.5 GHz, in Homosil by Bucaro and Dardy [6] at 34 GHz and in Puropsil by Vacher et al. [7,8] from 16 to 40 GHz show that the attenuation above the peak temperature, although dependent on the square of the frequency $\Omega$, is nearly independent of temperature in contrast to the decrease with temperature observed at ultrasonic frequencies. Its magnitude is about three times greater than that calculated from distributed structural relaxations [8]. In addition, the calculated maximum is too flat to account for the experimental results. These authors have argued in favor of attributing the excess hypersonic attenuation to anharmonic three-phonon interactions although unreasonably large values of the effective Grüneisen parameter $\bar{\gamma} \simeq 200$ are required to fit some of the data [8].

at high temperatures in fused silica is re-examined by the use of ultrasonic Grüneisen parameters (UGP). It is found that, if the anisotropic elastic continuum model is assumed to hold for fused silica, there is gross disagreement between the calculated UGP and those extracted from the experimental absorption. Finally, it is proposed that an additional absorption mechanism of the hypersonic waves arising from anharmonic interactions with infra-red optical phonons may account for the observed excess attenuation at high temperatures.

It is generally accepted that in dielectric solids when $\Omega \tau<1$ (where $\tau$ is a mean thermal phonon lifetime) an important source of sound absorption is the Akhiezer mechanism whereby the thermal phonon gas extracts energy from the sound wave through anharmonic interactions. The original theory [9] has benefitted from additions and improvements in the course of time [10] which makes it possible to reasonably predict the acoustic loss from the known thermal and elastic properties of the materials. To calculate the Akhiezer contribution to the acoustic losses in fused quartz we make use of a formulation which is able, at least formally, to clarify the origin of the temperature dependence of the absorption [11]. In this formulation the absorption coefficient of a sound wave of polarization $j$ in anharmonic interaction with acoustic thermal phonons is given in $\mathrm{Np} / \mathrm{cm}$ by:

$$
\alpha_{j}=3 K T \Omega^{2} \Gamma_{j}^{2} / \rho c_{j}^{3} s^{2},
$$

where $T$ is the temperature, $\rho$ the mass density, $K$ is the thermal conductivity for temperature gradients along the wave propagation direction $\boldsymbol{k}, c_{j}$ and $s$ are the wave and Debye velocities, respectively, and $\Gamma_{j}^{2}$ is the effective UGP which is an average of the generalized Grüneisen parameters (GP) of the phonon modes $(\boldsymbol{q}, i)$ weighted by their thermal conductivities $K_{i, \boldsymbol{q}}$. In eq. (1) the implicit temperature dependence of $\alpha_{j}$ can be attributed either to the thermal conductivity or/and to the mean anharmonicity. Eq. (1) is appropiate for anharmonic interactions with the acoustic thermal phonons which determine the thermal conductivity but the calculation of $\Gamma_{j}^{2}$ requires that we know each mode's Grüneisen parameter $\gamma_{i j}$ and $K_{i, q}$. This is not possible even for crystalline solids unless one resorts to drastic approximations such as the elastic continuum model with a single phonon relaxation time $\tau$ [11] where the $\gamma_{i j}$ become functions of the second (SOEC) and third (TOEC) order elastic constants [12]. The model is usually adopted when dealing with semi-microscopic (sound wavelength $>$ interatomic distances) elastic properties of glasses at high temperatures in disregard of the fact that it fails to account for the anomalous thermal and elastic glassy properties. We will use the elastic continuum here only to assess, in a very rough way, the contribution from Debye phonons to the mean anharmonicity represented by $\Gamma_{j}^{2}$. In the above approximation one can write [11]:

$$
\Gamma_{j}^{2}=\left\langle\gamma_{i j}^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle\gamma_{i j}\right\rangle\left(\left\langle\gamma_{i j}\right\rangle-\left\langle\gamma_{i j} \beta_{i}^{2} / c_{j}^{2}\right\rangle\right) \delta_{j \mathrm{~L}} .
$$

At high temperatures, assuming that all phonon modes contribute equally to the specific heat, eq. (2) can be evaluated for fused quartz either by the use of Mason’s method [13] for a sound wave propagating along a (100) cubic axis, as a rough approximation for an isotropic solid, or by performing numerical averages in $q$-space over the hemisphere forward to the wave propagation direction of the $\gamma_{ij}(\theta,\ \phi)$ corresponding to one longitudinal and two degenerate transverse phonon modes propagating with polar angles $\theta$ and $\phi$. Table 1 shows the result of these calculations of $\Gamma_{j}^{2}$ together with other relevant thermal and elastic data of quartz. Mason’s scheme overestimates the various averages in eq. (2) for an isotropic solid since only phonon modes propagating along principal cubic axes are included.

The third order elastic constants of fused quartz have been measured by several authors [14–16]. In table 1 we list those TOEC reported in ref. [16] for G.E. Type-151 fused silica and some of those measured for crystal quartz [17]. We note that there are not every large differences in the value of the TOEC of both phases except that all TOEC of this fused silica but $C_{456}$ are positive. It has also been found for Suprasil that $C_{111}$ is nearly temperature independent from room temperature to 3 K [18]. The temperature dependences of the other TOEC are not available at present. Since the experimental thermal expansion of fused silica is constant above room temperature it may be reasonable to assume all TOEC to be temperature independent at high temperatures. Thus, eq. (1) would predict a quasilinear temperature dependence of $\alpha_{j} (j = \text{L, T})$, roughly given by the product $KT$, in contradiction to the experimental results that show a nearly temperature independent hypersonic absorption from room temperature to $1600^\circ\text{C}$ [6,7].

If we assume that the hypersonic absorption in Puropsil at room temperature in excess of the relaxational losses [8] (5500 dB/cm and 3000 dB/cm for 35 GHz longitudinal (L) and 16 GHz transverse (T) waves, respectively) is correctly given by eq. (1) we deduce for the corresponding UGP the values: $\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}=11.3$ and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}=5.4$. Although these values are not as high as those reported previously [8,19], being approximately 5 and 10 times greater than those for crystalline quartz [20], they are considerably larger than the calculated values listed in column (3) of table 1. Fig. 1 is a graphical comparison of the calculated and the experimental absorption at high temperatures and shows that the anharmonicity of the elastic continuum cannot account for the observations. The thermal conductivity used for this graph was taken from ref. [21].

The large discrepancy for fused silica between the value of $\langle\gamma_{i j}\rangle=-2.4$ calculated from the elastic constants and the experimentally determined thermal Grüneisen parameter $\gamma=0.18$ may also be noted from table 1. Such a small value of $\gamma$ has been explained as arising from the alternate positive and negative mode GP of some bands of the infra-red spectrum of fused silica [22]. The high temperature heat capacity can also be reasonably well fitted to 1000 K by the contributions from three optical modes of Einstein temperature 370, 1100 and 1220 K, respectively [23]. This influence of the infra-red modes in the quasi-harmonic and anharmonic properties of fused silica makes it conceivable

that the large excess hypersonic losses observed at high temperatures in Puropsil could be due to anharmonic interaction with optical modes; an absorption mechanism that has been found of importance for crystals with low-lying optical modes [24]. We will thus extend eq. (1) to include optical phonons by assuming for simplicity that hypersonic waves interact with a narrow band of infra-red optical modes. Neglecting intermode coupling the absorption coefficient for $\Omega \tau_{\mathrm{o}} \ll 1$, takes the more convenient form:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{o} j}=C_{\mathrm{o}} T \Omega^{2}\left\langle\gamma_{\mathrm{o} j}^{2}\right\rangle \tau_{\mathrm{o}} / \rho c_{j}^{3}, \quad (3)
$$

where the subscript o identifies the optical phonon band.

There is insufficient data about the anharmonicity and relaxation times of the optical modes of fused silica to attempt a good fitting of eq. (3) to the experimental results. For a very rough estimate of the required value of $\gamma_{\mathrm{o} j}$ at room temperature we may take for $C_{\mathrm{o}}$ the specific heat of the 370 K transverse oxygen mode [23] and assume $\tau_{0} \simeq 10^{-13} \mathrm{~s}$. We find that a value of $\gamma_{\mathrm{oL}}= \pm 4.3$ and of $\gamma_{\mathrm{oT}}= \pm 3.0$ are necessary to account, respectively, for the excess attenuation at 25 GHz and 16 GHz. Furthermore, if one assumes that at high temperatures optical phonons of frequency $\omega_{0} \leq k T / \hbar$ decay by splitting processes [25] then $\tau_{\mathrm{o}} \sim 1 / T$ and the calculated excess absorption coefficient predicted by eq. (3) roughly reproduces the experimental dependence to 1000 K as shown by the lines labelled $\alpha_{\mathrm{oL}}$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{oT}}$ in fig. 1. This agreement and the reasonable values obtained for $\gamma_{\mathrm{o} j}$ lend plausibility to the proposition that the excess hypersonic attenuation at high temperatures may be due to anharmonic interactions with some of the modes of the infra-red spectrum of the glass.

Published experimental evidence shows that the attenuation of hypersonic waves in fused silica at high temperatures is anomalously large compared to the structural relaxational losses which account well for the results at ultra- sonic frequencies. In the anisotropic continuum model anharmonic interactions with acoustic thermal phonons do not reproduce the magnitude or the temper- ature dependence of the excess high temperature hypersonic attenuation. The disagreement being most serious for shear waves. Considering anharmonic interactions of the waves with infra-red optical phonon modes yields a rea- sonable magnitude for the excess attenuation which would be nearly tempera- ture independent above room temperature for an inverse temperature depen- dent phonon relaxation time.
