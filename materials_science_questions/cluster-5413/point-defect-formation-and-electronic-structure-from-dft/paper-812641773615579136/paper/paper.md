# To Promote Ozone Catalytic Decomposition by Fabricating Manganese Vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ Catalyst via Selective Dissolution of Mn-Li Precursors

## GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

![](./images/812641773615579136_2.jpg)

## HIGHLIGHTS

- A series of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ catalysts with Mn vacancies and appropriate amounts of Li⁺ were prepared.

- Fabricating Mn vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ via selective dissolution of Mn-Li precursors in HCl solution facilitated oxygen vacancy formation.

- Mn vacancies and appropriate amounts of Li⁺ in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ promoted the ozone decomposition.

- A mechanism for ozone decomposition by the synergy of Li⁺, Mn vacancies, and oxygen vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ was proposed.

**ABSTRACT**

$\varepsilon$-MnO₂ catalysts with Mn vacancies and appropriate Li⁺ were designed with the help of density functional theory calculations (DFT) and prepared by a novel selective dissolution strategy. Their physiochemical properties were characterized and catalytic activity for ozone decomposition evaluated. DFT calculations showed that Mn vacancies and appropriate amounts of Li⁺ in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ facilitated the formation of oxygen vacancies, decreased adsorption ability for H₂O and O₂, and maintained high adsorption ability for O₃ on these oxygen vacancies. Characterization results showed that preparing $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ by selective dissolution of Mn-Li precursors with 0.5 M HCl produced more Mn vacancies, and, thus, weaker crystallinity, larger specific surface area, superior reducibility, better oxygen storage capacity, and higher oxygen vacancies. This catalyst exhibited excellent activity and stability for ozone decomposition. Furthermore, a possible mechanism for ozone decomposition by the synergy of Li⁺, Mn vacancies, and oxygen vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ was proposed.

Keywords: Manganese vacancies, lithium, oxygen vacancies, $\varepsilon$-MnO₂, ozone decomposition mechanism

### 1. Introduction

Ground-level ozone accumulation derives from both photochemical reactions between precursor gases, such as NOₓ and VOCs, and the extensive use of electrical appliances in our daily life, including air purifiers, printers, televisions and electronic disinfector, has made ozone one of the main air pollutants in modern society [1-3]. Long-term exposure to high levels of ozone can cause severe cardiopulmonary, respiratory, and cardiovascular diseases [4, 5]. In addition, long-term inhalation of secondary pollutants, derived from ozone reacted with other chemicals, can even result in irreversible damage to an organism's DNA [6]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop highly efficient and environmentally benign approaches for ozone elimination. Catalytic decomposition has been highlighted as one of the most effective technologies for removing ozone with safety, economy, and efficiency [7, 8].

Manganese (Mn) oxides with various polymorphs, such as α-, β-, γ-, and δ-types, are considered to have the most potential as catalysts for ozone decomposition, owing to their various crystal structures, variable Mn valence (2+, 3+, and 4+), low cost, and easy production [9-11]. As oxygen vacancies have been proposed and confirmed as the main active centers for ozone adsorption and decomposition [12-15], great effort has been devoted to increasing oxygen vacancies in Mn-based oxides by tuning their crystal structures [9, 16], precursors [10], and morphologies [8, 13], as well as by adding promoters or dopants [15, 17-20]. Recent studies have found that ε-MnO₂, which possesses

a specific hexagonal symmetry crystal structure, emerges as a prospective catalyst with excellent performance in various heterogeneous reactions because of its tunable and abundant oxygen vacancies [21-25]. In 2018, Yang et al. [23] have found that $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ nanosheets exhibit much higher catalytic activity and excellent durability, compared with commercial Pt/Al₂O₃ and Pd/Al₂O₃ catalysts for ethyl acetate decomposition, which mainly originates from activation of lattice oxygen and the oxygen vacancy enhancement after irradiation treatment. Furthermore, a study reported by Wasalathanthri et al. [24] has shown that the high density of surface oxygen vacancies, generated in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ via a novel mild transformation method, promoted catalyst lattice oxygen mobility and thus provided superior catalytic performance for methane combustion. Apparently, the highly tunable and ample oxygen vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ yield it to have great potential in ozone decomposition, which, however, has not been investigated until now.

On the other hand, many studies have shown that cation vacancies, which have been regarded as a type of important Ruetschi defect, can be adopted to regulate the oxygen vacancy concentration in different polymorphs of Mn oxides [26-30]. Wang et al. [28] have reported notable catalytic property improvements for formaldehyde oxidation, might have been due to the formation of abundant oxygen vacancies, resulting from weakened Mn-O bonds after fabricating more Mn vacancies in MnO₂, with the help of ammonium binoxalate as dopants. A similar oxygen vacancy promotion effect has been observed by fabricating Mn vacancies via acid treatment to replace original Mn cations in the MnO₂ framework [26]. Also, their DFT calculations further confirmed that Mn vacancies existed in the MnO₂ framework, which facilitated the formation of more oxygen vacancies [26, 28]. Nonetheless, several findings have pointed out that excessive Mn vacancies in MnO₂ can

significantly deteriorate crystalline structure, which results in crystal structure collapse and exerts a negative effect on catalytic properties [26, 31]. Although theoretical calculations conducted by Balachandran et al. [30] have made it clear that the special structure of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ makes it possible to stabilize more Mn vacancies via a charge compensation effect, crystal structure collapse caused by Mn vacancies formation, which is irreversible, requires further improvement. Rong et al. have found that preparing Mn vacancy-rich MnO₂, by introducing additional potassium (K⁺), significantly alleviates the degree of crystal structure collapse and thus maximizes the catalyst oxygen vacancy content. This is mainly because the introduced K⁺, located near the Mn vacancies, compensates for the charge imbalance [27]. In addition to the large effects on catalyst crystal structure, alkali metal introduction is also an effective means for improving catalytic performance [26, 29, 32, 33]. Zhang et al. [29] have demonstrated that the introduction of alkali metal ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, and K⁺) into platinum/titanium oxide (Pt/TiO₂) catalysts effectively disperses Pt and thus promotes the oxidation activity for formaldehyde. Our previous study [34] and Liu's publication [32] have also indicated that the physiochemical properties of MnO₂ catalyst, including the specific surface areas, reducibility, oxygen storage capacity, and especially oxygen vacancy concentration, can be significantly improved by introducing an appropriate amount of alkali metal ions [27], thus leading to significantly increased ozone catalytic decomposition efficiency. It has also been further confirmed by the DFT calculations that alkali metal ions in MnO₂ facilitate the dissociation of O₂ and H₂O via charge transfer [28], which is considered to be beneficial for catalytic ozone decomposition. In particular, Xu and coauthors [35] have shown that Li⁺ doped in a NiO crystal lattice promotes the mobile surface oxygen species, makes oxygen vacancies form more easily, and thus improves catalytic activity for CO oxidation. On the

whole, a series of studies have shown that alkali metal ions doping on catalysts have a significant effect on oxygen vacancy formation, and the role and advantage of $Li^+$ in various heterogeneous catalytic reactions has gradually been discovered [35]. Furthermore, our previous investigation has shown that $\alpha$-MnO₂ with high stability, instead of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂, is formed by solid-state reaction between MnO₂ and NaNO₃, KNO₃, RbNO₃, or CsNO₃ [34]. And some similar studies have also shown that it was difficult to transform $\alpha$-MnO₂ doped with $Na^+$, $K^+$, $Rb^+$ and $Cs^+$ into other types of crystal structure, even when treated with high concentration acid [31, 32, 36-38]. However, $Li^+$ in the framework of manganese oxide were found to possess superior mobility, compared with other alkali metal ions ($Na^+$, $K^+$, $Rb^+$ and $Cs^+$) [31, 32, 34, 36-38], which thus makes it possible to tune the $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ crystal structure, $Li^+$ content, and manganese and oxygen vacancies in the catalyst to alter the catalytic properties. To the best of our knowledge, the promoting effects of $Li^+$ on the ozone catalytic decomposition activity of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ with Mn vacancies has not yet been studied.

In this study, DFT calculations were adopted to evaluate the effects of Mn vacancies and $Li^+$ on the formation energy of oxygen vacancies and their adsorption energy for O₃, H₂O, and O₂ over $\varepsilon$-MnO₂. Based on this, a novel selective dissolution strategy was designed for preparing a series of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ catalysts with Mn vacancies and appropriate $Li^+$, and their catalytic performance investigated for ozone decomposition. In addition, characterizations, including ICP-OES, XRD, SEM, TEM, BET, H₂-TPR, O₂-TPD, XPS, EPR, and in situ DRIFTS were performed to elucidate the catalyst structure-ozone decomposition activity relationship. Also, a possible mechanism for ozone decomposition by the synergy of $Li^+$, Mn vacancies, and oxygen vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ was proposed.

## 2. Catalyst design and experimental methodology

### 2.1 Catalyst designation

Oxygen vacancies are considered as active sites for ozone catalytic decomposition [6, 8, 11, 17, 34]. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used for the calculation of the formation energy of oxygen vacancies and their adsorption energy for O₃, H₂O, and O₂ over ε-MnO₂, to clarify the role of Mn vacancies and Li⁺ in the ε-MnO₂ structure, aimed to provide a theoretical basis for catalyst designation and preparation. Detailed calculation methods, which were carried out with the help of the Vienna *Ab initio* Simulation Package (VASP 5.4), are described in the Support Information (SI).

### 2.2 Catalyst preparation

In this study, all reagents were of analytical grade and used as received. The Mn-Li precursors were prepared by a facile solid-state reaction method for the reaction of pristine MnO₂ and LiNO₃ solution, in which the preparation process for pristine MnO₂ is described in the SI. First, 27, 40.5, 54, and 67.5 mL of 1 M LiNO₃ solution, corresponding to resulting Li/Mn molar ratios of 1/1, 1.5/1, 2/1 and 2.5/1, were added into 2.35 g of pristine MnO₂ and with stirred constantly for 1 h. Then, the resulting mixtures were dried at 85°C for 3 h under rotary vacuum evaporation and further dried at 120°C for 12 h to form mixed powders. Finally, powders were calcined in a muffle furnace in static air at 450°C for 24 h. The obtained Mn-Li precursors were denoted as Li1Mn1, Li1.5Mn1 Li2Mn1, and Li2.5Mn1, respectively.

The ε-MnO₂ catalysts with abundant Mn vacancies, were prepared using a selective dissolution method, which is similar to the acid treatment process adopted in the separation of metallic minerals [26, 31, 39]. The detailed synthetic strategy and principle for fabricating Mn vacancies are described

in Section S2.2 (SI). In brief, 2.5 g of Mn-Li precursors with different Li/Mn molar ratios were treated with 0.5 M hydrochloric acid solution (250 mL) at 60°C with a constant stirring speed of 750 rpm for 24 h. The resulting products were washed with deionized water 3 times and dried at 120°C for 12 h. After the MnO₂ catalyst was obtained by acid treatment, the Li2Mn1 precursor was found to possess a pure akhtenskite structure and exhibited better ozone conversion (Figures S5 and S6). To elucidate the effects of Mn vacancies and Li⁺ on ozone decomposition activity of akhtenskite structure MnO₂, a series of ε-MnO₂ catalysts with different Mn vacancy contents were prepared by treating the Li2Mn1 precursors with 0, 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 M HCl under the same preparation process. The obtained samples were named MnO₂-H0, MnO₂-H1, MnO₂-H2, and MnO₂-H3, respectively.

### 2.3 Catalyst characterizations

The physiochemical properties of catalysts were investigated by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), inductively coupled plasma/optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), N₂ adsorption-desorption isothermy (BET), electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (EPR), hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR), oxygen temperature-programmed desorption (O₂-TPD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The related reaction intermediates which resulted in decreasing activity of ozone decomposition were investigated by in situ diffused Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (in situ DRIFTS). Detailed characterization methods are described in the SI.

### 2.4 Evaluation of ozone decomposition activity

The schematic diagram of the experimental setup is provided in Figure S7. The ozone decomposition performances over the catalysts were assessed under steady-state conditions in an inhouse-assembled U-type fixed-bed quartz reactor (inner diameter, 8 mm) using 100 mg of catalyst of 40–60 mesh in each experiment. The total flow rate through the reactor was 1000 mL/min, which contained 800 mL/min N₂ and 200 mL/min O₂, and corresponded to a gas-hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 600,000 h⁻¹. The temperature was precisely controlled at 25 ±1°C via an automatic calibration system of thermocouples. The relative humidity (RH) of 50 ±2%, unless otherwise specified, was controlled by bubbling the gas mixture into water and measured by a 7116-CP hygrometer (Cole-Parmer Instrument Co., LLC., Vernon Hills, IL, USA). The desired ozone concentration of 45 ±2 ppm was supplied by flowing 1.0% O₂/N₂ compressed gas through an HTU-500E Ozone generator (Azco Industries Ltd., Langley, Canada). The inlet and outlet ozone concentration were monitored by an ozone analyzer (2B Technologies, Boulder, CO, USA). The ozone conversion was calculated as

$$\text{Ozone conversion }(\%) = \frac{[\mathrm{O}_{3}]_{\text{in}}-[\mathrm{O}_{3}]_{\text{out}}}{[\mathrm{O}_{3}]_{\text{in}}} \times 100 \% \tag{R1}$$

where: $[\mathrm{O}_{3}]_{\text{in}}$ and $[\mathrm{O}_{3}]_{\text{out}}$ are the ozone concentrations in the inlet and outlet gases, respectively.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1 Catalyst designation

It is commonly accepted that the formation ability of oxygen vacancies and their adsorption ability for ozone, water, and oxygen molecules (O₃, H₂O, and O₂) are decisive factors for ozone adsorption and catalytic decomposition [9, 11, 17, 34]. For this reason, catalyst designation was

determined with the help of DFT calculations by considering (1) the effect of Mn vacancies on the bulk and surface oxygen vacancy formation energy and (2) the synergistic effects of Mn vacancies and $Li^+$ on the adsorption energies for $O_3$, $H_2O$, and $O_2$ molecules over $\varepsilon$-MnO₂, to provide guidance for the preparation of a catalyst for ozone decomposition.

### 3.1.1 The formation energy of oxygen vacancy

The effects of Mn vacancies on the bulk and surface oxygen vacancy formation energies of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ were first calculated. The oxygen vacancy formation energies of the bulk and surface $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ without Mn vacancies were 4.877 and 1.823 eV, respectively (Figure 1). After Mn vacancies were fabricated in the $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ model, the bulk and surface oxygen vacancy formation energies decreased to 3.129 and 1.382 eV, respectively. Theoretically, the lower oxygen vacancy formation energies implied easier oxygen vacancy formation. These results confirmed that fabricating Mn vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ was indeed an effective design method for promoting oxygen vacancy formation.

![](./images/812641773615579136_3.jpg)

Figure 1. Bulk (a) and surface (b) oxygen vacancy formation energies in perfect/defect $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ structures with Mn vacancies.

The oxygen vacancy formation energies of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ with different amounts of Li⁺ were further calculated to confirm the role of Li⁺ on surface oxygen vacancy formation (Figures 2 and S2). The formation energies of oxygen vacancies on both perfect and defect $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ surfaces with Mn vacancies were all observed to first decrease and then increase to a high level with increased Li⁺, indicating that appropriate Li⁺ amounts located around the surface Mn vacancies were beneficial for oxygen vacancy formation. This result was similar to the calculation results for birnessite-type MnO₂ reported by Rong et al. [27], which suggested the two alkali cations might play similar roles on the catalyst surfaces for oxygen vacancy formation [16, 17, 32, 40]. On the other hand, it was also seen that the oxygen vacancy formation energies of the defect surface were all lower than perfect $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ surfaces with the same Li⁺ number, further indicating that Mn vacancies had a positive effective on surface oxygen vacancy formation.

![](./images/812641773615579136_4.jpg)

Figure 2. Effects of $Li^+$ number on surface oxygen vacancy formation energies on both perfect (25-Mn atoms) and defect (24-Mn atoms with a Mn vacancy) $\varepsilon$-MnO$_2$ surfaces.

### 3.1.2. Adsorption energy of oxygen vacancies for $O_3$, $H_2O$, and $O_2$

![](./images/812641773615579136_5.jpg)

Figure 3. The calculated adsorption energies of $O_3$, $H_2O$, and $O_2$ on perfect (a) / defect (b) $\varepsilon$-MnO$_2$ surfaces with different number of $Li^+$.

The ozone adsorption energy significantly decreased from -0.79 to -2.92 eV after the oxygen vacancies were constructed as the sites for the adsorption of ozone on the ε-MnO₂ surfaces, while the adsorption energies for water and oxygen only slightly decreased to -1.00 and -0.94 eV, which indicated that, although there existed competitive adsorption behavior among these molecules, oxygen vacancies were still the main active sites for ozone adsorption (Figure 3a and S2). However, the ozone adsorption energy increased to -1.96 eV after oxygen and Mn vacancies (V_O and V_Mn, respectively) were simultaneously fabricated on the catalyst surface, suggesting that Mn vacancies in ε-MnO₂ surfaces had a certain negative effect for ozone adsorption on oxygen vacancies (Figure 3a and S2). Notably, it was found that these negative effects were effectively alleviated by appropriate Li⁺ presence on the catalyst surface (Figure 3b and S2). On the one hand, the appropriate Li⁺ presence on catalyst surfaces decreased the ozone adsorption energy to a low level (-2.65 eV), but on the other hand, increased the water and oxygen adsorption energies, up to nearly 0 eV (-0.86 and -0.54 eV, respectively). These results further suggested that appropriate amounts of Li⁺ on ε-MnO₂ surfaces promoted the adsorption of ozone and desorption of water and oxygen molecules on oxygen vacancies.

DFT calculation results showed that Mn vacancies and appropriate amounts of Li⁺ in ε-MnO₂ not only reduced the oxygen vacancy formation energy, and thus facilitated oxygen vacancy formation, but also significantly decreased the adsorption ability of oxygen and water molecules, and retained the ozone adsorption ability at a high level on these oxygen vacancies. These results all indicated that fabricating Mn vacancies and introducing appropriate amounts of Li⁺ was indeed a reasonable catalyst design that made the ε-MnO₂ become a high-efficient catalyst for ozone catalytic decomposition. Based on this, a novel strategy, based on the selective dissolution of Mn-Li precursors

via HCl treatment, was proposed and applied for the preparation of the above-designed structure ε-
MnO₂ catalyst (see in section 2.3), aimed at high efficiencies for the catalytic decomposition of ozone.

### 3.2 Catalyst characterizations

#### 3.2.1 Elemental composition of the catalysts

The elemental compositions of the prepared catalysts were confirmed by ICP-OES, XPS, and
EDS. The Li/Mn molar ratios of the three HCl-treated MnO₂ samples were in the range of 0.05/1–
0.15/1, which was much lower than those of the MnO₂-H0 sample (Li/Mn = 1.98/1), showing that
largely Li⁺ was selectively dissolved by acid treatment (Table 1). More importantly, the Mn/O molar
ratios of the samples decreased as the treatment HCl concentration increased, which were all smaller
than the stoichiometric ratio 1/2 of MnO₂. Also, as confirmed by ICP-OES analysis results (Table
S2), Mn²⁺ was partly leached out in the supernatant solution (0–21.9 mg/g) after acid treatment, and
there was no Mn²⁺ detected in the MnO₂ catalyst (XPS results, Figure 7a). These results all indicated
that the generated Mn²⁺, mainly derived from the transformation of Mn⁴⁺ and Mn³⁺ in the catalyst
framework, was dissolved in the supernatant solution after HCl treatment and the Mn vacancies were
thus formed in the catalyst framework [26, 28]. Rong et al. [27] and Wang et al. [28] have also adopted
the above-mentioned methods to confirm the amount of Mn vacancies in MnO₂. In addition, although
Li⁺ and Mn²⁺ were further leached out as the HCl concentration increased from 0.5 to 0.8 M, the
Mn/O molar ratios in samples changed little, which might have been due to Mn²⁺ dissolution
weakening the Mn-O bond energy in the cross-linked MnO₆ octahedral units, thus resulting in the
escape of lattice oxygen in the crystal framework. Overall, the rate of Mn vacancy in MnO₂ was
indeed in a reasonable range of 1–4% without considering oxygen vacancies [27, 41].

### 3.2.2 Crystal structure, morphology, and specific surfaces

![](./images/812641773615579136_6.jpg)

Figure 4. XRD patterns of the catalysts.

The structural features of the samples were investigated by X-ray diffraction (Figure 4). MnO₂-
H0 sample displayed the intense and sharp diffraction peaks corresponding well with those of the
standard Li₂MnO₃ sample (JCPDF 81-1953). In addition, HR-TEM images showed that the lattice
spacings of 0.474, 0.409, and 0.244 nm were consistent with the (002), (110), and (200) planes,
respectively, further indicating that the MnO₂-H0 sample possessed the pure monoclinic Li₂MnO₃
phase (Figure S8c) [42, 43]. On the other hand, all three HCl-treated MnO₂ samples had the same
diffraction peaks, corresponding to a pure akhtenskite structure manganese oxide (JCPDF 30-0820),

suggesting that an intense phase transition occurred during acid treatment and the catalysts totally converted to $\varepsilon$-MnO₂. In addition, the (100) diffraction position around 37.1° in these three samples slightly shifted to a higher angle region as the HCl concentration increased. Correspondingly, the calculated lattice constant and lattice distance of (100) plane gradually decreased (Table 1), which might have been attributed to increased Mn vacancy contents after treatment with different HCl concentrations. Also, the intensity of all these peaks were observed to become weaker with increased acid concentration, indicating that the degree of crystallinity was clearly decreased [26, 31]. A similar phenomenon was confirmed by observation of TEM images (Figures S6c–S6f), in which the lattice fringes in some parts became fuzzier and more indiscrete, which was ascribed to damage of the long-range ordered crystalline structure and the formation of more Mn vacancies after HCl treatment. It was generally believed that the catalyst with proper crystallinity had the advantages of both good crystal structure and appropriate bulk/surface defects, which were favorable for ozone decomposition [15, 17, 34]. MnO₂-H2 catalyst exhibited appropriate crystallinity with good $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ structure, which was ascribed to the dissolution of the appropriate amounts of $Li^+$ and $Mn^{2+}$ of the Mn-Li precursor when the 0.5 M HCl solution was used in the acid treatment.

![](./images/812641773615579136_7.jpg)

Figure 5. FESEM images of MnO₂-H0 (a, b), MnO₂-H1 (c, d), MnO₂-H2 (e, f), and MnO₂-H3 (g, h).

Catalyst morphologies were investigated by means of FE-SEM and TEM. MnO₂-H0 sample, not treated with HCl, exhibited typical globular cluster structures, which were composed of dense non-oriented nanofibers with diameters of 10–30 nm (Figure 5a and 5b). After treating the catalyst with 0.2 and 0.5 M HCl, compact globular clusters were converted into creviced globular clusters with more slit pores, which were composed of thinner and finer interlaced rumpled nanofibers (Figures 5c–5f). This phenomenon was explained by the dissolution of $Li^+$ both in the catalyst framework and on catalyst surfaces. However, from SEM images, it was clearly observed that, after treatment with 0.8 M HCl, the catalyst's porous structure collapsed and disappeared, and the irregular globular structure became more compact, which was attributed to over-erosion of the crystal structure and thus led to a significant decrease in the pore volume and specific surface areas (confirmed by BET analysis results, Figures 5g and 5h).

The N₂ adsorption-desorption isotherms and corresponding Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) pore-size distribution curves for these samples showed that all four samples possessed a characteristic type

II isotherm pattern owing to the existence of a hysteresis loop, which was related to the presence of abundant slit-like mesopores (Figure S9). As the HCl concentration increased from 0 to 0.5 M, the catalysts' BET surface areas significantly increased from 27.86 to 105.99 $m^2$/g and the pore volume increased from 0.205 to 0.233 $m^3$/g, respectively (Table 1). However, it was found that there was a sudden decrease in both BET surface areas (80.47 $m^2$/g) and pore volume (0.216 $m^3$/g) as the HCl concentration increased to 0.8 M, which apparently stemmed from pore blockage of exterior porous surfaces due to the great deterioration of the crystal structure. These results were consistent with the information obtained from SEM images and XRD results. Normally, porous structures with large pore volumes and specific surface areas can provide more possibilities for ozone adsorption on oxygen vacancy sites. MnO₂-H2 catalyst had larger pore volumes and specific surface areas, compared with other MnO₂ catalysts, indicating that it possessed certain advantages in ozone adsorption and decomposition.

### 3.2.3 Reducibility, surface chemical properties, and oxygen vacancies.

H₂-TPR was performed to investigate catalyst reducibility (Figure 6). Theoretically, Li⁺ cannot be reduced under the conditions of an H₂-TPR experiment. For this reason, the observed reduction peak of these four catalysts were mainly ascribed to the reduction of MnO₂ to MnO with Mn₂O₃ and Mn₃O₄ as intermediates. The first reduction peak of these four samples was found to shift to lower temperature with the increased treatment HCl concentration, indicating that the lattice oxygen (O²⁻) in the ε-MnO₂ samples became more active and their reduction properties enhanced. A similar phenomenon has also been reported in isolated-type K⁺-birnessite MnO₂, as the strong bonding between alkali metal ions with surface unsaturated O inhibits lattice oxygen reactivity [26]. Catalyst

reducibility was thus deduced to decrease in the order of MnO₂-H3 > MnO₂-H2 > MnO₂-H1 > MnO₂-H0. Generally, once Mn³⁺ was formed in the catalyst, oxygen vacancies were created to preserve charge conservation. Therefore, the total H₂ consumption, quantified by a standard silver oxide sample, was adopted for evaluating the Mn³⁺ abundance in the crystal as indicative of oxygen vacancy content in the four catalysts. Accordingly, the oxygen vacancy concentrations of the MnO₂ catalysts were further deduced to follow the order of MnO₂-H2 > MnO₂-H1 > MnO₂-H3 > MnO₂-H0 (Table 2). These results corresponded well with the following XPS test results.

![](./images/812641773615579136_8.jpg)

Figure 6. H₂-TPR (a) and O₂-TPD (b) profiles of the catalysts.

O₂-TPD experiments were performed to further investigate the catalysts' deoxidation process. The O₂-TPD spectra were classified into three parts: desorption of chemically adsorbed oxygen species and surface active oxygen species (O²⁻, O⁻, and OH⁻; <400°C), desorption of sub-surface lattice oxygen (400–650°C), and evolution of bulk lattice oxygen (>650°C, Figure 6b). MnO₂-H2 catalyst exhibited the largest desorption peak areas, compared with the other catalysts, which meant that this sample had the best oxygen storage capacity [15]. In addition, the release of subsurface and bulk lattice oxygen moved to lower temperatures with increased HCl concentration, implying the

lattice oxygen in the framework had become more unstable. This phenomenon was explained by increased Mn vacancies, which weakened Mn-O bonds in the crystal framework and thus enhanced lattice oxygen mobility. These results again verified that Mn vacancies increased in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ after acid treatment, which was consistent with XPS results. Another possible explanation was that decreased Li⁺ weakened lattice oxygen stability in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂, which allowed the lattice oxygen to more easily escape the crystal framework. In addition, oxygen released at low temperature ($<$$400^\circ$C) was theoretically considered as labile oxygen species and the lower the temperature of labile oxygen species release was, the looser they had been adsorbed on the catalyst surface [18, 27]. Also, more active oxygen species were detected on the catalyst surface, which indicated more oxygen vacancies existed in the catalyst, as the generation, activation, and migration of surface reactive oxygen species typically relied on oxygen vacancies. As mentioned above, it was reasonably concluded that, compared with those of other catalysts, MnO₂-H2 catalyst possessed the lower desorption temperature and more active oxygen species and oxygen vacancies (Table 2).

Figure 7. Mn-$2p^{3/2}$ (a), and Mn-$3s$ (b), and O-$1s$ (c) XPS spectra of the catalysts.

Information related to chemical states and oxygen vacancy concentration of catalysts was collected using XPS experiments. Each spectrum of Mn-$2p^{3/2}$ was deconvoluted into two peaks, assigned to $Mn^{3+}$ at ~641.8 eV and $Mn^{4+}$ at ~642.8 eV (Figure 7a). The molar ratio of $Mn^{3+}$/$Mn^{4+}$ on the catalysts' surfaces decreased in the order of $MnO_2$-H2 > $MnO_2$-H1 > $MnO_2$-H3 > $MnO_2$-H0 (Table 2). Correspondingly, $MnO_2$-H2 also possessed the lowest average oxidation state (AOS) of Mn, which was calculated based on the formula [44]: $\text{AOS} = 8.956 - 1.126\triangle Es$, where $\triangle Es$ represents the binding energy difference between the two Mn-$3s$ peaks (Figure 7b). These results all showed that the $Mn^{3+}$/$Mn^{4+}$ molar ratio first increased and then decreased as HCl concentration

increased. This phenomenon was explained by the fact that, while the catalysts were treated with 0–0.5 M HCl, the $Mn^{4+}$ in the catalyst frameworks was gradually reduced to $Mn^{3+}$ and then to $Mn^{2+}$, with its two 3s electrons transferred to Mn–O orbitals [45], which thus resulted in increased Mn vacancies and $Mn^{3+}/Mn^{4+}$ molar ratios. However, $MnO_{2}$-H3 catalyst treated with 0.8 M HCl resulted in structural collapse, because of the excessive extraction of $Li^{+}$, and thus reduced the conversion of $Mn^{4+}$ to $Mn^{3+}$ to maintain total charge balance. This was accompanied with $Mn^{3+}$ being continuously converted to $Mn^{2+}$ to form Mn vacancies, which eventually resulted in decreased $Mn^{3+}/Mn^{4+}$ ratios in $MnO_{2}$. Theoretically, $Mn^{3+}$ presence in the catalyst crystal facilitates the oxygen vacancy formation to preserve the total charge conservation [46-48]. $MnO_{2}$-H2 was found to present the highest abundance of $Mn^{3+}$ species among the four samples and, thus, the conclusion that $MnO_{2}$-H2 had the highest oxygen vacancy concentration could be obtained.

The chemical bonds of oxygen in Mn oxide catalysts were further characterized and three peaks at ~529.6, ~531.1 and ~532.3 eV identified from the O-1s spectra were attributed to the lattice oxygen $(O_{I})$, low-coordinated oxygen species adsorbed in oxygen vacancies $(O_{II})$, and surface absorbed water $(O_{III})$, respectively (Figure 7c) [15, 49]. The O-1s spectra was seen to represent a slight shift toward higher binding energies with increased HCl concentration, which was explained by the reason that the Mn vacancies led to reduced electron density around O atoms, thus resulting in increased binding energy of lattice oxygen [28, 50]. In addition, it has been generally reported that the amount of $O_{II}$ represents the surface oxygen vacancy concentration in catalysts [7, 15, 17]. It was found here that $MnO_{2}$-H2 catalyst exhibited the most $O_{II}$, compared with other catalysts, and thus it had the most abundant oxygen vacancies.

![](./images/812641773615579136_10.jpg)

Figure 8. EPR detection of oxygen vacancy concentration (a), DMPO-·OH (b), and DMPO-·O₂⁻ (c) over the catalysts in aqueous solution, using DMPO as the spin-trapping agent.

EPR measurements were performed to confirm the oxygen vacancy concentrations in the four catalysts. The signal located at around $g=2.003$ was ascribed to oxygen vacancies (Figure 8a) [51-53]. Generally, greater peak intensity means a higher oxygen vacancy concentration. The peak intensity here was found to decrease in the order of MnO₂-H2 > MnO₂-H1 > MnO₂-H3 > MnO₂-H0. Correspondingly, both of the two free radical concentrations (·OH and ·O₂⁻), detected using DMPO

[19, 54, 55] as the spin trapping agent in aqueous solution (Figures 8b and c), were also found to follow the same decreasing trend mentioned above, which was explained by these active oxygen species being generally adsorbed on oxygen vacancy sites [15, 55]. These results all indicated that MnO₂-H2 possessed more oxygen vacancies than the other three catalysts.

### 3.3. Catalytic performance and deactivation reason for ozone decomposition

#### 3.3.1 Performance for ozone decomposition

![](./images/812641773615579136_11.jpg)

Figure 9. Ozone conversion over the catalysts. (a) Effects of HCl concentration, (b) effects of relative humidity, (c) effects of alternate humidity conditions, and (d) durability of MnO-H2 catalyst.

Reaction conditions: $O_3$ = 45 ±2 ppm, temperature = 25 ±1°C, RH = 50 ±2% for (a) and (d), space velocity = 600,000 h⁻¹ for (a) and (c), and 300,000 h⁻¹ for (d).

Ozone conversions over the different catalysts were assessed at room temperature (25 ±1°C) with an ozone concentration of 45 ±2 ppm at relative humidity (RH) at 50 ±2%, and space velocity at 600,000 h⁻¹. The ozone conversion of MnO₂-H0 dropped rapidly to below 10% within 1 h (Figure 9a), while MnO₂-H2, which was treated with 0.5 M HCl, maintained at 91.3% after reaction for 6 h under the same reaction conditions, indicating good stability for ozone decomposition. However, when the HCl concentration increased to 0.8 M, ozone conversion was sharply decreased to 41.5%, indicating that excessively high concentration acid treatment resulted in a significant reduction of catalytic performance [16, 26]. The descending order of ozone decomposition activity was MnO₂-H2 > MnO₂-H1 > MnO₂-H3 > MnO₂-H0.

The ozone decomposition performance of MnO₂-H2 catalyst under different relative humidities was measured to investigate its humidity resistive property (Figure 9b). The decreasing trend of the ozone decomposition rate accelerated with increased RH, suggesting that higher water vapor inhibited the ozone decomposition process. This result was consistent with reported literature [10, 15, 56], which indicated that the competitive adsorption of water molecules on the catalyst surface was still a main reason for catalyst poisoning and thus deactivation. Notably, ozone conversion by MnO₂-H2 maintained at ~70% after reaction for 6 h even with RH values up to 75%, which indicated that the sample was more stable under high humidity and did not suffer from dramatic deactivation. Comparison of catalytic performance for ozone decomposition over the Mn-based catalysts reported in the literature is summarized in Table S4. To confirm the deactivation effects caused by humidity

for ozone decomposition, ozone conversion by MnO₂-H2 catalyst under the alternating humidity were further evaluated (Figure 9c). While the MnO₂-H2 catalyst exposed in the wet gas condition (90%), the ozone conversion sharply decreased due to the competitive adsorption effect [57]. However, once the RH was switched from 90% to <5%, the ozone conversion was quickly restored to its original level and maintained at ~95%, which suggested that the adsorption of water molecules on the catalyst surface was very weak and easily desorbed from active sites under dry gas flow (<5%). Therefore, it was concluded that the catalyst deactivation derived from water vapor was reversible, and this deactivation effect could be effectively alleviated with the help of simple dehumidification strategies in practical application. Furthermore, the durability of MnO₂-H2 for ozone conversion was further tested and the ozone conversion of MnO₂-H2 found to maintain at nearly 70% after 80 h of use, which further indicated that this catalyst was not easily deactivated and possessed excellent stability (Figure 9d). From the above, MnO₂-H2 catalyst with 0.64% Li⁺ content was concluded to be a promising catalyst for applications in ozone decomposition due to its high catalytic activity and excellent humidity resistance (Table 1).

Both the experimental and DFT studies described above indicated that the excellent ozone conversion of these ε-MnO₂ catalysts significantly correlated with their enhanced oxygen vacancy concentration, which resulted from the formation of more Mn vacancies after treating Mn-Li precursors with HCl solution. The presence of appropriate amounts of Li⁺ facilitated the Li₂MnO₃ transformation into pure ε-MnO₂ with acid treatment, and thus increased Mn vacancies, which promoted oxygen vacancy formation, and resulted in decreased adsorption ability for H₂O and O₂ while the O₃ adsorption ability remained at a high level on these oxygen vacancies. In addition, the

synergetic effects between Mn vacancies and appropriate amounts of $Li^{+}$ in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ produced appropriate crystallinity (Figure 4), larger specific surface area (Table 1), superior reducibility (Figure 6a), and higher oxygen storage capacity (Figure 6b). All these factors were beneficial for ozone reactions on oxygen vacancies, and thus facilitated ozone decomposition [15, 17, 34, 56]. Although MnO₂-H3 with 0.40% $Li^{+}$ content, showed better reducibility, excessive dissolution of $Li^{+}$ and $Mn^{2+}$, because of the acid treatment with high HCl concentration, resulted in smaller specific surface area, worse oxygen storage capacity, and lower oxygen vacancy concentration, which were unfavorable for ozone catalytic decomposition. Based on the above discussion, the excellent activity of MnO₂-H2 catalyst for ozone decomposition was concluded to be mainly due to abundant oxygen vacancy formation resulting from the presence of appropriate $Li^{+}$ content (0.6–0.7%) and increased Mn vacancies generated in catalyst framework after the selective dissolution strategy using an HCl solution.

3.3.2 Reasons for decreased activity during ozone decomposition process

![](./images/812641773615579136_12.jpg)

![](./images/812641773615579136_13.jpg)

Figure 10. In situ DRIFTS spectra of characterizing the oxygen-related intermediates over MnO₂-H2 catalysts. (a) Effects of reaction time, (b) effects of HCl concentration, (c) effects of alternate reaction temperature conditions, and (d) effects of reaction temperature, unless otherwise specified.

As confirmed by the ozone decomposition activity evaluation results, ozone conversion over the catalysts decreased with time, indicating that there existed an important transformation step for intermediates product conversion during the reaction process that significantly affected ozone decomposition performance, which remains unclear and requires further investigation (Figure 9a). In situ DRIFTS examination revealed a peak located at ~1400 cm⁻¹, which was ascribed to generated oxygen-related intermediates (Figure 10) [17, 34]. This peak's intensity became stronger with increased reaction time, indicating that oxygen-related intermediates gradually accumulated on catalyst surfaces during ozone decomposition (Figure 10a). In addition, the O-1s spectra of fresh and used ε-MnO₂ catalyst clearly showed that the Oₙ concentration increased from 25.91 to 30.38% (Figure S10), further confirming this oxygen-related intermediate accumulation phenomenon [17, 34, 56]. Also, the decreasing order of the peak intensity at 1400 cm⁻¹ for the catalysts after 6 h reaction

was MnO₂-H3 > MnO₂-H1 > MnO₂-H2 (Figure 10b). This order was the reverse of the order of ozone conversion among the three MnO₂ catalysts (Figure S11a), suggesting these generated oxygen-related intermediates had an adverse effect on ozone decomposition performance. In contrast, this peak's intensity became stronger when the reaction temperature was first increased from 25 to 75°C and then decreased to 25°C (Figure 10c), accompanied with slightly increased ozone conversion (~10%), and further decreased to a low level (Figure 10c and S11b). This phenomenon indicated that these intermediates were not apparently effectively eliminated and difficult to convert to oxygen even at higher reaction temperature and they were generated and accumulated on the catalyst surface, thus significantly affecting ozone conversion. Moreover, as the reaction temperature increased, the peak's intensity became weaker and the decreasing trend of ozone conversion of the corresponding catalysts slowed down (Figures 10d and S11c), which was attributed to the high reaction temperature accelerating the conversion of accumulated oxygen-related intermediates on the catalyst surface and thus promoting the overall ozone conversion process. Based on the above discussion, oxygen-related intermediates were deduced to play a significant negative role in ozone conversion and the accumulation and dissociation of these oxygen-related intermediates on oxygen vacancies were the main reason resulting in the decreased activity during the ozone decomposition process.

3.4 The proposed reaction mechanism and discussion

![](./images/812641773615579136_14.jpg)

Figure 11. Proposed reaction pathway for ozone catalytic decomposition over $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ catalyst.

Considering the strong relationship between ozone conversion performance and oxygen vacancies, it was concluded that, similar to Mn oxides with other morphologies reported in literature, ozone catalytic activity mainly hinged on the oxygen vacancy concentration in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ [9, 11]. The mechanism for catalytic ozone decomposition on oxygen vacancies for Mn oxides as reported [9, 11, 17, 34] follows the scheme (R2-R4, below). However, considering the significant difference in crystal structure between the prepared $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ and other Mn oxides, as well as our investigation results concerning the effects of oxygen-related intermediates accumulation on ozone catalytic decomposition, the present mechanism was considered insufficient for describing the ozone decomposition process of $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ and requires further improvement.

$$\mathrm{O}_{3}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}} \rightarrow \mathrm{O}_{2}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}-\mathrm{O}^{2-} \tag{R2}$$

$$\mathrm{O}_{3}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}-\mathrm{O}^{2-} \rightarrow \mathrm{O}_{2}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}-\mathrm{O}_{2}{ }^{2-} \tag{R3}$$

$$\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}-\mathrm{O}_{2}{ }^{2-} \rightarrow \mathrm{O}_{2}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}} \tag{R4}$$

Based on the above experimental results, a possible mechanism was proposed here for ozone decomposition by the synergy of $Li^+$, Mn vacancies, and oxygen vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂. The $Li^+$ were located around Mn vacancies to compensate for charge imbalance, thus stabilizing the $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ surface structure and facilitating oxygen vacancy formation in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ (Figure 11). This weakened the adsorption ability for oxygen and water molecules on oxygen vacancies, thus contributing greatly to the catalytic decomposition of ozone, as confirmed by the present DFT calculations (Figures 2 and 3). The complete process for ozone decomposition over $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ is described in the following reaction equations (Step 1–4). First, ozone molecules were inclined to adsorb on oxygen vacancies due to weakened adsorption ability of water and oxygen molecules in the presence of Mn vacancies and $Li^+$ (Step 1). Second, absorbed ozone quickly decomposed to oxygen molecules, accompanied with the generation of oxygen-related intermediates ($O^{1/2-}$ and $O_2^{2-}$, Step 2 and 3, respectively), most of which quickly decomposed to oxygen molecules to release oxygen vacancies for participating in the next reaction cycle (Stage I), thus exhibiting the excellent catalytic activity. Meanwhile, a portion of these intermediates were further reacted with other ozone molecules to form more stable oxygen intermediates ($O_{2+n}^{2-}$, Step 4) and gradually accumulated on oxygen vacancies to maintain the total charge balance (Stage II) [56], thereby resulting decreased the ozone catalytic decomposition performance. Finally, these accumulated oxygen intermediates were gradually transformed into oxygen molecules, which released the occupied oxygen vacancies to participate in the next reaction cycle. In this slow transformation step, the accumulation and dissociation of these intermediates on oxygen vacancies gradually tended to be balanced, thus the decreasing trend of ozone conversion was slowed down with increased reaction time. In the whole reaction cycle, the desorption of generated

oxygen molecules from oxygen vacancies was further accelerated due to weakened oxygen adsorption ability resulting from the presence of appropriate amounts of $Li^+$ and Mn vacancies.

## 4. Conclusions

In this study, a novel selective dissolution strategy was demonstrated for preparing $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ catalysts with appropriate $Li^+$ content and Mn vacancies and their catalytic activity for ozone decomposition investigated. Theoretical and experimental results were combined to elucidate the promoting effects of enhanced oxygen vacancies in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂, which resulted from the presence of Mn vacancies and $Li^+$, on the catalytic activity for ozone decomposition. DFT calculation results showed that Mn vacancies and appropriate amounts of $Li^+$, on the one hand, reduced the oxygen vacancy formation energy, and thus facilitated oxygen vacancy formation in $\varepsilon$-MnO₂. On the other hand, Mn vacancies and $Li^+$ decreased the adsorption ability of oxygen and water molecules, and maintained the ozone adsorption ability at a high level on these oxygen vacancies, thus contributing greatly to the whole ozone catalytic decomposition process. Characterization results showed that preparing these $\varepsilon$-MnO₂ catalysts by selective dissolution of Mn-Li precursors with HCl produced more Mn

vacancies, and, thus, yield larger specific surface area, better oxygen storage capacity, more active oxygen species, and abundant oxygen vacancies on catalysts' surfaces. The optimal value of the $Li^{+}$ content in $\varepsilon-MnO_{2}$ was in the range of $0.6-0.7 \%$, and $MnO_{2}-H 2$ catalyst with $Li^{+}$ content at $0.64 \%$ possessed the greater oxygen vacancy concentration. This catalyst exhibited superior ozone conversion at $91.3 \%$ at $25 \pm 1^{\circ} C$ after reaction for $6 ~h$ under an initial ozone concentration of $45 \pm 2$ ppm, RH of $50 \pm 2 \%$ , and space velocity of $600,000 ~h^{-1}$ . This catalyst also showed excellent stability for ozone decomposition after 80 h of use. The accumulation and dissociation of oxygen-related intermediates on oxygen vacancies of $\varepsilon-MnO_{2}$ was the slow transformation step in the whole reaction process, therefore resulting the decreased activity for ozone decomposition. In summary, the proposed approach for the design and preparation of a novel catalyst with high performance for ozone decomposition showed great promise for promoting practical applications of Mn-based catalysts in future industrial settings.
