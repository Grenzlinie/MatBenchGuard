# Plasmon-Assisted Zone-Selective Repair of Nanoscale Electrical Breakdown Paths in Metal/Metal Structures for Near-Field Optical Sensing

Mohamed Yousef Hassan$^{a}$, Yu Zhou$^{a}$, Chenjie Gu$^{b}$, Hailong Liu$^{c}$, Joel Kwang Wei Yang$^{c,d}$, and Diing Shenp Ang$^{a+}$

$^{a}$ Nanyang Technological University, School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Singapore 639798, Singapore.

$^{b}$ Ningbo University, School of Science, Ningbo 315211, Zhejiang, China

$^{c}$ Singapore University of Technology and Design, 8 Somapah Road, Singapore 487372, Singapore.

$^{d}$ Institute of Materials Research and Engineering, A*STAR (Agency for Science, Technology and Research), 2 Fusionopolis Way, #08-03 Innovis, Singapore 138634, Singapore.

$^{+}$Corresponding author: edsang@ntu.edu.sg

KEYWORDS: Near-field optical sensor, optical confinement, optical reversal of oxide breakdown, plasmonics, surface plasmons

ABSTRACT

We report the selective repair of nanoscale oxide breakdown paths, by the localized surface plasmon effect, in the sandwiched oxide layer of a metal/oxide/metal structure. The test device has an aluminum/SiO₂ stack, patterned in the form of a nanodisc (of diameter ~100 nm) over a surface oxidized, planar bottom aluminum electrode (i.e. Al/SiO₂/AlOₓ/Al) and can support plasmonic resonance modes in the visible spectrum. Two-dimensional profiling of the top Al electrode by conductive atomic force microscopy, to obtain the distribution of the current within the nanodisc stack, reveals multiple breakdown paths in the SiO₂/AlOₓ layers following an electrical stressing event. Re-profiling of the current after white-light illumination shows preferential repair of the breakdown paths within specific "zones" in the nanodisc, and a corresponding decrease of the average leakage current. Breakdown paths situated near the top and bottom regions of the nanodisc are eliminated, leaving behind breakdown paths in the central part of the nanodisc. The resultant post-illumination leakage current map of the nanodisc agrees very well with the two-dimensional optical-field-enhancement pattern calculated using the finite-difference time-domain method, indicating that the zone-selective repair stems from enhanced optical fields arising from the localized surface plasmon effect in the top and bottom regions of the nanodisc. The findings of our study point to the prospect of realizing an electrically readable

nanoscale metal-oxide-metal near-field optical sensor based on the light-triggered restoration of oxide breakdown.

## INTRODUCTION

Plasmon-enhanced light-matter coupling has garnered significant attention in recent years due to its effectiveness in improving the performance of optoelectronic devices¹. In principle, plasmonics is a research field that exploits the superior optical properties of metallic nanostructures to manipulate light and render it to be absorbed in nanoscale volumes by as much as 352%²ˑ³. The unique optical properties possessed by these ultra-subwavelength metallic nanostructures are mainly due to their ability to support surface plasmon oscillations at certain resonance frequencies, upon excitation by external electromagnetic (EM) radiations. Eventually, these metallic nanostructures have become one of the most promising solutions for scaling down many photonic devices to chip-scale dimensions and for surpassing the optical diffraction limit challenge due to their excellent optical field absorption efficiency at the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) frequency⁴⁻⁶.

Most of the plasmonic nanostructures reported thus far are fabricated from noble metals like gold and silver due to the extreme absorption efficiency exhibited by such materials at certain plasmonic resonance frequencies. Accordingly, they are widely exploited for boosting the performance of various optoelectronic devices such as solar cells⁷, photodetectors⁸, optical sensors⁹, as well as enhancing the photoluminescence efficiency¹⁰ and more recently for dynamic color switches in display technologies¹¹. However, both gold and silver are incompatible with the complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology besides being relatively expensive materials. Therefore, plasmonic nanostructures made from aluminum (Al) are recently reported as an attractive alternative toward building cost-effective and scalable optoelectronic devices compatible with the CMOS processing technology¹³⁻¹⁸.


Of late, researchers have started to incorporate surface plasmon effects into traditional electronic devices for realizing numerous, innovative as well as beneficial applications based on the electro-optical interaction¹⁹⁻²⁶. For example, a novel optoelectronic device that recently emerged from the integration of plasmonics and the resistive random access memory (RRAM) device is the low-power optical switch (or so-called the plasmonic memristor)²⁷⁻²⁸. The device consists of a Ag-based conducting-bridge type RRAM cell integrated above a silicon waveguide, where the logic state of the memory cell (determined by the absence or presence of an Ag filament) directly controls the transmission of light along the waveguide. By leveraging on the ability of an Ag nanostructure to confine light, light transmission can be impeded beyond the point where the Ag filament is formed. Conversely, the transmitted light power implicitly carries information on whether a Ag filament exists within the RRAM cell or not. Another example is an electrically readable photocapacitor that can detect EM waves in the mid-infrared range²⁹. The device traps light waves in a plasmonic nanostructured Si channel sandwiched between two gold electrodes. The trapped light enhances electron-hole pair generation which results in capacitance modulation of the device by a factor greater than $10^3$.

In this paper, we show that white light could repair electrical breakdown paths formed in the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers of a conventional Al/SiO₂/AlOₓ/Al nanodisc structure. The role of localized surface plasmons in producing an interesting zone-selective repair effect is revealed, based on the good agreement observed between the post-illumination two-dimensional leakage current profile of the nanodisc and the simulated optical field enhancement pattern within the sandwiched oxide layers. Oxides such as SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ are ubiquitous in the current mainstream integrated circuit technology but are known to be non-photoresponsive due to their relatively large bandgaps. Thus, the idea of these oxides serving as active components in optical

sensing applications was unrealistic until recently when photoactivity of these materials after electrical breakdown was revealed $^{30-31}$. To-date, there has been no report that exploits this phenomenon for device-level optical sensing application. The ability to engage the light-trapping capability of the nanoscale metal/oxide/metal structure to effectively repair the breakdown paths in the oxide gap junction, as demonstrated in this work, raises the prospect of deploying these simple structures as electrically readable near-field optical sensors in compact plasmonic circuits.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

To realize the $Al/SiO_2/AlO_x/Al$ nanodiscs, the bottom Al electrode was first deposited on a pre-cleaned Si substrate via electron-beam evaporation. Afterward, a $100\ \mu\text{m} \times 100\ \mu\text{m}$ square array of nano-holes, each of diameter ~100 nm, was formed by electron-beam lithography (EBL). During annealing of the EBL photoresist, the surface of the Al electrode was inevitably oxidized to give a thin aluminum sub-oxide ($AlO_x$) layer$^{14}$. A 2-nm thick $SiO_2$ film followed by a 35-nm thick Al film were then deposited by electron-beam evaporation. The EBL resist was finally lifted off to create the array of $Al/SiO_2/AlO_x/Al$ nanodiscs. The Method section describes the details of the fabrication process. To simplify the test sample fabrication, individual nanodiscs without external electrical contacts were adopted. Electrical access to the nanodiscs was provided by a conductive atomic force microscope (C-AFM) probe as explained below.

We also determine the thickness of the top Al electrode to be 33 nm, and the angle that its sidewalls make with respect to the normal to be $11^\circ$.

A 2-D topography image of the fabricated sample showing a sub-array of nanodiscs was first obtained by a C-AFM probe scan at a bias voltage of 0 V (Figure 2a). Compared to the SEM image in Figure 1b, the nanodiscs appear bigger and closer to each other in the topography image. The discrepancy may be ascribed to broadening in the measured profile due to the interaction between the side of the CAFM probe and the sidewall of the nanodisc (Figure S6). The region scanned has an area of 750 nm $\times$ 750 nm. Following this, a high-resolution probe scan was made on a randomly selected nanodisc to obtain a 2-D current mapping of the nanodisc at a bias voltage of 5 V. The scan was repeated several times to ensure that there were no pre-existing high leakage current sites in the nanodisc (Figure 2c). Next, the C-AFM probe was landed on the nanodisc, and an electrical "soft-breakdown" procedure was carried out with the probe position fixed. The applied voltage was gradually swept from 0 to 10 V, and the current was measured (Figure 2b, gray curve). During the initial sweep, the current can be seen to increase progressively until ~10 V at which an abrupt jump to the set current compliance $I_{cc}$ (75 pA) occurs, signalling the electrical breakdown of the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers. After the breakdown, several consecutive voltage sweeps (also from 0 to 10 V) were applied. In the first sweep, $I_{cc}$ was maintained at 75 pA but was relaxed to a higher value in each subsequent sweep, until a final value of 450 pA for the fifth and last sweep (Figure 2b, orange curve). With an $I_{cc}$ setting, the parameter analyser limits the current (hence the related Joule heating effect) through the breakdown oxide to the set value by automatically lowering the applied voltage. This serves a

two-fold purpose. Firstly, by gradually relaxing $I_{cc}$ to higher values in subsequent voltage sweeps helps achieve a stable oxide breakdown, without allowing the

Secondly, the limited Joule heating effect helps keep the dissociated oxygen ions within the proximity of the breakdown oxide. Atomic scale spectroscopic studies on oxide breakdown have found the breakdown region to be deficient in oxygen content³³. This implies that breakdown proceeds via the dissociation of Si-O and Al-O bonds. When the applied oxide electric field causes sufficient lowering of the bond dissociation energy barrier, dissociation of Si-O and Al-O bonds occurs. The released oxygen ions then drift toward the anode under the applied electric field as well as migrate laterally outwards from the breakdown region due to Joule heating³⁴. A simulation study has shown that under limited Joule heating, some of the released oxygen ions remain in the vicinity of the breakdown region after aborting the breakdown process³⁵. As will be discussed later, these interstitial oxygen ions surrounding the breakdown site play a crucial role in the repair of the breakdown paths under white-light illumination³⁰. A 3-D schematic illustration of oxygen-deficient breakdown regions (comprising clusters of oxygen vacancies denoted by yellow spheres) surrounded by interstitials oxygen ions (denoted by red spheres) released during the breakdown process is shown in Figure 2e.

After the soft-breakdown procedure, the nanodisc was again subjected to several consecutive C-AFM probe scans at 5 V to obtain a 2-D current mapping of the breakdown oxide. As partial recovery of the breakdown oxide has been observed³², the repeated scans were thus meant to check the stability of the breakdown oxide before the illumination step. The 2-D current map of the first post-breakdown scan, overlapped with the topography image of the nanodisc, is shown in Figure 2d. As compared to the current map obtained before breakdown (Figure 2c), which

shows a relatively uniform shade throughout, bright spots corresponding to localized high leakage current sites can be seen scattered over the entire nanodisc area in the current map obtained after breakdown. Since the probe only made a localized contact to the top $AlO_x$ film throughout the entire soft-breakdown procedure, there should only be one breakdown path in the top $AlO_x$ film after the procedure. The numerous bright spots therefore imply that multiple breakdown paths were formed in the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers. This occurred when the top $AlO_x$ broke down upon the electric field exceeding its breakdown field, allowing the high-voltage probe to make a direct resistive contact with the Al nanodisc, causing breakdown to occur at multiple locations in the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers. The formation of multiple oxide breakdown paths was previously reported and found to depend on several factors like the thickness of the oxide and its stoichiometry, as well as the breakdown process conditions$^{36-37}$. The average breakdown current is ~145 pA, around ten times higher than the average leakage current measured before the breakdown. In subsequent scans, the number of bright spots is visibly increased (Figure S7 shows the current maps for the $2^{nd}$, $3^{rd}$ and $4^{th}$ scans). Thus, not only were the breakdown oxide stable, but also more breakdown paths were created in the structure in the subsequent scans.

At this juncture, it is necessary to point out a caution in the interpretation of the current maps obtained from the post-breakdown probe scans. During a probe scan, one needs to consider two possible scenarios: (1) Additional breakdown paths may be generated in the top $AlO_x$ film when the probe is situated directly above a breakdown path in the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers (Figure S3a). This is because a major part of the applied voltage would be dropped across the top $AlO_x$ film, since the breakdown path in the $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers has a much lower resistance than a non-breakdown position. The higher voltage across the top $AlO_x$ layer may then trigger its

breakdown. (2) The further breakdown of the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers may also occur when the probe is in contact with a breakdown path in the top AlOₓ layer (Figure S3c). This is because the probe makes a direct resistive with the nanodisc through a breakdown path in the top AlOₓ layer. Due to the higher voltage now dropping across the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers, multiple breakdown paths could be further generated. Thus, one should generally interpret the bright spots in a post-breakdown current map as the superposition of breakdown paths in both the top AlOₓ and sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers. However, in this study, the greater likelihood of scenario (1) dominating over scenario (2) may be ruled out based on the multiple probe scans made on the nanodisc concerned before the electrical soft-breakdown procedure. During these scans, the same 5 V was applied to the probe. From capacitive coupling consideration (Figure S2), it should be noted that nearly the entire 5 V is dropped across the top AlOₓ layer. No bright spots were detected in the current map despite after the several repeated scans (Figure 2c), indicating that there was no breakdown of the oxide layers in the structure. This is verified in the subsequent soft-breakdown step, which shows that the initial breakdown of the structure occurred around 10 V (Figure 2b, gray curve). Thus, during a post-breakdown probe scan, even if the probe is situated directly above a breakdown path in the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers (Figure S3a), breakdown of the top AlOₓ layer should not occur. It is therefore reasonable to ascribe the multiple bright spots seen in the current maps to breakdown paths situated in the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers. The generation of more breakdown paths in these sandwiched layers could occur during post-breakdown probe scanning when the probe contacted the original breakdown path in the top AlOₓ layer, formed during the soft-breakdown step. At this position, the probe made a resistive contact with the Al nanodisc and the entire 5 V was developed across the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers. The breakdown field of an oxide has been shown to decrease

when its thickness is increased$^{38}$. For a 6-nm thick $Al_2O_3$, the breakdown field is < 10 MV/cm. On the other hand, the breakdown field for a 2-nm thick $Al_2O_3$ is ~20 MV/cm. Thus, it is reasonable to ascribe the additional bright spots obtained during the post-breakdown scans to the further breakdown of the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers but not the top $AlO_x$ layer.

To elucidate the negative photoconductivity response of the breakdown oxide, the test sample was then illuminated briefly with the LED lamp. This was followed by another subsequent current-mapping scan of the nanodisc. The current map before illumination (i.e. from the $4^{th}$ consecutive scan made after the soft-breakdown procedure as mentioned above) and that obtained after the illumination are shown in Figure 3. Compared to the current map before illumination (Figure 3a), the number of bright spots is evidently reduced in the current map obtained after illumination (Figure 3b). Considering that the breakdown oxide was stable before the illumination, the observation clearly indicates that some of the breakdown paths were repaired by the light. The average leakage current after illumination is reduced to ~68 pA, approximately half the value before illumination, and this corroborates the disappearance of some of the bright spots (leakage current paths) in the current map.

We should point out the rather unusual and interesting spatial distribution of the bright spots remaining after the illumination (Figure 3b). Unlike the case before illumination (Figure 3a) where the bright spots were distributed over the entire nanodisc, the post-illumination distribution seems to indicate that bright spots were preferentially repaired in certain regions of the nanodisc during the illumination. The number of bright spots in the top and bottom of the nanodisc are clearly reduced as compared to that in the central region, indicating that preferential repair of the breakdown paths had occurred in the former two regions. For a better visualization of the effect of illumination on the distribution of the leakage currents in the nanodisc, 3-D

current distribution plots before and after illumination (Figure 3c and 3d, respectively) are compared.

Figure 4b shows both the simulated (red, solid curves) and measured (blue, solid curves) reflectance spectra of the sample. Good agreement between the simulated and experimental curves is apparent around the resonance frequency. The frequency at the minimum reflectance point depends weakly on the periodicity of the nanodisc array (Figure S9b). However, it strongly depends on the nanodisc diameter (Figure S9c). The more significant shift of the minimum reflectance point in response to a change in nanodisc diameter implies that the nanodisc supports localized surface plasmons⁹,¹⁵,¹⁸,⁴¹. The frequency at which the LSPR occurs is 625 nm. At this resonance frequency, light is strongly confined or trapped within the volumetric gap of the oxide (or so-called plasmonic resonant cavity) and therefore the reflectance at this frequency drops to a minimum value. Based on the image-charge model, the resulting LSPR mode is due to the strong coupling between the oscillations of the top Al nanodisc dipole and its image dipole inside the bottom Al film⁴²⁻⁴⁴. Since the nanodisc is elliptical in shape, it is essential to also consider the reflectance spectra for the TE and TM polarized light. The respective simulated reflectance curves are also shown in Figure 4b. Relative to the reflectance curve for TM polarized light (electric field along the major axis D₁ direction), the curve for the TE polarized light (electric field along the minor axis D₂ direction) is shifted towards shorter wavelengths.

By monitoring the electric field intensity or $|E|^2$ distribution along three cross-sectional planes A-A', B-B' and C-C', we can better visualize the optical response of the nanodisc. Both the A-A' and C-C' planes are parallel to the XY plane; the former cuts horizontally through the middle of the SiO₂ layer and the latter through the middle of the top AlOₓ layer (inset in Figure 4a). The B-B' plane is parallel to the XZ plane and cuts vertically across the center of the nanodisc along the D₂ axis, as illustrated in Figure 4a. To select the frequency for plotting the $|E|^2$ distribution, we have taken reference to the LED lamp's spectra output, which peaks at two

wavelengths, 450 nm and 550 nm (Figure S1c). At 450 nm, the reflectance is high (>80 %) and there is no LSPR mode, thus this frequency was not considered. Figure 4c shows the $|E|^2$ profile (due to non-polarized excitation) on the A-A' plane for wavelength 550 nm, the other peak of the lamp's spectral output and near the LSPR mode for the TE-polarized excitation. A pronounced asymmetric optical field enhancement can be clearly seen inside the $SiO_2$ layer. The field intensity in the top and bottom of the nanodisc are substantially enhanced by a factor of more than 60, relative to the incident field. This enhancement is due to "squeezing" of the incident light energy in the nanoscale volumetric gap formed by the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers. On the other hand, the enhancement is considerably less pronounced on the left and right side and around the centre of the nanodisc, giving an elliptical "dark band" that extends through the middle portion of the nanodisc. The $|E|^2$ profile on the B-B' plane shows similar results (Figure 4d); significant enhancement of the optical field on the left and right (corresponding to the bottom and top of the nanodisc in Figure 4c), with little or no enhancement in the middle.

The asymmetrical optical field enhancement revealed in the sandwiched $SiO_2/AlO_x$ layers may be attributed primarily to the elliptical shape of the Al nanodisc. As a comparison, simulation study was also performed on a perfectly circular nanodisc. In this case, a symmetric optical field enhancement occurs in an annular region around the circumference of the nanodisc, with a resultant concentric "dark spot" in the inner part of the nanodisc (Figure S10). In contrast to the perfect symmetry in circular nanodisc structure, ellipsoidal nanodisc have major and minor symmetry axes which result in the splitting of the plasmon resonance into two LSPR frequencies$^{13}$. The LSPR at 600 nm is ascribed to coherent electrons oscillation along the minor axis due to TE-polarized excitation, whereas the resonance at 650 nm is ascribed to coherent electrons oscillation along the major axis due to TM-polarized excitation. In the experimental

nanodisc, the optical field enhancement in the top and bottom of the nanodisc is expected to be even stronger due to the peaked spectral output of the LED lamp at 550 nm, which is closer to the LSPR frequency for TE polarization (600 nm) than for the TM polarization (650 nm). Based on the field intensity profile on the A-A' for 550 nm (Figure S11), the field enhancement in the top and bottom of the nanodisc due to only TE-polarized light beam excitation is approximately two times greater than in the left and right sides of the nanodisc due to TM-polarized excitation. Additionally, due to the decreasing spectral output of the lamp for wavelengths exceeding 550 nm, enhancement on the left and right sides of the nanodisc by the LSPR of the TM polarization would be weaker.

The $|E|^2$ profile on the C-C' plane (Figure 4e) which cuts through the middle of the top AlOₓ layer is, however, significantly different as compared to that on the A-A' plane (through the middle of the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers). Except for the marginal enhancement by a factor of ~1.5 extending slightly beyond the edges of the nanodisc, the optical field within the entire top AlOₓ is significantly depressed to less than 20 % of the incident field.

Insight into the peculiar zone-selective repair of the breakdown paths in the nanodisc is obtained by comparing the post-illumination current map (Figure 3b) to the simulated $|E|^2$ profile or optical field enhancement pattern on the A-A' and C-C' planes (Figures 4c and 4e, respectively). From the comparison, it is clear that a good correspondence is only found between the post-illumination current map and the $|E|^2$ profile on the A-A' plane. An excellent qualitative agreement between the post-illumination current map and the $|E|^2$ field profile inside the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ is clearly evident. In regions (top and bottom) of the sandwiched SiO₂/AlOₓ layers where there is significant optical field enhancement, nearly all the breakdown paths there are repaired. In the central dark band where there is minimal field enhancement, there

are still numerous breakdown paths remaining after the illumination. This finding strongly implies that the "bar-like" distribution of the bright spots in the center of the post-illumination current map stems from the zone-selective repair of breakdown paths within the $SiO_2/AlO_x$, due to the LSPR effect occuring in this layer. On the other hand, there is a complete lack of correlation between the post-illumination current map and the $|E|^2$ profile inside the top $AlO_x$ layer. The simulation result shows a uniformly depressed field intensity inside the $AlO_x$, across the entire nanodisc, and thus it cannot explain the post-illumination current map.

The LSPR-assisted zone-selective repair of the oxide breakdown paths revealed above could be explained based on the oxygen migration model proposed by Zhou et al. $^{30}$. At or near the resonance frequency, the optical fields are strongly confined to particular regions inside the nanoscale oxide gap, resulting in field enhancement by a factor of about ~60, relative to the incident field, at these locations. The boosted optical fields may excite the oxygen ions which were released during the breakdown process and remained at interstitial sites in the vicinity of the oxygen-deficient breakdown paths (Figure 2c). Ab-initio simulation studies on interstitial oxygen movement in $SiO_2$ have revealed a relatively low energy barrier, ranging from 0.11-0.27 $eV^{44}$. The white light source used in our study has photon energy ranging from ~1.8-3.1 eV. Assisted by optical field enhancement due to the LSPR phenomenon, it may thus be possible for the interstitial oxygen ions to gain sufficient energy from the photons to overcome the energy barrier and migrate back to the vacancy sites in the breakdown path. The directional movement of oxygen ions towards a nearby vacancy may be facilitated by the internal electric field of the oppositely charged oxygen-vacancy (Frenkel) pair $^{46}$. The resultant recombination between the oxygen ions and vacancies annihilates the breakdown path. Therefore, in a region with significant optical field enhancement, nearly all the breakdown paths are repaired. On the other

hands, breakdown paths in areas where there is weak or no optical confinement (dark band) are relatively less affected.

The above proposed mechanism for light induced repair of the oxide breakdown path is corroborated by contemporary studies on the effect of light on the properties of perovskite films $^{47-49}$. Unger *et al.*$^{50}$ reported a strong dependence of the hysteresis behavior of hybrid organo-metal perovskite films on the illumination condition and ascribed the observation to photon-induced ion migration. By correlating the changes in confocal photoluminescence microscopy with chemical composition variations in the CH₃NH₃PbI₃ film under illumination, deQuilettes *et al.*$^{51}$ showed that the photo-induced brightening effect in the film is a result of a significant reduction in the trap state density. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectroscopy data obtained on the photo-brightened regions revealed a net migration of iodine ions away from these regions. Other studies have also reached a similar conclusion on the possibility of halide redistribution under illumination $^{52-53}$.

To check the proposed hypothesis on breakdown path disruption, *ab-initio* simulation was performed on an amorphous SiO₂ supercell generated by molecular dynamics$^{54}$. The energetics surrounding the formation of a $V_O$-$O_i$ defect (due to breakage of a stretched Si-O bond) and its annihilation by light are examined through minimum energy path (MEP) calculations based on the nudged elastic band method$^{55}$. Details on the simulation are found in the Supplementary Information, pages S-14 and S-15.

## CONCLUSION

Conductive atomic force microscopy measurement reveals an interesting outcome on the spatial distribution of the breakdown current in the Al/SiO₂/Al nanodisc after illumination by unpolarized white light. Before illumination, multiple highly conductive breakdown paths

distributed throughout the area of the nanodisc is observed. After illumination, however, the resultant distribution features a unique pattern comprising a band of multiple highly conductive breakdown paths in the central region of the nanodisc, flanked by a top and bottom region each having a distinctively lower number of breakdown paths. 3-D FDTD simulation study on the optical response of the nanodisc under unpolarized white light illumination provides valuable insights into the post-illumination breakdown current distribution. The nanodisc is shown to support LSPR, characterized by substantial optical field enhancement in the upper and lower regions separated by a central band with almost zero or weak optical field presence. The simulated optical field profile at the resonance frequency correlates very well qualitatively with the post-illumination breakdown current distribution. This result implies that the disappearance of the breakdown paths in the top and bottom regions of the nanodisc after illumination is due to restoration of the breakdown oxide by the enhanced optical fields there. It is proposed that the increased light energy in these regions may excite interstitial oxygen ions (released during breakdown from Si-O bond dissociation) near the breakdown path, resulting in their migration and recombination with the vacancy sites within the path and thus restoring the breakdown oxide.

The ability to disrupt the breakdown paths and reduce the leakage current in the $Al/SiO_2/Al$ nanodisc through the LSPR effect suggests that these structures, which involve standard integrated-circuit materials, may be readily incorporated into photonic circuits to serve as electrically readable, nanoscale near-field optical sensors. Before the sensing takes place, the oxide is first placed into a soft-breakdown state by a controlled procedure and then having the breakdown oxide restored by optical excitation. An appropriate voltage bias could be applied to re-trigger the soft breakdown of the oxide and then the device is used for photosensing again. Further work on the deployment of the prototype in an optical circuit is in progress.

## METHODS

Device fabrication: The $Al/SiO_2/AlO_x/Al$ nanodisc array was achieved by electron-beam lithography (EBL) and a wet chemical lift-off process. A 100-nm thick aluminum (Al) layer was first deposited by electron-beam evaporation on a pre-cleaned silicon substrate as the bottom electrode. Subsequently, a 150-nm thick layer of EBL resist, poly methyl methacrylate (PMMA), was deposited on top of the evaporated Al layer by spin coating and then thermally annealed. During this step, partial oxidation of the surface region of the bottom Al layer occurred. A lithography process was then performed with an EBL system (Raith eLINE Plus operating at 30 kV) to write the nanodisc array pattern onto the PMMA layer. After resist development, the nanoholes formed were filled with 2-nm $SiO_2$ and 35-nm Al sequentially using electron-beam-assisted evaporation. Finally, the resist together with its $Al/SiO_2$ top layer were lifted-off by acetone/n-ethyl-2-pyrrolidone (NEP), leaving the $Al/SiO_2$ stack in the nanoholes, and thus forming the 2-D nanodisc array pattern.

Optical characterization: The reflectance spectra were measured with a home-built optical set up based on WITEC ALPHA300 system. A halogen lamp was used as the light source, and the UHTS-spectrometer was employed to measure the reflectance spectra.

Simulations: We performed 3D-FDTD simulation using FDTD Solutions by Lumerical Inc. During the FDTD simulation, the periodicity of the Al nanodisk arrays was considered by applying Bloch boundary conditions on the side boundaries of a unit cell of size 190 nm × 190 nm. Perfectly matched layer was used on the z-axis along the propagation direction. A plane wave source with broad range bandwidth (400-900 nm) and an angle of incidence $35^\circ$ was used

to illuminate the simulated device structure. The incident field intensity is normalized to unity.

For all simulations, both the TE-polarized and TM-polarized reflection spectra and field intensities were averaged to obtain the overall result for a non-polarized light⁴⁰. A very high accuracy mesh with a resolution of 0.025 nm was used in all simulations. The refractive index functions of $Al$, $SiO_2$, $Al_2O_3$, and $Si$ are all chosen according to Palik data included in the material database of Lumerical. First-principles simulations were carried out using the Vienna *Ab-initio* Simulation Package (VASP). Details are given on pages S-14 and S-15.
