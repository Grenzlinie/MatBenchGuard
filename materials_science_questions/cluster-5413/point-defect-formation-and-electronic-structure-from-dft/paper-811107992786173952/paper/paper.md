## 20.1 ReRAM: the Next Generation of Non-Volatile Memory

The major driving force in the current development of microelectronics is the search for a universal memory, which would combine the most favorable properties of both high-speed dynamic random access memory and nonvolatile flash memory. The candidates for universal memory are ferroelectric memory, phase-change memory, magneto-resistive memory, and resistive memory. Each option has advantages and disadvantages. One of the most promising options for universal memory is resistive random access memory (ReRAM) [1–5], which has many advantages: a simple metal–insulator–metal (MIM) structure, a small memory cell, the potential for 3D integration, high read-and-write operation speeds, low power consumption, and the ability to save information for a long time. A ReRAM operation is principally based on switching back and forth from the insulating medium's high resistance state (HRS) to a low resistance state (LRS) when current flows. Hafnia $\text{HfO}_2$ and hafnium suboxides $\text{HfO}_{x<2}$ are the most promising materials for use as the active medium of resistive memory [6,7]. Hafnia conductivity is limited by trap ionization [6] when ReRAM is switched to the HRS state. However, the atomic structure of the defect that affects localization and charge transport still remains unclear. Currently, the accepted hypothesis is that oxygen vacancies are responsible for charge transport in dielectric and ReRAM switching [5]. Although the theory of the atomic and electronic structure of oxygen vacancies in hafnia was investigated in many studies [8–15], direct experimental data regarding the presence of oxygen vacancies in hafnia have not been reported.

Here, we clearly show both experimentally and theoretically, that oxygen vacancies are respon- sible for HRS charge transport in resistive memory elements based on $HfO_2$. This study also shows that the charge transport mechanism is described in terms of percolation through haf- nium suboxides when hafnia-based ReRAM is switched to the LRS state. This study describes electronic transport in the ReRAM insulator medium completely.

## 20.2 ReRAM Element Fabrication and Characterization
Our devices were fabricated as follows. $HfO_{x<2}$ films were produced using ion beam sputter- ing deposition. For substrates, we used silicon plates, Si(100), with a resistivity of $4.5\ \Omega\times cm$, deep-cleaned using a technique provided by the RCA Company. The target was sputtered by a beam of $Ar^{+}$ ions, while highly pure oxygen $(O_{2}>99.999\%)$ was simultaneously delivered into the area near the target and substrate. The composition (x-parameter) of the $HfO_x$ film was defined using a gas flow controller to regulate the partial oxygen pressure. In our experiments, we grew two sets of $HfO_x$ samples at partial oxygen pressures of $9\times 10^{-3}\ Pa$ and $2\times 10^{-3}\ Pa$. Under such conditions, we produced samples of near-stoichiometric $(x\approx 2)$ and nonstoichio metric compositions $(x<2)$.

The valence band spectra of grown hafnia films were obtained using a SPECS X-ray photo- electron spectral machine with monochromatic $AlK_{\alpha}$ radiation $(h\nu=1486.74\ eV)$. The defects in $HfO_{2}/Si$ films were generated by $Ar^{+}$ ion bombardment with an energy of 2.4 keV and an ion current density of approximately $10\ \mu A/cm^{2}$. It is known that sputtering by $Ar^{+}$ preferen tially removes oxygen from hafnium oxides, leading to the formation of hafnium suboxides $(HfO_{x<2})$ [16]. The [O]/[Hf] atomic ratio was estimated according to XPS peak areas combined with Scofield cross-sections. The electron energy loss spectra (EELS) were measured as described in Ref. [17]. The [O]/[Hf] atomic ratio for nonstoichiometric and $Ar^{+}$-bombarded hafnia film is approximately 1.9.

The cathodoluminescence (CL) spectra of the $HfO_x$ samples were measured using a Camebax electron probe microanalyzer. The CL spectra were recorded in the 1.5–3.8 eV range at 300K. The sample was irradiated by a beam of 1000 eV electrons, penetrating to a depth of $0.2\ \mu m$ with a current density of $1.2\ A/cm^{2}$.

Transport measurements were recorded for metal-insulator semiconductor (MIS) and MIM structures of $Si/HfO_x/Ni$ and $Si/TaN/HfO_x/Ni$, respectively. To fabricate $Si/HfO_x/Ni$ structures, the 20-nm thick amorphous hafnia $(HfO_{x<2})$ was deposited on an $n$-type Si wafer using the atomic layer deposition system. Tetrakis dimethyl amino hafnium (TDMAHf) and water vapor were used as precursors at a chamber temperature of $250^{\circ}C$ for $HfO_x$ film deposition. For the ReRAM measurements, $Si/TaN/HfO_x/Ni$ structures were used. To fabricate these structures, we deposited 8-nm thick amorphous hafnia on 100-nm thick TaN films, and these films onto Si wafers using physical vapor deposition. A pure $HfO_2$ target was bombarded by an electron beam, and $HfO_2$ molecules were deposited on the wafer. We did not apply any postdeposition annealing to produce the most nonstoichiometric films. Structural analysis showed that the resulting $HfO_x$ films were amorphous. All samples for transport measurements were equipped with round 50-nm thick Ni gates with a radius of $70\ \mu m$.

Transport measurements were performed using semiconductor parameter analyzers and precision LCR meters. The current compliance of $1\ \mu\text{A}$ was set for ReRAM measurements.

The *ab initio* simulations of electronic and optical properties were performed based on density functional theory using Quantum-ESPRESSO software [18]. We adopted a hybrid functional B3LYP to reproduce accurate band gap values. We used 81-atom cubic hafnia (c-HfO₂) supercells to simulate the oxygen vacancy. The choice of cubic phase is justified according to its simple structure.

## 20.3 Transport Properties in High Resistance State

Fig. 20.1A shows a typical hysteresis of experimental current–voltage characteristics of TaN/HfOₓ/Ni MIM structures at room temperature. The memory window (i.e., current difference) is more than three orders at a bias voltage of 0.5 V. To study the electronic transport mechanism in hafnia, we performed transport measurements in simpler metal–insulator–semiconductor structures, specifically, $n$-Si/HfOₓ/Ni [19]. The experimental current–voltage characteristics in $n$-Si/HfOₓ/Ni structures, measured at different temperatures with a positive bias applied to the Ni contact, are shown in Fig. 20.1B, graphed by characters in different colors. We attempted to qualitatively explain the experimental data by using different models, such as the Frenkel trap ionization model [20], the Hill trap ionization model [21], and the model of multiphonon ionization of an isolated trap [22]. To describe the experiments quantitatively and qualitatively, we performed simulations based on the model of phonon-assisted tunneling between traps [23]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J &= \frac{e}{s^2} \frac{n_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\left(1-\frac{n_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\right) P, \\
P &= \frac{\sqrt{2 \pi} h W_{\mathrm{t}}}{m * s^{2} \sqrt{k T\left(W_{\mathrm{opt}}-W_{\mathrm{t}}\right)}} \exp \left(-\frac{W_{\mathrm{opt}}-W_{\mathrm{t}}}{2 k T}\right) \exp \left(-\frac{2 s \sqrt{2 m * W_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\mathrm{h}}\right) \sinh \left(\frac{e F s}{2 k T}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{20.1}
$$

where $J$ is the current density, $e$ is the electron charge, $n_{\mathrm{t}}$ is the density of filled traps, $P$ is the probability of electron tunneling between traps per second, $\hbar$ is the Planck constant, $W_{\mathrm{t}}$ is the trap thermal excitation energy, $W_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is the trap optical excitation energy, $s=N_{\mathrm{t}}^{-1 / 3}$ is the distance between traps, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $F$ is the electric field. Analysis shows that taking $n_{\mathrm{t}} / N_{\mathrm{t}}=1 / 2$ allows us to use uniform electric field approximation $F=V / d$, where $V$ is voltage bias applied to MIM structure, and $d$ is the dielectric layer thickness. The results of this multiparameter fitting procedure are shown in Fig. 20.1B, graphed in solid lines. This procedure yielded the values of different transport parameters, such as $N_{\mathrm{t}}=2.5 \times 10^{20} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}, \quad W_{\mathrm{t}}=1.25 \mathrm{eV}, \quad W_{\mathrm{opt}}=2 W_{\mathrm{t}}=2.5 \mathrm{eV},$ and $m^{*}=0.8 m_{\mathrm{e}}$. Quantitatively, there is full agreement between the model of phonon-assisted tunneling between traps and the experimental data. The electron trap energy in hafnium oxide was previously determined in Ref. [24] to be 1.2 eV. The trap thermal energy value of 1.25 eV that we obtained is close to that of $W_{\mathrm{t}}=1.36 \mathrm{eV}$ observed in the experiment in [25]. Furthermore, the trap optical energy value of $W_{\mathrm{opt}}=2.5 \mathrm{eV}$ is close to the calculated value of 2.35 eV for the negatively charged oxygen vacancy in hafnia reported in Ref. [14].

The configuration diagram of a negatively charged oxygen vacancy (electron trap) in haf- nia is shown in Fig. 20.2. The vertical transition with the value of 2.5 eV corresponds to the optical trap excitation. The thermal trap energy value was 1.25 eV.

Consequently, we found that the nature of charge-carrier transport in the HRS of ReRAM structures is phonon-assisted tunneling between traps.

## 20.5 Transport Properties in Low Resistance State

The most commonly used LRS model in ReRAM structures consists of a metal filament of approximately 1–10 nm [29,30]. This metal filament must have linear current–voltage char- acteristics. This approach exhibits two “bottlenecks”: (1) it cannot describe ReRAM in $GeO_{x}$ and $SiO_{x}$-based structures [31,32], and (2) the experimentally measured current is much lower than that predicted by Ohm's law. To illustrate the second case, we calculated the cur- rent through a pure metal Hf wire, 1 nm in diameter and 8 nm long. The results are shown in Fig. 20.3 by a dashed-dotted green line (dark gray in print versions). The calculations are approximately $10^{3}$ times higher than those obtained in the experiments. In the random mixture model, SiO$_x$ consists of a mixture of two phases, Si and SiO$_2$. A 2D structural image of nonstoichiometric HfO$_x$, regarding the IM, is presented in Fig. 20.6A. According to this model, the filament in hafnium oxide consists of a mixture of metallic hafnium and hafnium suboxide HfO$_y$ clusters sprinkled into HfO$_2$. Fig. 20.6C is an energy diagram of HfO$_x$ in the IM model. According to this diagram, spatial fluctuations of HfO$_x$ chemical composition lead to spatial fluctuations in local band gap width. The maximal fluctuation of the energy scale is equal to the HfO$_2$ band gap width of $E_{\text{g}}=5.6$ eV [38]. The

work function of metallic hafnium is 3.9 eV. The maximal fluctuation scale of the HfO$_x$ conduction band is 1.8 eV, which equals the electron barrier height of Hf/HfO$_2$. The maximal fluctuation scale of the HfO$_x$ valence band is 3.8 eV, the hole barrier height of Hf/HfO$_2$ (Fig. 20.6B).

The large-scale fluctuations of the bottom of conduction band $E_c$ and the top of valence band $E_v$ are close to the model developed by Éfros and Shklovskii [39,40], as shown in Fig. 20.6D. The charge transport in such electron systems can be described according to percolation. This model assumes that excited electrons are thrown to flow level $E^\text{e}$ and, rounding a random potential, they transfer the charge. The hole conductivity is realized through the excitation of electrons with energy $E^\text{h}$ to the Fermi level, with the formation of a hole-type quasi-particle with a further charge transfer according to the quasi-particle obtained. In other words, to be involved in the transport processes, electrons and holes must overcome energy thresholds ($W^{\text{e,h}}$ here and $W^\text{e} \neq W^\text{h}$ in general).

The current–voltage characteristics are exponentials [39]:

$$
I=I_{0}(T) \exp \left(\frac{\left(C e F a V_{0}^{\nu}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\nu}}}{k T}\right), \tag{20.2}
$$

$$
I_{0}(T) \sim \exp \left(-\frac{W}{k T}\right), \tag{20.3}
$$

$$
\begin{gathered}
k T\left(\frac{k T}{V_{0}}\right)^{\nu} \ll e F a \ll V_{0}, \\
k T \ll V_{0},
\end{gathered} \tag{20.4}
$$

where $a$ is the space scale of fluctuations, $V_0$ is the energy fluctuation amplitude, $C$ is the numeric constant, and $\nu$ is the critical index. The constants' values were derived from Monte Carlo simulations and evaluated at $C \sim 0.25$ [40], and $\nu=0.9$ [39]. The fitting procedure for LRS simulations by the percolation model in Eq. (20.2) return the combination value as $CaV_0^\nu = 0.45$ nm eV$^\nu$, which corresponds to $V_0=1.9$ eV when $a=1$ nm and $C=0.25$. Simulations in preexponent $I_0(T)$ (Eq. 20.2) correspond to the percolation threshold of $W \approx 1$ eV. Assuming that $W \leq V_0 \leq 1.8$ eV, one can estimate the space size of nanoscale fluctuations as $a \approx 1-2$ nm. The found values satisfy the model limits in Eq. (20.4).

We also attempted to simulate the LRS regarding phonon-assisted tunneling between traps, as we did for HRS. The results are shown in Fig. 20.1A by a *dashed green line* (light gray in print versions). Using the same values for energy and effective mass obtained from HRS simulations, we found that the results from the LRS simulations and the experimental data were in very good agreement using the value of $N_{\text{LRS}}=1.1 \times 10^{22}$ cm$^{-3}$. We understand that the trap concentration is close to the density of atoms in metals $\sim 10^{22}$ cm$^{-3}$, and that considering vacancies with such a high concentration is inappropriate; hence, the $N_{\text{LRS}}$ value obtained is outside the model limits. However, surprisingly, phonon-assisted tunneling between traps formally describes LRS conduction.

The previous experiments in charge transfer have demonstrated that hafnium oxide conductivity is bipolar (or two-band) [41–44]; electrons are injected from a negatively shifted

contact in the dielectric, and holes are injected from a positively shifted electrode in the dielectric. $\text{HfO}_2$ conductivity in an HRS was examined in these previous experiments. In our model, the filament conductivity in LRS can also be studied using electrons and holes. For reasons of simplicity, the current study was limited to considering monopolar electron conductivity both in the HRS and LRS.

### 20.6 Conclusion

To sum up, we examined the transport mechanisms of hafnia, demonstrating that transport in an HRS is described according to phonon-assisted tunneling between traps. Simulating the current–voltage characteristics of this model, and comparing experimental data with calculations, revealed the energy parameters of the traps in hafnia: the thermal excitation energy of 1.25 eV and the optical excitation energy of 2.5 eV. Oxygen vacancies and the vacancy-conditioned luminescence band of 2.7 eV were identified in the current study by using hafnium oxide photoelectron spectroscopy and quantum–chemical simulations. The photoelectron valence band spectra in stoichiometric $\text{HfO}_2$ and slightly nonstoichiometric $\text{HfO}_{x<2}$ were investigated. The photoelectron spectroscopy data revealed electron states in the $\text{HfO}_x$ band gap. Quantum mechanical simulation indicated that these states are conditioned by oxygen vacancies. These simulations established that the cathodoluminescence band of 2.7 eV in $\text{HfO}_x$ and the excitation peak of 5.2 eV are also conditioned by oxygen vacancies. These results facilitated the determination that oxygen vacancies act as charge-carrier traps. We also demonstrated that charge transport in LRS is described according to the percolation model in electron systems exhibiting potential large-scale fluctuations. Our results shed light on the processes in ReRAM, and are useful for producing higher-quality ReRAM devices in the future.

Recently, it has been demonstrated that $\text{HfO}_x$-based ReRAM elements exhibit intermediate or multilevel states between HRS and RLS. The study of the transport mechanisms in these states is beyond the scope of the current work, and is a subject of further research.
