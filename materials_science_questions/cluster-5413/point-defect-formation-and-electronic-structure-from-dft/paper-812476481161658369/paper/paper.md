## ABSTRACT

In this study, perovskite oxides $SrFe_{1-x}Cu_{x}O_{3-\delta}$ are systematically evaluated as potential cathodes for intermediate temperature solid oxide fuel cells. Cu doping is found to decrease the unit cell volume, while the average valence of iron and the oxygen vacancy content increase steadily in $SrFe_{1-x}Cu_{x}O_{3-\delta}$. The conductivity relaxation test proves that the higher the doping amount of Cu, the larger the $D_{chem}$ and $k_{chem}$ values of the material. Among the copper doped materials, $SrFe_{0.7}Cu_{0.3}O_{3-\delta}$ shows the best catalytic performance for oxygen reduction reaction. The polarization resistance is $0.107\ \Omega\ cm^{2}$, and the peak power density reaches $858\ mW\ cm^{-2}$ at $700\ ^{\circ}C$. Furthermore, the charge transfer process is identified to be the rate control step of the oxygen reduction reaction on this cathode. Finally, the first-principles computation result confirms that introducing Cu reduces the formation energy of oxygen vacancies, which yields favorable conditions for producing oxygen vacancy in Cu-doped $SrFeO_{3}$.

## 1. Introduction

Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are a kind of green energy technology that could be used in power generation, thermoelectric reuse, transportation, space navigation and many other fields, due to their wide fuel adaptability, high energy conversion efficiency and zero pollution [1-4]. One of the key research directions for SOFCs is to develop cathode materials with excellent performance at reduced operation temperatures. In this research field, cobalt based perovskite oxides, mainly $Ba_{0.5}Sr_{0.5}Co_{0.8}Fe_{0.2}O_{3-\delta}$, $La_{0.6}Sr_{0.4}Co_{0.2}Fe_{0.8}O_{3-\delta}$ and $Sm_{0.5}Sr_{0.5}CoO_{3-\delta}$ exhibit high electrochemical performance in the intermediate temperature solid oxide fuel cells (IT-SOFCs) [7–9]. However, these Co based cathodes have the intrinsic disadvantages of high thermal expansion coefficient (TEC), poor stability and high cost, which limit their practical application. Therefore, developing Co-free cathodes with high electrocatalytic activity remains to be important issues in IT-SOFCs [10,11].

In recent years, Fe-based perovskite-type oxide $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ has attracted widespread interest due to its high oxygen ion and electron mixed conductivity and satisfactory thermodynamic stability under reduction conditions. It has potential applications in the fields of oxygen permeation membranes, oxygen sensors and SOFC [12,13]. Synthesis of single-phase stoichiometric $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ has always been a challenge, because the crystal structure and electrochemical properties of $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ are closely related to the ordering of oxygen vacancy in the lattice. The value of $\delta$, which is the oxygen deficiency in $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$, decides the oxygen vacancy ordering that leads to different crystal structures, including cubic/tetragonal perovskite and orthorhombic brownmillerite. Most probably, the oxygen vacancies in the cubic phase $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ are disordered, whereas in the tetragonal and orthorhombic phase they are ordered [14–17]. However, the tendency of forming ordered oxygen vacancy is not favorable for practical applications. It hinders the transport of oxygen ions, and decreases both the mobility and concentration of hole carriers [18,19]. Consequently, many studies have attempted to substitute Fe with aliovalent elements to maintain the high symmetry cubic phase $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$. For example, the cubic phase $SrFe_{1-x}Ti_{x}O_{3-\delta}$, $SrFe_{1-x}Nb_{x}O_{3-\delta}$, $SrFe_{1-x}Si_{x}O_{3-\delta}$, and $SrFe_{1-x}Zr_{x}O_{3-\delta}$ have been reported as promising IT-SOFC cathode materials [20–23].

By survey the literature, it is found that most of the dopants in $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ are high valence state elements (such as $Ti^{4+}$, $Nb^{5+}$, $W^{6+}$ et al. [24–26]). It is more interesting to substitute Fe with low valence state elements to generate oxygen vacancies, at the same time maintaining the cubic phase $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$. Recently Zhang et al. prepared a series of doped $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ materials. They found that Cu dopant is better than Co, Ni and Cr to enhance the oxygen transport properties of $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ material [27]. This result indicates that Cu doped $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ material is a potential cathode for IT-SOFCs. In this study, $SrFe_{1-x}Cu_{x}O_{3-\delta}$ series materials are synthesized, and the influence of Cu doping on the thermal expansion conductivity and electrochemical properties of the material are discussed in detail.

## 2. Experimental

### 2.1. Material synthesis

$SrFe_{1-x}Cu_{x}O_{3-\delta}$ (SFCx) cathode powders were synthesized by glycine-nitrate combustion method. The analytically pure metal nitrates Cu $(NO_{3})_{2}\cdot5H_{2}O$, $Sr(NO_{3})_{2}$ and $Fe(NO_{3})_{3}\cdot9H_{2}O$ were dissolved in appropriate amount of deionized water, to which glycine was added according to the molar ratio of glycine to the total metal ions 2:1. The obtained transparent and clear solution was heated until spontaneous combustion occurs to obtain the fluffy black ash. The ash then was heated in air at $1000\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 12 h to obtain the target product.

### 2.2. Cell fabrication

CGO (Ningbo SuoFuRen Energy Co., Ltd.) powder was pressed into disc at 220 MPa, and sintered at $1400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 12 h to obtain a compact CGO electrolyte pellet with 15 mm diameter and 0.6 mm thickness. For the construction of symmetric cells, the SFCx cathode powder was mixed with terpineol containing 5% ethyl cellulose to obtain viscous cathode ink. A symmetrical cell with an electrode area of $0.36\ \text{cm}^2$ (thickness is about $25\ \mu\text{m}$) was obtained by coating the ink uniformly and symmetrically on both sides of the CGO pellet. For the three-electrode cell preparation, the cathode ink was painted on one side of the CGO pellet to act as working electrode (WE). The Ag-paste was painted on the opposite side in symmetric configuration to form the counter electrode (CE). Ag wire was used as reference electrode (RE) and put on the same side of the working electrode. The cell was then sintered at $900\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 3 h to complete the preparation procedure. In order to evaluate the possible applications of SFCx cathode on IT-SOFCs, the anode-supported single cell was further prepared. First the anode-supported half-cell NiO-YSZ|YSZ|CGO was obtained from Ningbo SuoFuRen Energy Co., Ltd., where NiO-YSZ is the anode, YSZ is the electrolyte, and CGO is the buffer layer. The SFC0.3 cathode ink was then uniformly coated on the CGO buffer layer, and sintered at $900\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ for 3 h to form cathode area of $0.36\ \text{cm}^2$.

### 2.3. Characterization

The phases of the powder were characterized by Bruker D8-advance X-ray powder diffractometer. The X-ray source was Kα ray of Cu target ($\lambda=1.5418\ \mathring{\text{A}}$). The voltage of the X-ray tube was 40 kV and the current was 10 mA. The scanning angle range was $20$–$80^{\circ}$ and the scanning step was $0.02^{\circ}$. The XRD data was analyzed to obtain the structural parameters by Rietveld refinement method using Rietica program. The detailed structural information of the samples was obtained by the combination of high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping with a JEOL F200 electron microscope. The spacing of the lattice fringe and fast Fourier transform (FFT) of HRTEM micrographs were calculated with the software Digital Micrograph 1.8.3 (Gatan). The average valence state of Fe and the nonstoichiometric ratio of oxygen ($\delta$) in SFCx materials were determined with two methods: iodometric titration method and X-ray photoelectron spectrum (XPS) measurement. XPS was collected by Kratos axis ultra DLD photoelectron spectrometer equipped with Al Ka (1486.6 eV) radiation source. The iodometric titration experiments were performed according to the reported procedure in literature [28,29]. The $\delta$ values at high temperatures were obtained with thermogravimetric data according to the formula:

$$
\delta=\frac{\text{M} \times \text{m}0 - (\text{M} - 15.9994 \times \delta0) \times \text{m}}{15.9994 \times \text{m}0} \tag{1}
$$

where $m_0$ and $m$ are the initial mass and mass at high temperatures, $M$ is the molar mass of the test material, and $\delta_0$ is the non-stoichiometric oxygen ratio at room temperature.

The conductivity of SFCx was measured by DC four probe methods with Keithley 2400 digital source meter in the temperature range of 100–800 °C. The thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) of SFCx material was measured using a dilatometer (SETARAM Setsys Evolution 16/18) in the temperature range of 50–800 °C. The electrochemical performance of SFCx cathode was studied by AC impedance spectroscopy (Autolab PGSTAT302 N electrochemical workstation). The test frequency range was $10^{-2}$ Hz-1 MHz, and the temperature range was 500–700 °C. The electrochemical impedance spectrum (EIS) of data was analyzed using the Z-view software. The oxygen partial pressure was controlled by gas mass flow meter. The gases used were high-purity oxygen (99.99%) and nitrogen (99.99%). The test range of oxygen partial pressure was 0.02–0.21 atm. The output performance of the anode supported single cell was tested by IM6ex, ZAHNER, with humidified $\text{H}_2$ (containing about 3% volume fraction of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) as fuel gas and static air as oxidation gas. The DC polarization curves were measured by chronoamperometry method [30]. The cathode overpotential was calculated according to the expression:

$$
\eta_{\text{WE}}=\Delta U_{\text{WR}}-iR_{\text{el}} \tag{2}
$$

in which $\eta_{\text{WE}}$ was the cathode overpotential, $\Delta U_{\text{WR}}$ was the potential difference between the working electrode and the reference electrode, $i$ was the current flowing through the cell, and $R_{\text{el}}$ was the electrolyte

resistance.

### 2.4. Computational methods
The periodic density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP). The exchange-correlation interaction was considered using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew-Burke- Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was applied with Sr ([Kr] $5s^2$), Fe ([Ar] $3d^6 4s^2$), Cu ([Ar] $3d^{10} 4s^1$), and O ($1s^2 2s^2$ $2p^4$) to solve the interaction between ionic core electrons and valence electrons. The $1 \times 2$ surface with a four-layer slab was built to act as the surface model to explore the impact of Cu doping on the oxygen vacancy formation (both on surface and in the bulk of the material). A vacuum spacing of $15\ \text{Å}$ along the c direction was placed on the top layer to avoid the inter-slab interaction between two neighboring cells. The bottom two layers (FeO₂ layer and SrO layer) were fixed, while the top two layers were fully relaxed. 400 eV kinetic energy cutoff, $8 \times 4 \times 2$ k-point grid, and $0.05\ \text{eV}\ \text{Å}^{-1}$ force convergence threshold were used for the geometry relaxations. The oxygen vacancy formation energy ($E_{V,O}$) of $\text{SrFe}_{1-x}\text{Cu}_x\text{O}_3$ was calculated following the reported method by Jiang and co-workers [31], according to the expression $E_{V,O}=E_{[SFC-slab-V-O]} + 1/2E_{[O2]}-E_{[SFC-slab-perfect]}$, where $E_{[SFC-slab-V-O]}$ and $E_{[SFC-slab-perfect]}$ represent the calculated electronic energies of SFC materials with and without oxygen vacancies, respectively. $E_{[O2]}$ is the predicted energy of triplet $\text{O}_2$ in a $10\ \text{Å}$ cubic box.

## 3. Results and discussion
### 3.1. Phase structure and chemical compatibility
Fig. 1a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of SFCx ($x=0.1$, 0.2 and 0.3). All the samples are single-phase materials and the major diffraction peaks can be indexed to primitive cubic perovskite with space group $Pmm$, in accordance with the standard data (JCPDS PDF No. 40-0905). For the sample with $x=0.4$, some impurity peaks could be found in the XRD patterns. As comparison, $\text{SrFeO}_{3-\delta}$ is found to crystallize in tetragonal phase (Fig. S1). To obtain more detailed information about the crystal structure of the synthesized SFCx, the typical Rietveld refinement result of SFC0.3 is presented in Fig. 1b, and the cell parameters of SFCx are listed in Table S1. It can be seen that the cell volume decreases gradually with the increase doping amount of Cu. This is possible, when the low-valence $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ is doped into $\text{SrFeO}_{3-\delta}$, part of $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ will be converted to $\text{Fe}^{4+}$ due to the charge neutrality requirement. It is known that the radius of $\text{Fe}^{4+}$ is smaller than $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ [32], which leads to the shrinkage of the unit cell volume. The similar result has been reported before in the $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ doped $\text{La}_{0.8}\text{Sr}_{0.2}\text{Fe}_{1-x}\text{Cu}_x\text{O}_{3-\delta}$ [33]. Fig. 1c shows the XRD pattern of SFCx-CGO mixture that has been pre-sintered at $1100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ in air for 24 h. All the diffraction peaks can be attributed to SFCx and CGO materials, and there is no impurity phase formed. This result proves the good chemical compatibility of these two materials at $1100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

In order to further confirm that copper doping stabilizes the cubic phase $\text{SrFe}_{1-x}\text{Cu}_x\text{O}_{3-\delta}$, the HRTEM images of SFC0.3 and $\text{SrFeO}_{3-\delta}$ are collected and the corresponding phases are identified (Fig. 1d-f and Fig. S2). The well-defined lattice fringes can be observed from the HRTEM image of SFC0.3 (Fig. 1e). The measured lattice fringe spaces are 0.386 nm and 0.273 nm, which match well with the (100) and (110) diffraction planes of the cubic SFC0.3 that determined by XRD analysis. This conclusion is confirmed by the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) image along the [00]- zone axis with the relevant (100) and (110) lattice planes that marked in Fig. 1f. The TEM-EDX mappings show clearly the homogeneous distributions of Sr, Fe and Cu elements in SFC0.3, indicating the successful Cu-doping in the SFCx material

### 3.2. XPS analysis of SFCx
Fig. 2a shows the typical O1s XPS spectra of SFCx. The peak at 531.3-531.6 eV is attributed to surface adsorbed oxygen $O_{ads}$, while the other one with binding energy at 528.4-528.9 eV belongs to lattice oxygen $O_{lat}$ [34]. The area percentage values of $O_{ads}/O_{lat}$ are summarized in Table 1. It is worth noting that SFC0.3 has the largest proportion of $O_{ads}/O_{lat}$ (3.89), which indicates that SFC0.3 oxide has the strongest oxygen absorption capacity [35]. Fig. 2b shows the typical Fe $2p_{3/2}$ XPS spectrum of SFCx. This spectrum can be fitted into two peaks. The peak with binding energy at 712.2 eV belongs to $Fe^{4+}$, and the peak with binding energy at 710.7 eV belongs to $Fe^{3+}$ [36]. The area percentage ratios of $Fe^{4+}/Fe^{3+}$ are presented in Table 1. It can be seen that with the increase of Cu doping concentration, the content of $Fe^{4+}$ increases gradually. The average valence of Fe in SFC0.1, SFC0.2 and SFC0.3 is +3.58, +3.69 and +3.79, respectively. Considering the charge neutrality requirement of the Cu doped material, the corresponding $\delta$ values of SFC0.1, SFC0.2 and SFC0.3 are calculated to be 0.289, 0.324 and 0.374 respectively, which indicates that Cu doping leads to the gradual increase of oxygen vacancy concentration.

### 3.3. The thermal expansion and conductivity of SFCx
In order to maintain the high temperature thermal compatibility of the cell components, the cathode materials should exhibit comparable thermal expansion behavior with electrolytes. The thermal expansion curves of SFCx materials are therefore measured, and the corresponding thermal expansion coefficient values (TECs) are calculated and listed in Table S2. Clearly all the samples show thermal expansion behavior with the increase of temperature, and an inflexion point is observed in the expansion curve around $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ (Fig. S3). The TECs above $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ are significantly larger than that below $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. According to the thermogravimetric plots presented in Fig. 3a, SFCx loses lattice oxygen and produces a large number of oxygen vacancies above $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. At the same time, the average valence of Fe in the material gradually decreases, due to the thermal reduction of $Fe^{4+}$ above $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. Therefore, the corresponding iron ion radius ($Fe^{4+}=0.585\ \mathring{A}$, $Fe^{3+}=0.645\ \mathring{A}$) increases, which enlarges the TECs of the material [37-39]. The similar phenomenon has been reported before in the study of $BaFe_{1-x}Cu_{x}O_{3-\delta}$ [40]. Fig. 3b comparably shows the variation of conductivity with temperature for SFCx materials that measured by DC four probe methods. It can be seen that below $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$, the conductivity of the material gradually increases with the increasing temperature, following the thermal-activated small polaron hopping mechanism. The conductivity begins to decrease again with the further increase of temperature above $400\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. The similar conductivity behavior has been observed in Nb, Zr, Si, Ti and Ta doped $SrFeO_{3-\delta}$ materials [20-23,41]. The decrease of conductivity with temperature can be attributed to the loss of lattice oxygen and the thermal reduction of $Fe^{4+}$ at high temperatures. In addition, the conductivity of SFCx increases gradually with the doping of Cu. This can be understood considering that the conductivity of SFCx depends on the carrier concentration and the electron transport process between $Fe^{3+}$-$O^{2-}$ and $Fe^{4+}$. From the results presented in Table 1 and Table S1, it is observed that the cell volume decreases, and the $Fe^{4+}$ content increases with Cu doping. These two factors work together to increase the conductivity of SFCx with Cu doping.

### 3.5. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

Fig. 5a shows the typical EIS of symmetric cell SFC$x$|CGO|SFC$x$ at 700 °C. Clearly some distinctly irregular shaped concave arcs are observed in the impedance spectrum. The difference between the intercept of high frequency and low frequency arcs on the $Z'$ axis corresponds to the total polarization resistance ($R_\mathrm{p}$) of SFC$x$ cathode. The $R_\mathrm{p}$ of SFC0.1, SFC0.2 and SFC0.3 cathodes at 700 °C are 0.140, 0.130 and $0.107\ \Omega\mathrm{cm^2}$, respectively, which are smaller than some reported Fe-based cathodes measured under the same condition, such as $\mathrm{SrFe_{0.9}Nb_{0.1}O_{3-\delta}}$ ($0.29\ \Omega\mathrm{cm^2}$ at 700 °C) [44] and $\mathrm{SrFe_{0.8}W_{0.2}O_{3-\delta}}$ ($0.81\ \Omega\mathrm{cm^2}$, at 700 °C) [23]. In addition, the activation energy ($E_\mathrm{a}$) of the SFC$x$ cathode is calculated from the corresponding Arrhenius plots (Fig. S4). For $x=0.1,0.2$ and 0.3, the activation energies are 1.21, 1.17 and 1.20 eV, respectively. It can be seen that Cu doping significantly improves the catalytic performance of the electrode. This may be due to the fact that Cu doping increases the concentration of oxygen vacancies in the material, thus improves the transport performance of oxygen ions. Fig. 5b shows the DRT analysis results of the EIS spectrum for SFC$x$ cathode. Three typical peaks ($P_\mathrm{LF}$, $P_\mathrm{MF}$ and $P_\mathrm{HF}$) can be observed in the DRT curve from low frequency to high frequency for SFC0.1 cathode, which indicates three electrode reaction processes occur on SFC0.1 cathode. It is observed that the peaks at middle and low frequency ($P_\mathrm{LF}$ and $P_\mathrm{MF}$) diminish gradually with Cu doping. Especially for the SFC0.3 cathode, the peak at low frequency almost disappears, whereas the peaks at high frequency side ($P_\mathrm{HF}$) remain almost unchanged for the three Cu-doped cathodes. This result demonstrates that Cu doping has a great effect on the cathode reactions at middle and low frequency regions. It is known that there are several conjunctive reaction processes on cathode for ORR reaction, including the oxygen surface diffusion, adsorption/dissociation and charger transfer reactions. Clearly two peaks could be found in the DRT plots, indicating two semicircles existed in the EIS spectrum. The polarization resistance increases gradually with the decrease of $P_{\mathrm{O}2}$, showing that the oxygen partial pressure in the test atmosphere has a great influence on the ORR process. The EIS spectra are fitted with an equivalent circuit $R_{\mathrm{ohm}}-(R_{\mathrm{HF}}-\mathrm{CPE}_{\mathrm{HF}})-(R_{\mathrm{LF}}-\mathrm{CPE}_{\mathrm{LF}})$. The fitting elements have their normal meanings, as that explained in Fig. S5. The polarization resistance ($R_{\mathrm{P}}$) is the summation of $R_{\mathrm{HF}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{LF}}$. Fig. 6b shows the change of $R_{\mathrm{P}}$ versus $P_{\mathrm{O}2}$. Clearly liner relationship between $\log R_{\mathrm{P}}$ and $\log P_{\mathrm{O}2}$ can be observed from the plots.

Generally, the variation of polarization resistance with oxygen partial pressure can be described by the following relationship:
$$R_{\mathrm{i}}=k(P_{\mathrm{O}2})^{-n} \tag{3}$$
where $R_{\mathrm{i}}$ represents the high-frequency ($R_{\mathrm{HF}}$) and low-frequency ($R_{\mathrm{LF}}$) resistance, respectively. $k$ is the independent constant. The ORR process on the electrode can be determined according to the value of $n$ [48,49]:
$$n=1, O_{2}(g) \leftrightarrow O_{2, a d s} \tag{4}$$
(The molecular oxygen adsorption and diffusion at the electrode surface)
$$n=1/2, O_{2, a d s} \leftrightarrow 2O_{a d s} \tag{5}$$
(The oxygen surface adsorption-dissociation)
$$n=3/8, O_{TPB}+e^{\prime} \leftrightarrow O_{TPB}^{-} \tag{6}$$
$$n=1/8, O_{TPB}^{-}+e^{\prime} \leftrightarrow O_{TPB}^{2-} \tag{7}$$
(The charge transfer reaction at the three-phase interface)
$$n=0, O_{TPB}^{2-}+V_{O}^{\cdot \cdot} \leftrightarrow O_{O}^{x}$$
(The ionic oxygen transfers from TPB region to the electrolyte).

The corresponding $n$ values for $R_{\mathrm{HF}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{LF}}$ are 0.32 and 1.0, respectively. Therefore, it concludes that the reaction process corresponding to the high frequency arc is the charge transfer process at TPB, while the low frequency arc corresponds to the diffusion process of gaseous oxygen in the porous electrode. Because $R_{\mathrm{HF}}$ is always larger than $R_{\mathrm{LF}}$ in the test range of $P_{\mathrm{O}2}$, the charge transfer process at TPB is identified to be the rate control step of ORR reaction on SFC0.3 cathode.

### 3.6. Single cell performance with SFC0.3 cathode and the polarization behavior of SFC0.3

To check the possible application of SFC0.3 as cathode in IT-SOFCs, the anode-supported single cell NiO-YSZ|YSZ|CGO|SFC0.3 is fabricated, and the output performances are evaluated at different temperatures (Fig. 7a). ### 3.7. First-principles calculation

To understand the doping effects of Cu to the promoted oxygen reduction activity on $SrFe_{1-x}Cu_{x}O_{3}$ cathode, the oxygen vacancy formation energies $(E_{V,O})$ are further calculated based on the first-principles DFT calculation. Fig. 8 schematically presents the crystal structures of $SrFeO_{3}$ and $SrFe_{0.75}Cu_{0.25}O_{3}$, respectively. Considering the cubic symmetry of the material, there are two kinds of oxygen vacancies in $SrFeO_{3}$. The $V_{O1}$ is the oxygen vacancy between Fe-Fe in the surface layer, and $V_{O2}$ is the oxygen vacancy in the second layer. For the Cu doped material, two additional oxygen vacancies could be found. The $V_{O3}$ locates in between $FeO_{6}$ and $CuO_{6}$ octahedrons in the first layer, whereas $V_{O4}$ is a possible oxygen vacancy in the second layer, next to Cu ion along C direction. The calculation results (Fig. 8c) show that the $E_{V,O}$ is around 2.0 eV for $SrFeO_{3}$ material, and it decreases sharply to 0.9 eV for the Cu doped $SrFe_{0.75}Cu_{0.25}O_{3}$. This result proves that Cu doping can effective promote the formation of oxygen vacancy in $SrFeO_{3}$. The result also indicates that oxygen vacancy prefer to form near Cu ions, because the $E_{V,O}$ of $V_{O3}$ is much smaller than that of $V_{O1}$. Therefore the oxygen ions diffusion and transport processes could be greatly improved in the Cu-doped materials.
