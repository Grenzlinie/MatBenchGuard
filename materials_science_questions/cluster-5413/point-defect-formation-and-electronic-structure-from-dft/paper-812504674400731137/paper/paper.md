# Rewritable High-Mobility Electrons in Oxide Heterostructure of Layered Perovskite/Perovskite

Xiaofeng Chen, Tingting Zhang, Yang Yu, Xiangbin Cai, Tianyi Gao, Tianwei Zhang, Haoying Sun, Chenyi Gu, Zhengbin Gu, Ye Zhu, Jian Zhou,* Yuefeng Nie,* and Xiaoqing Pan

Cite This: ACS Appl. Mater. Interfaces 2021, 13, 7812−7821

![](./images/812504674400731137_1.jpg)

**ABSTRACT:** Perovskite oxide $SrTiO_3$ can be electron-doped and exhibits high mobility by introducing oxygen vacancies or dopants such as Nb or La. A reversible after-growth tuning of high mobility carriers in $SrTiO_3$ is highly desired for the applications in high-speed electronic devices. Here, we report the observation of tunable high-mobility electrons in layered perovskite/perovskite $(Sr_{n+1}Ti_nO_{3n+1}/SrTiO_3)$ heterostructure. By use of $Sr_{n+1}Ti_nO_{3n+1}$ as the oxygen diffusion barrier, the oxygen vacancy concentration near the interface can be reversibly engineered by high-temperature annealing or infrared laser heating. Because of the identical elemental compositions (Sr, Ti, and O) throughout the whole heterostructure, interfacial ionic intermixing is absent, giving rise to an extremely high mobility (exceeding $55000\ \mathrm{cm^2\ V^{-1}\ s^{-1}}$ at 2 K) in this type of oxide heterostructure. This layered perovskite/perovskite heterostructure provides a promising platform for reconfigurable high-speed electronic devices.

**KEYWORDS:** high mobility, oxide heterostructure, oxygen vacancies, layered perovskite, rewritable control

## ■ INTRODUCTION

$SrTiO_3$ (STO)-based epitaxial heterostructures host a variety of exotic phenomena and have attracted considerable attention over the past decades. $^{1−10}$ As high electron mobility is essential for the applications in electronic devices, numerous methods have been reported to create conductive STO thin films or STO-based heterostructures and enhance the electron mobility, including strain engineering, $^{11−13}$ complex interfacial effects, $^{14−18}$ and optimization of the density and types of dopants. $^{19−31}$ In most cases, the cation dopants and carrier concentration are fixed and not tunable after growth. In contrast, the oxygen-vacancy-induced conductivity is expected to have greater tunability since oxygen vacancies are rather mobile and can be modulated after growth easily. High-temperature reduction in a vacuum or in hydrogen, $^{32,33}$ low-energy Ar ion bombardment, $^{34}$ reactive ion etching, $^{35}$ and surface scratching $^{36}$ have been employed to tune the oxygen vacancies and carrier concentrations in bulk STO or STO-based heterostructures. Nonetheless, the electron mobility values obtained by these methods are not very high, and the sample surface may be damaged in some cases. Moreover, in the case of bare STO, the oxygen-vacancy-induced charge carriers cannot be preserved in ambient conditions without a protection layer. A new type of oxide heterostructure with robust and high mobility created by clean, nondestructive, and reversible after-growth modulation method is of vital significance for the applications in high-mobility devices but is still lacking to date.

Here, we develop a new type of nonpolar/nonpolar heterostructure to achieve rewritable high-mobility oxygen vacancy doping layer between the Ruddlesden−Popper layered perovskite $Sr_{n+1}Ti_nO_{3n+1}$ and perovskite STO. The formation energy and diffusion barrier of oxygen vacancies in the $Sr_{n+1}Ti_nO_{3n+1}$ layer are properly designed to allow the effective after-growth creation, preservation, and removal of oxygen vacancies and charge carriers near the interface. ■ RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The structure design and regulation mechanism of rewritable conducting channel are schematically illustrated in Figure 1. For bare STO, oxygen vacancies and charge carriers are created under high temperature in a vacuum since STO is known to be prone to give out oxygen atoms and form oxygen vacancies.²⁴,³⁷ Without a protection layer, however, the oxygen vacancies and charge carriers are not stable and easily refilled in ambient conditions. Note that the oxygen refill process can even take place in an ultrahigh vacuum which will be discussed later in the paper. In contrast, with a proper capping layer, oxygen vacancies and charge carriers can be created and preserved near the interface through an annealing cycle. As shown in Figure 1b, under an elevated temperature in a vacuum, the capping layer can form oxygen vacancies and open leaking channels of oxygen atoms to create oxygen vacancies at the top part of STO and all other surfaces around STO. Upon cooling, oxygen vacancies in the capping layer are refilled by dilute residual oxygen atoms, which close the leaking channels and preserve the charge carriers near the interface in ambient conditions. Without the capping protection, the oxygen vacancies around the other surfaces will disappear. Further annealing the sample in an oxygen atmosphere at moderate temperature can refill the oxygen vacancies and erase the conducting channel. As such, the conducting channel can be erased and rewritten reversibly in this designed structure. Moreover, this reversible modulation of conductivity at STO side can also be done by laser heating (Figure 1c).

There are some considerations on the materials selection of the capping layer. (1) To achieve high mobility, the interface should be of high crystalline quality and the cation intermixing defects should be minimized. (2) To form oxygen vacancies at the STO side near the interface, the capping layer should open leaking channels of oxygen atoms at high temperature. Accordingly, the formation energy and diffusion barrier of oxygen vacancies in the capping layer should not be too high. (3) To preserve oxygen vacancies at STO side, the formation energy and diffusion barrier of oxygen vacancies in the capping layer should be larger than those in STO so that the diffusion path can be first closed, leaving oxygen vacancies at the STO side during the cooling process. (4) The capping layer should not be conducting after the annealing process.

In addition, the in-plane lattice constant of $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ is similar to that of STO, so the epitaxial $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ film suffers little strain from the STO substrate and can be grown with high crystalline quality. $^{38-40}$ Moreover, intriguing physical properties of $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ films can be efficiently tuned by varying the $n$ value. $^{41-43}$

To further investigate whether $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ can act as an effective valve to control the formation of oxygen vacancies at the STO side near the interface, we calculated the formation energies and diffusion barriers of oxygen vacancies in STO and $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ by first-principles density functional theory (DFT). As shown in Figures 2c and 2d, the energy barrier of oxygen vacancies to diffuse through the rock-salt SrO-SrO layers ($E_{\text{b}} = 1.34$ eV) in $Sr_{3}Ti_{2}O_{7}$ ($n = 2$) is much higher than the diffusion barrier in perovskite STO ($E_{\text{b}} = 0.48$ eV), which can serve as a good barrier for our purpose. While higher $n$ members are out of our calculation capability, the calculations for $n = 1, 2, 3$, and $\infty$ members exhibit a clear trend (see the Supporting Information, Table S1). The overall formation energy and diffusion barrier of oxygen vacancies decrease with increasing $n$ values because of the lower density of SrO-SrO rock-salt layers. With a proper $n$ value, the $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ capping layer can satisfy the aforementioned requirements and serve as a suitable barrier to tune the oxygen vacancies and form high-mobility electron doping layer in the $Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$/STO heterostructure.

$Sr_{n+1}Ti_{n}O_{3n+1}$ films were grown on (001) STO substrates by reactive molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) using the method reported previously. $^{39}$ The X-ray $2\theta-\omega$ scan of $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$/STO in Figure 3a shows sharp diffraction peaks of all reflections, manifesting the good crystalline quality of the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ film. The flat step-and-terrace surface and clear Kiessig fringes indicate that the whole film is atomically smooth (Figures S1 and S2). Reciprocal space mapping around the $103_{\text{STO}}$ reflection in Figure 3b shows the same $H$ value for film and substrate, suggesting that the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ film is completely strained on STO substrate without relaxation. The high-resolution TEM image, atom-resolved annular dark field (ADF), and annular bright field (ABF) STEM images as well as the sharp selected area electron diffraction patterns in Figure 3 also show the good film quality, which is comparable with those shown in the literature. $^{40,43}$ Additionally, the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) data show that the interface is free of ionic intermixing because of the similar SrO and $TiO_{2}$ sublayers in the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ film and STO substrate.

After film growth, heterostructures were annealed under low oxygen partial pressure at high temperature to create conducting channel and then quickly cooled to room temperature. To measure the conductivity, four point contacts were made by ultrasonic aluminum wire bonding (left panel of Figure 4a). The resistance is out of the measurement range (>40 M$\Omega$) if the four point contacts are made by pressing on the sample surface with indium foil (right panel of Figure 4a), indicating that the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ layer might be insulating. Additionally, the back side and lateral side of the heterostructure are insulating because of the lack of protection layer and the oxygen atom filling during the cooling process, so the preserved conducting channel exists at the STO side near the interface. To further confirm that oxygen vacancies and charge carriers do not exist in bulk $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$, we also grew the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ film on $(LaAlO_{3})_{0.3}(Sr_{2}AlTaO_{6})_{0.7}$ (LSAT), a substrate that is hard to form oxygen vacancies. In line with our expectation, $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$/LSAT is insulating after high-temperature annealing even under a higher vacuum. The DFT calculations and annealing experiments indicate that oxygen vacancies exist in STO because $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ layer is more difficult to form oxygen vacancies at high temperature and easier to be filled by surrounding oxygen upon cooling compared to STO.

The transport measurements of $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ ($n = 6$)/STO heterostructures were performed by using a Van der Pauw geometry with aluminum wires as the leads. As shown in Figure 4b, the transport properties depend on the annealing condition and the amount of oxygen vacancies created in the sample. For the sample annealed at 800 °C under $1 \times 10^{-6}$ Torr of $O_{2}$, the heterostructure is insulating. Heterostructures annealed at higher temperature exhibit metallic behavior, and the negative slopes in $R_{xy}$-$H$ curves suggest the carriers are n-type (see Figure S3). The best mobility result has been obtained in the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ ($n = 6$)/STO heterostructure after annealing at 920 °C for 12 min, showing a mobility up to $55600$ cm$^{2}$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ at 2 K, which is rather high compared to that of bulk$^{32,33}$ and many STO-based heterostructures. $^{15-17}$ A very-high-mobility STO-based heterostructure at low temperature was observed before. For instance, Chen reported that 2.5 u.c. oxygen-vacancy-deficient $\gamma$-$Al_{2}O_{3}$/STO grown at 600 °C exhibits a mobility value as large as $140000$ cm$^{2}$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$, and such high electron mobility of STO-based oxide materials at low temperatures is generally related to the polarization shielding of the ionized defect scattering centers driven by the large dielectric constant of STO. $^{23}$ There are several important facts that contribute to the high mobility. In our case, the $Sr_{7}Ti_{6}O_{19}$ film has the same building blocks and the same in-plane lattice parameter as the STO substrate, so the ionic intermixing and lattice mismatch are negligible, allowing us to

Further in situ transport measurements in the laser heating experiments indicate that the conducting channel formed at high temperature cannot be preserved (see Figure S5), implying the importance of material selection in forming stable oxygen vacancy doping layer. It should be noted that the transport properties of the heterostructure are closely related to the substrate termination conditions. In the above experiments, all STO substrates were acid-etched and annealed, $^{46}$ exhibiting TiO₂ termination with step-and-terrace like surface morphology. When as-received STO substrate with nonspecific terminations was used, the heterostructure can form a conducting channel at lower annealing temperature but showed a lower residual resistance ratio (see Figure S6), which is most likely due to the more defect-related leaking channels for oxygen vacancies in the $Sr_7Ti_6O_{19}$ layer.

In view of the applications in all-oxide electronic devices, we anticipate to create such a high-mobility heterostructure through infrared laser beam heating as it is a clean, fast, and nondestructive process. Using a homemade infrared laser heating setup (Figure 5a), we performed in situ resistance measurements on $Sr_7Ti_6O_{19}/STO$ and bare $SrTiO_3$ samples using a two-point method. A conducting channel was successfully created and preserved in $Sr_7Ti_6O_{19}/STO$ by infrared laser heating in a vacuum with a background pressure of $\sim 1 \times 10^{-8}$ Torr (Figure 5c). The sample temperature increases when the laser is turned on and cools when the laser is turned off. Initially, the whole sample is insulating, and the resistance decreases as the temperature increases (laser on). Also, at high enough temperature, the reduction process occurs and oxygen vacancies are created, which further decreases the resistance. With the protection of the $Sr_7Ti_6O_{19}$ capping layer, oxygen vacancies can be preserved in the sample, and the resistance does not recover to the pristine high-resistance state as it cools. Depending on the amount of oxygen vacancies (charge carriers) preserved in the system, the sample can exhibit metallic or insulating behavior. As a result, with low oxygen concentration, the sample shows insulating behavior, and the resistance increases during the sample cooling process after the laser is turned off (curve 1 in Figure 5c). After several laser on−off cycles, the accumulated oxygen vacancies are sufficient enough to drive the sample into a metallic state, and the resistance of the sample decreases after the laser is turned off (curve 2 in Figure 5c). By laser heating, high-mobility carriers were achieved in $Sr_7Ti_6O_{19}/STO$ as shown in Figure 5d. To confirm that the metallic behavior is due to the oxygen vacancies instead of the detrapped carriers excited by laser illumination, we also performed a similar laser heating experiment on $Sr_7Ti_6O_{19}/STO$ in ambient conditions. As shown in Figure S7, the sample remains insulating when the laser is turned off, indicating the charge carriers are indeed mainly from oxygen vacancies.

In contrast, oxygen vacancies and charge carriers can be created in bare STO by laser heating in a vacuum, but they fully disappear soon after the laser is turned off (Figure 5b) because of the absence of a protection layer. This in situ experiment indicates that oxygen vacancies created in the STO surface can be quickly refilled with dilute oxygen even in such a high vacuum $(\sim 1 \times 10^{-8}$ Torr). ### EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

**MBE Growth and Film Characterizations.** Epitaxial $Sr_{n+1}Ti_nO_{3n+1}$ films were grown on (001) STO single-crystalline substrates ($10 \times 10 \times 1$ mm³ with miscut angle $<0.1^\circ$) under $1 \times 10^{-6}$ Torr of a mixed oxidant (10% $O_3$ + 90% $O_2$) at 550 °C by reactive molecular beam epitaxy.³⁹ Before deposition, (001) STO substrates were etched in buffered HF acid at room temperature and annealed in oxygen at 950 °C for 80 min to obtain $TiO_2$-terminated step-and-terrace atomically flat surfaces.⁴⁶ During the calibration

process, the fluxes of Sr and Ti were estimated by quartz crystal microbalance (QCM). Then real-time reflective high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) with an electron beam energy of 15 keV was used to further correct the flux ratio between Sr and Ti to be exactly1:1 by stabilizing the RHEED oscillations for more than nine cycles. $^{39}$  The growth time of each $SrO$ layer and $TiO_{2}$ layer was determined by the interval of contiguous oscillation. After calibration, $Sr$ and Ti molecular beams were shuttered in accordance with the required deposition sequence for a series of $n$ values in $Sr_{n+1} Ti_{n} O_{3 n+1}$ . The film thickness of $Sr_{7} Ti_{6} O_{19}$ in this paper is seven unit cells. The surface topography and diffraction peaks were characterized by atomic forcemicroscopy (AFM) and high-resolution X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer, respectively. Temperature-dependent resistance and Hall effects of $Sr_{n+1} Ti_{n} O_{3 n+1} / STO$  heterostructures were measured via the Van der Pauw geometry in a Quantum Design physical property measurement system (PPMS).

High-Temperature Annealing and Laser Heating Process. During the annealing process, the heterostructure was put in the holder and transferred into a MBE vacuum chamber. For creating oxygen vacancies, it was heated to targeted temperature (measured by thermal couple) at a rate of $50^{\circ} C / min$ and maintained the temperature for $12 ~min$ . Subsequently, the sample was cooled to room temperature at a rate of $100^{\circ} C / min$ . In laser heating experiments, the infrared laser wavelength was $1064+10 ~nm$ and the power was $0.1 ~W$ (1\% of the $10 ~W$ full power). During the laser heating process, the vacuum was maintained at the base pressure of $10^{-8}-10^{-9}$ Torr.

Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) Meas- urements. Cross-sectional heterostructures were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) techniques and observed immediately in a high vacuum for the pristine features. Atomic-resolution annular dark- field (ADF) and annular bright-field (ABF) images were acquired by JEOL JEM ARM 200F, equipped with a cold field emission gun and an ASCOR fifth-order probe corrector. Spectrum imaging by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed under $200 kV$  accelerating voltage with a $26 mrad$ convergence angle for the optimal probe condition.

First-Principles DFT Calculations. The formation energies and diffusion barriers of oxygen vacancy migration in Ruddlesden- Popper $Sr_{n+1} Ti_{n} O_{3 n+1}$ and cubic $SrTiO_{3}$ were calculated by the density functional theory in the generalized gradient approximationimplemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code, $^{48,49}$ in which the projected augmented wave method $^{50,51}$ and the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof exchange correlation $^{52}$ were used. The plane-wave cutoff energy was $520 eV$ throughout the calculations. The energy barriers were calculated by the climbing image nudged elastic band method implemented in the VASP and assisted by the transition state tools (VTST). $^{53-55}$ The structure of cubic $SrTiO_{3}$ was fixed at the experimental lattice constant $a=3.905 \AA,^{56}$ and the in-plane lattice constant of Ruddlesden-Popper $Sr_{n+1} Ti_{n} O_{3 n+1}$ was also fixed to be $3.905 \AA$ .

To simulate the isolated oxygen vacancy, one oxygen atom was removed in a $3 \times 3 \times 3$ cubic $SrTiO_{3}$ or in a $3 \times 3 \times 1$ Ruddlesden Popper $Sr_{n+1} Ti_{n} O_{3 n+1}$ unit cell. Then the atomic positions were optimized in these defective supercells. The formation energy of the oxygen vacancy was calculated by the equation

$$
\Delta H=E_{\text {tot }}^{\text {def }}-E_{\text {tot }}+\frac{E_{\mathrm{O}_{2}}}{2}
$$

where $E_{tot }^{def }$ and $E_{tot }$ are the total energy of defective and perfect supercells, while the $E_{O_{2}}$ is the total energy of an oxygen molecule.

## CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we designed a new type of layered perovskite/ perovskite oxide heterostructure to create a rewritable high- mobility conducting channel. With a proper combination of formation energy and diffusion barrier of oxygen vacancies in the heterostructure, the $Sr_{n+1} Ti_{n} O_{3 n+1}$ layer can act as an effective valve to precisely control the formation of oxygen vacancies and charge carriers near the interface in a clean, nondestructive, and reversible manner. With a suitable focused infrared laser system equipped with a cooling platform to realize well-controlled local heating, a high-mobility conduct- ing channel can be written in artificially designed patterns. Our work provides a promising platform to design reconfigurable high-speed electronic devices using functional oxide hetero- structures.
