(Received 11 May 2017; accepted 16 October 2017; published 15 November 2017)

The general tendency in industrial machining and forming operations toward further reduced operation time and increased efficiency requires continuous improvements of protective coatings regarding their tribo-mechanical properties. Alloying arc evaporated $Ti_{1-x}Al_{x}N$ with small amounts of molybdenum (Mo) has shown very promising results to further improve the tribological properties of the industrially widely used Ti-Al-N. Therefore, the authors study in detail the effect of Mo on the thermo-mechanical properties of $Ti_{1-x}Al_{x}N$ coatings, as well as their wear behavior at elevated temperatures. First principle density functional theory (DFT) calculations are conducted for face-centered cubic $c-Al_{1-y}Mo_{y}N$, $c-Ti_{1-y}Mo_{y}N$, and $c-Ti_{1-x-y}Al_{x}Mo_{y}N$ showing increasing energy of formation with increasing Mo incorporation for all structures investigated. However, the thereby (DFT) obtained/calculated lattice parameters for comparable compositions perfectly matches with those of the single-phase face-centered cubic structured $c-Ti_{0.55}Al_{0.42}Mo_{0.03}N$, $c-Ti_{0.53}Al_{0.41}Mo_{0.06}N$, and $c-Ti_{0.50}Al_{0.38}Mo_{0.12}N$ coatings studied. These coatings have an as deposited hardness of about 28 GPa, which can be maintained upon vacuum annealing up to $T_{a}=800^{\circ}C$. At higher temperatures, their hardness decreases as the formation of the weaker w-AlN and $Mo_{3}Al$ phases occurs. In contrast to that, the abrasive wear of Mo-containing $c-Ti_{1-x-y}Al_{x}Mo_{y}N$ during dry-sliding high temperature ball-on-disk tests (against 6mm alumina balls at $700^{\circ}C$) is significantly reduced to $1-2.0\times 10^{-6}\ \text{mm}^3/\text{Nm}$, as compared to the Mo-free $Ti_{0.56}Al_{0.44}N$ counterpart. This is the result of the presence of solid lubricant $MoO_{3}$ phases in the wear tracks of the arc evaporated $c-Ti_{1-x-y}Al_{x}Mo_{y}N$ coatings investigated—confirmed by a combined energy dispersive spectroscopy and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis. Based on their findings, the authors can conclude that the addition of up to 12 at.% Mo (of the metal fraction) to $Ti_{0.56}Al_{0.44}N$ significantly improves their high temperature wear resistance by simultaneously keeping the outstanding coating characteristics of pure $Ti_{1-x}Al_{x}N$. *Published by the AVS*.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Hard protective coatings based on transition metal nitrides are widely used in industrial applications, especially in machining and forming processes. TiN is due to its well-defined and in-depth studied properties still commonly in use, although it was one of the first hard coatings applied for protecting various tool materials. $^{1,2}$ However, due to its limited thermal stability and oxidation resistance, various reports focus on the enhancement through alloying Al. $^{3-8}$ Therefore, Ti-Al-N is nowadays one of the most versatile PVD coatings due to the improved oxidation resistance and the age-hardening effect (included by a spinodal decomposition process of the Al-supersaturated face-centered cubic TiN lattice upon annealing or application at elevated temperatures), which leads to an increased hardness during thermal loading. $^{9}$ Nevertheless, the ever growing demands in forming and machining processes encourage the development of quaternary coating systems to further enhance thermal stability, oxidation resistance, as well as wear performance. $^{10-13}$

Especially, the reduction of the major failure mechanisms (crater and flank wear) during dry high-speed machining requires tailor-made coating systems to control the typical wear phenomena such as abrasion, adhesion, or tribo-oxidation. $^{14}$ Voevodin *et al.* pointed out that a promising approach to gain proper wear performance at elevated temperatures is the *in situ* formation of solid lubricants, e.g., $MoS_{2}$, $V_{2}O_{5}$, or $MoO_{3}$. $^{15-18}$ Easy-shear planes characterize phases for solid lubrication, whereby the so-called Magnéli phases depict a major role for high temperature applications. $^{19,20}$ However, the low melting or even volatile character of the Magnéli phases (e.g., $V_{2}O_{5}$, or $Mo_{n}O_{3n-1}$) is a limiting factor. Therefore, Mo, V, MoN/VN thin films are not suitable for high temperature applications due to the reduction of the

These competing processes, solid lubricant formation and oxidation (formation of an adherent, dense oxide scale), needs to be balanced from kinetic point of view.

The addition of Mo may also enhance the thermal stability and toughness, whereby Mo is known as a creep-resistant material. $^{26,27}$ In addition, a recent study highlighted the outstanding tribological properties of monolithically grown $Ti_{1-x-y}Al_xMo_yN$ coatings at room temperature. $^{23}$

Therefore, we focus our work on the thermal stability, oxidation resistance as well as high temperature tribological behavior of monolithically grown $Ti_{1-x-y}Al_xMo_yN$ coatings prepared by cathodic arc evaporation. For an in-depth understanding how Mo influences the phase formation of $Ti_{1-x}Al_xN$, our experimental results are correlated with *ab initio* calculations.

## II. EXPERIMENT

Cathodic arc evaporated $Ti_{1-x-y}Al_xMo_yN$ coatings were prepared by using $(Ti_{0.5}Al_{0.5})_{1-y}Mo_y$ cathodes (powder-metallurgically manufactured by Plansee Composite Materials GmbH). Four $(Ti_{0.5}Al_{0.5})_{1-y}Mo_y$ cathodes with a diameter of 160 mm containing 0, 2, 5, and 10 at.% of molybdenum were mounted on four out of six arc sources in an Oerlikon Balzers INNOVA industrial deposition facility. Only for the coatings prepared with the $Ti_{0.45}Al_{0.45}Mo_{0.10}$ cathodes, the two remaining arc sources—equipped with pure Ti cathodes—were utilized to realize a very thin TiN adhesion layer prior to the actual deposition process, which lasts for 60 min. During deposition, the arc- and coil-current were set to 150 and 0.5 A, respectively. The deposition temperature, nitrogen pressure, and the DC substrate bias potential—applied to the two-fold rotational carousel—were kept constant at $500\,^\circ\text{C}$, 3.5 Pa, and $-80$ V.

All coatings were deposited on alumina $(Al_2O_3)$ platelets, high-speed steel disks (S600, DIN/EN 1.3343), and steel foil (mild steel, thickness of 0.05 mm). The substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone and ethanol, for 5 min each. Prior to any deposition, the substrates were plasma etched for about 25 min in argon (Ar) atmosphere by utilizing the central beam etching technique (Oerlikon Surface Solutions AG) to ensure a sufficient adhesion.

To avoid substrate interference during annealing treatments, the coatings deposited on low alloyed steel foil were dissolved in concentrated hydrochloric acid and grinded in a conventional ceramic mortar to obtain coating powder material.

A Centorr LF22-2000 vacuum furnace with a maximum operation temperature of about $1600\,^\circ\text{C}$ was used for high temperature vacuum annealing treatments using peak temperatures ($T_\text{a}$) of 600, 800, 1000, 1200, and $1400\,^\circ\text{C}$, respectively (holding time at $T_\text{a}$ of 1 h). The heating rates were set to 20 K/min, whereas cooling was performed passively (inside the furnace, leading to an approximately exponential cooling behavior down to room temperature).

The thermal stability of the coating powder materials was investigated in detail by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) utilizing a NEZTSCH STA 449 F1 Jupiter system equipped with a rhodium oven. The powdered coating materials—placed in a Pt crucible to ensure best thermal conductivity—were heated up in helium atmosphere to a temperature, $T_\text{a}$, of $1500\,^\circ\text{C}$ with heating rates of 20 K/min, followed by a dwell time of 1 min. After that, the samples were cooled down to the deposition temperature with 50 K/min to inhibit undesired phase formation. In addition, the oxidation behavior was investigated by exposing the coatings in a conventional chamber furnace at 850 and $950\,^\circ\text{C}$ in ambient air for 20 h.

The crystal structure and phase formation of the $Ti_{1-x-y}Al_xMo_yN$ coatings (for the coating powder materials) were examined by using a PANalytical X'Pert 2 diffractometer in Bragg-Brentano configuration (equipped with a Cu-$K_\alpha$ radiation source, a Bragg-Brentano high definition mirror and a fixed antiscattering slit).

The morphology of the coatings was studied by a FEGSEM Quanta 200 scanning electron microscope (SEM) in cross-sectional view. The chemical composition of the $Ti_{1-x-y}Al_xMo_yN$ thin films in the as deposited as well as heat treated state was analyzed by energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) line-scans using an EDAX EDS detector.

To investigate the formation of the proposed Magnéli phases (e.g., $Mo_nO_{3n-1}$) after ball-on-disk experiments, a SPECS x-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) equipped with an Al-$K_\alpha$ radiation source ($\mu$Focus 350) and a hemispherical analyzer (WAL-150, angle of acceptance: $60^\circ$) were utilized to clarify the bonding natures. The patterns were evaluated with CASA XPS (with applied sensitivity parameters after Scofield$^{28}$) and the standard databases, $^{29,30}$ enabling the classification of the individual components within the coatings. All spectra were corrected in binding energy to show the main C component (adventitious carbon) at 284.8 eV.

The mechanical properties were investigated by an Ultra-Micro Indentation System (UMIS) equipped with a Berkovich diamond tip (Fischer-Cripps Lab.). Shallow calottes were grinded into the surface of the samples to minimize the effect of coating roughness induced by macroparticles. The resulting load displacement curves were assessed after the method of Oliver and Pharr$^{31}$ to gain the indentation modulus and hardness.

Tribological investigations were performed (in unlubricated condition) on a Nanovea ball-on-disk tribometer

equipped with a high temperature module. Alumina balls with a diameter of 6 mm were utilized as counter bodies and a normal load of 1 N at a radius of 5 mm was applied. The coated high-speed steel disks were tested with a linear speed of 0.1 m/s at temperatures of 700 °C in ambient air (relative humidity of about $40\pm10\%$). This testing procedure was performed two times for verifying the results. The obtained wear tracks were analyzed by the use of a chromatographic, confocal profilometer (Nanovea PS50).

In addition to the experimental studies, the structure and phase stability of ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ coatings was analyzed by *ab initio* calculations applying density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package $^{32\text{–}34}$ code. We used the projector augmented plane waves method to treat the exchange-correlation function as suggested by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof. $^{35}$ To ensure high accuracy (an energy convergence of a few meV/at), all energy cut-offs and k-point meshes were carefully chosen. Furthermore, the energy of formation (${\text{E}}_{\text{f}}$) of all structures investigated [especially the B1, NaCl type face-centered cubic structures of c-AlN, c-TiN, c-TiAlN, c-Ti(Mo)N, c-Al(Mo)N, and c-TiAl(Mo)N] was studied by using the Alloy-Theoretic Automated Toolkit $^{36}$ for $2\times2\times2$ supercells containing 64 atoms. In terms of Mo alloyed structures, 1, 2, and 4 metallic sublattice atoms were substituted by molybdenum resulting in Mo contents of 3, 6, and 12.5 at.%. These contents are chosen based on the experimental compositions of ${\text{Ti}}_{0.55}{\text{Al}}_{0.42}{\text{Mo}}_{0.03}\text{N}$, ${\text{Ti}}_{0.53}{\text{Al}}_{0.41}{\text{Mo}}_{0.06}\text{N}$, and ${\text{Ti}}_{0.50}{\text{Al}}_{0.38}{\text{Mo}}_{0.12}\text{N}$. To respect the influence of vacancies on the Mo content and resulting structures, as suggested by Klimashin *et al.*, $^{37}$ the number of Mo atoms and their adjacent nitrogen vacancies were adjusted.

### III. RESULTS
#### A. Structure
The influence on the energy of formation, ${\text{E}}_{\text{f}}$, of alloying Mo into face-centered cubic (fcc) TiN, AlN, as well as ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x}{\text{Al}}_{x}\text{N}$ (Mo substitutes Ti or Al atoms at the metal sublattice) structures is calculated by DFT. Figure 1(a) presents the Mo-dependent evolution of the lattice parameter and Fig. 1(b) the evolution of ${\text{E}}_{\text{f}}$ for ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ (blue hexagons) and their N-vacant counterparts containing, e.g., one N vacancy for two Mo atoms according to the studies for Mo-N after Ref. 37 (green half-open stars), ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}y}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ (purple half-filled triangles), and ${\text{Al}}_{1\text{-}y}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ (yellow open diamonds). In general, for all structures investigated, the ${\text{E}}_{\text{f}}$ increases with increasing Mo contents. Lattice parameters about 4.17, 4.18, 4.19, and 4.21 Å are obtained for ${\text{Ti}}_{0.5}{\text{Al}}_{0.5}\text{N}$, ${\text{Ti}}_{0.50}{\text{Al}}_{0.47}{\text{Mo}}_{0.03}\text{N}$, ${\text{Ti}}_{0.50}{\text{Al}}_{0.44}{\text{Mo}}_{0.06}\text{N}$, and ${\text{Ti}}_{0.50}{\text{Al}}_{0.37}{\text{Mo}}_{0.13}\text{N}$, respectively. This is in good agreement with *ab initio* calculations of ${\text{Ti}}_{0.5}{\text{Mo}}_{0.5}\text{N}$, $^{38}$ which exhibits an increase of the lattice parameter by about 0.05 Å as compared with TiN. Furthermore, increasing the Mo content within the supercells of ${\text{Al}}_{1\text{-}y}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ leads to an increase of its lattice parameters comparable to the observations for ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$. Contrary, for ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}y}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ the lattice parameter stays nearly constant [see Fig. 1(a)].

The ${\text{E}}_{\text{f}}$ of N-vacant ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}{\text{N}}_{z}$ structures [green half-filled stars in Fig. 1(b)] is slightly less negative than that of the nondefected counterparts [blue hexagons in Fig. 1(b)], indicating that the formation of nitrogen vacancies requires energy. Hence, N-defect structures are energetically not preferred, corresponding to the studies for ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x}{\text{Al}}_{x}\text{N}$ (Ref. 39), see the gray crosses in Fig. 1(b).

The mixing enthalpy for N-vacant c-${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}{\text{N}}_{z}$ (${\text{E}}_{\text{f mix}}^{\text{TiAlMoN-vac}}$) phase is slightly more positive with $y\geq0.03$ as compared to the corresponding nondefected one (${\text{E}}_{\text{f mix}}^{\text{TiAlMoN}}$), see Fig. 1(c). This is in agreement with the above presented results, that the formation of N-vacancies would require energy (for the compositions of ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ studied). We expect, that for higher Mo contents, the formation of N-vacancies becomes preferred, as the energy of formation of face-centered cubic MoN is more negative (hence more stable) if 50% of the N-sublattice is vacant, and ${\text{MoN}}_{0.5}$ is formed. $^{37}$ But more important is that the mixing enthalpy for nondefected c-${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ decreases with increasing Mo content [see Fig. 1(c)]. This suggests that if such supersaturated phases are formed, their driving force for decomposition toward their constituents (c-TiN, c-AlN, and c-MoN) is smaller as compared with ${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x}{\text{Al}}_{x}\text{N}$. This in turn suggests for a less pronounced effect of spinodal decomposition on, e.g., the mechanical properties, like hardness, of c-${\text{Ti}}_{1\text{-}x\text{-}y}{\text{Al}}_{x}{\text{Mo}}_{y}\text{N}$ coatings.

### B. Thermal stability
The thermal stability of the supersaturated phases present in the Ti₁₋ₓ₋ᵧAlₓMoᵧN coatings studied is investigated by x-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of vacuum annealed (substrate free) powdered coating materials. The development of the XRD patterns up to Tₐ = 800 °C indicates the isostructural decomposition of the supersaturated cubic structured Ti₁₋ₓ₋ᵧ AlₓMoᵧN matrix phase toward Al- and Ti-rich cubic structured domains; see Figs. 2(a)–2(c) for Ti₀.₅₅Al₀.₄₂Mo₀.₀₃N, Ti₀.₅₃Al₀.₄₁Mo₀.₀₆N, and Ti₀.₅₀Al₀.₃₈Mo₀.₁₂N, respectively. In agreement with detailed studies on supersaturated c-Ti₁₋ₓAlₓN,⁴²⁻⁴⁴ these XRD developments indicate spinodal decomposition, which suggests for improved mechanical properties at elevated temperatures.⁴⁵,⁴⁶ Here, we need to mention that the pronounced formation of a TiN XRD peak already with Tₐ = 600 °C for Ti₀.₅₀Al₀.₃₈Mo₀.₁₂N [Fig. 2(c)] is due to the used TiN adhesion layer (which is only used for this coating).

The formation of hexagonal structured, wurtzite-type phases can be detected after annealing at Tₐ = 1000 °C (see Fig. 2). A comparable Mo-free Ti₀.₅₆Al₀.₄₄N coating already shows w-AlN formation after annealing at 900 °C—when using the same annealing conditions. Consequently, already the addition of 3 at.% Mo to the metal sublattice can postpone the precipitation of w-AlN. After annealing at 1200 °C, a cubic structured Mo₃Al phase (ICDD: 00-011-0018) can be detected, with increasing intensity for increasing Mo content of the coatings investigated. Even after annealing at Tₐ = 1400 °C, the XRD peaks do not perfectly match the 2θ diffraction positions for c-TiN or w-AlN. All XRD peaks, except those representing the Mo₃Al phase, are shifted toward higher diffraction angles as compared to the standard diffraction angles of c-TiN and w-AlN, which is indicative for the presence of solid solutions. The corresponding developments of the lattice parameters with Tₐ, obtained by evaluating the XRD patterns, are presented in Figs. 3(a)–3(c) for Ti₀.₅₅Al₀.₄₂Mo₀.₀₃N, Ti₀.₅₃Al₀.₄₁Mo₀.₀₆N, and Ti₀.₅₀Al₀.₃₈Mo₀.₁₂N coatings, respectively. For comparison, the lattice parameters of c-TiN (4.24 Å, ICDD: 00-038-1420), c-AlN (4.12 Å, ICDD: 00-025-1495), w-AlN (a = 3.11 Å and c = 4.98 Å, ICDD: 00-025-1133), and Mo₃Al (4.95 Å, ICDD: 00-011-0018) are indicated in Fig. 3 by horizontal lines. The lattice parameter of face-centered cubic structured MoN₀.₅ is 4.16 Å (ICCD: 00-025-1366) and that of the hexagonal closed-packed structured MoN are a = 5.73 Å and c = 5.61 Å (ICCD: 00-025-1367). This suggests for decreasing lattice parameters of face-centered cubic structured Ti₁₋ᵧ MoᵧN_z solid solutions

Whereas in correlation with structural analysis obtained by XRD results, for $\text{Ti}_{0.55}\text{Al}_{0.42}\text{Mo}_{0.03}\text{N}$ coatings the highest stability of all coatings studied is assumed as the highest onset temperature of $1047\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ is observed for the detected mass loss. In addition, the structural analysis obtained after vacuum annealing treatments indicated the same phase composition (c-TiN, $\text{c-Mo}_{3}\text{Al}$, and w-AlN) as proposed in Figs. 2 and 4.

A mass loss of Mo-free $\text{Ti}_{1-x}\text{Al}_{x}\text{N}$ coatings in the temperature range where significant w-AlN formation occurs is often reported in literature. $^{47}$ The massive volume expansion of about $24\%$ of the c- to w-AlN phase transformation $^{48,49}$ will lead to the formation of a significant crack network and provides many high diffusion pathways for nitrogen. While the mass of Mo-free $\text{Ti}_{0.56}\text{Al}_{0.44}\text{N}$ remains nearly constant for $\text{T}_{\text{a}}\geq1300\,^{\circ}\text{C}$, ongoing mass reduction can be detected for the Mo-containing $\text{Ti}_{1-x-y}\text{Al}_{x}\text{Mo}_{y}\text{N}$ coatings. The increasing mass loss rate with increasing Mo contents is in agreement with the observed increasing $\text{Mo}_{3}\text{Al}$ phase fraction (compare Figs. 2 and 5).

## D. Tribological properties

The high temperature tribological behavior of our $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.56}\mathrm{Al}_{0.44}\mathrm{N}$, $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.55}\mathrm{Al}_{0.42}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.03}\mathrm{N}$, $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.53}\mathrm{Al}_{0.41}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.06}\mathrm{N}$, and $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.50}\mathrm{Al}_{0.38}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.12}\mathrm{N}$ coatings—obtained by unlubricated ball-on-disk tests at $700\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in ambient air—highlight the significant improvement of $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.56}\mathrm{Al}_{0.44}\mathrm{N}$, when alloying with Mo. The wear rates, calculated from the quantity of the worn surface (volume) and evaluated in terms of positive (adhesion, half-filled red triangle) and negative (abrasion, open blue circle) wear, are improved by more than four times up to 1 order of magnitude, see Fig. 7(a). The shaded area between the symbols represents the deviation between the first and second run. The Mo-free $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.56}\mathrm{Al}_{0.44}\mathrm{N}$ exhibits an average abrasive wear rate of about $2.3\times10^{-5}$ $\mathrm{mm}^{3}/\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}$. The adhesive wear is comparably small with only about $1.2\times10^{-6}$ $\mathrm{mm}^{3}/\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}$; see red triangle symbols in Fig. 7(a). All Mo-containing coatings, $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.55}\mathrm{Al}_{0.42}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.03}\mathrm{N}$, $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.53}\mathrm{Al}_{0.41}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.06}\mathrm{N}$, and $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.50}\mathrm{Al}_{0.38}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.12}\mathrm{N}$, have only abrasive wear rates of $1$–$2\times10^{-6}$ $\mathrm{mm}^{3}/\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}$, and adhesive wear rates of $2$–$28\times10^{-7}$ $\mathrm{mm}^{3}/\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}$ with a minimum of about $1.7\times10^{-7}$ $\mathrm{mm}^{3}/\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}$ for $\mathrm{Ti}_{0.50}\mathrm{Al}_{0.38}\mathrm{Mo}_{0.12}\mathrm{N}$. This suggests that already by the addition of only 3 at.% Mo to the metal content, a significant *in situ* formation of solid lubricant phases is possible during the ball-on-disk tests. ## E. Oxidation behavior

All Mo-containing coatings studied, Ti₀.₅₅Al₀.₄₂Mo₀.₀₃N, Ti₀.₅₃Al₀.₄₁Mo₀.₀₆N, and Ti₀.₅₀Al₀.₃₈Mo₀.₁₂N, are already fully oxidized after the 20h exposure to ambient air at 850 °C, see Figs. 9(a)–9(c), respectively, showing their fully oxidized cross section. Hence, the oxidation resistance of Ti₀.₅₆Al₀.₄₄N—also fully oxidized after 20h at 850 °C—is not (significantly) improved by the addition of Mo. The Al and Ti distribution across the oxide scales of our Ti₁₋ₓ₋ᵧAlₓMoᵧN coatings suggests for Al-rich outer regions and Ti-rich regions underneath, corresponding to the observations made for Ti₀.₅₆Al₀.₄₄N and in agreement with literature reports for such coatings.⁷,⁸,⁴⁷,⁵³,⁵⁴ The CrN top layer, especially visible and detectable on the oxide scales of Ti₀.₅₅Al₀.₄₂Mo₀.₀₃N and Ti₀.₅₃Al₀.₄₁Mo₀.₀₆N [Figs. 9(a) and 9(b)] served as supporting layer for the preparation procedure (grinding and polishing) prior to the SEM investigations. The Mo content is especially higher in the Ti-rich region, and the outermost Al-rich region nearly contains no detectable Mo, see the EDS line-scans in Figs. 9(a)–9(c). Furthermore, the Mo content (with respect to the metal content present in these oxide scales) is lower as expected from the chemical composition of our Ti₁₋ₓ₋ᵧAlₓMoᵧN coatings. This suggests for significant formation of volatile Mo oxides, in agreement with their thermodynamics. Therefore, especially the outermost regions are depleted in Mo. XRD investigations (not shown here) indicate the formation of TiO₂, Al₂O₃, but no Mo-based oxide. Additionally, differential

## IV. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Alloying Mo to face-centered cubic structured $\text{c-Ti}_{0.56}\text{Al}_{0.44}\text{N}$, to form $\text{c-Ti}_{0.55}\text{Al}_{0.42}\text{Mo}_{0.03}\text{N}$, $\text{c-Ti}_{0.53}\text{Al}_{0.41}\text{Mo}_{0.06}\text{N}$, and $\text{c-Ti}_{0.50}\text{Al}_{0.38}\text{Mo}_{0.12}\text{N}$, leads to slightly increasing lattice parameters of 4.17, 4.18, and $4.19\,\text{\AA}$, respectively. Upon annealing, their single-phase cubic structure transforms toward coherent $\text{c-Ti}_{1-y}\text{Mo}_y\text{N}$ and c-AlN rich phases, leading to the formation of coherency strains. These coherency strains (acting as obstacles against further dislocation glide) result in slightly increased hardness values after annealing at $\text{T}_a=800\,^\circ\text{C}$. This behavior can especially be observed for the low-Mo-containing coatings. Moreover, these coatings also exhibit a slightly postponed (to higher temperatures) precipitation of hexagonal w-AlN, which is also accompanied a huge N release leading to the formation of $\text{Mo}_3\text{Al}$. Here, it has also to be mentioned, that the amount of this N loss increases with increasing Mo content within the coatings. Consequently, the coating hardness decreases after vacuum annealing treatments at $\text{T}_a\geq1000\,^\circ\text{C}$.

However, the abrasive wear during ball-on-disk tests at $700\,^\circ\text{C}$ is significantly reduced by 1 order of magnitude to $1$–$2.0\times10^{-6}\,\text{mm}^3/(\text{N}\,\text{m})$ when alloying already 3 at.% Mo to $\text{Ti}_{0.56}\text{Al}_{0.44}\text{N}$ (exhibiting an average abrasive wear rate of about $2.3\times10^{-5}\,\text{mm}^3/(\text{N}\,\text{m})$). This improvement in wear properties is primarily based on the predicated *in situ* formation of a lubricious $\text{MoO}_3$ phase within the tribo-contact—verified by a combined EDS and XPS analysis. Further increasing the Mo content up to $y=0.12$ does not significantly improve the wear resistance/performance of $\text{Ti}_{1-x-y}\text{Al}_x\text{Mo}_y\text{N}$.

Based on our results we can conclude that the addition of 3 at.% Mo to the metal sublattice of $\text{c-Ti}_{0.56}\text{Al}_{0.44}\text{N}$ significantly improves their abrasive wear resistance (especially at elevated temperatures) while keeping their excellent mechanical properties and thermal stabilities.
