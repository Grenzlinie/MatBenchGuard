# H dissolution and desorption in W (1 1 0) surface with the presence of He: A first-principles study

## ABSTRACT

Experiments shew that the presence of helium (He) can suppress total hydrogen (H) retention/penetration and surface blistering, while increase tungsten (W) surface H retention. It is thus necessary to reveal the micro-mechanisms of the dissolution and desorption of H in the W surface with the presence of He to understand these two opposite phenomena systematically. It has already known that a surface tungsten vacancy (V) is easier to form than in the bulk and has a large trapping effect on H dissolving. So, the first-principles method is employed to study the effect of the single-vacancy and He-vacancy (HeV) complex on the dissolution and desorption behaviors of H in the W (1 1 0) surface. We found that there are more H capture sites around HeV complex than that around V, which should response for the increase of H retention near surface. In addition, both the trapping energies and desorption barriers of H around HeV complex are lower comparing to those around V, which makes it easier for H to desorb with the presence of He. Therefore, under the irradiation of low-energy plasma, the pre-exposure of He can increase both the trapping sites and desorption rate of H in W surface, which would suppress H penetration into bulk as well as surface blistering.

## 1. Introduction

In magnetic confinement fusion reactor like ITER, materials endure the impact of high temperature plasma, including low energy (1-100 eV) and high fluxes ($\sim 10^{24}\ \text{m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$) H isotope and He ions, high energy neutrons (14 MeV), high heat flow (up to $20\ \text{MW}^{-2}$) and strong mechanical stress [1]. The plasma-materials interactions (PMI) process will cause damage to the plasma-facing materials (PFMs), affect tritium (T) inventory, and introduce impurities into the plasma [2,3]. Nowadays, tungsten (W)-based materials are the most promising candidates of PFMs in future fusion devices, due to its high melting point, high thermal conductivity, low T retention and high sputtering threshold [4,5]. Nevertheless, surface damages would form in W, such as voids, bubbles, sputtering erosion, fuzz and surface blistering [6-8]. In particular, H retention and desorption are still the major issues of W-based PFMs under plasma irradiation.

Many experimental researches have been performed to study the retention and desorption behaviors of H and He in W surface. About $10^{2}$ higher concentration of H near the surface region comparing to that in the bulk and different surface blisters in different orientations is observed under low-energy deuterium (D) ion irradiation [9,10]. The above two phenomenon are related to the trapping of natural defects such as vacancy (V) after the irradiation of plasma [9] and the lattice deformation and defect accumulation [11,12], respectively. In addition, the synergistic irradiation of H and He is also widely investigated. Under D-He mixture plasma, D retention can be reduced by as large as ~50% [13] or ~70% depending on the irradiation conditions like ion energy and temperature [14]. Meanwhile, the blister formation is obviously decreased [15,16]. Additionally, after He pre-implantation in W, subsequent D has been observed to accumulate in He saturated layer, limiting D diffusion into W bulk and increasing D trapping about 6 times near the surface [14,17,18]. Moreover, thermal desorption spectroscopy (TDS) spectra shows appreciable desorption occurs over a broader temperature range from room temperature to 1150 K [18]. However, with the presence of He, the micro-mechanisms are still unclear especially for the opposite phenomena between the obvious reduction of H blistering and higher H retention near surface, and also the enhancement of D desorption peak at lower temperatures of 300-500 K.

In theory, it has shown that in W bulk, He prefers to occupy the center of V comparing to H and blocks $\text{H}_{2}$ formation. In addition, He drives H segregation towards it, which can block H penetration into deeper bulk and thus suppress H blistering [19,20]. For H in W surface,

H retention and bubble formation mainly depend on surface stability, H dissolution and desorption properties [21-30]. The previous study has found that the W (1 1 0) surface is the most stable one, while the W (1 0 0) and W (1 1 1) surfaces will appear reconstruction and severe lattice deformation, respectively [21-28]. Piazza et al. [29] have found that, for the W (1 1 0) surface the three-fold sites are preferred. Pan et al. [28] explored H dissolution and diffusion behaviors in four low- Miller-index perfect W surfaces and found that the W (1 1 0) is the re- sistant surface for forming W bubbles. In addition, H is hard to dissolve into the W (1 1 0) surface with the highest diffusion barrier and for- mation energy. Li et al. [30] have found that the H dissolution is layer- dependent and V is easier to form at the W (1 1 0) surface than in the bulk. Furthermore, V at the surface has large trapping effect on H dis- solving and lead to the H accumulation at W near-surface regions. Yang et al. [20] have investigated the H solution and trapping behaviors by HeₓV complex in the W bulk and surfaces with different orientations. It indicated that HeV can attract H with the orientation-dependent binding energies. In addition, in the W (1 1 0) surface, the binding en- ergies of HeV complex and H decrease with increasing the depth of HeV complex and the number of H [20]. However, investigations on H desorption behaviors near the surface with defects are still limited till now. And it still lacks the systematically study of the behavior of H near surface to understand the different surface phenomena with andwithout He through understanding the interaction between H and V/ HeV complex.

In this paper, we will systematically explore the dissolution and desorption behaviors of H in the W (1 1 0) surface with V or HeV complex using the first-principles methods. This paper is divided into three parts: At first, the calculation model and method are present. Then, the formation energies of V, HeV complex, H and He as well as the diffusion barriers of H and He in the W (1 1 0) surface are calcu- lated. Finally, the comparison of the dissolution and desorption beha- viors of H around V or HeV complex are discussed.

## 2. Computational method

All calculations are performed using the DFT-based Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [31]. The interactions between the ionic cores and valence electrons are described by projector augmented wave (PAW) potentials [32]. The exchange correlation functional is con- sidered as the generalized gradient approximation of Perdew-Burke- Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE) with a plane-wave energy cutoff of 400 eV [33]. The W (1 1 0) surface is chosen in this work due to its stability with the lowest surface energy among various low-Miller-index surface or- ientations [21-28]. A slab model is used to construct the W (1 1 0) surface which consists of 11 W atomic layers and a vacuum layer thickness of $13\mathring{A}$, as shown in Fig. 1. Initial W surface structures are set up based on the relaxed W bulk structures with lattice constant of $3.17\mathring{A}$ after relaxation, which agrees well with the experimental value of $3.16\mathring{A}$ [34]. The slab model constructed with a $(3×2)$ surface periodic structure at the theoretical equilibrium lattice constant con- tains totally 132 W atoms and has dimensions of $8.95\mathring{A}$, $9.50\mathring{A}$, and $35.38\mathring{A}$ along the [0 0 1], [1 1 0] and [1 $\bar{1}$ 0] directions, respectively. The surface slab model is tested to be large enough for the examination of H and He dissolution in W given that the variation of H dissolution energy was < 0.01 eV when increasing vacuum thickness or slab atomic layers. The Brillouin zone integration is sampled with the $3×3×1$ k- points mesh by the Monkhorst-Pack scheme. The k-point spacing for the surface supercell is thus $<0.026$ $(2\pi/\mathring{A})$. Only H, He atom and the W atoms at the top seven atomic layers of the slab are allowed to relax until the forces on those atoms are less than $0.01$ eV/$\mathring{A}$, while the bottom four atomic layers are fixed at the bulk lattice with the theo- retical lattice constant. The climbing image nudged elastic band (CI- NEB) method and the Quick-Min optimization of ion step are used for searching the diffusion paths and desorption energy of H and He [35,36].

In this work, several definitions about the characteristic energies are declared as following. The formation energies of V and HeV complex at various surface layers are defined as,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& E_{\mathrm{V}}^{f}=E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}}-E_{\mathrm{S}}+E(\mathrm{W}) \\
& E_{\mathrm{HeV}}^{f}=E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}}-E_{\mathrm{S}}+E(\mathrm{W})-E(\mathrm{He})
\end{aligned}\tag{1}
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{S}}$ is the energy of the perfect W (1 1 0) surface supercell, $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}}$ are the total energies of the W (1 1 0) surface supercell with a V or HeV complex at a certain layer, $E(\mathrm{W})$ and $E(\mathrm{He})$ are the energies of a bulk W atom and a He atom, respectively.

When H is located at different tetrahedral interstitial sites (TISs) near surfaces with V or HeV complex, the dissolution energies of H are defined as,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{sol}}=E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}+E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{zpe}}-E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\mathrm{H}_{2}}-E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{zpe}} \\
& E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{sol}}=E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}+E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{zpe}}-E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}}-\frac{1}{2}E_{\mathrm{H}_{2}}-E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{zpe}}
\end{aligned}\tag{2}
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}$ are the total energies of the W (1 1 0) surface with a V or HeV complex and an interstitial H, respectively. $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{zpe}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{zpe}}$ are the zero-point energy (ZPE) of a H dissolved into the W (1 1 0) surfaces with a V or HeV complex, respectively. When H is located at the three-fold (TF) sites, the ZPE is 0.178 eV. The ZPEs are about 0.09-0.12 eV and 0.190-0.20 eV when H is around a V or HeV complex, and about 0.17-0.18 eV and 0.245-0.267 eV when H is far from a V or HeV complex, respectively.

The binding energies of H with V and HeV complex at different TISs are defined by the following equations,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& E_{\mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{b}}=E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}}+E_{\mathrm{S}+\mathrm{H}}-E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}-E_{\mathrm{S}} \\
& E_{\mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{b}}=E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}}+E_{\mathrm{S}+\mathrm{H}}-E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}-E_{\mathrm{S}}
\end{aligned}\tag{3}
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{S}+\mathrm{H}}$ is the energy of a H located at different TISs in the perfect W (1 1 0) surface, $E_{\mathrm{S}}$, $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}}$, $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}}$, $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{V}+\mathrm{H}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}, \mathrm{HeV}+\mathrm{H}}$ are defined the same as before, and those energy terms containing H are all corrected with H ZPEs.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Competition of H and He occupation at a vacancy

For the convenience of following discussion, we define a W sub- surface here as the region between two adjacent surface-layers. For instance, 1sub-surface is the region between the 1st and 2nd layers, and 2sub-surface represents the region between the 2nd and 3rd layers and so on. Comparing to the octahedral interstitial site (OIS), the TIS is the most stable site for both H and He in the W bulk [19]. In the W (1 1 0) surface, the TIS is also more favorable for H than the OIS with the solution energy lower by about 0.16 eV [30]. As shown in Fig. 2, when a H dissolving in the sub-surface, there are three unequal TISs, named as TIS1 (green sphere), TIS2 (yellow sphere) and TIS3 (red sphere), re- spectively. On the surface, the TF hollow site is the stable position for H at the vacuum with the dissolved energy of $-0.71$ eV.

V formation energies in different layers are given in Table 1, which increase gradually with depth increasing until to the bulk value. It means that the closer to the surface, the easier to form a vacancy.

The capability of H or He occupation in a V near W surface is in- vestigated at first. V can provide stable sites for H in W. When a V is located at the second layer, the most stable site for H around the V is found to be at the position near 1sub-surface OIS with the solution energy of $-0.37$ eV at an off-V-center position (about $1.25\mathring{A}$), as shown at site 4 in the right structure inserted in Fig. 3. The V center is the most stable site for He with the solution energies of 1.76 eV and 1.60 eV at the second and the third layer, respectively. But the binding energies

As shown in Fig. 3, the optimal diffusion path is the TIS → TIS path with a diffusion barrier of 0.07 eV for He from site 1 to site 2. Hence, like in the bulk, He also prefers to be stabilized by binding with V to form a HeV complex in the W (1 1 0) surface.

Thus, we further calculated the formation energies of HeV complex at different layers in the W (1 1 0) surface. As shown in Table 1, the formation energies of HeV complex in different layers increase with depth increasing but approaching to the value in W bulk. Namely, HeV complex is located at the atomic layers closer to the surface is easier to form due to the interlayer distance becomes smaller and thus the lattice deformation decreases gradually with depth increasing. In addition, at the first layer, HeV complex is unstable and escapes to the vacuum, since the interaction between He and the W surface is weak, which is a kind of physical bonding behavior at the vacuum layer away from the surface.

When a V or HeV complex is located at the second layer, the solution energies of H increases with depth increasing. For a V is located at second layer, there are only two stable sites for H among TIS1, TIS2 and TIS3 after relaxation. The stable sites of H are $1.25\mathring{A}$ and $1.20\mathring{A}$ from the V as shown at site 4 and site 3 in the right structure inserted in Fig. 3, with solution energy of $-0.34$ eV and $-0.31$ eV, respectively. When a HeV is located at the second layer, there are four different stable sites for H. When a HeV is located at the fourth layer, the solution energy of H is the same as that in the bulk and there are six stable sites of H around HeV with about $1.61\mathring{A}$ apart. Thus, there are more stable sites for H around HeV complex, because the repulsive interaction of H and He makes the isosurface of optimal charge density for H expands from about 20.25 to $33.37\mathring{A}^2$, which can provide more possible 'optimal charge density sites' for H in comparison with that of a V at different layers. It means that with the continuous irradiation, the HeV complex can capture more H than V and cause H concentration near surface increasing comparing to that without He. In addition, the solution energy of H around HeV complex is higher than that around V, because the presence of He makes the redistribution of charge density and the optimal charge density of H around HeV complex is 0.1 electron/$\mathring{A}^3$ higher than that around V. It means that H will firstly solute around V and then HeV complex under the same conditions.

According to the above results, V or HeV complex at the W surface atomic layers have an obvious trapping effect on H dissolving in the sub-surface, and there exists an effective trapping region for H. In Fig. 4(b), the positive binding energies indicate that H can be strongly trapped at those sub-surface TISs by these two defects. The binding energies closing to zero means V or HeV complex have almost no attraction to H. In general, a H at two adjacent sub-surfaces in the W (1 1 0) surface with V or HeV complex will be easily trapped. The H will penetrate towards V or HeV complex in the surface from its initial position and bond with them by a certain distance apart, which thus suppresses the penetration of H into the bulk. The binding energies of H-V and H-He are similar to those in the bulk, because the contribution by the interaction of H, He and W is larger than that of the W (1 1 0) surface deformation with small surface energy and interlayer distance change. In addition, the binding energy of H-He is lower comparing to that H-V due to the repulsive interaction of H and He. This means that the presence of He makes the trapping ability of H weaker and the desorption of H easier around HeV complex than around V, which can explain why the pre-exposure of He can cause H to desorb even at low temperatures observed in experiment [18].

### 4. Conclusions

The effect of V and HeV complex on H in the W (1 1 0) surface has investigated by calculating their dissolution, binding energy and diffusion using the first-principles methods. The formation energies of V and HeV are layer dependent, which increase with depth increasing until reaching the value in W bulk. V center is the most stable site for He, since He prefers to occupy the sites with the lowest charge density. V and HeV complex can act as trapping centers that drive H segregation towards them. H is more likely solute around V due to the lower solute energy and higher binding energy than that around HeV, while HeV has more solute sites for H. It should be the main reason for the higher concentration of H near W surface with He pre-exposure. H around HeV is more likely to desorb than that around V, which should induce H desorption at lower temperatures. Our calculation results explain the experimental phenomena and are helpful for understanding the synergistic effects of H and He in W surface like the suppression of H retention and surface blistering.
