# The investigation on the electronic structures of hybrid GNR-ZnO

Ping Yang · Xifu Song · Haiying Yang · Xialong Li ·
Yanfang Zhao · Dongjin Liu

Received: 1 February 2013 / Accepted: 21 May 2013 / Published online: 31 May 2013

## Abstract
To investigate the modification of graphene for a good semiconductor performance, the model of graphene nanoribbon (GNR) and the model of hybrid GNR-ZnO are presented, respectively. The electronic structures such as band structure, density of electronic states (DOS) are inves- tigated by using first-principles calculations based on den- sity functional theory. The results show that the ZnO has direct contribution to the formation of valence band with the composites, which changes the properties of graphene; the adsorption of ZnO nanostructure has an impressive im- pact on the graphene nanoribbon, which causes the band gap of the composite to become narrower than that of intrin- sic graphene nanoribbon. In terms of the graphene nanorib- bon, its differentiation of 2p state weakens gradually from the marginal zone to the central region. The mechanism of electronic structure of the graphene nanoribbon and hy- brid GNR-ZnO is also discussed to give a further expla- nation for the change. The results provide a potential way to improve the properties of intrinsic graphene and en- hance the controllability of graphene-based materials struc- ture.

## 1 Introduction
Graphene has earned great attention due to its excellent op- tical, electrical and mechanical properties [1–3]. Ordinary two-dimensional graphene layers often show very strong metal properties, there is no band gap between the valence and the conduction band. The semiconductor performance is not obvious. However, the band gap is the key to electrical applications, which enables the electron flow of materials to be on and off. The former research has found that graphene nanoribbons with width less than 10 nm [4, 5] show good band gap characteristics. Many studies commit to expand the band gap of graphene at present. The most outstanding research is conducted by Jinming Cai [6], who had got the 1.74 eV electrical band gap of graphene nanoribbons.

Wurtzite zinc oxide (ZnO), as the II–VI compound semi- conductor material with many unique features such as wide band gap, large excitation energy, high melting point, high temperature resistance, low cost and so on, is drawing more and more attention from both industries and academic com- munities [7]. Among the ZnO nanostructures, $Zn_3O_3$ ring has been confirmed to be stable [8], and the $(Zn_3O_3)_3$ has been reported to have good binding properties with the graphene [9]. In view of the advantages of the upper ma- terials, it is expected that the hybridization of graphene and ZnO maybe perform as a novel semiconductor material with high efficiency and desirable properties.

In fact, graphene-based nanocomposites have been syn- thesized recently [10–12]. Vertically aligned ZnO nanos- tructures which grown on graphene layers are good can- didates for field emission emitters due to their sharp tips, high aspect ratio, high thermal stability and high me- chanical stability. Meanwhile, first-principles calculations have been used to analyze the field emission properties of graphene-ZnO nanocomposite [9]. Although it has made

## 2 Models and calculate method

### 2.1 The model

Perfect graphene is made of carbon atoms arranged on a honeycomb structure with hexagons. So the perfect graphene can be considered as composition of benzene rings stripped out from their hydrogen atoms [13]. The graphene nanoribbon (GNR) can be obtained from two-dimensional graphene. In this paper, the computational model is a $C_{74}H_{20}$ supercell, which is formed by the arrangement of $5 \times 3 \times 1$ based on the primitive cell. The GNR has armchair shaped edges on both sides with hydrogen passivation, which is showed in Fig. 1. The width of the nanoribbon is 1.3 nm. It is assumed that the GNR is periodic along the length direction, which can be extended infinitely. The thickness of vacuum-slab is set to be $20$ Å. Apparently the transmission of electrons in the nanoribbon is subject to rigid restrictions along the width and height direction [14]. This model is exactly the same as the one in reference [4]. It is also well-known that the $(Zn_3O_3)_3$ six-membered ring is a stable and frequent structural motif in ZnO nanocrystals and nanoclusters, which is showed in Fig. 1b. In this paper, the GNR-ZnO nano-contact is formed by attaching a $(Zn_3O_3)_3$ cluster to the graphene nanoribbon by way of six additional carbon atoms as shown in Fig. 2.

### 2.2 Calculation method

The calculation is completed by DMol3 packages [15]. Valence electron configurations for C, O and Zn are considered as C-2s22p2, Zn-3d104s2, O-2s22p4 [16], respectively. The calculation for geometry optimization,energy of graphene nanoribbon and hybrid GNR-ZnO can be based on density functional theory (DFT) with generalized gradient approximation (GGA) method, adopting the PBE to the correlation gradient modification [17]. A non-spin polarized calculation is adopted. The all electron relativistic pseudo potential is used to reduce the computation. The convergence criteria applied during geometry optimization is $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ Ha for energy. The final cutoff energy is chosen to be 340 eV and the k-point set mesh parameter is $4 \times 1 \times 1$. The lowest energy state is selected as the ground state to analyze [18]. The global cutoff radius is set at 4.4 Å. In addition, we perform the harmonic vibrational frequency computation to ensure that the GNR-ZnO to be real minima instead of being saddle points on the potential energy surface of the nanostructure.

## 3 Results and discussion

### 3.1 The lattice constants of hybrid GNR-ZnO

Through the optimized structure of GNR-ZnO in Fig. 3, it shows that the structure has changed a lot. The lamel- lar structure of GNR shows obvious deformation. The bond length changes significantly and the bond angle turns out to be in disorder. There is also an obvious protrusion in the connected region of GNR. In theory, the C–C bond length in graphene is 0.142 nm. The graphene C–C bond lengths range from 0.130 nm to 0.154 nm under actual free states [19]. The bond length in Part 2 changes from 0.142 nm into 0.157 nm. Apparently it has gone beyond the normal value of C–C bond length in GNR. These changes of the C–C bond and the angle caused structure defects in the graphene nanoribbon, which will have certain influence on the properties of the GNR-ZnO. However, the structural de- formation does not affect the symmetry of the GNR. It still has symmetry and periodic properties along the length di- rection. In other regions such as the Part 1 and Part 0, the C–C bond length changes moderately and still remains the bond length features of graphene.

Part 1 represents the six C atoms in the ball structure of ZnO. Part 2 represents the six C atoms of graphene which are connected to the ball structure. Part 3 represents the C atoms in the transitionary area of graphene. Part 0 repre- sents the C atoms in the edge which are far away from the connected area.

### 3.2 The band structure of GNR and hybrid GNR-ZnO

We can get the band structure of the intrinsic GNR and that of hybrid GNR-ZnO along the high symmetry points of the Brillouin zone, the dashed is Fermi level. The re- sults are consistent with that in the literature [4]. The band gap of graphene nanoribbon in Fig. 4a is 1.4144 eV, which is smaller than the experimental value, which is a common problem of LDA or GGA. As we know, the DFT description lacks the excitation aspect that is inherently linked to exper- iments that measure these quantities. In addition, the semi- local approximation to exchange and correlation shows a self-interaction defect. For these reasons, the band gaps are

typically underestimated. We can get a good calculating re-sult by LDA in most cases, but sometimes the results about the band gap of the semiconductor tend to be with large error, the results are often smaller than the experimental test. In order to reduce the calculating error, we preferred the modificatory-LDA method by intruding GGA to obtain more accurate results.

Figure 4b shows the band structure of the hybrid GNR-ZnO, The band gap is 0.5618 eV, which is much smaller compared with that of the intrinsic graphene nanoribbon. The decline of the conduction band in energy band diagram is the mean reason for the change of the band gap. The top of the conduction band reduces from 0.12 Ha to 0.08 Ha, which mainly concentrates in the area range from 0.06 Ha to 0.08 Ha. The bottom of the conduction band becomes closer to the Fermi level. However, the valence band turns into the counter way, the distribution of the valence band is mainly below $-0.03$ Ha, most of the valence band becoming far away from the Fermi level and tending to be more intensive.

### 3.3 The DOS of graphene nanoribbon and hybrid GNR-ZnO

It is well known that the band gap of graphene can be con-trolled by changing of the graphene nanoribbon [20]. As can be seen from the total density of state of graphene nanorib-bon shown in Fig. 5, the valence band at the low energy side ($-0.7$–$0.3$ eV) mainly comes from the C 2s orbitals contribution, a small part from the C 2p higher orbitals. The valence band (VB) ($-0.3$–$0.07$ eV) contains a large peak, which plays a critical role in the formation of valence band. The conduction band (CB) part is mainly composed by C 2p orbitals. Generally speaking, the band gap in terms of graphene nanoribbon mainly comes from the differentiation of C 2p orbitals. Moreover the differentiation of C atom in the edge states of graphene nanoribbon is particularly pivotal. Compared Part 1 with Part 2 (in Fig. 6), it can be found that the peak in Part 2 (the edge state) is much higher than that in Part 1. This trend shows that the differentiation of the C 2p state weakens from the edges to the center. In addition, there are some waves of the DOS passing across the Fermi level in Fig. 5, which indicates that the graphene nanoribbon still shows a certain metallic character.

As seen from Fig. 6 and Fig. 7, the density of states of hybrid GNR-ZnO, the graphene nanoribbon in it shows a continuous state ranging from the marginal zone to the recombination center, and the peak weakens gradually compared with that of intrinsic graphene nanoribbon shown in Fig. 5. The differentiation of C-2p orbital of graphene nanoribbon in the connected center is weakened, which makes little contribution to the formation of the conduction band. The new peak of the density of states for the valence band becomes far away from the Fermi level, which is mainly around $-0.25$ Ha. It also can be seen from Fig. 7 that the valence band of hybrid GNR-ZnO has changed significantly. The small peak of the valence band declines compared to that of the intrinsic graphene nanoribbon, and the DOS distribution tends to be dispersed. By comparing with the PDOS,

## 4 Mechanism analysis

To analyze the mechanism for these changes, we consider the change of the lattice structure firstly. It can be seen through the optimized complex structure that the structure of ZnO nanostructure has led to significant change compared with the intrinsic graphene nanoribbon. After optimization, the C-C bond length turns into the largest value of 0.157 nm in connected area and the minimum 0.137 nm in the marginal zone. It is clear that the maximum bond length has gone beyond the conventional graphene bond length features [21]. It is inferred that the phase transition has taken place in the connection area of graphene nanoribbon, which is due to the strong adsorption of ZnO nanostructure. On the other hand, as we all know, there are three $\sigma$ bonds and a free $\pi$-electron in each C atom, which forms a stable internal state in graphene. However, the absorption of ZnO nanostructure destroys the $\sigma$ bond among the C-C atoms in graphene and also limits the freedom of $\pi$-electron. As a result, the electronic cloud distribution of graphene changed. These changes are different in mechanism compared with other ways in improving properties of graphene structure [22]. It can be inferred that the lattice of graphene is related to the internal states and the inner states of graphene is sensitive to the exterior conditions.

It can also be found from Fig. 6 that semiconductor properties of carbon atoms are weakened gradually from the marginal zone to the connected district. The C (0) still shows the band gap properties, while the C (2) in the connection region has disappeared. With the analysis of electronic structure of the former graphene nanoribbon, we know that the new surface states in the edge play a crucial role in modification of the semiconductor properties. After hybridization, the crystal structure of graphene nanoribbon is suddenly interrupted. Not only are the three $\sigma$ bonds of C atoms damaged, but the delocalized $\pi$-electron also cannot move freely in the connected area. Those changes also lead to many new local bands, which can be seen in Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 with the density of states. However, the semiconductor mechanism of graphene nanoribbon is different from that of ZnO. As the charge carriers concentration in graphene is much higher than that in ZnO [23], such a small amount of charge carriers in ZnO which is injected into the graphene leads to the formation of new local bands, which finally results in the density of states of the complex showing a continuous state.

## 5 Conclusions

By using first-principle method based on DFT, the electronic structures of GNR and hybrid GNR-ZnO were studied. The conclusions are as follows: The C atoms in the edge after hydrogen passivation make the main contribution to the semiconductor properties of graphene nanoribbon, the differentiation of 2p state weakens gradually from the marginal zone to the central region. The ZnO has direct

![](./images/813222325351612416_11.jpg)

contribution to the formation of valence band for the com- posites, which changes the properties of graphene; the ad- sorption of ZnO nanostructure has an impressive impact on the graphene nanoribbon, which leads to the structure de- fects in the graphene nanoribbon and finally causes the band gap of the composite to become narrower than that of intrin- sic graphene nanoribbon. The mechanism of the electronic structure of the graphene nanoribbon and hybrid GNR-ZnO is also discussed to give a further explanation for the change. This work builds a referential basis for improving the prop- erties of intrinsic graphene and enhancing the controllability of graphene-based materials in future research.
