# Electronic structures and charge carrier mobilities of boron-graphdiyne sheet and nanoribbons

Haifeng Wang $^{1,2^{*}}$, Yan Gao $^{1}$, Qingfang $Li^{3}$ and Jijun Zhao $^{2^{*}}$

$^{a}$Department of Physics, College of Science, Shihezi University, Xinjiang 832003, China

$^{b}$Key Laboratory of Materials Modification By Laser, Ion and Electron Beams (Dalian University of Technology), Ministry of Education, Dalian 116024, China

$^{c}$Department of Physics, Nanjing University of Information Science&Technology, Nanjing, 210044, China

**ABSTRACT:** Recently, a novel carbon based nanomaterial, boron-graphdiyne (BGDY) was experimentally realized through a bottom-to-up synthetic strategy, which is a $\pi$-conjugated structure comprised of all $sp$-hybridized carbon skeleton and boron heteroatoms in a well-organized two-dimensional (2D) molecular plane, and has been demonstrated to exhibit good semiconducting properties with excellent conductivity. The successful synthesis of this new graphyne material consequently raise the importance of the evaluation of its intrinsic properties, especially the electronic transport properties. In this paper, we firstly investigate the geometry and electronic structure of BGDY sheet based on density functional theory (DFT) calculations, then combined with the Boltzmann transport equation with relaxation time approximation, we predict its charge mobility. It's found that monolayer BGDY is a direct band-gap semiconductor with a moderate bandgap of about 1.26 eV, more importantly, both the electron and hole mobilities are isotopic and high, especially, the electron mobilities can approach as high as $10^{5}\mathrm{cm^{2}V^{-1}s^{-1}}$. Furthermore, we report the energy band gaps and carrier mobilities of two types of BGYD nanoribbons, called zigzag and armchair nanoribbons. Our numerical calculations show that all the nanoribbons showing semiconducting property and their mobilities are comparable to or even larger than that of BGDY sheet.


**Keywords:** graphdiyne; DFT; nanoribbon; band structure; carrier mobility

## 1. Introduction

The successful exfoliation of graphene [1,2], one monolayer of carbon atoms with a hexagonal honeycomb structure, has ignited more and more research interests of two-dimentional (2D) materials. In the last few decades, a variety of novel graphene-like 2D materials were theoretically proposed or experimental realized [3-10]. Among of them, carbon allotropes, especially alkynyl-group-containing graphyne (GY) family with $sp$-hybridized carbon have come into focus recently mainly because of diverse structures formed by a variety of hybridized forms and molecular shapes, which bring about many unique physical and chemical properties and tremendous application potentials in the fields of electronics, optoelectronics, catalysis, sensor and energy. Graphynes were first proposed by Ba ughman R. H. *et. al* in 1987 with various atomic structures [11]. Then tremendous efforts have been devoted to realize such carbon allotropes [12-22]. Among all the graphynes, graphdiyne (GDY), which can be described as GY networks, where all acetylenic ($-C\equiv C-$) linkages are replaced by diacetylenic linkages ($-C\equiv C-C\equiv C-$) was the first reported to be prepared practically and, hence, attracted most of the attention of researchers [11-12,16,18-25].

According to the different arrangement of carbon atoms, GDY could be mainly divided into three forms, namely, $\alpha$-, $\beta$-, and $\gamma$-GDY [26]. Till now, the synthesis of a stable form of GDY still remains elusive. Fortunately, two type GDY structures, i.e., $\beta$- and $\gamma$-GDY have been experimentally fabricated successively. In 2010, Li and coworkers successfully synthesized $\gamma$-GDY on copper substrates through acetylenic coupling reaction using hexakisethynylbenzene as precursor [27]. It has been predicted to be the most stable structure among the three forms with a band gap of about 0.46 eV and own unique anisotropic properties [28]. These advantages make

$\gamma$-GDY promising for various applications [18-25]. In 2017, $\beta$-GDY-containing thin film was successfully synthesized on copper foil using modified Glaser-Hay coupling reaction with tetraethynylethene as precursor [29]. $\beta$-GDY possesses higher percentage of acetylenic linkages (67%) than $\gamma$-GDY (50%), meaning stronger $\pi$-conjunction. Calculations of the electronic structure reveal the $\beta$-GDY is a zero band gap material and presents a metallic behavior, and the existence of Dirac points leads to the intrinsic carry mobility of $\beta$-GDY as high as graphene [30].

Compared with the successful synthesis of $\beta$- and $\gamma$-GDY, there has been no available method to prepare $\alpha$-GDY. However, very recently, boron-graphdiyne (BGDY), which has a very similar structure with $\alpha$-GDY (see Fig. 1) was successfully realized in experiment [31]. Unlike many other graphene based materials and boron-doped carbon-rich materials, this new material is composed of pure $sp$-hybridized carbon skeleton and boron heteroatoms in a well-organized 2D molecular plane. Benefitting from the unique geometry structure, BGDY is found possessing excellent conductivity and electrochemical properties [31]. As is known, the excellent electrical conductivity is related to high carrier mobility. Thus, it is expectable that BGDY could own unique electrical transport properties. Worthy to mention that the carrier mobilities of $\beta$-GDY and $\gamma$-GDY have been reported in 2013 [30] and 2011 [32], respectively, and have attached much attentions. Furthermore, the electrical band structures and the carrier transport properties of the corresponding one-dimentional (1D) nanoribbons are essential to the further application of the carbon-based graphyne materials. Most recently, Li's group reported a facile method for fabrication of $\gamma$-GDY nanoribbons with uniform width through stepwise inter- and intramolecular Glaser-Hay coupling reaction of ethynyl groups [33], so there is reason to believe that the successful preparation of BGDY nanoribbons is expectable in the near future. Thus, the study of the electrical transport properties of this newly fabricated BGDY sheet and its 1D nanoribbons are of great significance in the view of both theory and practices.

In this paper, we firstly report the geometry and band structure of 2D BGDY sheet, then based on the Boltzmann transport equation with relaxation time approximation, we investigate the carrier mobility of BGDY sheet. It's found that different from the metallic $\alpha$-GDY sheet [26,28], monolayer BGDY is a semiconductor with a moderate direct band gap of 1.26 eV, importantly, both the electron and hole mobilities are found isotropic and ultra-high, for example, the electron mobility along zigzag direction can approach as high as $10^5$ cm$^2$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$. In addition, we reported the computed band structure of 1D boron-graphdiyne nanoribbons (BGDYNRs), and then predicted the electrical transport properties of BGDYNRs with different ribbon width. The numerical results show that both the zigzag and armchair BGDY nanoribbons demonstrate semiconducting behaviors and also possess ultra-high carrier mobilities.

## 2. Computational details

In inorganic semiconductors, the electron is mainly scattered by the acoustic phonons [34], especially for 2D materials, the carrier mobility is dominated by acoustic phonon scattering via intra- and inter-valley deformation potential couplings at room temperature [35]. Thus, on the basis of the effective mass approximation [36], the deformation potential (DP) theory has been successfully applied to predict the carrier mobilities of many typical nanomaterials [32,37-39] with the following form:

$$
\mu_{2 D}=\frac{2 e \hbar^{3} C_{2 D}}{3 k_{B} T\left|m^{*}\right|^{2} E_{1}^{2}},
\tag{1}
$$

where $T$ is the temperature, $m^{*}$ is the effective mass. $C_{2 D}$ is the elastic modulus of a uniformly deformed 2D system for simulating the lattice distortion activated by the strain, which can be calculated as $C^{2 D}=\left[\partial^{2} E / \partial \delta^{2}\right] / S_{0}$, here $E$ is the total energy of the supercell, $\delta$ is the applied uniaxial strain, and $S_{0}$ is the area of the optimized supercell. $E_{1}$ is the DP constant which denotes the shift of the band edges

[conduction band minimum (CBM) for electrons and valence band maximum (VBM) for holes] induced by the strain. An orthogonal supercell was created in order to calculate the mobilities of zigzag and armchair directions, as shown in Fig. 1a.

For 1D nanoribbons, the carrier mobility can also be obtained by using the same simple model based on the deformation potential (DP) theory and effective mass approximation. The carrier mobilities of some typical one-dimensional cases have been successfully studied based on this model, such as carbon nanotubes [40], graphene nanoribbons [41], $MoS_2$ nanoribbons [35] and $\gamma$-GDY nanoribbons [32]. It can be expressed as

$$
\mu_{1 D}=\frac{e \hbar^{3} C_{1 D}}{\left(2 \pi k_{B} T\right)^{1 / 2}\left|m^{*}\right|^{3 / 2} E_{1}^{2}},
\tag{2}
$$

where $C_{1 D}$ is the stretching modulus for a 1D crystal along the longitudinal ribbon, which is defined as $C^{1 D}=(\partial^{2} E / \partial \delta^{2}) / L_{0}$, the uniaxial strain $\delta$ is applied along the ribbon direction, and $L_{0}$ is the lattice constant of the optimized ribbon.

All the first-principles calculations are performed based on density functional theory (DFT) using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method as implemented in the Vienna ab-initio simulation package (VASP) [42]. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) of generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [43] and hybrid functional (HSE06) [44] are chosen as the exchange-correlation functional. The kinetic energy cutoff of wave functions and the energy convergence threshold are chosen as 500 eV and $10^{-6}$ eV, respectively. The $k$-meshes for the sheet and nanoribbons are $11 \times 11$ and $1 \times 9$. The vacuum spacing is set as at least $15$ Å along the out-of-plane direction that is sufficiently large. Both the cell shape and volume are fully optimized and all atoms are allowed to relax until the maximal Hellmann-Feynman force acting on each atom is no larger than $0.001$ eV $\mathrm{\AA}^{-1}$.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Geometry and band structure

The atom structure of monolayer boron-graphdiyne was shown in Fig. 1(a). For comparison, we also plotted the atom structure of $\alpha$-GDY in Fig. 1(c), as it is clear, the only difference is the boron atom replacing the $sp^2$-hybridized carbon atom in $\alpha$-GDY. Just like graphene and $\alpha$-GDY, BGDY has the same hexagonal symmetry of space group P6/mmm (No. 191). Our optimized lattice constant of monolayer BGDY is 11.842 Å, and the C-C bond lengths in the three butadiyne linkages

$(-C\equiv C-C\equiv C-)$ were measured to be $1.235\ \mathring{A}$ and $1.346\ \mathring{A}$, while the B-C bonds exhibit the maximum length with $1.512\ \mathring{A}$, which are in accordance with the original experimental work [31] and other theoretical study [45].

![](./images/812587938142486528_3.jpg)

Fig. 2. Band structures of BGDY sheet in (a) the primitive cell and (b) the orthogonal supercell.

Fig. 2(a-b) plot the band structures of BGDY sheet in the primitive cell and the orthogonal supercell derived from HSE06 functions. As can be seen, BGDY sheet is a direct band gap semiconductor with a bandgap about 1.26 eV. Compared with metallic $\alpha$-GDY [26,28], the replacement of the $sp^2$-hybridized C atom to B atom induced rather weaker hopping energies between the lattice sites in BGDY, an opening of energy gap is a natural consequence. Note that the M point in FBZ of the hexagonal lattice will be folded into the $\Gamma$ point in FBZ related to the orthogonal lattice, which is general for the 2D monolayers with this symmetry, such as $MoS_2$ [37], $C_3N$ [38]. Therefore, in the following section of calculating the carrier mobilities of BGDY along zigzag and armchair directions, we only need to focus on the $\Gamma$ point.

### 3.2. Carrier mobilities of 2D BGDY sheet

In this section, we investigate the electron transport properties of 2D BGDY sheet. we begin with the calculation of the effective masses for electrons ($\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{e}^\ast$) and holes ($\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{h}^\ast$) in 2D BGDY sheet. They can be derived by fitting the parabolic functions to the

CBM and VBM of BGDY sheets according to the following equation:

$$\frac{1}{\mathrm{~m} *}=\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}} \frac{\partial^{2} E_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial k^{2}}$$

where $E_{\mathrm{k}}$ is the energy corresponding to the wave vector k. Our obtained effective masses are $0.16 m_{0}$ for electrons and $0.72 m_{0}$ for holes along the armchair direction, associated with $0.15 m_{0}$ for electrons and $0.73 m_{0}$ for holes along the zigzag direction ($m_{0}$ is the effective mass of free electrons). It's obvious that the effective masses for both electron and hole are very isotropic, this is reasonable since the atoms structure of 2D BGDY owns high isotropy. Otherwise, the effective mass for electron in BGDY is much smaller than many typical 2D materials, such as monolayer $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ (the effective masses are nearly isotropic and about $0.5 m_{0}$ [37]) and single-layer black phosphorus (the effective mass along zigzag direction is $1.12 m_{0}$ and $0.17 m_{0}$ along armchair direction [39]).

Then we turn to derive the elastic modulus ($C^{2D}$), which can be obtained through fitting the energy-strain curves. Fig. 3(a) shows the variation of total energy (E) with uniaxial strain applied along the zigzag and armchair directions, respectively. As can be seen, the two curves are nearly identical, and the difference in $C^{2D}$ along the two directions is very small (43.9 N/m along zigzag direction and 43.1 N/m along armchair direction), i.e., the elastic modules of BGDY show great isotropy owning to the obvious isotropic hexagonal geometry structure. Besides, it's found that the elastic modules of BGDY sheet are much smaller than many other carbon allotropes, e.g., graphene [40] and graphyne [26]. The arrangement of carbon atoms in BGDY is much sparser than that in graphene and graphyene because of the all-$sp$ hybridized linkages in C-C bonds, and as a result BGDY shows much softer elastic modules.

![](./images/812587938142486528_4.jpg)

Fig. 3. (a) Energy-strain relationship along zigzag and armchair directions. (b) Shifts of conduction band and valence band under uniaxial strains along zigzag and armchair directions of 2D BGDY.

In Fig. 3(b), we present the calculated VBM and CBM positions of BGDY monolayer at the $\Gamma$-point as a function of the uniaxial strain along zigzag and armchair directions. The band edge position changes linearly with the lattice deformation, and the slope is the DP constant $E_1$, which is a characterization of the coupling strength of the electron or hole to the acoustic phonon. Our obtained DP constants of 2D BGDY along the zigzag direction are -0.51 for electrons and -2.05 for holes, respectively. Along the armchair direction, the values are -0.49 (electrons) and -2.09 (holes), very close to that along the zigzag direction. It can be seen that along the two directions, the deformation potential of holes are much larger than that of electrons, indicating the holes are more strongly scattered by the acoustic phonon than the electrons in 2D BGDY sheet.

Table 1 shows all the calculated effective masses, stretching modulus, DP constants, and the obtained carrier mobilities for both electron and hole in 2D BGDY. One can see that both the electron and hole mobilities demonstrate obvious isotropy. More importantly, the carrier mobilities of BGDY are found rather high, especially for the electrons, which can approach as high as $10^5$ cm$^2$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$, much higher than that of several typical 2D materials, such as MoS$_2$ [37], black phosphorous [39]. So, there is

reason to believe that layered BGDY owing moderate direct bandgap, isotropic and ultra-high electron mobility will become attractive nanomaterial in electronics, optoelectronics, and photonics.

### 3.3. Energy band gaps and band structures of 1D-BGDYNBs

Next, we focus on the the 1D boron-graphdiyne nanobibbons (BGDYNRs), which are constructed by cutting the corresponding 2D BGDY sheet, as shown in Fig. 4. We terminate all the edge C atoms with H atoms to remove the effect of dangling bonds. Similar to graphene nanoribbons (GNRs), the BGDYNRs exhibit two typical edge structures, namely, zigzag and armchair, for which the corresponding nanoribbons are denoted as z-BGDYNRs and a-BGDYNRs, respectively. Following the convention, we refer to a boron-graphdiyne nanoribbon

with $Nz$ zigzag chains as $Nz$-z-BGDYNR, and a nanoribbon with $Na$ armchair chains as $Na$-a-BGDYNR, as shown in Fig. 4(a-b).

![](./images/812587938142486528_5.jpg)

Fig. 4. Schematics of (a) zigzag and (b) armchair BGDYNRs. The unit cell is delimited by the black dashed lines. $Nz$ and $Na$ identify the width of zigzag and armchair nanoribbons, respectively. The edge carbon atoms are saturated with hydrogen atoms.

To evaluate the electronic transport properties of 1D BGYD nanoribbons, we first need to determine whether the BGDYNRs are metal or semiconductor, i.e., is there exist bandgap in armachair and zigzag BGDYNRs. Through examining the band structures of BGDYNRs, it's found that both the armachair and zigzag BGDYNRs are semiconducting, exhibiting a similar feature to GNRs [46] and $\alpha$-GDYNRs [47]. In Fig. 5, we plot the bandgaps of the armachair (1≤N≤17) and zigzag BGDYNRs (1≤N≤13). When the width N≤6, the bandgaps are calculated from both the PBE and hybrid functional (HSE06), when N≥7, we only give the PBE calculated bandgaps in order to save the computation. It's found that for all cases, the bandgaps calculated from HSE06 are about 0.8 eV larger than that of PBE calculation results. It's reasonable that the PBE function always underestimate the band-gap values of semiconductors. The bandgaps of BGDYNRs decreases monotonically with the

ribbon width N for both armchair and zigzag nanoribbons and should reach 0.51 eV (PBE level) for the 2D BGDY sheets.

![](./images/812587938142486528_6.jpg)

Fig. 5. The bandgaps depending on the ribbon width (namely, N) of BGDYNRs.

The energy band structures of BGDYNRs with different width N are examined carefully. It's found that all the band structures with the same width show apparent similarity. As an example, we give the electric band structures of 6-z-BGDYNR and 6-a-BGDYNR in Fig. 6. It's clear that the electric band structures of the two type BGDYNRs demonstrate strong isotropy.

### 3.4. Carrier mobilities of 1D-BGDYNRs

With the same theoretical methods employed in 2D BGDY nanosheets, we then calculated the electrical transport properties of 1D BGDYNRs.

The effective stretching modulus $C^{1D}$ of z-BGDYNRs and a-BGDYNRs with the increment of ribbon width N are illustrated in Fig. 7. One can be seen that both of them increase continuously with N. In fact, these stretching modulus for 1D ribbons are in agreement with the the elastic modulus $C^{2D}$ for 2D sheet. Assuming a finite width $L_0$ of the optimized ribbon, the elastic modulus $C^{2D}$ for 2D sheet can be obtained as $C^{2D}=C^{1D}/L_0$. For example, when $1\leq N\leq12$, the $L_0$ value of the zigzag nanoribbons is calculated as $1.1\ \text{nm}\leq L_0\leq12.5\ \text{nm}$, then we obtain the effective $C^{2D}$ ranging from 3.70 to 43.5 N/m, slightly smaller than the calculated elastic modulus of the corresponding 2D sheet in Sec 3.2 (43.9 N/m). The reason for this is that the phonon spectrum of nanoribbons broadens toward the high frequency region due to the phonon mode quantization and occurrence of edge phonon modes, then the lattice modes are softened and hence, the elastic modulus are reasonably reduced compared with the 2D sheet. Another noteworthy fact is the stretching modulus $C^{1D}$ of z-BGDYNRs and a-BGDYNRs showing obvious anisotropy. The phenomenon is mainly originated from the significant difference in the ribbon width ($L_0$) along two directions, for example, the width of 6-z-BGDYNR is 10.26 nm, while the width of 6-a-BGDYNR is 5.93 nm. Note that with the increase of ribbon width, the difference gradually decrease and tend to the same value, as can be seen in Fig. 7, the $C^{1D}$ of 12-z-BGDYNR ($3.456\times10^{10}\ \text{eV/cm}$) and 16-z-BGDYNR ($3.452\times10^{10}\ \text{eV/cm}$) are nearly same.

The calculated effective masses for electrons and holes in the zigzag and armchair BGDY nanoribbons are shown in Fig. 8(a). What is slightly abnormal is that the effective masses for holes in the armchair BGDY nanoribbons are rather large when N is small (N<4). While in general, all the effective masses for electrons and holes in the ziagzag and armchair nanoribbons decrease monotonously with the width N and gradually approaches the effective masses for 2D BGDY sheet. Fig. 8(b) shows the

DP constant $E_{1}$ of z-BGDYNRs and a-BGDYNRs due to the quasi-static deformation of the ribbon, both of them display an oscillating behavior as N increases, which might be caused by the edge of nanoribbons.

![](./images/812587938142486528_10.jpg)

Fig. 9. The calculated (a) electron and (b) hole mobilites of z-BGDYNRs and a-BGDYNRs increasing with width N.

From Eq. (2) and the above calculated parameters, we predict the room temperature $\mu$ for z-BGDYNRs and a-BGDYNRs with the ribbons width N, as illustrated in Fig. 9(a-b). In general, both the electron mobilities present a rising trend and reach to the 2D $\mu_{\mathrm{e}}$ (about $1.0 * 10^{5} \mathrm{~cm}^{2} \mathrm{~V}^{-1} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$). Surprisingly, the hole mobilioties of the two type nanoribbons are much larger than the mobility of the 2D sheet when $\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{z}} \geq 6$ and $\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{a}} \geq 9$.
It is worthy to note that similar abnormal phenomenon was also found in another famous 2D materials $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$, when the 2D monolayer $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ sheet is cutted into 1D nanoribbons, the electron mobilities can approach even 3-fold larger than the mobility of 2D sheet [37]. In general, our present work reveals that the mobility of BGDY nanoribbons is comparable to or even larger than that of monolayer BGDY sheet. The width-insensitive carrier mobility is another appealing feature of BGDY for applications in nanoscale electronics and photonics devices.

## 4. Conclusions

In summary, we reported the intrinsic charge carrier mobility of newly synthesized boron-graphdiyne nanosheet and nanoribbons scattered by the longitudinal acoustic phonon, using first-principles density functional theory and the Boltzmann transport equation with the relaxation time approximation. Both the electron and hole mobilities of 2D BGDY sheet are found isotropic and ultra-high, especially, the electron mobility can reach $10^5\ \text{cm}^2\ \text{V}^{-1}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at room temperature. More importantly, all the 1D boron-graphdiyne nanoribbons are found possessing semiconducting behaviors and high carrier mobilities comparable to or even larger than that of 2D BGDY sheet. The numerical results illustrate that BGDY nanosheet and nanoribbons owing moderate direct bandgap, isotropic and ultra-high intrinsic charge carrier mobility have great potential in electronics, photonics and optoelectronics.

### Highlights

1. The charge mobility of a newly synthesized carbon allotrope, the boron-ggraphdiyyne (BGDY) sheet was predicted based on DFT coupled with Boltzmann transport equation with relaxation time approximation for the first time.

2. It's found that monolayer BGDDY is semiconductor with a moderate direct bandgap of about  1.26 eV, both the electron and hole mobilities are isotopic and high, especially, the electron mob can approach as high as $10^5 \ \mathrm{ cm^2 V^{-1 s^{-1$.

3. The electric structures of zigzag and armchair boron-ggraphdiyyne nanonbonsons (BGDDYNRRs) were studied and the results show that all the nan showing semiconductding property.

4. The carrier mob of BGDDYNR Rs are found comparable to or even larger than that of monolayer BGDDY sheet.
