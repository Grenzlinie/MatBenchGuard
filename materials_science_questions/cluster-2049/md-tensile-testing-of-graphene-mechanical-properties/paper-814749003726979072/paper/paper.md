## ABSTRACT

Molecular dynamics simulations have been adopted to study the dependence of number of layers, chirality and hydrogen coverage on the mechanical properties of hydrogen functionalized graphene. We found out that increasing hydrogen coverage results in a drop in critical strain and ultimate strength. The addition of hydrogen atoms from 0% to 30% dramatically weakens the material. Beyond that limit, the critical strain showed less sensitivity to the variation of hydrogen coverage. Further increase in the number of hydrogen atoms results in another sharp decrease in the critical strain. Our results showed that the mechanical properties of hydrogen functionalized graphene depend on the chirality of the sample. Zigzag samples have higher critical strain compared to the armchair counterparts due to the difference in bond alignment between these two structures. In addition, increasing the number of layers produces a weakening effect on the overall mechanical performance in terms of critical strain. Additionally, we studied the mechanical properties of patterned hydrogen functionalized graphene and our results showed that the critical strain and ultimate strength are dependent on the edge geometry of the overall structure. The difference of the edge configuration of the inner graphene nano-ribbon (GNR) also leads to minor discrepancy of these two properties. Our work underlined the importance of considering the configuration of hydrogen functionalized graphene when designing hydrogen functionalized graphene for different applications.

## 1. Introduction

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice. It is the basic building block of 3D graphite and 1D carbon nanotube (CNT), which is reported to exhibit extraordinary mechanical and thermal properties [1–6]. Graphene is first discovered and successfully synthesized in laboratory few years ago [7–9]. Since then, numerous attempts have been devoted into studying its fundamental properties and exploring the potential applications of graphene. For example, its extraordinary electronic properties, such as high electron mobility under room temperature [10], make it an ideal material for next generation logic device [11]. Since the electronic properties of graphene are sensitive to the absorption of gas atoms, it can also be used to fabricate chemical sensors with high sensitivity [12]. The intrinsic strength of graphene, which have been shown to exceed that of others [13], makes it a promising reinforcement material for composite material. A graphene/poly nano-composite material is reported to give a 76% increase in tensile strength and 60% in Young's modulus [14].

Graphene can be used in nano-electro-mechanical system (NEMS) device as well. For instance, single-layer graphene has been suspended and made into nano-resonator because of its robust, stiff and stable structure [15]. Chemical functionalization of graphene via atoms or clusters attachment is considered as an effective way of manipulating, controlling and altering its physical, chemical and electronic properties [16–18]. The resultant functionalized graphene can be used to strengthen composite material [19].

Despite its superior properties and promising applications, the fact that it has a band gap of zero prevents it from being a silicon alternative for the electronic industry [20]. Considerable efforts have been invested into opening an energy gap within this material while conserving other properties. For instance, the dimensions and geometry of the graphene can affect the band gap [21], while the electronic properties will be altered by stacking the graphene to form double-layer or more graphene structure [22]. Chemically, the band gap can be obtained through attaching atoms or clusters onto the graphene sheet. Among many of the chemical functionalization methods, reversible hydrogenation of graphene stands out for the following reasons. Firstly, it opens up a substantial band gap through the process of transforming $sp^2$ to $sp^3$ bonds in the hydrocarbon system [23]. Moreover, the absorption of

In order to further improve the electronic properties of hydrogenated graphene, a prominent boundary separating the carbon atom clusters and the hydrogen attached carbon atoms is preferred [25]. Last but not least, the combination of physical and chemical methods results in greater improvement of the material properties. For instance, GNR can be embedded in a fully hydrogenated graphene in order to achieve better electronic properties [26].

Although there are considerable literatures that detailed the influence of the absorbed atoms on the chemical and electronic properties in chemically functionalized graphene, there is a lack of reports on how the mechanical properties vary as a result of the chemical functionalization. This is especially important for applications of graphene as nano-resonator and nano-filler in the composite material. For example, the resonant frequency depends on the mass and the elastic properties of the material [27]. Functionalizing the graphene by attaching atoms onto it will affect the mechanical properties, and therefore exert an influence on the resonant frequency of the nano-resonator. For applications of graphene as nano-filler, the variation of the elastic properties of the filler has an influence on the overall performance of the composite material. Therefore, it is crucial to understand how functionalization affects the mechanical properties of the material.

In this work, a comprehensive study of hydrogen functionalized graphene is presented. The results were obtained by using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Our results revealed that the attachment of the hydrogen atoms to the graphene has a dramatic impact on the mechanical properties of the material. Specifically, the critical strain and ultimate strength decrease with the increase in hydrogen coverage. Moreover, the chirality and thickness of the material were found to be capable of altering the mechanical performance. Mechanical properties of patterned hydrogen functionalized graphene were studied as well. The critical strain and ultimate strength varied dramatically due to a change in the overall edge configuration. Minor influence of the edge geometry of the inner GNR was also observed. Our results underlined the importance of taking the edge configuration of the material into consideration when designing and producing hydrogen functionalized graphene for different applications.

## 2. Simulation method

The MD simulations were carried out using LAMMPS [28], which is a classical molecular dynamics code, and an acronym from large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator. We adopted the adaptive intermolecular reactive bond order (AIREBO [29]) potential which allows for covalent bond breaking and forming that enables accurate description of interactions between bonds. All the simulations were carried out at a temperature of 300 K. Prior to tensile loading, all samples were relaxed to ensure that the structures were equilibrated. The simulation time step was 0.01 ps.

A molecular model of $50\,\mathring{A}$ by $50\,\mathring{A}$ graphene sample that consists of 1005 carbon atoms is shown in Fig. 1. The graphene sheet exhibits armchair edges along the x-axes and zigzag edges along the y-axes. Specifically, the graphene exhibits an armchair configuration if the external loading is applied along the x direction. Similarly, the graphene is known to show a zigzag configuration if the external loading is applied along the y direction. To characterize the mechanical properties, we applied axial tensile loading to the sample either in the x or y direction.

Hydrogenated graphene, with hydrogen coverage ranging from 10% to 100%, were obtained by attaching hydrogen atoms onto the carbon atoms of the graphene sheet randomly. Specifically, in hydrogen functionalized graphene, the neighbouring hydrogen atoms were attached to the opposite side of the graphene sheet as shown in Fig. 2. However, frustrated hydrogen domain was reported to exist in Flores' work [30]. And double-layer hydrogenated graphene sample was obtained by stacking up two layers of hydrogen functionalized graphene on top of each other. The sample of patterned hydrogen functionalized graphene was obtained by fully attaching hydrogen atoms to graphene sheet, leaving the centre rows or columns of carbon atoms unattached.

## 3. Results and discussions

### 3.1. Randomly distributed hydrogenated graphene

To apply tensile loading to the sample, the bottom (or left) two rows of carbon atoms are fixed, while the top (or right) two rows of carbon atoms are moved outwards 0.0005 nm in each loading increment. Between each loading application, we ran a further 1000 timesteps to relax the structure.

Typical force-strain curves of single-layer hydrogen functionalized armchair graphene with hydrogen coverage of 30% and 100% together with that of a single-layer pristine graphene sample are plotted in Fig. 4. It can be observed from these curves that each of these samples undergoes similar loading process at the initial stage up to strain = 0.02, at which the three curves are very close to one another. The critical strain and ultimate strength of the armchair pristine graphene are 11.3% and 1190 eV/Å, both of which agree well with published data [31]. However, samples of hydrogen functionalized graphene fail at lower strain with lower ultimate strength compared to pristine graphene. For example, according to Fig. 4, graphene with 30% hydrogen coverage fractures at a critical strain of 6.7% and exhibits an ultimate strength of 393 eV/Å, which is a drop of 68.4% and 67.0% respectively compared to its pristine counterpart. Moreover, with higher hydrogen coverage, the weakening effect on mechanical properties becomes more prominent. This is exemplified by the low critical strain of 2.5% and ultimate strength of 124 eV/Å of the fully hydrogenated graphene's. Therefore, we conclude that the addition of hydrogen atoms through functionalizing the graphene sheet deteriorates the tensile properties of the structure, resulting in a dramatic drop in both critical strain and ultimate strength.

The critical strain reduces dramatically at region one, in which the hydrogen coverage increases from 0% to 30%, which constitutes to a drop of about 45% in the critical strains of the single-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene (both armchair and zigzag, see Fig. 5(a) and (b)). As the hydrogen coverage continues to increase from 30% to 70%, the critical strain becomes less sensitive to the variation in hydrogen coverage. Compared with that of pristine graphene, the drop in the critical strain of the single-layer of hydrogen functionalized graphene with 30% hydrogen coverage are 13.0% and 9.1% for armchair and zigzag sample respectively. At the final region where the hydrogen coverage increases from 70% up till 100%, the critical strain drops dramatically by 43.5% and 20.9% for the armchair and zigzag sample respectively.

Similar to the critical strains of the single-layer samples, three regions can be identified from the curves of double-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene samples as shown in Figs. 5(c) and (d). At the initial stage, where the hydrogen coverage increases from 0% to 30%, we observed a sharp drop in the critical strain. For example, the double-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene with 30% hydrogen coverage amounts up to 50% drop in the critical strain for both the armchair and zigzag samples. However, as the hydrogen coverage increases from 30% to 70%, subtle change in the critical strain is evident. Critical strain of the double-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene with 70% hydrogen coverage drops by 15.6% and 21.2% for armchair and zigzag sample respectively, compared to those of the pristine graphene. When the double-layer graphene is 100% functionalised with hydrogen, we observed 34.5% and 37.4% drop in the critical strain for armchair and zigzag sample respectively.

The variation in the sensitivity of critical strain of randomly hydrogen functionalized graphene can be explained by the percolation theory [32], which deals with problems of mutually independent events. Here we can consider the simulation sample as a porous material with sites to be filled in by bonds. As the hydrogen atoms are randomly distributed, the process can be considered as a mutually independent event. It is reported that the site percolation threshold is 0.6962 for a honeycomb lattice material [32]. In other words, a hydrogen coverage of less than 30% would translate to more than 70% $sp^2$ bonds. The large number of $sp^2$ bonds provides a huge possibility for $sp^2$ clusters to connect with one another spanning across the graphene sample. As the hydrogen coverage increases, the number of $sp^2$ clusters will reduce, resulting in a change in the mechanical properties. However, when there is 30% to 70% hydrogen coverage, both $sp^2$ and $sp^3$ bonds will co-exist at the same time. This prohibits the formation of connected $sp^2$ or $sp^3$ clusters spanning across the graphene sample. As a result, at 30% to 70% hydrogen coverage, the hydrogen content has minimal effect on the mechanical properties. When the hydrogen coverage exceeds beyond 70%, there will be a greater number of clusters of $sp^3$ bonds. This allows the formation of connected $sp^3$ clusters spanning across the graphene sample that leads to weaker mechanical properties. Moreover, the increase in hydrogen content of the sample results in more corrugation of the graphene membranes. And these out-of-plane deformations lead to extra strain energy which eventually weakens the material.

Fig. 6 shows the illustrations of a single-layer armchair and zigzag graphene. By applying an external force to the hydrogen functionalized armchair graphene as shown in Fig. 6(a), the bonds which are aligned along the x direction (type A bonds, in red) resist most of the loading. Further increase in the external force results in elongation of these bonds. Upon further elongation, the bonds break and lead to the failure of the whole structure. When the external loading is applied to the hydrogen functionalized zigzag graphene as shown in Fig. 6(b), the bonds (type B bonds, in red)

Further increase in the loading will then result in direct stretching of these bonds. Therefore, hydrogen functionalized zig- zag graphene undergoes an angle widening deformation followed by an elongation of the bonds before it fails. As such, hydrogen functionalized zigzag graphene exhibits greater critical strain com- pared to their armchair counterpart.

Last but not least, we consider the dependence of critical strain on the number of layers in the simulation samples. It is well known that layering up two single-layer pristine graphene sheets results in better mechanical performance in terms of critical strain and ultimate strength, although any further increase in the number of layers (3 or more) leads to decline of the mechanical properties [33]. And it can be observed from Fig. 5 that double-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene samples give lower critical strain than their single-layer counterpart regardless of the chirality. One of the possible reasons which lead to the weakening effect can be explained as follows.

Firstly, applying tensile loading to both double-layer pristine graphene and double-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene results in the deformation of both samples. As a result, each of the two layers of the pristine graphene will undergo the same load- ing and deformation process due to their identical structure. How- ever, since each of the two layers of hydrogen functionalized graphene is randomly hydrogenated, it is highly probable that these two layers have different structures. Therefore, the external loading may result in different deformation process in these two layers and the mismatch may induce extra strain energy to the whole structure. Additionally, the difference of the bonding condi- tion in these two samples may have an impact on the mechanical properties of these two samples. On one hand, the external loading leads to an increase in the repulsive force between carbon atoms from different layers of double-layer pristine graphene. On the other hand, the tension leads to a rise in the repulsive force between not only the carbon atoms and hydrogen atoms but also the hydrogen and carbon atoms from different layers of the dou- ble-layer hydrogenated graphene. As a result, the extra repulsive force due to the interaction between the hydrogen atoms and, the carbon and hydrogen atoms add on extra strain to the whole structure. Therefore, instead of strengthening the mechanical properties, stacking up single-layer hydrogen functionalized graphene to double layer or more actually weakens the structure.

From the results above, we concluded that the edge configuration of the overall structure of patterned hydrogen functionalized graphene has a strong influence on the mechanical properties. For example, the zigzag edged configuration will yield 62.3% better in critical strain and 76.0% better in ultimate strength compared to the armchair edged configuration. In addition, the critical strain and ultimate strength show minor dependence on the inner GNR edge geometry (less than 9.4% and 10.0% for critical strain and ultimate strength respectively).

Next, we consider the difference in mechanical properties between patterned hydrogen functionalized and randomly hydrogen functionalized graphene with 93.4% hydrogen coverage. A number of hydrogen atoms are randomly distributed on a pristine graphene sheet to achieve the same hydrogen coverage as the patterned sample. Fig. 8 plots the force–strain curves of Z-A patterned hydrogen functionalized configuration and the random sample with 93.4% hydrogen coverage. It can be observed that the Z-A patterned sample exhibits more than twice the critical strain and more than triple the ultimate strength than the sample with hydrogen atoms randomly distributed and attached.

The difference can be explained as follows. It is well known that hydrogen functionalization of graphene results in transformation of $sp^2$ bonds into sp3 bonds [27]. When the hydrogen coverage is 93.4%, there will be less than 70% $sp^2$ bonds, which results in a lower likelihood of forming connected $sp^2$ clusters that span across the graphene sample. Therefore, in this situation, the capability of resisting external loading is heavily dependent on the remaining majority $sp^3$ bonds. However, in the case of patterned hydrogen functionalized graphene, the carbon–carbon bonds in the GNR are still $sp^2$ hybridized since there is no hydrogen atoms attached. With connected $sp^2$ bonds clusters spanning across the two opposite edges, it becomes more difficult to break the material. So we attribute the discrepancy of mechanical properties between patterned and randomly hydrogen functionalized graphene to the different bonding conditions formed in these two structures. These results suggest that with the same hydrogen coverage, it is more desirable to dope the hydrogen atoms in a patterned manner to achieve better mechanical properties.

## 4. Conclusion
In summary, we carried out comprehensive MD simulations to study the mechanical properties of hydrogen functionalized graphene. Mechanical properties of hydrogen functionalized graphene with different edge geometries, hydrogen coverages and number of layers have been obtained and compared. We observed that with 0 to 30% hydrogen coverage, the critical strains drop dramatically due to the sharp decreasing number of $sp^2$ bond clusters. The mechanical properties become less sensitive to further increase in hydrogen atoms until the coverage reaches 70%. Further increase in hydrogen coverage results in the formation of $sp^3$ bond clusters, which lead to another dramatic drop of critical strain.

In addition, we conclude that chirality exerts influence on the mechanical performance of the material. Hydrogen functionalized zigzag graphene exhibits greater critical strain compared to its armchair counterpart due to the difference in the bond alignment. Moreover, stacking up hydrogen functionalized graphene to double-layers has a weakening effect on the mechanical properties of the material. This is due to the rise in the repulsive force between the hydrogen atoms and, the hydrogen and carbon atoms from different layers.

Mechanical properties of patterned hydrogen functionalized graphene have been studied as well. Tensile tests have been performed on hydrogen functionalized graphene samples with four different patterns. We found that the critical strain and ultimate strength largely depend on the edge geometry of the overall structure. And the chirality of the inner GNR has a minor impact on the mechanical performance of the material. Our work suggests that the configuration (patterned or randomized) of the hydrogen functionalized graphene has to be considered when designing hydrogenated graphene for different applications to ensure the desirable mechanical features are achieved.
