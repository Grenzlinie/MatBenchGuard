(Received 20 August 2006; in final form 3 March 2007)

We have established the cohesive law for carbon nanotube (CNT) walls in multi-wall CNTs. The interactions between CNT walls are characterized by the van der Waals force. The tensile cohesive strength and cohesive energy are given in terms of the area density of CNT and parameters in the van der Waals force. For an infinitely long CNT, the shear cohesive stress between CNT walls vanishes, and the tensile cohesive stress depends only on the opening displacement. For a finite CNT, the tensile cohesive stress remains the same, but the shear cohesive stress depends on both opening and sliding displacements, i.e. the tension/shear coupling. The simple, analytical expressions of the cohesive law are useful to study the interaction between walls in multi-wall CNTs.

### 1. Introduction

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) display superior mechanical properties and have many potential applications such as nanocomposites. For multi-wall CNTs, the wall-to-wall interaction is the van der Waals force [1, 2].

Continuum models have been developed for carbon nanotubes [3–11]. As compared to atomistic simulations such as molecular dynamics, continuum models are not constrained on the length and time scales. However, the modelling of interactions between CNT walls has always been a challenge because it is difficult to account for the van der Waals force in continuum models. Pantano *et al.* [12, 13] used nonlinear springs to represent van der Waals force in their finite element analysis of multi-wall carbon nanotubes.

Cohesive zone models have been widely used in the continuum study of fracture [14–16]. A cohesive zone model assumes a relation between the normal (and shear) traction(s) and the opening (and sliding) displacement(s). When implemented in the finite element method, the cohesive zone model is capable of simulating interface

The purpose of this paper is to establish a cohesive law for CNT walls directly from the van der Waals force. The energy between two atoms of distance $r$ due to van der Waals force is usually represented by the Lennard–Jones 6–12 potential,

$$
V(r)=4 \varepsilon\left(\frac{\sigma^{12}}{r^{12}}-\frac{\sigma^{6}}{r^{6}}\right), \tag{1}
$$

where $\sqrt[6]{2} \sigma$ is the equilibrium distance between the atoms, $\varepsilon$ is the bond energy at the equilibrium distance, and they take the values $\varepsilon=0.00239 \mathrm{eV}$ and $\sigma=0.3415 \mathrm{nm}$ for carbon atoms [2]. In section 2 we establish a cohesive law for CNT walls in an infinitely long multi-wall CNT based on the van der Waals force in equation (1), and extend such an approach to a finite multi-wall CNT in section 3. The analytical cohesive laws are suitable for the study of multi-wall CNTs.

## 2. The cohesive law for an infinitely long multi-wall carbon nanotube

### 2.1. Graphene|graphene

We first neglect the effect of CNT radius and study the interaction between two parallel graphenes (i.e. two infinite plane of carbon atoms). Let $h$ denote the equilibrium distance between two graphenes (figure 1a). In order to establish a continuum cohesive law, we homogenize carbon atoms on each graphene and represent it by an area density, $\rho_{c}=4 /\left(3 \sqrt{3} l_{0}^{2}\right)$, where $l_{0}$ is the equilibrium bond length of graphene prior to deformation. Such homogenization neglects the effect

![](./images/811991215208660993_2.jpg)

Figure 1. A schematic diagram of two parallel graphenes: (a) the distance between two graphenes is $h$; (b) the graphene is subjected to the opening and sliding displacements.

of lattice registry, whose effect is rather small as to be shown in appendix A.
The number of carbon atoms over an area $dA$ on the graphene is $\rho_c dA$.

The distance between two points on two graphenes is $r=\sqrt{h^2+z^2}$, where $z$ is the projected distance between the two points on each graphene (figure 1a). The energy due to the van der Waals force is given by $V(r)$ in equation (1). For an infinitesimal area $dA$, the energy in the graphene due to the van der Waals force is

$$
\rho_{c} \mathrm{d} A \int_{A} V(r) \rho_{c} \mathrm{d} A=2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \mathrm{d} A \int_{0}^{\infty} V(r) z \mathrm{d} z,
\tag{2}
$$

where the integration is over the infinite area $A$ of the graphene. The cohesive energy $\Phi$ is the energy per unit area, and is given by

$$
\Phi=2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \int_{0}^{\infty} V(r) z \mathrm{d} z=2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma^{2}\left(\frac{2 \sigma^{10}}{5 h^{10}}-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{h^{4}}\right),
\tag{3}
$$

where $\sqrt[6]{2} \sigma$ is the equilibrium distance between two carbon atoms interacting via the van der Waals force, and $\varepsilon$ is the corresponding bond energy. The equilibrium distance $h$ is determined by minimizing the energy, $\partial \Phi / \partial h=0$, as

$$
h=\sigma,
\tag{4}
$$

i.e. the equilibrium distance $h$ between two graphenes is the same as the characteristic length $\sigma$ in the van der Waals force.

For the opening displacement $v$ and sliding displacement $u$ beyond the equilibrium distance $h$, as shown in figure 1b, the cohesive energy can be similarly obtained as

$$
\Phi(u, v)=2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma^{2}\left[\frac{2 \sigma^{10}}{5(\sigma+v)^{10}}-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{(\sigma+v)^{4}}\right],
\tag{5}
$$

which can also be obtained from equation (3) by simply replacing $h$ with $\sigma+v$.
Equation (5) is independent of the sliding displacement $u$ because sliding does not change the van der Waals force for two infinite graphenes. This leads to a vanishing shear cohesive stress

$$
\tau_{\text {cohesive }}=\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial u}=0.
\tag{6}
$$

The tensile cohesive stress is obtained from equation (5) as

$$
\sigma_{\text {cohesive }}=\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial v}=8 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma\left[\frac{\sigma^{5}}{(\sigma+v)^{5}}-\frac{\sigma^{11}}{(\sigma+v)^{11}}\right].
\tag{7}
$$

Equations (6)–(7) give the cohesive law for two graphenes based on the van der Waals force. Such a cohesive law gives the following cohesive properties:

(i) initial slope (initial value of $\mathrm{d} \sigma_{\text {cohesive }} / \mathrm{d} v$ at $v=0$) $48 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon$;
(ii) cohesive strength (maximum cohesive stress) $\sigma_{\max }=(48 \pi / 11)(5 / 11)^{5 / 6} \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma$;
(iii) critical separation $\delta_{0}=\left[(11 / 5)^{1 / 6}-1\right] \sigma$ at which the cohesive strength is reached;

(iv) total cohesive energy (area underneath the $\sigma_{\text{cohesive-v}}$ curve)
$$\Phi_{\text{total}}=6\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma^2/5.$$

For $\varepsilon=0.00239\,\text{eV}$, $\sigma=0.3415\,\text{nm}$ [2], $\rho_c=4/(3\sqrt{3}l_0^2)$ and $l_0=0.142\,\text{nm}$ for graphene, the total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}=0.245\,\text{J}\,\text{m}^{-2}$, which is rather small and reflects the weak van der Waals interactions. It is on the same order as the CNT adhesion energy reported in experiments and atomistic simulations [33]. The cohesive strength, however, is very large, $\sigma_{\text{max}}=1.35\,\text{GPa}$.

The cohesive law in equations (5) and (7) can be rewritten in terms of the cohesive strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ and total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ as

$$
\Phi(u, v)=\frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}}{3}\left\{\frac{2}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{10}}-\frac{5}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{4}}\right\}, \quad (8)
$$

$$
\sigma_{\text{cohesive}}=3.54\sigma_{\text{max}}\left\{\frac{1}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{5}}-\frac{1}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{11}}\right\}. \quad (9)
$$

The initial slope and critical separation are given in terms of $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ and $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ by $11.3(\sigma_{\text{max}}^2/\Phi_{\text{total}})$ and $0.265(\Phi_{\text{total}}/\sigma_{\text{max}})$, respectively.

### 2.2. Carbon nanotube walls
We now account for the effect of CNT radius and study two concentric CNTs. Let $h$ now denote the equilibrium distance between two CNT walls (figure 2). We homogenize carbon atoms on each CNT and represent them by an area density $\rho_c$ (which may be slightly different from the area density of graphene due to the effect of CNT radius).

The cylindrical coordinates $(R,\theta,z)$ are used, where $z$ denotes the central axis of CNTs. Without losing generality, we take a point $(R,0,0)$ on the inner CNT wall and a point $(R+h,\theta,z)$ on the outer CNT wall, where $R$ is the radius of inner CNT.

![](./images/811991215208660993_3.jpg)

Figure 2. A schematic diagram of two carbon nanotube (CNT) walls. The radius of inner CNT is $R$, and the distance between two CNTs is $h$.

The distance between these two points is $r = \sqrt{(R+h)^2 - 2R(R+h)\cos\theta + R^2 + z^2}$.
The energy due to the van der Waals force is still given by $V(r)$ in (1.1).

We examine a section of CNTs with height dz. The energy in this section of CNTs due to the van der Waals force is

$$
\rho_c 2\pi R\mathrm{d}z \int_A V(r)\rho_c \mathrm{d}A = 2\pi\rho_c^2 R(R+h)\mathrm{d}z \int_0^{2\pi} \mathrm{d}\theta \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(r)\mathrm{d}z', \tag{10}
$$

where $r = \sqrt{(R+h)^2 - 2R(R+h)\cos\theta + R^2 + z^2}$. The cohesive energy $\Phi$ is the energy per unit area, and is given by

$$
\Phi = \frac{2\pi\rho_c^2 R(R+h)\mathrm{d}z \int_0^{2\pi} \mathrm{d}\theta \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(r)\mathrm{d}z'}{2\pi(R+h/2)\mathrm{d}z} = \frac{\rho_c^2 R(R+h) \int_0^{2\pi} \mathrm{d}\theta \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(r)\mathrm{d}z'}{R+(h/2)}, \tag{11}
$$

where $2\pi(R+h/2)\mathrm{d}z$ is the average of inner CNT area, $2\pi R\mathrm{d}z$, and outer CNT area, $2\pi(R+h)\mathrm{d}z$. The above integral can be expressed in terms of the elliptic integral, as shown in appendix B.

Figure 3 shows the cohesive energy, normalized by the total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}} = 6\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma^2/5$ which equals $0.245\ \mathrm{J\ m^{-2}}$ for graphene, versus the distance $h$ between two CNT walls for several radii of the inner CNT, where $h$ is normalized by the characteristic length $\sigma=0.3415\ \mathrm{nm}$ in the van der Waals force. The curves for different CNTs are very close, and they are all close to that for graphene. This suggests that the CNT radius has little effect on the cohesive energy. Each curve has a minimum corresponding to the equilibrium distance between two CNT walls, and this distance is very close to $\sigma$.

![](./images/811991215208660993_4.jpg)

Figure 3. The cohesive energy, normalized by the total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ for graphene, versus the distance $h$ between two carbon nanotubes (CNT) for several CNT radii and graphene, where $h$ is normalized by the characteristic length $\sigma=0.3415\ \mathrm{nm}$ in the van der Waals force.

In order to gain further insight into the effect of CNT radius, we expand equation (11) to the Taylor series of $h/R$ for $R>h$,

$$
\Phi=2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma^{2}\left(\frac{2 \sigma^{10}}{5 h^{10}}-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{h^{4}}\right)\left[1+O\left(\frac{h^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)\right],
\tag{12}
$$

where the leading term (zeroth order) corresponds to graphene, and first order term vanishes such that the CNT radius $R$ appears only in the second order term. The equilibrium distance $h$ is determined by minimizing the energy, $\partial \Phi / \partial h=0$, as

$$
h=\sigma\left[1+O\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)\right].
\tag{13}
$$

Similar to two graphenes, the cohesive energy between two CNT walls is independent of the sliding displacement $u$ such that the shear cohesive stress vanishes. If we keep only the leading (zeroth order) and first order terms, the cohesive energy and tensile cohesive stress are still given by equations (5) and (7), respectively, and the cohesive law in equations (8)-(9) also holds.

Figure 4 shows the tensile cohesive stress, $\sigma_{\text {cohesive, }}$ normalized by the cohesive strength $\sigma_{\max }$ which equals $\sigma_{\max }=1.35 \mathrm{GPa}$ for graphene, versus the normalized opening displacement $v / \delta_{0}$ between two graphenes, where $\delta_{0}=0.265\left(\Phi_{\text {total }} / \sigma_{\max }\right)$ is the critical separation at which the cohesive strength is reached, and $\delta_{0}=0.0480 \mathrm{~nm}$ for two graphenes. The cohesive stress increases rapidly at small opening displacement, and gradually decreases after the cohesive strength is reached.

![](./images/811991215208660993_5.jpg)

Figure 4. The tensile cohesive stress, $\sigma_{\text {cohesive, }}$ normalized by the cohesive strength, $\sigma_{\max }$, versus the normalized opening displacement, $v / \delta_{0}$, where $\delta_{0}=0.265\left(\Phi_{\text {total }} / \sigma_{\max }\right)$ is the critical separation at which the cohesive strength is reached and $\delta_{0}=0.0480 \mathrm{~nm}$ for carbon nanotubes.

### 3. The cohesive law for two finite carbon nanotubes

#### 3.1. Graphene|graphene

We first study the interaction between two graphenes that overlap over a length $L$, as shown in figure 5. The distance between two graphenes is $h$. The area density of graphene is still denoted by $\rho_c$.

Figure 5 shows the Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$, where $x$ is the direction normal to the graphenes, and $y$ is along the graphene free edge. The free edge of one graphene is represented by $(0,y,0)$ while the free edge of the other is $(-h,y,L)$. Without losing generality we consider a point $(0,0,z_c)$ ($z_c \geq 0$) on one graphene, and a point $(-h,y,z_c')$ ($z_c' \leq L$) on the other graphene. The distance between these two points is $r = \sqrt{h^2 + y^2 + (z_c' - z_c)^2}$. The energy due to van der Waals force is given by $V(r)$ in equation (1).

Unlike the analysis in section 2, the deformation for finite graphenes is non-uniform. We define the energy per unit thickness $\Phi_{\text{line}}$ (along the free edge) stored between two graphenes due to van der Waals forces as

$$
\Phi_{\text{line}} = \int_{0}^{\infty} \rho_c \mathrm{d}z_c \int_{A} V(r)\rho_c \mathrm{d}A = \rho_c^2 \int_{0}^{\infty} \mathrm{d}z_c \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{d}y \int_{-\infty}^{L} V(r)\mathrm{d}z_c'. \tag{14}
$$

Here we have taken $\infty$ as the upper limit of integration for $\mathrm{d}z_c$ since the van der Waals force decreases very fast with the distance. Similarly, the limits of integration for $\mathrm{d}y$ and the lower limit of integration for $\mathrm{d}z_c'$ are taken as $\pm\infty$ and $-\infty$, respectively. After integration, equation (14) becomes

$$
\Phi_{\text{line}}(L,h) = 2\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma^2 \left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\sigma^{10}}{5h^{10}} \left[ L + \sqrt{h^2 + L^2} - \frac{h^2}{2\sqrt{h^2 + L^2}} \right. \\
&\quad\quad\left. - \frac{h^4}{8\sqrt{(h^2 + L^2)^3}} - \frac{h^6}{16\sqrt{(h^2 + L^2)^5}} - \frac{5h^8}{128\sqrt{(h^2 + L^2)^7}} \right] \\
&-\frac{\sigma^4}{2h^4} \left[ L + \sqrt{h^2 + L^2} - \frac{h^2}{2\sqrt{h^2 + L^2}} \right]
\end{aligned}
\right\}. \tag{15}
$$

![](./images/811991215208660993_6.jpg)

Figure 5. A schematic diagram of two parallel graphenes with overlap length $L$; the distance between two graphenes is $h$.

For the overlap length $L$ (figure 5) larger than $h$, the Taylor expansion of equation (15) with respect to $h/L$ gives

$$
\Phi_{\text{line}}(L, h)=2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma^{2}\left(\frac{2 \sigma^{10}}{5 h^{10}}-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{h^{4}}\right) L\left[1+O\left(\frac{h^{4}}{L^{4}}\right)\right],
\tag{16}
$$

where $2 \pi \rho_{c}^{2} \varepsilon \sigma^{2}[2 \sigma^{10}/(5 h^{10})-\sigma^{4}/h^{4}]$ is identical to equation (3) for two graphenes; the terms neglected are on the order of $h^{4}/L^{4}$, and therefore are very small.
The equilibrium distance $h$ determined from $\partial \Phi_{\text{line}}/\partial h=0$ is

$$
h=\sigma\left[1+O\left(\frac{h^{4}}{L^{4}}\right)\right],
\tag{17}
$$

which is essentially the same as equation (4) for infinite graphenes. Figure 6 compares the energy $\Phi_{\text{line}}$ given by the exact solution [equation (15)] and the approximate solution [equation (16)], where $\Phi_{\text{line}}$ is normalized by $\Phi_{\text{total}}/h$, and the equilibrium distance $h$ is taken as $\sigma$. For length $L>h$, equation (16) is an excellent approximation of the exact solution [equation (15)].

For the opening displacement $v$ and sliding displacement $u$ beyond the equilibrium distance $h$, $\Phi_{\text{line}}$ is obtained from equation (15) by replacing $L$ and $h$ with $L-u$ and $h+v$, respectively, that is

$$
\Phi_{\text{line}}=\Phi_{\text{line}}(L-u, h+v).
\tag{18}
$$

![](./images/811991215208660993_7.jpg)

Figure 6. The energy $\Phi_{\text{line}}$, normalized by $\Phi_{\text{total}}/h$, versus the normalized overlap length $L/h$, where $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ is the total cohesive energy for a graphene and $h$ is the distance between two graphenes.

It depends on the sliding and opening displacements through the current overlap length $L-u$ and distance $h+v$ between two graphenes. For $L-u\gg h+v$, the Taylor expansion in equation (16) becomes

$$
\Phi_{\text{line}} \approx 2\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma^2\left[\frac{2\sigma^{10}}{5(\sigma+v)^{10}}-\frac{\sigma^4}{(\sigma+v)^4}\right](L-u),\tag{19}
$$

where $h$ has been replaced by $\sigma$.

The tangential and normal forces (per unit thickness along the graphene free edge) are

$$
F_{\text{tangent}}=\frac{\partial\Phi_{\text{line}}}{\partial u}\approx 2\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma^2\left[\frac{\sigma^4}{(\sigma+v)^4}-\frac{2\sigma^{10}}{5(\sigma+v)^{10}}\right],\tag{20}
$$

$$
F_{\text{normal}}=\frac{\partial\Phi_{\text{line}}}{\partial v}\approx 8\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma\left[\frac{\sigma^5}{(\sigma+v)^5}-\frac{\sigma^{11}}{(\sigma+v)^{11}}\right](L-u).\tag{21}
$$

We define the average shear and tensile cohesive stresses over the current overlap length $L-u$ as

$$
\tau_{\text{cohesive}}=\frac{F_{\text{tangent}}}{L-u}\approx 2\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma\left[\frac{\sigma^4}{(\sigma+v)^4}-\frac{2\sigma^{10}}{5(\sigma+v)^{10}}\right]\frac{\sigma}{L-u},\tag{22}
$$

$$
\sigma_{\text{cohesive}}=\frac{F_{\text{normal}}}{L-u}\approx 8\pi\rho_c^2\varepsilon\sigma\left[\frac{\sigma^5}{(\sigma+v)^5}-\frac{\sigma^{11}}{(\sigma+v)^{11}}\right],\tag{23}
$$

where the terms neglected are on the order of $\sigma^4/(L-u)^4$.

Equations (22)-(23) give the cohesive law for finite graphenes based on the van der Waals force. The average tensile cohesive stress $\sigma_{\text{cohesive}}$ is identical to equation (7) for infinite graphenes, and does not depend on the sliding displacement $u$. Therefore, the tensile cohesive law, equation (9), given in terms of the cohesive strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ and total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ still holds. The average shear cohesive stress $\tau_{\text{cohesive}}$ in equation (22) is much smaller than $\sigma_{\text{cohesive}}$ because $L-u\gg\sigma$, and it depends on both sliding and opening displacements, $u$ and $v$, i.e. tension/shear coupling in the cohesive law. The shear cohesive law, equation (22), can be rewritten in terms of the cohesive strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ and total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ as

$$
\tau_{\text{cohesive}}=\frac{5\sigma_{\text{max}}}{3}\left\{\frac{1}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^4}-\frac{2}{5\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{10}}\right\}\frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}}{\sigma_{\text{max}}(L-u)}.\tag{24}
$$

Figure 7 shows the average shear cohesive stress, $\tau_{\text{cohesive}}$, normalized by the current overlap length $L-u$ and total cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}$, $(L-u)(\tau_{\text{cohesive}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})$, versus the normalized opening displacement $v/\delta_0$, where $\delta_0=0.265(\Phi_{\text{total}}/\sigma_{\text{max}})$ is the critical separation at which the tensile cohesive strength is reached, and $\delta_0=0.0480$ nm for two graphenes. The average shear cohesive stress is not zero even at $v=0$ because the two sides of the overlap length $L$ are not symmetric such that the tangential force does not vanish. In fact, at $v=0$, $\tau_{\text{cohesive}}$ has a zero

### 3.2. Carbon nanotubes walls
Similar to section 2.2, it can be shown that the effect of CNT radius is on the order of $h^2/R^2$. For example, the tensile and shear cohesive stresses in equations (9) and (24) now become

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{\text{cohesive}} &= 3.54 \sigma_{\text{max}} \left\{ \frac{1}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^5} - \frac{1}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{11}} \right\} \\
&* \left[ 1 + O\left( \frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}/\sigma_{\text{max}}}{R} \right)^2 + O\left( \frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}/\sigma_{\text{max}}}{L-u} \right)^4 \right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{25}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_{\text{cohesive}} &= \frac{5\sigma_{\text{max}}}{3} \left\{ \frac{1}{\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^4} - \frac{2}{5\left[1+0.530(\sigma_{\text{max}}/\Phi_{\text{total}})v\right]^{10}} \right\} \frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}}{\sigma_{\text{max}}(L-u)} \\
&* \left[ 1 + O\left( \frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}/\sigma_{\text{max}}}{R} \right)^2 + O\left( \frac{\Phi_{\text{total}}/\sigma_{\text{max}}}{L-u} \right)^4 \right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{26}
$$

Therefore, the analytical expressions of the cohesive law for graphenes can be effectively used for CNTs.

### 4. Concluding remarks and discussion

We have obtained the cohesive law for carbon nanotube walls based on the van der Waals force. Such a cohesive law can be used in the continuum study of wall-to-wall interactions in multi-wall carbon nanotubes. The cohesive law is governed by two parameters, namely the tensile cohesive strength $\sigma_{\text{max}}$ and cohesive energy $\Phi_{\text{total}}$ which are given in terms of the area density of carbon nanotube (CNT) and the parameters in the van der Waals force, and are equal to 1.35 GPa and $0.245\,\text{J}\,\text{m}^{-2}$, respectively. For an infinitely long CNT, the shear cohesive stress $\tau_{\text{cohesive}}$ vanishes, and the tensile cohesive stress $\sigma_{\text{cohesive}}$ depends only on the opening displacement $v$. For a finite CNT, $\sigma_{\text{cohesive}}$ remains the same, but $\tau_{\text{cohesive}}$ depends on both opening displacement $v$ and sliding displacement $u$, i.e. the tension/shear coupling. The simple, analytical expressions of the cohesive law are useful to study the interaction between CNT walls in multi-wall CNTs.
