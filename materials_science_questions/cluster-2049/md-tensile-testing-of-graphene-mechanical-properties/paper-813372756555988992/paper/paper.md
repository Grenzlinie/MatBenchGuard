## Regioselectivity control of graphene functionalization by ripples†

Xingfa Gao, $^{a}$ Ying Wang, $^{b}$ Xin Liu, $^{c}$ T.-L. Chan, $^{d}$ Stephan Irle, $^{*b}$ Yuliang Zhao $^{a}$ and Shengbai B. Zhang $^{*d}$

Received 3rd August 2011, Accepted 3rd September 2011
DOI: 10.1039/c1cp22491c

Ripples naturally occur in graphene sheets. First-principles calculations reveal that, by altering the pyramidalization angles of the carbon atoms, these ripples can be used to direct the chemical reactivity of graphene towards hydrogenation. A fraction of the carbon atoms of a rippled graphene, located around the crests and troughs, show significantly increased reactivity.
The remaining carbon atoms have comparable reactivity to those in a flat graphene. To illustrate the increased reactivity, we show that hydrogenation becomes exothermic when the characteristic ratio between the amplitude and wavelength reaches $\sim$0.55. This finding offers a practical chemical venue for regioselectivity control of graphene functionalization. While the rippling does not directly affect the band gap of the graphene, the rippling-induced hydrogenation does.

### 1. Introduction

Graphene sheets are not strictly flat over a long range; instead they bear topographic ripples. The formation of ripples either owes to random thermal fluctuation $^{1-3}$ or mechanical perturbation from external substrates. $^{4-9}$ The ripples, spontaneously formed and usually disordered (Fig. 1A), can break the sixfold symmetry of graphene $^{10}$ and create a mid-gap state, $^{11}$ thereby strongly influencing graphene's electronic properties $^{10,12-15}$ and transport behavior. $^{16}$ Although disordered corrugations have been shown to scarcely affect the van der Waals interactions of graphene with physisorbed water molecules, $^{17}$ a corrugation-enhanced covalent chemical binding of graphene with hydrogen atoms has indeed been predicted. $^{18}$ Therefore, it was proposed to utilize graphene with ripples as novel devices. $^{19,20}$ However, the spontaneously-formed disordered ripples fall short of the required stripe-like ordered structure $^{21}$ for most practical uses.

Recently, *Bao et al.* developed a method for synthesizing well-ordered ripples in a controllable fashion by the thermal cycling of the suspended graphene. $^{22}$ In contrast to the randomly shaped ones (Fig. 1A), *these ripples nearly have a 1-dimensional (1D) periodic structure* (Fig. 1B). The orientations, wavelengths and amplitudes of these ripples can be further engineered by controlling the boundary conditions during their syntheses. $^{22}$ Given the enhanced C-H binding energies that have been predicted for the uplifted carbons in disordered graphene corrugations, $^{18}$ *Bao et al.* demonstrated a fascinating possibility to control the regioselective reactivity of graphene by introducing ripples, an idea that was recently theoretically investigated in ref. 23. After regiospecific functionalization, the product is expected to be stable against stretching, which will yield functionalization "lines" with controllable electronic properties. $^{21}$

Noticeably, the hydrogenation of graphene with disordered corrugations has been recently achieved experimentally. $^{25}$ Fluorine atoms are single radicals, and in that sense are similar to hydrogen. ### 2. Computational methodology

We generated the initial geometries for rippled graphene sheets according to the following equation,

$$
y = a\sin(bx) \tag{1}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ designate the amplitude and wavelength of the ripple function. $y$ represents the ordinate value at position $x$ of the ripple, provided that the ripple wavelength is aligned along the $x$ direction. Similar models of 1-D graphene ripples as sinusoidal structures have been used before, $^{11,15,16,23}$ and they are consistent with experimental observations. $^{9,22}$ Fig. 2 shows a series of rippled graphene sheets generated by eqn (1). For this series, $b$ is $0.2$ $\text{\AA}^{-1}$ while $a$ takes values of 5, 10, 15, and $20$ $\text{\AA}$ (Table 1). Therefore, $\mathbf{b-f}$ all have the same wavelengths of $31.42$ $\text{\AA}$ (i.e. $10\pi$ $\text{\AA}$) but different amplitudes, varying from 5 to $20$ $\text{\AA}$. Among this series, the wavelengths of $\mathbf{b-e}$ are all parallel to the armchair direction of the respective graphene sheets, whereas $\mathbf{f}$ has its wavelength parallel to the zigzag direction (Fig. 2). The graphene sheet with a function of $y=0$ (a) stands for a flat, unrippled graphene sheet, which is also included here as reference.

The geometries of the flat graphene (a) and all rippled graphenes (b-f) were optimized using the spin-unpolarized GGA-PBE/DZP method. The geometries of the hydrides of $\mathbf{b-e}$ were optimized using the spin-polarized GGA-PBE/DZP method. GGA-PBE designates the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) form, $^{29}$ and DZP designates the optimized numerical basis sets of double-$\xi$ quality including polarization functions. A periodic boundary condition (PBC) and a mesh cutoff of 250 Ry were used in the calculations. During geometry optimizations for $\mathbf{b-f}$ and their hydrides, the unit cell sizes along the $x$ direction were fixed at the respective wavelengths, and those along the $y$ direction including the vacuum space were fixed at $100$ $\text{\AA}$, whereas those along $z$ were allowed to relax. As for the geometry optimization of $\mathbf{a}$, only the unit cell size along $y$ was fixed at $100$ $\text{\AA}$. All geometry optimizations were performed until forces were smaller than $0.04$ eV $\text{\AA}^{-1}$. A $1\times1\times7$ $k$-point sampling was used for total energy and geometry optimization, whereas a dense $7\times1\times35$ $k$-point setting was used for band structure calculations. All these calculations were carried out with the SIESTA program. $^{30}$

### 3. Results and discussion

#### 3.1. Geometries and electronic structures

Geometry optimizations suggested that the side-view profiles of the 1-D periodic ripples can indeed be well described by the respective sinusoidal functions that were used to generate their initial geometries, especially for those with smaller amplitude/ wavelength ratios $(A/\lambda)$. This is in agreement with experimental observations. $^{8,22}$ To evaluate how the optimized profiles fit the sinusoidal functions, we calculated the root mean square deviations (RMSDs) for $\mathbf{b-f}$ using the following equation:

$$
\text{RMSD} = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - y)^2}}{n} \tag{2}
$$

where $y_i$ is the calculated ordinate value for the $i$th carbon atom in the unit cell, $y$ is the value derived from the sinusoidal function using the abscissa value of the $i$th carbon, and $n$ is the total number of carbon atoms in the unit cell. The calculated RMSDs for $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$, $\mathbf{d}$ and $\mathbf{f}$ are 0.05, 0.16, 0.12 and $0.06$ $\text{\AA}$, respectively (Table 1). However, that for $\mathbf{e}$ is as large as $0.34$ $\text{\AA}$. This suggests a general trend for the ripple to deviate from the ideal sinusoidal function with increasing $A/\lambda$. In each ripple, the derivations are not distributed equally along the wavelength; larger deviations localize around the crest and trough sites.

A flat graphene has a zero band gap. It was reported that ripples change a graphene sheet from metal to semiconductor by opening a band gap. $^{15}$ However, our first principles band structure calculations of 1D ripples revealed that b-f all have zero band gaps, similar to flat graphene a (for the band structure of e before hydrogenation, see the top panel of Fig. 3). b-e have the orientations and wavelengths aligned parallel to the armchair directions of the graphene sheets, but with different $A/\lambda$ (Fig. 2 and Table 1). In contrast, f has a different wavelength and orientation, which are parallel to the zigzag direction of the graphene (Fig. 2). The zero band gaps for b-f reveal that 1-D periodic ripples do not alter the band gap of graphene, regardless of the $A/\lambda$ and wavelengths and orientations of the ripples. This gapless feature is related to the non-curved dimension perpendicular to the wavelength of a 1D rippled graphene sheet, as opposed to 2D ripples, which do exhibit band gap opening. $^{15}$

### 3.2. Local curvature and chemical reactivity
Ripples introduce local curvature in graphene sheets and therefore are expected to alter the local chemical reactivity. $^{23,31-34}$ The curvature of carbon atoms in non-planar $\pi$-conjugated systems is often quantified using Haddon's concepts of $\pi$-orbital axis vector (POAV) and pyramidalization angle $(\theta_{P})$, determined by the varying degree of p-orbital mixing in carbon $sp^{x}$ hybrid orbitals. $^{35-37}$ We used $\theta_{P}$ to analyze the rippled graphene sheet e. Fig. 4 shows the results for each carbon atom in the unit cell: the filled black circles portray the side-view profile of the atomic coordinates of e, and the empty black circles display the corresponding values of $\theta_{P}$ for the carbons. In a flat graphene, all carbons are free from curvature with $\theta_{P}=0$. However, about half of the carbons in e (i.e. carbons with $x$ from 5 to $12\mathring{A}$ and from 20 to $27\mathring{A}$ in Fig. 4) have their $\theta_{P}$ significantly enhanced above zero. The enhanced $\theta_{P}$ are distributed in a normal fashion around the crest and trough of e. Consequently, these carbons would have larger chemical reactivity compared to those in a flat graphene. These carbons are marked as reactive regions in Fig. 4. In contrast, the remaining carbons (i.e. carbons with $x$ from 0 to $5\mathring{A}$, from 12 to $20\mathring{A}$ and from 27 to $\sim32\mathring{A}$ in Fig. 4) have $\theta_{P}<0.7^{\circ}$. Therefore, these carbons would have comparable reactivity to those in a flat graphene. The crest and trough (at $x=7.2$ and $23.4\mathring{A}$ respectively), where the largest $\theta_{P}(8.9^{\circ})$ is located, are thus the most reactive positions of e.

### 3.3. Hydrogenation and fluorination
To find the proper chemical groups whose reactions with graphene can be fine-tuned by ripples, we studied the hydrogen- ation and fluorination processes for b-e. For all graphene sheets, only the most reactive sites, i.e. the positions closest to the crests and troughs were considered.

The crystal structure of graphene can be regarded as carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal structure consisting of a trian- gular lattice with a base of two atoms per unit cell, sublattices A and B. $^{38,39}$ We first calculated the hydrogenations occurring at two pairs of carbons of e-"C1 and C2" and "C1 and C3" (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5A, C1 and C3 are both black, hence belong to the same sublattice A, whereas C2 is grey and belongs to sublattice B. In e, the number of A-carbons equals that of B-carbons; e thus has a zero net spin. However, the hydrogenations at C1 and C3 saturate two A-carbons while keeping the number of B-carbons unchanged. This destroys the balance between the two sublattices. In contrast, the hydrogena- tions at C1 and C2 saturate one A-carbon and one B-carbon,

Accordingly, one would expect that the hydrogenations at C1 and C3 result in an adduct with a non-zero net spin in the electronic ground state, whereas the hydrogenation at C1 and C2 yields an adduct with zero spin. Indeed, spin-polarized DFT calculations revealed that the addition of H both on C1 and C3 leads to a ferromagnetic (FM) ground state, whereas the addition on C1 and C2 leads to an antiferromagnetic (AFM) ground state. The latter C1-C2 hydride (AFM) was energetically lower than the C1-C3 one (FM) by ~0.2 eV and can be considered to be less reactive. Therefore, only additions occurring on different sublattices closest to the crests and troughs of b-e were further considered.

The bond lengths for C-H and C-F of hydrogenated and fluorinated adducts were found to be roughly 1.1 Å and 1.4 Å, respectively, for systems b-e. The reaction energies for b-e were calculated using the following equation: $E_{r}=E(\text{adduct})-E(\text{graphene})-E(\text{X}_{2})$, where $\text{X}=\text{H},\text{F}$. The energies are plotted in Fig. 6 and listed in Table 2. As expected, both $E_{r}$ decrease with increasing $A/\lambda$, which is consistent with the result reported for the hydrogenation of graphene with disordered corrugations. $^{18}$ All values of $E_{r}(\text{F})$ are negative, suggesting high reactivity of $\text{F}_{2}$ with graphene. Apparently, $\text{F}_{2}$ is too reactive to exhibit regioselectivity control by graphene ripples. In sharp contrast, the hydrogenation shows a distinct feature: the $E_{r}(\text{H})$ for a rippled graphene with smaller $A/\lambda$ (e.g. b-d) is positive; however, as $A/\lambda$ increases (as in e), $E_{r}(\text{H})$ changes sign. By interpolation, the reactivity changes sign at $A/\lambda \sim 0.55$. The negative $E_{r}(\text{H})$ for e indicates that its hydrogenation is a thermodynamically spontaneous process, at least in terms of reaction exothermicity. Therefore, the hydrogenation and dehydrogenation of graphene sheets can in principle be tuned by rippling: increasing $A/\lambda$ of the ripple makes the hydrogenation process more thermally favorable, whereas decreasing $A/\lambda$ gradually initiates the dehydrogenation process.

The band structure of e after hydrogenations at C1 and C2 is shown in the bottom panel of Fig. 3. In contrast to the zero band gap that has been predicted for pristine e (Fig. 3, top panel), the hydride of e has a band opening of ~0.15 eV (Fig. 3, bottom panel). In the band structure for the hydride, the "gap" states near the Fermi level can be ascribed to the contribution of carbons neighboring the addition sites (i.e. C1 and C2 in Fig. 5A), which have dangling $\pi$-electrons similar to carbons on the edges of nanographene. $^{40}$ The tunable hydrogenation reactions can be used to control graphene properties such as band gaps and gap states. $^{23}$ This prediction is in agreement with the recent experiment in which a reversible hydrogenation was used to control the properties of graphenewith disordered ripples. $^{25}$

### 3.4. Reaction driving force
To study the driving force for the additions, we calculated the local atomic curvatures for the two carbons in b-e (denoted as C1 and C2) where H or F atoms were attached. For each carbon, the local atomic curvature was approximated by using the inverse radius of a sphere best fitted to this carbon and its three bonded neighbors. Table 2 shows the curvatures at C1 and C2 and the average values in addition to the values of $E_{r}$. To illustrate the relationship between the average curvatures and reaction energies, we plotted Fig. 7. It is revealed that the curvature and the functionalization energy show approximately a linear dependence on the average local carbon curvature. Such a linear relationship, although not as perfect as that found for the side-wall reactions of carbon nanotubes, $^{32,33}$ indicates that the increase in local atomic strain is mainly responsible for the enhancement of chemical reactivity with $A/\lambda$, and that the relief of local atomic strain provides the main driving force for the addition reactions.

Fig. 5 portrays the relaxed unit cells for e before and after C1 and C2 hydrogenations. Before hydrogenation, all C-C bond lengths are ~1.43 Å (Fig. 5A), which are close to 1.42 Å known for a flat graphene. However, after hydrogenation, the bonds adjacent to addition sites (i.e. C1 and C2) are elongated to ~1.5 Å; the next adjacent bonds are also adjusted to accommodate the structural changes (Fig. 5B). To check how the additions relieve global strain energy, we further calculated $\theta_{\text{p}}$ for the $\text{sp}^{2}$-carbons in the hydride. Fig. 4 plots the result: after hydrogenation, $\theta_{\text{p}}$ of the carbon atoms around the crest and trough are obviously reduced. This suggests that the hydrogenations also partially release strain for carbons in the vicinities of the addition sites. Therefore, the relief of global strain around the addition site serves as the secondary driving force for the hydrogenation.

## 4. Conclusions
1D periodic ripples in graphene adapt a shape close to sinusoidal functions. In contrast to 2D ripples, no band gap opening occurs, due to the persistence of one "flat" dimension perpendicular to the ripple wavelength. In such a rippled graphene sheet, about half of the carbons located around the crest and trough have their reactivity enhanced, whereas the remaining carbons have comparable reactivity to those in a flat graphene. Crest and trough are the most reactive positions of the rippled graphene sheet, whose reactivity linearly increases with $A / \lambda$. The relief of local atomic strain pertaining to addition sites and the global strains pertaining to carbons in the vicinities of the addition sites serve as the main driving force for the enhanced reactivity of rippled graphene sheets, leading to stripe-like ordered functionalization patterns in contrast to 2D ripples.

Fluorine molecules $(F_{2})$ are too reactive to allow regio selectivity control by ripples in graphene. Although the reactions of $H_{2}$ with graphene are thermodynamically unfavorable in the case of small- $A / \lambda$ ripples, spontaneous hydrogenations will take place when the value of $A / \lambda$ reaches roughly 0.55. Therefore, the regioselectivity of hydrogenation reactions can be well tuned by a strongly rippled graphene. The regioselectivity control to open band gaps along specific lines of 1D ripples opens new possibi- lities for the application of partially hydrogenated graphene for molecular electronics and photovoltaics.
