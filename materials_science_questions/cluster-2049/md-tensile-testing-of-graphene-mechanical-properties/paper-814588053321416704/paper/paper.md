# Atomic simulation of wrinkling and deformation in curved graphene nanoribbons under boundary confinement

Yu-Cheng Fan, Te-Hua Fang*, Chao-Chun Huang, Tao-Hsing Chen

Department of Mechanical Engineering, National Kaohsiung University of Applied Sciences, Kaohsiung, 807, Taiwan

*Corresponding author; E-mail: fang.tehua@msa.hinet.net ;Tel.: +886-7-381 4526 ext.5336

**Abstract**

Molecular dynamics simulation is used to investigate the properties of the curved and planar monolayer graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) under boundary confinement. The wrinkles are formed in curved-armchair GNRs not in curved-zigzag GNRs. With temperatures of 300-900 K and curvature radii of 3-6 nm, the wavelength of wrinkles did not change obviously. In nanoindentation tests, the zigzag graphene comparing to the armchair graphene had the larger contact stiffness about 50%. Further the concave graphene comparing to the convex graphene showed the higher contact stiffness due to its higher Young's modulus.

Key words: Molecular dynamics, Graphene, Nanoindentation, Wrinkles

### 1. Introduction

Graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) are an important two-dimensional material with a number of superior mechanical properties due to their unique $\text{sp}^2$-hybridized structures [1, 2]. These properties include high strength, high toughness, and excellent plasticity [3-5]. Owing to its remarkable physical, chemical, and optical properties, graphene has many applications in electronics, optics, chemistry, and nanodevices [6, 7]. The properties of graphene are obviously affected by the edge state [8].

Meyer *et al.* [9] discovered the existence of intrinsic wrinkles in suspended graphene, which are due to graphene's relatively low bending rigidity. In thin sheets and membranes, wrinkles or ripples are a general physical phenomenon under boundary confinement [10]. These wrinkles can be controllably produced by thermal manipulation [11]. Nearly periodic wrinkles have been observed [12]. Wrinkles are expected to strongly affect mechanical and electronic properties [13, 14].

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is commonly used for studying the mechanical and physical properties of nanomaterials. Atomic simulation, which avoids experimental noise, can be used to analyze interatomic interactions and mechanical properties. MD simulation has been applied to the study of nanoindentation [15], nanolithography [16], nanofriction [17], atomic deposition [18], and gas diffusivity [19].

The present work investigates the deformation properties of wrinkles in curved and planar GNRs with zigzag and armchair edges. To examine the effect of wrinkles on contact stiffness, MD was used to simulate the nanoindentation process in convex and concave GNRs, and planar GNRs with zigzag and armchair edges. The temperature, indentation velocity, and curvature radius were varied to determine their effects on wrinkles.

## 2. Simulation model

Physical models were created for convex, concave, and planar monolayer graphene samples and a closed-edge single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) indenter, as shown in Fig. 1. Zigzag and armchair orientations were used. From a three-dimensional (3D) view, the curved GNR sample has a semicircular surface with a hexagonal lattice. The width of the GNRs along the $y$-direction was 10 nm. The GNRs consisted of thermostat atoms and boundary atoms (fixed atoms). The thermostat atoms obeyed Newton's second law of motion and their velocities were adjusted to maintain them in an isothermal state at a specific temperature. The boundary atoms were fixed in space to prevent the substrate from being moved. The SWCNT (10, 10) indenter was a perfect closed-edge probe that consisted of 1769 carbon atoms. The radius of the indenter was 0.67 nm. The indenter was assumed to

be a rigid body during the simulation to simplify the indentation problem and focus on the nanomechanical properties and shape deformation of GNRs.

The interaction of graphene atoms was based on the Tersoff many-body potential function [20] to describe the bond bending, stretching, and torsion terms. The Lennard-Jones potential function [21] was adopted to describe the interaction between a GNR and the indenter. A time step of 1 fs was used for the implicit time integration of motion with the predictor-corrector algorithm [22]. The constant-temperature, constant-volume (NTV) ensemble was assumed for the GNRs. The GNR size, temperature, and nanoindentation velocity were varied to examine the physical properties.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1 Properties of wrinkles

The properties of wrinkles in curved armchair and zigzag GNRs were first investigated. Figure 2 shows the top view of GNRs in the $z$-direction. The dark color represents particles whose position exceeds the GNR radius. The temperature was set as 300 K. Obvious wrinkles appeared for the armchair GNRs. Increasing the radius did not affect the wavelength of the wrinkles, which was 1.0-1.5 nm in our simulation. Bending thin graphene sheets yields wrinklons, which assemble to mimic the behavior

of a complete hierarchy. A single wrinklon corresponds to the localized transition zone needed for merging two wrinkles of wavelength $2\lambda$ into a larger one [10].

Figure 3 shows the top view of armchair GNRs in the $z$-direction at temperatures of 300-900 K. The thermal effect did not greatly affect the wrinkling behavior under boundary confinement. It is because the wrinkling configuration at a non-zero temperature is the result of the interaction of boundary condition and thermal effect of graphene. Recently the researches [23,24] showed the wrinkling wavelength changed little at different temperature ranging from 0.01 to 500 K under shear strain tests. Wang *et al.*[24] showed the wrinkling wavelength decreased from 1.542 nm at 300 K to 1.517 nm at 500 K. Huang *et al.*[23] showed the wrinkling wavelength decreased from 2.65 nm at 300 K to 2.45 nm at 500 K. Even at 0 K under the shear strain the wrinkles still exited [25]. However in the free standing graphene the wrinkling wavelength changes obviously when the system temperature is close to the 0 K. For example, Ebrahimi [26] showed the wrinkles vanished as the temperature equal to 0 K. Further wrinkles are associated with flexural phonons [13] and have a quadratic dependence on the wave vector [27]. A previous study [28] found that wrinkles effectively soften graphene's in-plane stiffness.

### 3.2 Indentation deformation

Figure 4 shows the amplitude of the slip vector at an indentation depth of 1.0 nm with a reference depth of 0.5 nm. Here, the indenter was subjected to an external loading rate of 10 m/s at a temperature of 300 K and the radius of the curved GNRs was 5.0 nm.

For the convex GNRs, the slip amplitude was highest in the $y$-direction, whereas for the concave GNRs, it was highest in the $x$-direction. This is due to the deformation in convex GNRs being compressive, and that in concave GNRs being tensile. For the planar GNRs, the slip amplitude was isotropic.

### 3.3 Contact stiffness
Figures 5(a) and (b) show the contact stiffness and work of deformation, respectively. The radii of curved-armchair and -zigzag GNRs were set as 3, 4, 5, and 6 nm with a 10 m/s indentation velocity at 300 K. The concave-zigzag GNRs showed the highest contact stiffness (189±24 N/m). The convex-armchair GNRs showed the lowest contact stiffness (42±10 N/m). When the radius was increased, the contact stiffness of the concave graphene decreased. For curved-armchair graphene, the relation between the contact stiffness and radius was not obvious. The contact stiffness of concave GNRs was larger than that of convex GNRs.

The curved-zigzag GNRs had higher contact stiffness and work of deformation

than those of the curved-armchair GNRs. The wrinkles decreased the contact stiffness.

For planar GNRs, Lee [3] found a mean elastic stiffness of 342 N/m, an average breaking strength of 55 N/m, and an intrinsic strength of $42\pm4$ N/m based on the continuum model.

Figures 6(a) and (b) show the contact stiffness and work of deformation, respectively, of GNRs at temperatures of 300, 500, 700, and 900 K. The radius is 5 nm and the indentation velocity is10 m/s. The contact stiffness of curved graphene decreased with increasing temperature. Further the contact stiffness of concave graphene was larger than one of convex graphene. A higher temperature increased the thermal fluctuations and the degree of fluctuation of wrinkles. For the curved GNRs, the contact stiffness at 300 K decreased about 0.8 fold compared to that at 900 K. For the planar armchair GNRs, there was no obvious relation.

The effect of indentation velocity was studied with indentation velocities of 2, 5, 10, and 20 m/s. The radius of GNRs was 5 nm and the temperature was 300 K. Figures 7(a) and (b) show that the contact stiffness and work of deformation, respectively, increased with indentation velocity. The concave-zigzag GNRs had the largest contact stiffness. The contact stiffness of curved-armchair GNRs was lower than that of curved-zigzag GNRs. Concave graphene had a higher contact stiffness than that of convex graphene. It is because the wrinkles reduce contact stiffness.

### 4. Conclusion

With MD simulation and nanoindentation tests, we found that the concave GNRs have the higher contact stiffness due to graphene's high Young's modulus. Besides the wrinkles were formed in curved-armchair GNRs not in curved-zigzag GNRs, and the more wrinkles resulted into the lower contact stiffness. With varying temperatures, radii and indentation velocities, the zigzag graphene comparing to the armchair graphene had the larger contact stiffness about 50%. So the concave-zigzag graphene supports the higher contact stiffness and is suitable for potential applications in nanosystem that required contact stiffness.
