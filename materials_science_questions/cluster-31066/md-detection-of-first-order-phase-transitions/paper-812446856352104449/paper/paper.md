# THE LIQUID-LIQUID PHASE TRANSITION

A.C. MITUS $^{1}$, A.Z. PATASHINSKII and B.I. SHUMILO

Institute of Nuclear Physics, 630090 Novosibirsk, USSR

Received 23 August 1985; accepted for publication 24 September 1985

It is shown that a change in the local structure of a liquid may lead to a first-order liquid-liquid phase transition. A simple phenomenological model describing this phenomenon is proposed.

1. The experimental data and the results of com- puter simulations confirm indirectly the existence of local crystal ordering in liquids [1]. Nevertheless, a direct observation of the local structure of the matter is difficult and, to the best of our knowledge, has not been performed yet. Important evidence in favour of the existence of a local structure in the liquid phase might be the revealing of the liquid-liquid phase tran- sition (PT) - an abrupt change of the local structure of the matter.

The theory of the liquid state based on the hy- pothesis of local crystal ordering of the matter has recently been proposed by us (A.P. and B.Sh.) [2]. According to it, the configurations of atoms, statis- tically probable in the liquid, are of the following type: the greater part of the atoms belongs to a mul- tiply connected region of the "perfect matter", and the rest of the atoms belongs to pointlike and linear defects which are the complement of the "perfect matter" region with respect to the whole space. The local structure of the "perfect matter" is supposed to be similar to that of the crystal. More precisely, a local mapping of atoms onto the sites of some ideal lattice is assumed to be possible. The mapping preserves the neighbourhood relations, i.e. the lattice images of the nearest neighbours of an atom are the nearest neighbours of the image of this atom. The space orientation of this tangent lattice and its posi- tion as a whole is determined with the help of a suitable minimization (over an element of the mat- ter under consideration) of displacements of atoms with respect to the corresponding lattice sites. Every configuration of atoms in the condensed state can be represented as some realization of the fields of ro- tations of the tangent lattice and of its relatively small deformations. The type of the lattice and its param- eters are fixed by the minimization of, say, the elas- tic energy integrated over the whole body. In contrast to the crystal, in the liquid relative rotation of the tangent lattices of distant elements can be arbitrary because of the existence of a finite density of linear defects. These linear defects are locally equivalent to dislocations. The Burgers vector of such a defect is not constant along its line but rotates together with the tangent lattice. The grain-boundary like structures are of small probability in the liquid phase. A section of the liquid by an arbitrary plain is a surface struc- turally "perfect" everywhere except for isolated patches of intersections with the lines of defects. The described configurations of atoms are of dominant statistical weight in the liquid. (For a detailed discus- sion of the point see ref. [2].)

Very often the structure of a particular substance is determined by the number of competing interac- tions. This may lead to transformations of the local structure - the polymorphic phase transitions (PPT). The phenomenon is well known for the crystal. Since the long-range ordering is not of decisive importance one can imagine a similar phase transition to take place in the locally ordered liquid. Let us call it the PPT of the tangent lattice. 2. In order to describe the PPT one has to study fluctuations of the crystal order parameters - the fields characterizing the local structure. As was pro- posed in refs. [3,4], such is the tensor field $T_{i j k l}(r)$ (the hexagonal local structure is described by a tensor field of the sixth rank). This field is one of the relevant variables in the formalism of condensed matter theory [2]. The irreducible tensor $T_{i j k l}(r)$ , invariant under the local point-symmetry group transformations, de- termines at each point $r$ the orientation of the local anisotropy axes of the matter with respect to a cer- tain fixed coordinate system. The scalar field $p^{(\lambda)}(r)$ , the invariants of the field $T_{i j k l}(r)$ characterize the type of the tangent lattice (for more details, see ref. [6]). In terms of these parameters the melting PT is the loss of the long-range orientational order of the field $T_{i j k l}(r)$ while the PPT is the discontinuous change of the parameters $\langle p^{(\lambda)}\rangle$ averaged over the system. In what follows we shall generalize the model suggested in refs. [4,5] in order to take into account the possi- bility of a polymorphic transition of the matter.

Let us introduce two tensor fields $T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r),(\alpha=1,2)$ and let the local crystal order be characterized by
$$T_{i j k l}(r)=T_{i j k l}^{(1)}(r)+T_{i j k l}^{(2)}(r). \quad(1)$$

The components of the tensor fields $T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r)$ are sup posed to take the following values
$$T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r)=\left(\hat{g}^{(\alpha)}(r) T_{0}\right)_{i j k l}.$$

Here $T_{0}$ is a fixed tensor (indices are omitted) invari ant with respect to transformations of, say, the point group of the cube; the operator $\hat{g}(r)$ determines its transformation due to some $O(3)$ group rotation. Thus, at every point the tensors $T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r)$ are free to "rotate" independently from each other, while their invariants are fixed. Obviously, the total crystal order parameter (1) is characterized, above all, by the relative rotation of the sublattices:
$$\hat{g}(r)=\hat{g}_{(1)}(r) g_{(2)}^{-1}(r),$$
which depends on eulerian angles, i.e. on the three scalar parameters $p^{(\lambda)}(r)$ . Let one of them be:
$$p^{(1)}(r)=T_{i j k l}^{(1)}(r) T_{i j k l}^{(2)}(r). \quad(2)$$

The local symmetry of the system is that of the cube only if the operator $\hat{g}(r)$ is trivial. The jump of $\langle p^{(\lambda)}\rangle$ indicates the change of the relative orientation of the sublattices, i.e., in general, the change of the point-symmetry group.

Consider the following effective hamiltonian:
$$\begin{aligned}
& -H\left\{T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r)\right\} \\
& \quad=\int \mathrm{d} V \mathrm{~d} V^{\prime} T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r) \Lambda^{\alpha \beta}\left(r-r^{\prime}\right) T_{i j k l}^{(\beta)}(r) \\
& \quad+U\left\{p^{(\lambda)}(r)\right\}. \quad(3)
\end{aligned}$$

The first term is the hamiltonian of the phenomeno- logical theory of melting [4,5] generalized to the case of two local sublattices. The interaction of the fields $T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r)$ is assumed to be isotropic; its anisotropicpart can be treated perturbatively. The matrix $\Lambda^{\alpha \beta}(r)$ reads as follows:
$$\Lambda^{\alpha \beta}(r)=\left(\begin{array}{ll}
I(r) & \gamma(r) \\
\gamma(r) & I(r)
\end{array}\right). \quad(4)$$

Let the interactions be of finite radius.

The second term in the hamiltonian (3) plays a central role in the description of the PPT of the matter. It should satisfy the following two conditions: to have, as a function of $\langle p^{(\lambda)}\rangle$ , two or more distinct minima corresponding to different phases and to give large values of the energy for the heterostructural configu- rations (i.e. a mixture of the domains with different local structures). The simple functional satisfying these requirements is
$$U\left\{p^{(\lambda)}(r)\right\}=\int \mathrm{d} V\left[\mu\left(\nabla p^{(\lambda)}\right)^{2}+u\left(p^{(\lambda)}\right)\right], \quad(5)$$
where $\mu>0$ . The first term in (5) gives rise to a sur face tension of the heterostructural interfaces. The coefficients of the polynomial $u(p^{(\lambda)})$ depend on the thermodynamic variables such as the pressure, etc. At a particular value of, say, the pressure, two of the minima are equally deep and the system exhibits the PPT.

The analysis of the model in the mean-field approx-

imation (MFA) is straightforward. However, computa- tions are inevitable in this case. We prefer to present here the analytical results obtained for a somewhat modified model. Let us choose a subgroup of the O(3) group, i.e. replace the variety of all possible rota- tions by a number of discrete rotations. The most triv- ial example of such subgroup is the point-group of the tetrahedron. Then, each of the tensors $T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}(r)$ can have four different "orientations" and the parameter $p^{(1)}(r)(2)$ takes only two possible values. In this case one can assume the second term of the hamiltonian(3) to depend only on one of the three scalar param- eters $p^{(\lambda)}(r)$ . We choose it to be that determined by formula (2). The second term of the hamiltonian thenhas the form:

$$U\{p(r)\}=\alpha \sum_{r} p(r)+\sum_{r, r^{\prime}} \mu\left(r-r^{\prime}\right) p(r) p\left(r^{\prime}\right). \quad (6)$$

The continuous space is replaced by a lattice.

Two local symmetries are possible in the model: the first one is that when the average orientations of the sublattices at a given site are the same (p-phase) and the second one when they are different (o-phase).

The MFA-equations are

$$\left\langle T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}\right\rangle=Z^{-1} \sum_{\text {conf }} T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)} \exp \left(-\beta H_{\mathrm{MFA}}\right),$$

$$\langle p\rangle=Z^{-1} \sum_{\text {conf }} p \exp \left(-\beta H_{\mathrm{MFA}}\right),\qquad(7)$$

where $\beta$ denotes the inverse temperature and

$$Z=\sum_{\text {conf }} \exp \left\{-\beta H_{\mathrm{MFA}}\right\},$$

$$H_{\mathrm{MFA}}=n \Lambda^{\alpha \beta}\left\langle T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}\right\rangle T_{i j k l}^{(\beta)}+(\alpha+n \mu\langle p\rangle) p, \quad (8)$$

$n$ is the number of nearest neighbours in the lattice approximation (6). The summation is carried out over all possible orientations of the tensor parameters $T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}$ . In fact, they are two interacting Potts variables. The self-consistent solution of eqs. (7) delivers the mini- mum to the MFA thermodynamic potential $F(\beta$ , $\langle T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}\rangle,\langle p\rangle)$ .

3. Let us consider the case $\mu \gg I, \mu \gg \gamma$ . The melt ing temperature is $T_{c} \sim max (I, \gamma)$ and at temperatures of the order of $T_{c}$ the second term of the hamiltonian is dominant. Eqs. (7) can be treated perturbatively with respect to the parameters $I / \mu$ and $\gamma / \mu$ . In the liq uid state $\langle T_{i j k l}^{(\alpha)}\rangle=0$ and the last of eqs. (7) reduces to the form

$$c\langle p\rangle=[\exp (V)-1] /[\exp (V)+3] ; \quad (9)$$

where $V=A \beta(\alpha+n \mu\langle p\rangle). A$ and $c$ are numerical con stants. The phase diagram of the liquid is shown in fig. 1. The line of the first-order PT (solid line in fig.1) is determined by

$$\frac{\alpha}{n \mu}=-14(4 / 27)^{2}+\frac{27 \ln 3}{16 n \mu \rho}, \quad (10)$$

where $\rho=\frac{56}{27} \beta$ . The dashed lines are spinodals of p and o-phases, respectively. For $-\frac{224}{729}<\alpha / n \mu ≤0$ , the first-order PPT is the transition between the low-tem- perature p-phase and the high-temperature o-phase of the liquid. Under the transition the local symmetry changes. For positive values of the ratio $\alpha / n \mu<$ (a/nu)。, the PT is rather isomorphic than polymorphic because the local structure of both phases is of the p-type. The line of the first-order phase transition ofthe liquid terminates at the critical point $(\alpha / n \mu)_{cr}$ ≈3 X 10-2, (pnu)cr=1.185. In the vicinity of the critical point the MFA is inapplicable. The critical mode in this case is scalar (a combination of the fields p(A)(r)) and the universal theory of critical fluctua- tions is valid [7].

$T$ and pressure $P$. Thus the phase diagram of the system can be mapped onto the $P-T$ plane. A typical resulting $P-T$ diagram is depicted in fig. 2. The p-crystal - liquid PTs (BF-line) are of first order. The melting of the o-crystal may be of first order or be a continuous PT, depending on the parameters of the hamiltonian. In the last case a tricritical point exists on the segment BE of the PT line. The continuous PT is most likely to be the artifact of the MFA. The lines AB and CD are the lines of the PPT; D is the critical point. A description of the gaseous phase is beyond the framework of the model.

4. The PPT in the liquid may exist when the local structures of phases differ strongly and cannot be transformed one into another by a small deformation. The surface tension of the interface is large and the metastable p-liquid can exist in the region of stability of the o-liquid. Such a state can be obtained under the transition across the BC line.

There may exist other types of the $P-T$ phase diagram besides the one given in fig. 2. For example, the triple point C may be at a lower pressure compared to the triple point B; there may be no PPT in the crystal. Finally, the line of the PPT in the liquid is possible to exist only for the values of $P$, $T$ corresponding to the metastable liquid, and the PPT in the stable liquid is absent.

Note that the term "the line of PT" is not quite well defined for metastable (nonequilibrium) states of the overcooled liquid or the glass. The structure of these states results from the relaxation of small-scale degrees of freedom of the liquid towards "the better local crystallinity", i.e. the structure of the glass is correlated with that of the initial liquid. Hence, one can expect the glasses of the same chemical composition to have different local structures. Each of the phases of the glass can be obtained (with prevailing probability) in its own characteristical region of temperatures and pressures.

The existence of different phases of liquid and the liquid-liquid PT might be found by the experimental observation of anomalies of such physical characteristics as density, heat capacity, viscosity, etc. The polymorphism of the liquid might be confirmed by revealing the relaxation of a structurally metastable liquid phase or by finding the "phases" of the glass.

These phenomena were probably observed [8] but were not interpreted properly.
