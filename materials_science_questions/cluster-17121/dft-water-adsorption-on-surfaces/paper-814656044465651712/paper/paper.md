## ABSTRACT

In this work, we focus on the atomic structure of the water interlayer of Na-exchanged montmorillonite. For two different surface charge densities, namely $-0.086$ and $-0.172\ C/m^{2}$, the adsorption process in the presence of water is described by first principles calculations. We describe the interactions and forces for every water molecule entering the interlayer during the swelling process. In particular, the dielectric permittivity of the water interlayer is calculated. Finally, we confirm our results performing ab initio thermodynamics calculations leading to a wide range of realistic experimental scenarios.

## 1. Introduction

Clay minerals were the first industrial minerals to be used and have developed into a multi-million tons business today [1].

Large-scale/high-quantity applications of swellable clay minerals that are controlled by hydration comprise (geo-)technical applications (e.g. binders for molding sands [2], hydraulic sealing systems in underground depositories of radioactive waste [3,4]), geological and petrologic processes (e.g. formation and production of crude oil [5], borehole (in-)stabilities) to their role in the Earth's climate [6] and the moisture balance of soils, e.g. for agriculture [7]. In small-scale applications, swellable clay minerals are important as compounds of nanocomposites as well as catalysts or templates for synthesis of inorganic, organic [8] and even prebiotic biomolecules like RNA in terrestrial and extraterrestrial environment [9], where mainly dehydration is the decisive process.

Here, we describe one main component of clays, Na-exchanged montmorillonite. Na-exchanged montmorillonite is formed as a 2:1 layer. The outer sheets consist of tetrahedral coordinated Si and the inner sheet of octahedral coordinated Al. Thereby Al is occupying 2/3 of the octahedral positions resulting in either cis-vacant or trans-vacant arrangements [10]. The dioctahedral montmorillonite layers are negatively charged due to exchange of octahedral Al by Mg, thus requiring counter ions such as Na to ensure electroneutrality.

Hydration and swelling is the most important reaction of montmorillonites. Understanding of the process at the atomic scale has a high impact on a great diversity of applications and thus for a broad range of communities from chemists and material scientists to geoscientists and engineers.

While understanding of the hydration process itself and its influence on further reactions of smectites is the primary challenge the prediction of resulting water properties, e.g. permittivity is desirable for parameterization of broadband mixing rules in moisture sensing at different scales [11] not only for monitoring but also controlling reactions of smectites.

Due to several geological formation pathways under partly not yet reconstructed conditions not only the multiphase composition of clays (e.g. bentonites) and soils (e.g. vertisols) but also the structure and size of the swellable clay minerals varies over different spatial scales. Therefore, smectites are recognized as the most heterogeneous class of minerals with a pronounced variety in reactivity. Our previous study revealed 96 possible structures in the montmorillonite-beidellite series of dioctahedral smectites [12], where several structures can occur simultaneously in a natural sample. In order to better understand reactivity (e.g. the hydration) of smectites we need to understand the structure-functionality-relation that can be derived only from theoretical analysis of well-defined structures. Screening of natural samples with respect to the targeted functionality like hydration is important but not satisfying due to the large number of different structures and the interference of polydisperse structural and geometrical parameters (e.g. [13,14]). Recent studies on interlayer swelling of smectites are performed with synthetic trioctahedral smectites carrying exclusively tetrahedral charges [15]. Results cannot be easily transferred to dioctahedral smectites without tetrahedral charge as with increasing tetrahedral charge the interaction between the water molecules and the surface oxygen of the 2:1 layers increases [16].

Many researchers have studied the interactions of water with Na-exchanged and other homoionic saturated smectites and a detailed picture of interactions and driving forces has been developed by means of empirical force field methods [15,17-23]. Nevertheless, there is a lack in the understanding of the atomic structure of the interlayer water and its electronic structure in dioctahedral smectites in dependence of structural diversity of the 2:1 layers.

The aim of the present work is to investigate the Na-exchanged montmorillonite/water interface within the density functional theory (DFT). A more detailed insight is given in Section 4.2 "Forces and interactions at the Na-exchanged montmorillonite/water interface" and we try to confirm all our results finally in Section 4.3 "Thermodynamic confirmation of the model". ### 2. Methodology

The total-energy and ground-state structure calculations in the present work were performed using DFT as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation program (VASP) [24]. The electron-ion interaction is treated within the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [25]. The valence electron wave functions are expanded into plane waves up to a kinetic energy cutoff of 360 eV. This energy cutoff was found to yield converged structural parameters for bulk montmorillonite. The Brillouin zone sampling was performed with a $1 \times 1 \times 1$ mesh of Monkhorst-Pack k-points, respectively [26]. The electron-electron exchange and correlation (XC) energy is approximated within the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA), using the XC potential developed by Perdew et al. [27]. The PW91 functional was found to describe reliably the structure and energetics, especially of hydrogen bonded water molecules [28-30].

The optimization of the atomic coordinates and unit cell size/shape for the bulk materials was performed via a conjugate gradients technique which utilizes the total energy and the Hellmann Feynman forces on the atoms and stresses on the unit cell. The Na-exchanged montmorillonite is modeled employing three-dimensional periodic boundary conditions (PBC) [31]. In addition to the k-point density and $E_{\text {cut }}$ discussed above, the convergence in calculations of clay minerals is also dependent on the thickness of the mineral layer, $d_{\text {layer }}$, and the thickness of the water films, defined as $d_{\text {layer }}^{\mathrm{n} * \mathrm{H} 2 \mathrm{O}}-d_{\text {layer }}^{0}$, between the layers. For every atomic configuration we checked convergence by running a series of calculations with different layer thicknesses. The thermodynamic minimum was then constructed by solving the Birch-Murnaghan equation of state.

### 3. Models

Many workers have studied the effect of cations on the water adsorption and interlayer expansion of Na-exchanged montmorillonite and despite small differences their general conclusions are in accord. In order to describe the influence of Na-ions inside the water layer of Na-exchanged montmorillonite, we prepare two similar $2 \times 1$ supercells that differ from each other concerning the chemical potential of Na. Thereby the supercell corresponds to two crystallographic unit cells of montmorillonite to obtain integer mol ratios of atoms as basis for calculations. The stoichiometric description of these supercells is given by $\left[\mathrm{Na}_{1}\left(\mathrm{Si}_{16}\right)\left(\mathrm{Al}_{7} \mathrm{Mg}_{1}\right) \mathrm{O}_{40}(\mathrm{OH})_{8}\right]$ for a low chemical potential of $\mathrm{Na}$ and $\left[\mathrm{Na}_{2}\left(\mathrm{Si}_{16}\right)\left(\mathrm{Al}_{6} \mathrm{Mg}_{2}\right) \mathrm{O}_{40}(\mathrm{OH})_{8}\right]$ for a high chemical potential of Na. For both structures trans-vacant octahedral sheets with a symmetry of $\mathrm{C} 2 / \mathrm{m}$ was chosen [32].

Starting from the lowest energy configuration of a water monomer in the supercell, the water coverage was systematically increased to 14 water molecules per supercell.

We validate our model choice by the calculation of the surface charge density

$$
\sigma=\frac{Q_{\text {layer }}}{2 * a_{0} * b_{0}} \tag{1}
$$

where $Q_{\text {layer }}$ is the amount of electric charge that is present on the basal surfaces of one crystallographic unit cell with a given area $A=2 * a_{0} * b_{0}$. The lattice constants $a_{0}$ and $b_{0}$ of the dioctahedral smectites were determined to be $5.18 \AA$ and $8.98 \AA$. Consequently, our model systems have surface charge densities of $-0.086 \mathrm{C} / \mathrm{m}^{2}$ for a low chemical potential of $\mathrm{Na}$ and $-0.172 \mathrm{C} / \mathrm{m}^{2}$ for a high chemical potential of Na. The layer charge of natural montmorillonites varies between 0.21 and 0.39 per formula unit or 0.42 and 0.78 per unit cell and can reach up to 0.6 per f.u. and 1.2 per unit cell [33]. These layer charges result according to Eq. (1) in surface charge densities of $-0.072 \mathrm{C} / \mathrm{m}^{2}$ to $-0.134 \mathrm{C} / \mathrm{m}^{2}$ and $-0.206 \mathrm{C} / \mathrm{m}^{2}$, respectively.

### 4. Results and discussion

#### 4.1. Microscopic structure of the water interlayer

We now start from the water-free supercells and systematically increase the number of water molecules. The first water to enter the interlayer positions is the result of hydration of the Na-ion. Fig. 1A and B show how the formation of the Na-ion hydration shell

coincides with the surface/water interaction via hydrogen bonds. In particular, there is no water-water interaction at this stage of hydration. When more water molecules enter the supercell and the hydration shell of the Na-ion is saturated, also water-water interactions start to form. Lowest energy structures obtained from various starting geometries for the respective water coverage in the supercell corresponding to a low chemical potential of Na are shown in Fig. 1. The water forms discrete layers which increase in number. Fig. 1A and B depict a so called 1 W layer containing 3 water molecules per supercell and Na-ion hydration shell. Fig. 1C and D depicts a so called 2 W layer containing 11 water molecules per supercell and 6 water molecules per Na-ion hydration shell. The water molecules have formed an octahedral complex surrounding the Na-ion. The interaction of this octahedral complex with its environment is optimized in a way that two triangular sides of the complex are turned against the layer surfaces.

Lowest energy structures obtained from various starting geometries for the respective 1 W and 2 W water coverage in the supercell corresponding to a high chemical potential of Na are shown in Fig. 2A and B. Fig. 2C and D depict a supercell containing 14 water molecules and 6 water molecules per Na-ion hydration shell. The water still form an octahedral complex surrounding the Na-ion. Now the interaction of both octahedral complexes has changed. They are standing symmetrically on top, with a square-planar ring of four water molecules surrounding them. This might be discussed as 3 W layer model. Finally, a distribution balance of different types of geometries, as already suggested in the literature, seems to be a very plausible model [34].

### 4.2. Forces and interactions at the Na-exchanged montmorillonite/water interface

We now turn to discuss the main forces and interactions at the Na-exchanged montmorillonite/water interface which we consider here to be (1) the hydration energy of Na-ions, (2) water-water attraction, (3) water-surface attraction and (4) attraction of the electric double layer. We consider the adsorption energy ($E_{\text{ads}}$) as a function of the number of water molecules ($n$) adsorbed in the corresponding supercell as the sum of hydration energy of Na-ions ($E_{\text{hyd}}$), water-water attraction ($E_{\text{coh}}$) and water-surface attraction ($E_{\text{adh}}$)

$$
E_{\text{ads}} = \frac{E_n - E_0 - n * E_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{n} = E_{\text{hyd}} + E_{\text{coh}} + E_{\text{adh}} \tag{2}
$$

where $E_0$ and $E_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$ correspond to the energy of the clean surface and one gas phase water molecule. $E_{\text{adh}}$ and $E_{\text{coh}}$ were calculated for every configuration, following an universal relationship between the H-bond length and strength from Jones et al. [35].

The 2:1 layers form a lamellar structure making Na-exchanged montmorillonite a seemingly perfect model system for an electrical double layer where the swelling and stability in saline solution strongly depend on counter ion valence and surface charge density. The attraction of the electric double layer ($E_{\text{EDL}}$) as a function of the number of water molecules, $n$, adsorbed in the corresponding unit cell is calculated by

$$
E_{\text{EDL}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{\text{layer}}} * \frac{q_{\text{counter-ion}} * Q_{\text{layer}}}{d_{\text{layer}}} \tag{3}
$$

where $\varepsilon_{\text{layer}}$ is considered as the dielectric function of the water layer. The exact charge of the counter ion ($q_{\text{counter-ion}}$) was calculated with help of Bader charge analysis [36].

The resulting energies are depicted in Fig. 3A corresponding to a low chemical potential of Na and Fig. 3B corresponding to a high chemical potential of Na. The total energy difference of $E_{\text{EDL}} - E_{\text{ads}}$ allows to predict the entrance of further water into the clay mineral, since this energy difference is the driving force of the swelling of clay minerals. The two chosen systems of Na-exchanged montmorillonite are completely swellable, meaning that we consider the

### 4.3. Thermodynamic confirmation of the models

In the following part of the work, we want to validate our hypothesis of discrete water layers in the Na-exchanged montmorillonite. With the help of ab initio thermodynamics we will be able to correlate the energy states to temperature and partial pressure of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. While aforementioned plane wave studies could already underline the importance of different scenarios of discrete water layer in the montmorillonite interlayer [37], the following discussions will be the first which makes them applicable for a wide range of realistic conditions, e.g. realistic environmental situations of varying temperatures and pressures.

The sign of energy alone does not allow concluding on the stability of a specific structure. Rather, one has to take into account the chemical potentials $\mu(A_i)$ of the constituents $A_i$ in order to compare interfaces with different stoichiometries energetically [38,39]. Here, the thermodynamic ground state of the interface is determined by the minimum of the grandcanonical potential.

$$
\Omega=F-\sum_{i} \mu\left(A_{i}\right) * n_{A_{i}} \tag{5}
$$

where $F=E-TS$ is the interface free energy which we approximate by the interface total energy $E$ at zero temperature assuming similar entropy contributions $S$ for different adsorption configurations. In fact, the differences in vibrational free energy and electronic entropy are typically several orders of magnitude smaller than the adsorption energies calculated here [40].

If we now assume a Na-exchanged montmorillonite interface in the presence of water, the grandcanonical potential will only depend on the number and chemical potential of the water molecules. ### 4.4. Comparison with experimental results and limits

Early studies of Bockriss et al. reported permittivities of bound water in the diffuse double layer close to charged surfaces of $\varepsilon_{W1}=6$ and $\varepsilon_{W2}=32$, and for the remaining water $\varepsilon_{ew}=80$ corresponding to the permittivity of free water. Sun et al. reported permittivities of bound water in clay minerals of $\varepsilon_{W1}=3.2$ for the first water layer, $\varepsilon_{W2}=7.2$ for the second water layer and $\varepsilon_{W3}=16$ for the third, based on an exponential increase of permittivity with distance of water layer to the surface, reaching the permittivity of free water with the fifth monolayer of water [45,46]. Both models do not fit with experimental data and calculated permittivity of bound water in interlayers of smectites and thus cannot be applied for calibration of mixing rules for in-situ water content measurements of smectitic clays.

In the considerations above we had to accept limitations concerning comparison to experimental results. Modeling clay minerals using a plate capacitor model (capacity) requires two capacities to describe electric field configuration in a supercell. One plate of each capacity is located in a plane of the adjacent 2:1 layer, which can be regarded as a two dimensional anion, and two counter plates are situated in the plain of the counter ion (see Fig. 8).

![](./images/814656044465651712_11.jpg)

Fig. 8. Simplified charge distribution in a supercell and equivalent circuit of capacitors.

Because the counter ion is positively charged and 2:1 layer ions negatively, this can be interpreted in the simplified equivalent circuit as a parallel configuration of the two capacities $C_1$ and $C_2$ with $C_1 = C_2 = C$. The total capacity is then $C_{\text{total}} = 2 * C$. Each capacity $C$ carries half of the charge $Q_{\text{layer}}$ of the supercell and the spacing of one plate capacitors is $d_{\text{layer}}/2$. Therefore we have

$$
C = \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{layer}} * \varepsilon_0 * A}{0.5 * d_{\text{layer}}} \tag{6}
$$

where the layer permittivity $\varepsilon_{\text{layer}}$ is equivalent to Eq. (4). This model does not take into account real field configuration between charge centers of ion in 2:1 layer and counter ion, but the electric field is symmetrical with respect to counter ions. Additionally, the frequency dependency is neglected, meaning that modeling is static and no additional polarization effects are considered [44]. Neglecting all low frequency effects could lead to a layer permittivity comparable to results of laboratory results within frequencies around 1 GHz. Nevertheless, a fairly good agreement was found between calculated permittivity of bound water in smectites (see Fig. 5B) and experimental data. The bound water permittivity for a Na-rich montmorillonite with the structural formula $\text{Na}_{1.04}\ (\text{Si}_{15.88}\text{Al}_{0.12})(\text{Al}_{6.36}\text{Fe}_{0.72}\text{Mg}_{0.92})\ \text{O}_{40}\ (\text{OH})_8$ was determined to be $\varepsilon_{\text{W}1} \approx \varepsilon_{\text{W}2} \approx \varepsilon_{\text{W}3} = 22$ in the range of gravimetric water content between 6.4% and 28.9% related to the dry weight of the material. Data were obtained from fitting of measured bulk permittivity at 1 GHz by a semi-empirical mixing law. These water contents correlate to 1 W and 2 W hydration states of the material and the nearly linear parts of the curves in Fig. 5A and B. The $\varepsilon_{\text{W}3}$ refers to additional water molecules if hydration of the interlayer Na exceeded a 6-fold coordination in 2 W state.

Finally, we need to arrange our microscopic description of the Na-exchanged montmorillonite in order of a more macroscopic frame. The atomic supercell in this work does not describe the aspect ratio characteristic for natural montmorillonite layers and the edges of the system. As a consequence, our model has to be considered for crystalline interlayer swelling but not for inter-particle osmotic swelling. Furthermore, no dependence on the chemical potential of the protons (change of pH) is considered. This approximation is reasonable due to the fact that reactive groups depending on the pH-value are mainly located at the edges of the particles. Work is in progress to investigate the open points.

## 5. Conclusions

In this paper, we discuss the main forces influencing the microscopic structure of the water layer in Na-exchanged montmorillonite. The stoichiometric description of Na-exchanged montmorillonite is given by $[\text{Na}_1(\text{Si}_{16})(\text{Al}_7\text{Mg}_1)\text{O}_{40}(\text{OH})_8]$ corresponding to a low chemical potential of Na and $[\text{Na}_2(\text{Si}_{16})(\text{Al}_6\text{Mg}_2)-\text{O}_{40}(\text{OH})_8]$ corresponding to a high chemical potential of Na. The first water to enter the interlayer positions is the result of hydration of the ions with hydration energy being the dominating force in the system. The water forms discrete layers which increase in number from one to three (1–3 W). According to our calculations, 2 W water films are present at the surface corresponding to a high chemical potential of Na at ambient condition with a relative humidity of about 50% and 1 W water films are present at the surface corresponding to a low chemical potential of Na at ambient condition with a relative humidity of about 50%. In case the hydration shell of the Na is saturated, the water starts to reorient with its environment by forming hydrogen bridges. This leads to a reorientation of water in unit cells of lower chemical potential of Na, consequently with a lower relative dielectric permittivity of the water layer. In contrast, the relative dielectric permittivity of the water layer corresponding to a high chemical potential of Na approaches to the value of bulk water. We explain this by an additional disorder, which Na-ions bring into the water layer by forming the hydration shell. This additional disorder is different from the way, how the water molecules should form most adapted to the electric double layer in the Na-exchange montmorillonite. Thus, Na-ions can be understood as small point defects in the water layers. Because of the magnitude of the hydration energy, it is the most attractive interaction of water molecules.
