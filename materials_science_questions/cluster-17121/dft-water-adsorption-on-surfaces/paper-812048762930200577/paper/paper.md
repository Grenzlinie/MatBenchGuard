# First-principles study of oxygenated diamond (0 0 1) surfaces with and without hydrogen

## Abstract

The structural characterization of oxygenated diamond (0 0 1) surface with and without H-presence has been investigated by the density- functional theory within the generalized gradient approximation and the plane-wave pseudopotential method. It is shown that, without the H, the O- bridge site has lower energy than the O-on-top site for both half and fully monolayer (ML) oxygen coverage. For hydrogenated diamond (0 0 1) surfaces, we found for half ML O coverage, a more stable $(2×2):0.5H\\&0.5(OH)$ phase than the previously reported $(2×1):0.5H\\&0.5(OH)$ phase [H. Tamura, H. Zhou, K. Sugisako, Y. Yokoi, S. Takami, M. Kubo, K. Teraishi, A. Miyamoto, A. Imamura, M.N. Gamo, T. Ando, Phys. Rev. B 61 (2000) 11025]. Furthermore, at one ML O coverage, a $(2×2):(OH)$ phase is the most stable structure than the previously reported $(2×1):(OH)$ phase. With the increase of the (OH) coverage, the importance of the hydrogen bonds increases in terms of stabilizing these structures.

## 1. Introduction

Because of the unique combination of good electrical, optical, thermal, and chemical properties, diamond has generated great interests in numerous applications such as field effect transistors, high power electronics, high-tempera- ture semiconductor devices, heat sinks, Schottky diodes, as well as superhard coatings for cutting tools [1-4]. The recent development in the growth and doping of single-crystal diamond via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) has put this material under intensive studies [5]. It is well known that the growth of CVD diamond is generally in the hydrogen environment, where H is believed to play an important role in the maintenance of $sp^{3}$-bonded surfaces and the creation of surface radical sites [6]. It was reported that the addition of oxygen into CVD plasma might assist the diamond growth by improving the quality of the crystal [7-12]. In order to understand the role of the oxygen, chemical reactions of oxygen-containing species with diamond surfaces have been studied experimentally [13-16]. Ando et al. investigated the reactions with diamond powders using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and temperature programmed deposition (TPD) spectroscopies [14,15]. The chemisorbed species were examined as a function of the oxidation temperature, and various functional groups such as carbonyl (C=O), lactone [(C=O)O], carboxylic acid [(C=O)OH], cyclic ether (COC), and carboxylic anhydride [(C=O)O(C=O)] were found to present on the diamond surfaces [15]. Pehrsson observed the oxygen-containing species on single-crystal diamond (1 0 0) surface using HREELS. The work revealed that at higher temperatures the C=O groups preferentially desorbed whereas the ether-bonded groups remain on the surfaces [16].

Theoretical studies of oxygenated diamond (0 0 1) surfaces have been carried out by semiempirical and ab initio methods. An SLAB-MINDO molecular orbital calculation found that the lowest-energy configuration for one monolayer (ML) O coverage has a $1×1$ on-top structure, whereas for one half ML, it has a $2×1$ dimer bridge structure [17]. Based on cluster calculations, Skovkov et al. studied oxygenated diamond (0 0 1) surfaces in the presence of hydrogen [18]. Their

Using periodic density-functional calculations, Tamura et al. studied the oxidation processes in terms of reaction heat, and the oxidation mechanism of hydrogenated diamond $(0\ 0\ 1)$ surfaces [19]. An electrostatic-dipole model appears to explain the stability of the asymmetric alignment of the hydroxyl groups with respect to the symmetric one. Finally, the stability of the various oxygenated phases at different O coverage as a function of the oxygen chemical potential, $\mu_O$, is also analyzed based on the first-principles results.

## 2. Calculations

The calculations were carried out using the Vienna $ab$ initio simulation package (VASP) [20], in which the electron-core interactions were described by the Vanderbilt ultrasoft pseudopotential [21], and the generalized gradient approximation [22] was used for the exchange correlation energy. The Kohn-Sham orbitals were expanded in the plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 425 eV. A supercell approximation was used to mimic the surfaces in which each slab is separated from the neighboring slabs in the $(0\ 0\ 1)$ direction by at least $15\ \text{\AA}$ of vacuum. Such a cell size has been tested to be enough to give results independent of interactions between slabs [23]. And the slab contains 12 layers of C atoms. The Monkhorst-Pack scheme was used for the Brillouin zone integration with a $2\times2\times1$ k-point mesh [24]. In the energy minimization, we have allowed for the atoms to fully relax, except for those at bottom two layers, which are fixed in their respective relaxed $2\times1$ restructured positions. Hydrogen atoms are used to passivate the back surfaces. The energy convergence criterion for the structural relaxation is better than 0.5 meV per atom. Also, we used in the calculation the theoretical lattice constant of $3.574\ \text{\AA}$, which is only $0.2\%$ overestimated than the experimental value of $3.567\ \text{\AA}$.

3.1.2.2. The $(1 \times 1)$:O surface. Using cluster calculations, Whitten et al. suggested that atomic oxygen adsorption energies at the on-top (Fig. 1(c)) and bridge-bonded sites (Fig. 1(d)) were nearly identical [32]. However, our calculations turned out that the structure with oxygen on-top adsorption is less stable than the oxygen bridge-bonded structure, in agreement with other papers [17,33–35]. The energy difference in our calculation reaches as high as 0.33 eV per $(1 \times 1)$ unit cell, whereas it is 0.14 eV by Zhang and Smith [17].

In Fig. 1(c), the length of C=O bond is 1.20 Å, which is almost the same as that obtained by Zhang and Smith (1.20 Å) [17], but different from the length (1.16 Å) by Tamura et al. [19]. Due to the formation of C=O bonds, the bond length of C atoms of (S) and $(S-1)$ layers elongated to 1.54 Å. In Fig. 1(d), the length of C–O bond elongated to 1.51 Å, slightly longer than the 1.48 Å by Tamura et al. [19]. The bond length between C atoms of (S) and $(S-1)$ layers is 1.54 Å, almost the same as that of the structure with $(1 \times 1)$:O-top surface. The surface angle of O–$C_S$–$C_S$ is $33^\circ$.

### 3.1.3. Half ML O-covered diamond $(\begin{matrix}0&0&1\end{matrix})$ surfaces with H-presence
3.1.3.1. The O-bridge surface. Ether structures have been indicated on oxygenated diamond surfaces by IR spectra at the earlier stage of the oxidation [14]. In the present calculations, the O-bridge structure could be regarded as ether shown in Fig. 2(a). After geometry optimizations, the equilibrium site for oxygen atom is on the top of the monohydride dimer bond, in which the C–C bond of the dimer is opened and a new C–O–C structure is formed. Due to the insertion of the oxygen atoms at the dimer bonds, the distance between the hydrogen atoms from the adjoined dimers is decreased to 1.33 Å. It would cause large steric repulsions between adjacent H atoms, and the total energy is higher for 0.52 eV per $(1 \times 1)$ unit cell than the stable structure (Fig. 2(c)).

3.1.3.2. The hydroxyl surfaces. When diamond $(\begin{matrix}0&0&1\end{matrix})$ surface was fully covered with H and OH, a $(2 \times 1)$ periodic surface can be formed, as shown in Fig. 2(b), the C–C dimer was bonded with a H and a OH, forming H–C–C–OH structure on the surface. The OH formed hydrogen bonds along the direction vertical to dimerization. The hydrogen bond length is 1.64 Å, which is a strong H-bonding. The bond length of O–H is contracted to 0.99 Å (0.96 Å by Tamura et al. [19]).

The coverage of H and OH on the diamond $(\begin{matrix}0&0&1\end{matrix})$ surface can form a $(2 \times 2)$ periodic surface, as shown in Fig. 2(c), in which two H atoms on one dimer formed H–C–C–H structure, and two (OH)s on the next dimer formed HO–C–C–OH structure. Hereafter we denote C atoms with H as $C_H$ and C with

OH as $C_{OH}$. Hydrogen bonds are formed between neighboring (OH)s along the dimerization direction. The hydrogen bond length is $1.74$ Å on the dimer site, and the bond length is $1.59$ Å on the bridge site, both suggesting strong H-bondings. The lengths of C–C dimers are different due to the difference of adatoms. The length of $C_{H}$–$C_{H}$ dimer is $1.62$ Å, almost the same as that of C–C dimer on the $(2\times1)$:H surface, and the length of $C_{OH}$–$C_{OH}$ dimer was found to extend to $1.63$ Å. The bond length of O–H is $1.00$ Å, and the bond lengths of C–O are $1.41$ and $1.42$ Å [36].

For the half ML oxygen covered surfaces, our calculations revealed that hydroxyl surface (Fig. 2(b) and (c)) is more stable compared to the etherized surface (Fig. 2(a)), and further the new structure in Fig. 2(c) is favored to the structure in Fig. 2(b). The total energy of $(2\times2)$:0.5H&0.5(OH) (Fig. 2(b)) geometry is about 0.52 eV per $(1\times1)$ unit cell lower than that of O-bridge structure (Fig. 2(a)), whereas the difference is only 0.1 eV given by Skovkov et al. [18]. We ascribe the difference to the calculation models used: the calculations by Skovkov et al. were performed using a cluster model, while our model was a slab with periodic boundary condition. The repulsion between H atoms is neglected in their calculation. Our calculations give the length between two nearest H atoms is about $1.33$ Å, which indicates that the repulsion should be taken into account.

The total energy of hydroxyl surface we found in Fig. 2(c) is 90 meV lower than that in Fig. 2(b), which is reported as the most stable structure by Tamura. Those two structures differ in which the H-bonding is parallel to dimerization in Fig. 2(c), while vertical to dimerization direction in Fig. 2(b).

### 3.1.4. Full ML O-covered diamond $(0\ 0\ 1)$ surfaces with $H$-presence

#### 3.1.4.1. The O-bridge/hydroxyl surface.
The fully oxygen covered diamond $(0\ 0\ 1)$ surface can form an O-bridge/ hydroxyl structure as shown in Fig. 3(a). The bond length between C and O atoms in the C–O–C structure is $1.38$ Å, and the bond length between C and O atoms in the C–OH structure is $1.46$ Å. The hydrogen bonds are formed between the adjoined hydroxyl groups, and the length is $1.68$ Å.

The O-bridge/hydroxyl structure was regarded to be stable in Ref. [18], whereas its energy is 0.80 eV higher for per $(1\times1)$ unit cell than that of the stable hydroxyl one (Fig. 3(e)) in our calculations.

#### 3.1.4.2. The $(2\times1)$:OH surface.
The geometry with aligned hydroxyl groups on the surface is shown in Fig. 3(b) and (d), in which each surface C atom bonds to one OH.

In Fig. 3(b), the strong surface $\sigma$ bonds are weakened by the OH adsorption, the length of the surface dimers extended to $1.64$ Å ($1.62$ Å on the $(2\times1)$:H surface). And the bond lengths between the C atoms in the S and the $(S-1)$ layers increase to $1.55$ Å, much closer to bulk C–C bond ($1.545$ Å), which means that the adsorption of OH largely releases the strain. The difference of $C_{S}$–$C_{S-1}$ bond length is caused by the formation of asymmetrical H bonds between adsorbed (OH)s on the top of dimer and bridge sites. The C–O bonds are also effected by the asymmetrical H bonds and the lengths are contracted to $1.39$ and $1.41$ Å. The OH bond has no much change (0.99 and $1.00$ Å). This result is almost the same as reported by Tamura et al. [19].

As for the structure in Fig. 3(d), the bond length of OH is $0.99$ Å, and the hydrogen bond between (OH)s is about $1.64$ Å. This structure is much more stable than the structure shown in Fig. 3(b). However, our calculation turns out that there are more stable structures describe below.

#### 3.1.4.3. The $(2\times2)$:OH surface.
On the fully covered diamond $(0\ 0\ 1)$ surfaces with OH, if a hydrogen bond and an opposite direction hydrogen bond alternating along the direction normal to dimerization, then the $(2\times2)$:OH surface was formed as shown in Fig. 3(c). The bridge site hydrogen bond is strengthened compared to the hydrogen bond on the $(2\times1)$:OH surface and the length is contracted to $1.60$ Å. On the other hand, the hydrogen bond on the top of dimer site is weakened compared to the hydrogen bond on the $(2\times1)$:OH surface and the length is elongated to $1.79$ Å. The formation of asymmetrical hydrogen bonds between the nearest neighboring dimers (Fig. 3(c)) decreased the total energy for 0.12 eV per $(2\times2)$ unit cell lower than the symmetrical one (Fig. 3(b)).

When a hydrogen bond and an opposite direction hydrogen bond alternating along the direction parallel to dimerization, then another $(2\times2)$:OH surface was formed as shown in Fig. 3(e). In this structure, the bond length of OH is $0.99$ Å, and the length of hydrogen bond is $1.64$ Å. The total energy is lower than that of the structure shown in Fig. 3(c) for about 0.13 eV, which makes it to be the most stable structure. Sque et al. [35] also reported that the $(2\times2)$:OH structure is the most stable one. While their calculations are carried via LDA, our calculations are performed with GGA. GGA is known to be more accurate quantitatively in deal with H-bonding on surface [37,38].

The physical origin of the lower of the adlayer energy is due to the polarization of the OH. On the $(2\times1)$:OH surface, hydroxyl groups aligned on the surface form parallel electrostatic dipoles as shown in the inset of Fig. 3(d), while on the $(2\times2)$:OH surface hydroxyl groups form antiparallel electrostatic dipoles, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3(e). Thus, the antiparallel structure we obtained in Fig. 3(c) and (e) reduces the Coulomb repulsion between neighboring (OH)s, and consequently lower the total energy.

### 3.2.2. Chemical-potential analysis
In order to compare the stability among phases with different amounts of oxygen coverages, we used a chemical-potential analysis by considering the system in equilibrium with an oxygen reservoir characterized by a chemical potential $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$.

The formation energy $\Delta E$ of a $(1 \times 1)$ unit cell of the O-covered surface was defined as

$$
\Delta E=E-\left(E_{\mathrm{S}}+n_{\mathrm{O}} \mu_{\mathrm{O}}\right)
\tag{1}
$$

where $E$ is the total energy of some oxygen covered surface phase, $E_{\mathrm{S}}$ the total energy of the bare $(2 \times 1)$ structure, $n_{\mathrm{O}}$ the number of O atoms on the surface, and $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$ is the chemical potential of oxygen.

## 4. Summary

In this paper, we studied the bare oxygen covered and H-presence oxygenated diamond (0 0 1) surfaces via ab initio calculations. On oxygenated diamond (0 0 1) surfaces without H, the O-bridge structures are found to be more stable than the O-on-top sites. With H-presence, two low-energy structures are found, one with aligned hydroxyl groups parallel to surface carbon dimerization direction at one half ML O coverage, and one with asymmetric alignment of the hydroxyl groups at one ML O coverage. The formation of H-bond becomes more and more important in lowering the adlayer energy with the increase of oxygen coverage. The relative stability of various oxygenated phases at different O coverage as a function of the oxygen chemical potential, $\mu_O$, is also analyzed.
