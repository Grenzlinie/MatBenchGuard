# Structural controls on OH site availability and reactivity at iron oxyhydroxide particle surfaces†

Xiaowei Song and Jean-François Boily*

Received 24th August 2011, Accepted 2nd December 2011
DOI: 10.1039/c2cp22715k

Iron oxyhydroxides (FeOOH) are highly reactive minerals of widespread occurrence in natural and industrial settings. These minerals chiefly occur as nano- to submicron-sized particles and are covered by hydroxyl functional groups coordinated to one (-OH), two (µ-OH), or three (µ₃-OH) underlying iron atoms. These groups are reaction centers for gases, solutes as well as solvents and thereby play important roles in the fate and transformation of natural and industrial compounds. In this work we provide tools to identify hydroxyl groups on distinct crystallographic planes of two important FeOOH polymorphs, namely lepidocrocite (γ-FeOOH) and goethite (α-FeOOH).
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy was used to monitor O–H stretching vibrations of groups on particles with well-defined and distinct morphologies. Spectral responses to proton loadings and thermal gradients were used to assign bands to hydroxyl groups. These efforts were facilitated by the extraction of pure spectral components obtained by multivariate curve resolution.
Molecular dynamics simulations of dominant crystallographic planes of the particles guided band assignment procedures by identifying feasible hydrogen bond networks between surface groups.
Our findings provide new possibilities for molecular-scale resolution of important gas-phase processes on the surfaces of these important minerals.

## 1. Introduction
Adsorption reactions at iron oxyhydroxide (FeOOH) surfaces are central to a variety of processes of biogeochemical, atmospheric, industrial and catalytic importance.¹⁻² In soils they control contaminant transport and nutrient availability. In the atmosphere they can be sinks and catalysts for greenhouse gases as well as serve as nucleation points for ice. In the industry they can provide solutions for new technologies, such as pigments, catalysts and absorbents.³⁻⁵ Resolution of these processes requires a fundamental understanding of the chemistry of different types of hydroxyl functional groups present on these surfaces. These groups are generally bound to one (-OH), two (µ-OH) or three (µ₃-OH) underlying Fe atoms and occur in different proportions on different crystallographic planes. Knowledge of protonation, hydrogen bonding pattern as well as (de)hydration and (de)hydroxylation reactions involving these groups is consequently crucial in predicting surface reactivity.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy can be used to detect hydroxyl groups on surfaces of metal(oxy)(hydr)oxides.⁶⁻⁸ This technique typically retrieves narrow discrete-like bands in the high energy portion of the O–H stretching region (> 3400 cm⁻¹).

As stretching frequencies are highly responsive to variations in O–H bond length, in the order of 150–175 cm⁻¹ per pm,⁹·¹⁰ variations in the band position and intensity arising from interfacial reactions (e.g. ligand exchange, protonation, (de)sorption) can help identify surface groups. This approach has been applied to a wide range of inorganic materials, including catalytic alumina,¹¹·¹² zinc oxides¹³·¹⁴ and zeolites.¹⁵ Single crystal surfaces¹¹ have moreover been highly beneficial in this area, by providing *a priori* knowledge of OH groups present,¹⁶⁻²⁰ constraining results obtained from otherwise uncharacterized, highly reactive, powdered materials. Single FeOOH crystals are however of rarer occurrence than the nano- to micron-sized particles widely found in nature. Fortunately FeOOH particles can be prepared with well-defined morphologies (Fig. 1) and can therefore be used to resolve molecular-scale reactions with knowledge of predominant crystallographic terminations. Their large specific surface areas moreover provide sufficient throughputs required for FTIR spectroscopic measurements.

In this study we present detailed information on the identity and reactivity of OH sites present on important planes of submicron-sized FeOOH particles (Fig. 1). Although FTIR has been used on FeOOH previously,⁶·²¹⁻²³ measurements involving particles of undefined crystal habits, lack of variations in sorption loading, overlooked effects of co-sorbed protons, and limited quantitative and coupled experimental–theoretical analyses have resulted in disparate views on this matter. The dominant crystallographic planes of these particles were first simulated by molecular dynamics (MD) to build a conceptual model predicting distributions of OH groups as well as hydrogen bonding. ## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Materials

LL was synthesized in water at 298 K by oxidation of a carbonate-free solution of 0.02 M FeCl₂ in the presence of 0.2 M NaCl.$^{26}$ This solution was first filtered (0.2 $\mu$m nitrocellulose) to remove any adventitious akaganéite, then neutralized to pH 6.0 by CO₂-free 1.0 M NaOH under an atmosphere of N₂(g). Compressed air was then passed into the solution, resulting in the precipitation of a pH 6.0 suspension of lepidocrocite after a reaction time of 2.5 h. RL was also synthesized by oxidation by using this method$^{27}$ except that the procedure was carried out in 0.2 M FeCl₂ solution in the absence of dissolved NaCl and neutralized to pH 7.0.

All solutions and suspensions were continuously stirred with a polyethylene propeller.

Goethite was made by hydrothermal conversion of ferrihydrite.$^{6}$ Ferrihydrite was first made in a N₂(g)-filled glove box at 298 K by drop-wise addition of 2.5 M KOH to a 0.15 M Fe(NO₃)₃ solution that was continuously stirred with a polyethylene propeller. Titrant addition was stopped at pH 12.0. The resulting suspension was then converted to goethite in an oven at 343 K for 24 h in a sealed polyethylene bottle.

All precipitates were washed with carbonate-free doubly distilled deionized (DI) water (resistivity is 18 M$\Omega$ cm). All DI water used for the washing procedure was first degassed by boiling, then purged with N₂(g) overnight. A first washing cycle was made by centrifugation until the solids could no longer be centrifuged. The solids were thereafter dialyzed for 2 weeks at 298 K, with repeated changes of degassed DI water, until the resistivity of the supernatant exceeded 1000 k$\Omega$ cm. The resulting salt-free suspensions were stored in sealed polyethylene bottles stored in a N₂(g)-filled desiccator to minimize any contact with atmospheric CO₂.

Particle characterization was carried out on solids dried at 313 K for 7 days in a N₂(g)-filled oven. Dry samples were analyzed for structure and purity by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker d8 Advance working in $\theta$-$\theta$ mode with Cu K$\alpha$ radiation), FTIR (*cf.* Section 2.2) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos Axis Ultra using a monochromated Al K$\alpha$ source). Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (B.E.T.) specific surface area and microporosity (Table 1) were determined from a 90 point adsorption/desorption isotherm (TriStar, Micromeritics), on samples previously dried *in situ* at 100 °C for 16 h under a stream of N₂(g). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was carried out on mineral-water mixtures using a JE-1230 (JEOL) instrument (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Standardized solutions of HCl and NaOH were then added in aliquots to achieve total proton densities, ([HCl]−[NaOH]) nm⁻², ranging from −13.86 to 312.99 protons nm⁻² for LL, −7.85 to 141.13 protons nm⁻² for RL and −12.00 to 217.04 protons nm⁻² for G (Tables S1–S3, ESI†). All resulting suspensions were then equilibrated in sealed polyethylene test tubes for 24 h under N₂(g), then further exposed to a stream of N₂(g) for another 0.5 h prior to centrifugation and ATR-FTIR measurements.

Spectra were collected every 0.5 h during this evaporation procedure until all O-H stretching and bending modes of free water disappeared. As the water evaporated from the suspension, proton concentrations were increased, in turn increasing proton surface loadings beyond typical values reached in aqueous solutions. It should however be stressed that the actual proton loading does not necessarily correspond to the $([HCl]-[NaOH])$ nm$^{-2}$ values neither, as a portion of the acidity can be lost as volatile HCl. Measurements were carried out in the $600-4500$ cm$^{-1}$ range at a resolution of $2.5$ cm$^{-1}$ and at a forward/reverse scanning rate of 10 Hz, resulting in 1000 co-added spectra for each sample. Blackman-Harris 3-term apodization function was used to correct phase resolution.

### 2.3 TPD-FTIR spectroscopy

Three powder samples of LL, RL and G were prepared from mineral suspensions dried at 313 K for 7 days. The powder was pressed onto a fine tungsten mesh (Unique wire weaving,0.002" mesh diameter) squeezed into a copper-heating shaft and in direct contact with a K-type thermocouple. It was thereafter heated at a constant rate of $10$ K min$^{-1}$ from 318 to673 K in an IR chamber (AABSPEC #2000-A) equipped with KBr windows under an operating pressure below 2.5 mTorr, the detection limit of the pressure sensor (MKS, Baratron). The spectra were collected with a Bruker Vertex 70/V FTIR spectrometer, equipped with a DLaTGS detector. All spectrawere collected in the $600-4500$ cm$^{-1}$ range at a resolution of $4.0$ cm$^{-1}$ and at a forward/reverse scanning rate of 10 Hz and each spectrum was an average of 25 scans. Blackman-Harris3-term apodization function was used to correct phase resolution.

### 2.4 Chemometric analyses

All spectra were offset to zero absorbance at $4450$ cm$^{-1}$, where absorbances are negligible, baseline-corrected using a polynomial function (Fig. S1, ESI†), then normalized for area in the OH stretching region. Baseline correction was judged appropriate after several tests showing that it extracted clearer spectral components than any other strategy. Furthermore, as the background is smooth and its 2nd derivative is linear, it does not introduce any artificially generated bands.

Single value decomposition $^{28}$ (SVD) was used to generate noise/error reduced spectra, for which the number of chemical relevant vectors was estimated with the factor indicator function(IND). $^{29}$ A multivariate curve resolution (MCR) analysis was thereafter applied with the MCR-ALS program $^{24}$ to obtain the purest spectral components. This method therefore takes results of SVD, or principal component analysis, one step ahead by rotating eigenvectors into a real chemical space. This vector rotation procedure generates a concentration profile for each component which, in our case, corresponds to variations in populations of OH sites. As one of the most widely used "blind source separation" approaches, $^{30-32}$ MCR was chosen as it has been shown to retrieve comparable if not superiorresults to other competing methods including SIMPLISMA, $^{33}$ which is also based on MCR, MICLA as well as SNICA. $^{34} We$  have used this method on a variety of data types in several previous studies. $^{35,36}$ All calculations were performed with MATLAB 7.0.

### 2.5 Molecular dynamics (MD)

Two lepidocrocite $(\gamma-FeOOH)$ supercells were terminated bythe (010) and (001) planes and two goethite $(\alpha-FeOOH)$ supercells by the (110) and (021) planes using METADISEE. $^{37}$ Crystallographic parameters of Wyckoff $^{38}$ and Yang et al. $^{39}$  were used for lepidocrocite and goethite, respectively. Terminations with the smallest energy and dipole moment were chosen from the results of METADISE calculations. Dangling surface bonds were saturated with surface oxygens $(O_s)$ or protons, where applicable. Charge-neutral slabs were of $\sim 3$ nm in thickness andare of the following composition: $Fe_{1188}O_{1188}(OH)_{990}(O_sH)_{198}$  for the (010) and $Fe_{960}O_{840}(OH)_{840}(O_sH)_{180}$ for (001) planes oflepidocrocite, and $Fe_{792}O_{660}(OH)_{660}(O_sH)_{264}(O_s)_{66}$ for the (110) and $Fe_{864}O_{792}(OH)_{792}(O_sH)_{216}(O_sH)_{72}$ for (021) planes of goethite.

The cells were repeated infinitely in all three dimensions by periodic boundary conditions, leaving an 8 nm thick void between each slab. They were simulated by classical MD with the CLAYFF $^{40}$ force field for oxygen and hydroxyls, the revised CLAYFF parameters $^{41}$ for $Fe^{3+}$ , and the flexible SPC42 force field for the few water molecules present on the(021) plane of goethite. $^{41}$ All O-H stretches of goethite were set to the experimental value of $3120$ cm$^{-1}$. In forthcoming studies those of surface O-H groups will also be set to values determined in this study. All parameter values and equations are reported in Table S4 of the ESI. $\dagger$ This combination of parameters can reproduce bulk structures of lepidocrocite and goethite. We confirmed the validity of this combination of parameters in a preliminary set of calculations.

A NPT (constant number of particles, constant pressure and constant temperature) ensemble and a time step of 0.5 fs were used to integrate the equations of motions with the Verlet algorithm. $^{43}$ The temperature of the whole system(300 K) was coupled to the velocity-rescale thermostat at a0.1 ps relaxation time. The SETTLE algorithm $^{44}$ was used to treat the geometry of water, the LINCS algorithm $^{45}$ was used to treat O-H bonds of all hydroxyls. Position restraints were not used. A 0.8 nm cut-off was used for van der Waals interactions and the particle mesh Ewald method $^{46,47}$ was used to treat long-range electrostatics. All calculations were carried out with Gromacs (v. 4.5.3). $^{48}$ Simulation cells were first energy-minimized (double precision) using a steepest descent algorithm in typically less than $10^4$ steps, and equilibrated(single precision) by classical MD for $10^7$ steps (5 ns). Additional5 ns simulations were then generated for production runs to monitor atomic positions and energies. Hydrogen bond formation between surface groups was notably carried out with the g_hbond utility of Gromacs. In this program all

$O \cdots H$ distances within 0.3 nm were recorded for distance, donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle and fraction of donors involved in hydrogen bonding.

## 3. Results and discussions

### 3.1 Sample characterization
X-Ray powder diffraction confirmed that all samples consisted only of either crystalline lepidocrocite or goethite. Salient physical attributes of all three synthetic materials are reported in Table 1. Particles are no greater than 250 nm in length and 35 nm in width and thickness. Their $N_2(g)$ B.E.T. specific surface areas lie in the $55.6–81.3\ \text{m}^2\ \text{g}^{-1}$ range and, according to adsorption–desorption isotherms, are associated to 1.0–4.0% microporosity. XPS confirmed that the samples were free of impurities, other than trace aliphatic carbons intrinsically associated to these *in vacuo* measurements, and retrieved characteristic Fe $2p_{3/2}$ and O 1s regions of FeOOH minerals. FTIR measurements of dry samples exposed to $N_2(g)$ confirm the absence of hydrocarbons, carbonates and carboxyls under these conditions.

LL particles consist of wide thin platelets while those of RL consist of narrower and longer particles (Fig. 1, Table 1). LL is predominantly terminated by the (010) plane. The (100) edge and, less extensively, the (001) termination represent the minor planes of LL. RL displays the same planes, however with a greater proportion of the (001) edge. FTIR spectra of our RL and LL samples (Fig. S2, ESI†) are characteristic of the lepidocrocite phase with characteristic bulk OH bending modes at 1154, 1021, $755\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ for RL, and 1160, 1021, $744\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ for LL. Bulk OH stretching modes are centered at $3060\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ in RL but occur as a doublet at 2855 and $3115\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ for LL. This split is a characteristic feature of LL and arises from the preferential alignment²⁶ of the (010) plane on the ATR crystal surface. This alignment enhances the selection of O–H stretching components lying longitudinally to the propagating infrared radiation $(2855\ \text{cm}^{-1})$. Perpendicular components occur, on the other hand, at a higher energy region in the LL spectra $(3115\ \text{cm}^{-1})$. These effects are however of little or no significance in RL as the equally important (010) and (100) planes are randomly oriented on the ATR. As such, these measurements add further confirmation to the important morphological differences between LL and RL.

Goethite particles are acicular in shape and dominated by the structurally analogous (110) and (100) planes, as well as the terminal (021) plane.² The FTIR spectra (Fig. S2, ESI†) reveal no other OH stretching $(3130\ \text{cm}^{-1})$ or bending (896, $794\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) modes than those of goethite.⁴⁹ Note that bulk OH stretches of goethite are stronger while bends are weaker than those of lepidocrocite. These differences result from stronger hydrogen bonding as well as hydrogen bond cooperativity within the sheets lepidocrocite $({\text{O} - \text{H} \cdots \text{O} - \text{H} \cdots \text{O} - \text{H}})$, compared to those of goethite pseudo-channels $({\text{O} - \text{H} \cdots \text{OH}})$.⁴⁹ These differences are however not manifested on the dominant crystallographic planes of these minerals. As it will be shown in the following sections, surface structure rather induces highly specific O–H stretching vibrations for each of the surface hydroxyl functional groups.

### 3.2 Surface structure and OH site distribution
MD simulations of the dominant planes of lepidocrocite and goethite produced surface Fe–O distances within 0.015 nm of the original crystallographic values (Table S5, ESI†). All simulation cells retained their original structures with deviations in atomic positions of no more than 1.2% of the original crystallographic position. In lepidocrocite, the relaxed and neutrally-charged (010) surface is populated by isolated, non-interacting, μ-OH groups $(8.4\ \text{sites}\ \text{nm}^{-2})$, as shown in Fig. 2. Their (001) counterparts are, in contrast, hydrogen bond acceptors from an underlying triply-coordinated group (Fig. 2). Our hydrogen bonding analysis (Fig. S3, ESI†) reveals similar properties to those of the bulk, with a median donor–hydrogen–acceptor bond angle of $8^\circ$. Hydrogen bonds are also formed along rows of -OH groups but exhibit atypical bond angle distributions (Fig. S3, ESI†) for such interactions. They also involve about 12% of the sites during the course of the simulation and are therefore weaker than those of the bulk or in, for example, water.

MD simulations of the (110) plane of goethite confirm the existence of isolated μ-OH, μ₃,₁₁-OH as well as μ₃-O groups (Fig. 2; Fig. S4, ESI†). A network of hydrogen bonds is developed along the *c*-axis within -OH groups (-OH$\cdots$-OH-) as well as between -OH and μ₃,ᵣ-OH (μ₃,ᵣ-OH$\cdots$HO-). The former involves about every second -OH group and consists of a relatively large (*ca.* $25^\circ$) donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle and distance (Fig. S4, ESI†). Hydrogen bonds between μ₃,ᵣ-OH and -OH groups are, on the other hand, substantially shorter, involve virtually all surface groups throughout the 5 ns production run, and form typically small (*ca.* $11–12^\circ$) donor-hydrogen-acceptor angles, as in the goethite bulk. As it will be shown in the following sections, this network induces a red shift in the O–H stretching bands of -OH and μ₃,ᵣ-OH groups.

Simulations of the (021) plane of goethite display a more complex distribution of surface species. This plane is populated by two types of singly (-OH) and doubly (μ-OH) coordinated groups (Fig. S5A, ESI†). -OH groups are in turn distributed as 1 : 1 concentrations of -OH and -OH₂ groups to ensure charge-neutrality. Our simulations of this plane point to an extensive network of hydrogen bonds between all surface groups. The extent of this complexity can be appreciated by the results of Fig. S5 B–D (ESI†). As such, no discrete O–H stretching bands could specifically be assigned to groups of the (021) plane.

Hydrogen bonding, compounded by low overall site densities, is responsible for these results.

To summarize, lepidocrocite and goethite particles each exhibit distinct sets of surface terminations. Spectra of LL with RL are expected to reveal differences between $\mu$-OH of the (010) plane and the mixture of sites with the (001) plane. Differences between the (001) plane of RL and the (110) plane of G should provide evidence for differences in the hydrogen bonding patterns of -OH groups. Finally, it is important to note that none of the particle surfaces under study are expected to be free of defects. We therefore stress that the MD results remain conceptual guidelines for the band assignments to be made in the following sections (Table 2).

### 3.3 FTIR spectra

FTIR spectra of dry LL, RL and G particles prepared over a wide range of proton loadings are shown in Fig. 3. All spectra contain discrete-like O–H stretching vibration bands. All bands decrease in intensity with proton loading due to the formation of chemisorbed water (e.g. -OH$_2$, $\mu$-OH$_2$) and chloride (e.g. -Cl, $\mu$-Cl). Protonation moreover induces the formation of a network of hydrogen bonds between various sites, resulting in the rise of a broad range of intensities below $3600\ \text{cm}^{-1}$. All independent contributions to spectral features can be extracted into spectral components using MCR. This procedure produces linearly-independent (orthogonal) spectral components each encompassing groups of correlated OH bands (Fig. 4). Uncorrelated intensities are thereby separated from one another through this procedure. Concentration profiles (Fig. 4d–f) show the importance of each component in relation to proton loading. FTIR spectra of samples exposed to temperature gradients are shown in Fig. 5. All bands decrease in intensity with temperature due to surface dehydration and dehydroxylation reactions. MCR components (Fig. 6) capture salient bands at low and high temperatures. These components are discussed in the following sections in relation to differences in particle morphology and our molecular dynamics simulations.

#### 3.3.1 Lepidocrocite

LL particles provide the best reference spectra for $\mu$-OH sites of the (010) surface (Fig. 1 and 2). LL particles equilibrated in conditions where $\mu$-OH predominates exhibit one dominant band at $3625\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (band of $\circled{2}$ in Fig. 3a). The corresponding spectral component (AI; Fig. 4a) dominates at low proton loadings. At greater loadings, this discrete band was replaced by a broader range of red-shifted intensities (AII) associated to $\mu$-OH$_2$ and $\mu$-Cl.

The $3625\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ band (Fig. 3b) is also well expressed in RL (Fig. 1), as the (010) plane remains an important crystallographic termination. The greater surface area of the (001) plane in RL however gives rise to an additional band of comparable intensity at $3667\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (band of $\circled{1}$ in Fig. 3b). Note that it is also present in considerably lower intensities in LL, where the (001) plane represents a minor fraction of the particle total surface area. The strong response of the $3667\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ band to protonation supports its assignment to a site of strong proton affinity. The -OH site (Fig. 1 and 2) is a good candidate to these requirements, especially in the light of previous accounts on its proton affinity.$^{25}$ According to the multisite complexation,$^{25,50}$ -OH sites should have considerably stronger proton affinities than $\mu$-OH, while $\mu_3$-OH groups are already fully protonated under our working conditions. Consistent with these ideas, we note that temperature readily attenuated the $3667\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (-OH) band while the $3626\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ ($\mu$-OH) band only underwent thermal broadening (Fig. 5b). Resilience of the latter to temperature is consistent with a hydroxyl group of greater coordination number and, therefore, greater binding energies with underlying iron atoms. In this case, formation of $\text{-OH}_2$ along a row of $[\text{-OH}_2\cdots\text{-OH}\cdots]$ groups is expected to weaken O–H bond strengths and shift stretching frequencies to $3654\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (BII), then to $3646\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (BIII) at greater proton loadings (Fig. 4b, Table S6). These discrete-like bands should then be replaced by broader features below $3600\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ as the network of hydrogen-bonded interactions is developed.

A third set of bands occurs at 3534 and $3550/3552\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (band of ③ and ④ in Fig. 3a and b). These bands are present in all lepidocrocite spectral components (Fig. 4a and b) because they are largely unaffected by variations in proton loadings. Their assignment to $\mu_3\text{-OH}$ is thereby consistent with the inability of these sites in protonating any further.$^{25}$ Furthermore, their resilience to temperature (Fig. 5) points to stable OH groups of large Fe nuclearity. We can explain the presence of two bands by two distinct $\mu_3\text{-OH}$ groups on the (001) (5.2 sites $\text{nm}^{-2}$) and the terminal (100) (4.2 sites $\text{nm}^{-2}$) planes. The latter, which was not modeled for this study, represents 5% of the area in RL but a more considerable 17% in LL. The greater intensities of the $3550\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ band in RL motivate, in our view, assignment of this band to $\mu_3\text{-OH}$ sites of (001). The $3534\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ band should consequently be assigned to $\mu_3\text{-OH}$ sites of the (100) plane.

### 3.3.2 Goethite. Comparable, yet more complex, spectral features are noted for goethite particles (Fig. 3). Samples prepared under the most alkaline conditions exhibit two strong bands centered at 3661 and $3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$. A previous density functional theory (DFT) study$^9$ of the (110) plane supports assignment of these bands to -OH ($3661\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) and $\mu_{3,1}\text{-OH}$ ($3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) (band of ① and ③ in Fig. 3c). Both are interconnected by a network of hydrogen bonds, as suggested by MD simulations (Fig. 2). This network should also account for the weaker stretching frequencies of $\mu_{3,1}\text{-OH}$ in goethite ($3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) than in lepidocrocite (3534 and $3550\ \text{cm}^{-1}$). It should also account for the $6\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ red shifted -OH band of goethite ($3661\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) in relation to that of lepidocrocite ($3667\ \text{cm}^{-1}$).

Formation of $-OH_2$ at the greater proton loadings weakens the hydrogen bond in $\mu_{3,I}$-OH$\cdots$OH$_2$ and consequently tightens stretches of $\mu_{3,I}$-OH from $3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (CI) to 3545, 3576 (CIII) then to 3585 (CIV) $\text{cm}^{-1}$ (Fig. 4c and Table S6, ESI$\dagger$). Near complete attenuation of the $3661\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ region further resolves a band at $3648\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ (CII), with a similar proton loading dependence to the $3625\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ ($\mu$-OH) band of LL and RL. Its association to the $3661\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ region is in line with DFT frequency calculations$^9$ showing contributions from isolated $\mu$-OH and $\mu_{3,II}$-OH species. The greatest proton loadings give rise to, as in LL and RL, a broad set of stretching frequencies arising from a network of hydrogen-bonded $-OH_2$ and $\mu$-OH$_2$ groups. Finally, no discrete-bands could be specifically assigned to the (021) plane. The extensive hydrogen bonding between groups of this plane, as illustrated through MD simulations, is most likely responsible for this result and results in a broad range of O–H stretching vibrations.

The TPD data add further constrains to our assignments for goethite (Fig. 5 and 6). Both 3661 and $3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ bands undergo concomitant changes with temperature, the former red shifting to $3657\ \text{cm}^{-1}$. This shift can be explained by, as for the $3667\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ band of RL, hydrogen bonding to -O that resulted from the associative desorption of H$_2$O (Fig. 6). This (de)hydroxylation process not only creates a vacancy (Fe termination) in -OH, and thereby an isolated $\mu_{3,I}$-OH group, but also alters hydrogen-bonds of $\mu_{3,I}$-OH ($3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) to the remaining -OH and -O. Both of these changes affect stretching frequencies, thereby explaining the consumption of $3491\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ with temperature. Finally, note that the spectra of G at the highest temperatures, where all -OH groups were expected to have been removed, retained intensities in the broad $3650\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ region. This region was assigned to $\mu$-OH and $\mu_{3,II}$-OH groups. Comparisons with the TPD data of RL and LL reveal that $\mu$-OH, $\mu_{3,II}$-OH, as well as new isolated $\mu_{3,I}$-OH groups, should contribute to this broad band. This is also consistent with the aforementioned assignments and DFT calculations$^9$ of G.

### 3.3.3 Thermodynamic implications.
Concentration profiles extracted from our chemometric analyses (Fig. 7) provide additional validation to our band assignments. They moreover underscore important implications on the thermodynamics stabilities of surface groups. Spectral components for -OH in RL (BI) and G (CI) all exhibit nearly identical relative concentra- tions with proton loading. The proton loading dependence of $\mu$-OH in LL (AI) is, in contrast, considerably weaker than those of -OH. These experimentally-derived observations therefore fall in line with previous theoretical estimates of relative proton affinities of different surface groups.$^{25}$

We also noticed another strong correspondence in the proton loading of components AII (LL), BIII (RL), CIV (G). As stated earlier, these components contain broad ranges of intensities resulting from complex networks of hydrogen- bonded sites involving chemisorbed (e.g. $-OH_2$, $\mu$-OH$_2$) water molecules. These similarities point to comparable energies, as well as spectral signatures, in the development of networks of hydrogen bonds involving hydroxyls and chemisorbed water. Implications for these findings are currently the object of additional laboratory investigations aimed at resolving water sorption reactions on these surfaces.

## 4. Conclusions
Spectral components presented in this study open the door for molecular scale resolution of adsorption processes on different crystal planes of FeOOH. Such efforts can be carried out by monitoring variations in surface O–H stretching modes with sorbent loading. These should also point to concomitant changes in co-adsorbed protons/hydroxyls as well as to modifications to networks of hydrogen bonds. They moreover enable resolution of binding sites in other iron oxide minerals with less defined morphologies, such as the important ferrihydrite phase,$^{36}$ as well as nano-clusters.$^{51,52}$ Such efforts were recently made for akaganéite.$^6$ Efforts along these aforementioned lines will lead to the improvement of thermodynamic models, particularly in the extraction of more accurate adsorption energies through the use of physically sound surface species. This work should, in addition, serve as a sound stepping-stone to mineral/water interface studies by sum frequency generation spectroscopy where O–H stretching modes can be resolved in the presence of liquid water. Finally it is important to emphasize that the methods outlined in this work should be applicable to a wider range of hydroxyl-terminated surfaces (e.g. clays, zeolites, catalytic aluminas), and consequently impact our understanding of processes in many areas where the interfacial chemistry of minerals is of paramount importance.
