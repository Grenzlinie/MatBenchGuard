Abstract: Developing transition metal single-atom catalysts (SACs) for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) is of great importance. Zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) as a subgroup of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are distinguished as SAC precursors, due to their large porosity and N content. However, the activity of the formed metal sites is limited. Herein, we reported a decarboxylation-induced defects strategy to improve their intrinsic activity via increasing the defect density. Carboxylate/amide mixed-linker MOF (DMOF) was chosen to produce defective Co SACs (Co@DMOF) by gas-transport of Co species to DMOF upon heating. Comparing with ZIF-8 derived SAC (Co@ZIF-8-900), Co@DMOF-900 with more defects yet one fifth Co content and similar specific double-layer capacitance show better ORR activity and eight times higher turnover frequency (2.015 e s⁻¹ site⁻¹). Quantum calculation confirms the defects can weaken the adsorption free energy of OOH on Co sites and further boost the ORR process.

Single-atom catalysts (SACs) with coordinated transition metal (TM) as catalytic centers are one of the most promising catalysts, which benefit from the maximum atomic efficiency, unsaturated coordination environment, and unique electronic structure.[¹] They have been widely explored in thermocatalytic, photocatalytic, organic-catalytic, and electro-catalytic reactions.[²] Tremendous efforts have been devoted to exploring novel strategies for constructing high-performance SACs.[³] Particularly, owing to their abundant N content and well-developed porous skeleton, zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs)[⁴], a subgroup of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)[⁵] connected with metal cations and imidazolate-type organic linkers, have been widely used to construct SACs by pyrolyzing for electro-catalytic reaction.[⁶] It is because that the unique chemical and structural features of ZIF derivatives can guarantee the mass and electron accessibility to the resultant TM center. Beyond that, the activity of SACs is also greatly influenced by the electronic structure of the TM center. Heteroatom doping (such as B, P, S, and halogens)[⁷] represents an efficient way to improve their catalytic performance. In addition, defects in the carbon supports should also affect the electronic structure of the TM active sites.[⁸] Recently, it is reported that defects can be introduced into the N-doped carbon supports derived from ZIF by acid-etching of metal nanoparticles.[⁹] However, this process requires an environmental-unfriendly acidic solution, and its defect-induction efficiency is unsatisfactory.

Herein, we propose a facile decarboxylation-induced defect strategy to sufficiently increase the defect degree as well as optimize the electronic structure of TM sites and the adsorption energy for the key intermediates. Actually, the majority of MOFs are carboxylate coordination polymers, which exhibit more structural diversities compared with ZIFs. Studies have shown that the carboxylate groups in MOFs will dissociate from the aromatic rings to form $CO_x$ upon heating to a certain temperature,[¹⁰] and we believe that this decarboxylation process can lead to the increment of the defect and tuning the catalytic activity.

As a proof-of-concept, we prepared defective Co SACs (Co@DMOF) by pyrolyzing a carboxylate/amide mixed-ligand zinc MOF (DMOF) with simultaneously introducing Co atom via a gas transport method[³ᵇ] and investigated their structure–function (oxygen reduction reaction, ORR) relationship. Accelerating the cathodic sluggish multiple-electron ORR[¹¹] is of vital importance for fuel cells[¹²] and Zn–air batteries (ZABs),[¹³] which represent promising sustainable energy conversion and storage technologies.

Benefiting from the advantages of Zn-evaporation in the pyrolysis process and mixed-ligand (N containing ligand and carboxylic linker), the resulting Co@DMOFs own tunable degree of defects, atomic Co-N₄ sites, abundant N doping, and high specific surface area. The optimized catalyst demonstrates great catalytic ORR activity and ultrahigh stability in 0.1 M KOH compared to commercial 20 wt% Pt/C (Pt/C). In addition, the synergistic effect between defects and atomic Co-N₄ site was revealed by the density functional theory (DFT) calculation and

Crystalline DMOF with rod-shaped morphology (Figure S1a, and S2) was prepared according to the literature[¹⁴]. It is constructed from [Zn₂(O₂C)₄] square paddle-wheel secondary building units, which are coordinated with 2-aminoterephthalic acid and pillared with triethylenediamine. Co@DMOF-T (T represents pyrolysis temperature) was prepared by placing CoCl₂·6H₂O and DMOF in separated quartz boats and then pyrolyzing in a tube furnace under a N₂ flow at 700, 800, 900, or 1000 °C. As controls, DMOF-900 (derived from DMOF without CoCl₂·6H₂O), CoNPs/DMOF-900 (derived from DMOF via impregnation method), Co@MOF-5-900 (derived from carboxylate MOF-5 without N), Co@ZIF-8-T (derived from N containing-ZIF-8 without carboxylate group), and Co@IRMOF-3-900 (derived from IRMOF-3 consisted of Zn ion and 2-amino- terephthalic acid) were prepared.

Figure 1. a) PXRD patterns of Co@DMOF-T materials and the baseline of the sample stage. b-c) HAADF-STEM and HRTEM images of Co@DMOF-900 catalyst. d) Raman spectra of Co@DMOF-T catalysts. e) ¹³C ssNMR spectra of Co@DMOF-T catalysts.

The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of Co@DMOF-T (Figure 1a) exhibit characteristic peaks corresponding to the (002) and (100) planes of graphitic carbons (Figure S3b-d).[¹⁵] The diffraction peaks associated with Co₃C are only observed in Co@DMOF-1000, suggesting the amorphous dispersion of Co element in the products obtained in lower pyrolysis temperatures. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) show that the Co@DMOF-T materials adopt pumice-like morphologies and Co-based nanoparticles are only found in Co@DMOF-1000 (Figure 1b-c and Figure S4a-d). Raman spectra of Co@DMOF-T (Figure 1d) exhibit two broad peaks, designated as D band (~1350 cm⁻¹) and G band (~1587 cm⁻¹). Commonly, the peak intensity ratio (I_D/I_G) can be used to assess the defect level of graphitic support.[¹⁶] The results show that the defect level of Co@DMOF-T slightly changed with annealing temperatures and Co@DMOF-900 reaches the highest I_D/I_G value. We further examined the I_sp3/I_sp2 values by using solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) spectra[⁹] (Figure 1e) and the results showed similar tendency as the Raman results. Meanwhile, their porous structures were studied by nitrogen sorption measurements (Figure S5 and Table S1). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas of Co@DMOF-700, Co@DMOF-800, Co@DMOF-900, and Co@DMOF-1000 are 482, 467, 587, and 487 m² g⁻¹, respectively. The structure, crystallinity and morphology of other controlled catalysts were also characterized (Figure S3-S7 and Table S2).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to identify the valence and compositions of elements in Co@DMOF-T (Figure S8). The peaks in high-resolution N 1s spectra (Figure S9a, and Table S3) can be assigned to pyridinic-N (398.4 eV), Co-N₄ (399.2 eV), pyrrolic-N (399.8 eV), graphitic-N (401.1 eV), and oxidized-N (403~405 eV).[^17] Meanwhile, the peaks in high-resolution C 1s spectra (Figure S10 and Table S4) are deconvoluted to C=C ($sp^2$) (284.75 eV), C–C ($sp^3$) (285.6 eV), C–N/C–O (286.5 eV), and C=O (289 eV).[^18] The binding energies of Co 2p (Figure S9b) indicate that Co species in Co@DMOF-T is in an intermediate oxidation state with Co (II) and Co (III), which may result from the coordination between Co and N.[^19]

The above analyses demonstrate that atomically distributed Co-N₄ sites located on a defect-rich N-doped carbon were obtained at relatively low pyrolysis temperatures. To confirm this, further characterizations were conducted. Take Co@DMOF-900 as an example, abundant isolated bright dots in onion-like carbon sheet can be found in its aberration-corrected (AC) HAADF-STEM image[^20] (Figure 2a). Meanwhile, the electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) mapping (Figure 2b) further confirms the uniform distribution of C, N, and Co elements. The aberration-corrected low-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC LADDF-STEM) was applied to observe the defects in Co@DMOF-900. It is found that not only non-hexagonal carbon defects are formed in carbon skeletons, but also the Co-N₄ sites are indeed defective (Figure 2c). The synchrotron X-ray absorption fine spectroscopy (XAFS) was employed to characterize the coordinate structure of the Co atomic site. The Co K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectrum of Co@DMOF-900 (Figure 2d and S11) reveal that the Co atoms existed as a middle valence between Co (II) and Co (III) state.[^19b] This is in good agreement with XPS observations. Compared with the Fourier-transformed (FT) $k^3$-weighted extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of Co foils, Co@DMOF-900 exhibits a dominant peak at 1.34 Å corresponded to the Co–N scattering path[^19b], and the peak at 2.18 Å referred to Co–Co scattering path is absent (Figure 2e). The EXAFS fitting curve in $R$ space (Figure S12a and Table S5) suggests that the single Co atom is coordinated with four N atoms at 1.34 Å to form a Co-N₄ moiety. During the pyrolyzing procedure, volatile $CoCl_2$ diffused to the samples in gas phase (Figure S13). The Co ions can be easily trapped by the N sites in the carbon skeleton to form Co-N₄ sites, avoiding further reduction and Co atoms migration.

To demonstrate the importance of the decarboxylation for the formation of the defects, we further conducted control experiments by preparing Co@ZIF-8-T under similar conditions for the preparation of Co@DMOF-T. The ratios of $I_D/I_G$ and $I_{sp3}/I_{sp2}$ of Co@ZIF-8-T only exhibited slight differences upon changing the pyrolyzing temperatures (Figure S14). Yet these ratios are obviously lower than those of the catalysts (Co@DMOF-T, DMOF-900, and Co@MOF-5-900) derived from the carboxylate containing MOFs (Figure S15), revealing a much higher defect content induced by the decarboxylation process.

A rotating ring–disk electrode (RRDE) was used to explore the ORR performance of the resulting catalysts in $O_2$-saturated 0.1 M KOH. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurement was carried out to determine the ORR activity (Figure 3a, Figure S16, S17a–b, and Table S6). Co@DMOF-900 shows the most positive $E_{1/2}$ of 0.866 V vs. RHE compared with other DMOF-derived materials and higher than Pt/C and other controlled catalysts, indicating the key role of Co and N in the catalytic ORR. The specific double-layer capacitance ($C_{dl}$) was used to judge the effective electrochemical surface area, which is associated with the accessible surface area and the exposure of Co sites. Co@DMOF-900 shows the highest $C_{dl}$ (53.2 mF cm⁻²) among all the DMOF-derived catalysts (Figure 3b and S18). The ORR activity follows the tendency of both defect levels and $C_{dl}$ in these

catalysts (Figure S19). The electron transfer number of Co@DMOF-900 was determined as 3.85 and $H_2O_2$ yield was less than 10% (Figure S20). Koutecky-Levich (K-L) plots (Figure S21) also confirm the $4e^-$-dominated pathway.

The long-time durability of Co@DMOF-900 was examined by chronoamperometric responses (CA) and accelerated degradation test (ADT). After CA measurement holding on 0.86 V for 12 h, the Co@DMOF-900 remained 93.8 % current, while the Pt/C merely kept 76.6% current under the same condition (Figure S22). As for ADT, the $E_{1/2}$ of Co@DMOF-900 was negative shifted only 11 mV after 5000 cycles (Figure 3c), while Pt/C lost 37 mV (Figure S23a). The electron transfer number, $H_2O_2$ yield, and $C_{dl}$ of Co@DMOF-900 after ADT do not show obvious change (Figure S23b–c and Figure S24). The structural changes were also studied. There is no agglomeration of the Co atom occurred after ADT, as shown in AC HAADF-STEM and EELS mapping images (Figure S25). As determined by CA, Co@DMOF-900 shows good tolerance to methanol (Figure S26).

ZABs were further assembled. The Co@DMOF-900 cathode provides a higher open-circuit voltage (1.45 V vs. Zn), maximum power density (158 mW cm⁻²), specific capacity (815 mAh g⁻¹), and energy density (965 Wh kg⁻¹) than those of Pt/C (1.42 V vs. Zn; 121 mW cm⁻²; 714 mAh g⁻¹; 905 Wh kg⁻¹) (Figure 3d–e and Figure S27b). The theoretical capacity and energy density utilization degree of Co@DMOF-900 is achieved at 99.4% and 88.8%, respectively. In the galvanostatic cycling stability test at a current density of 5 mA cm⁻², Co@DMOF-900 combined with RuO₂ shows excellent stability with insignificant change after discharged/charged cycling over 200 h, while the mixture of Pt/C and RuO₂ was gradually polarized (Figure 3f).

To exclude the effect of Zn on the catalytic performance, we pyrolyzed DMOF, ZIF-8 and IRMOF-3 under 900 °C (denoted as DMOF-900, ZIF-8-900, IRMOF-3-900, respectively) without introduction of Co species. Then we treated the samples by concentrate HNO₃ to remove Zn residue (Table S7). The LSV curves (Figure S28) do not show obvious changes before and after removal of Zn residues. In addition, CoNPs/DMOF-900 also shows poor electro-catalytic performance (Figure S17b). These results indicate that Zn do not influence the catalytic performance and atomic Co single-atom is served as the active sites.

To further demonstrate the importance of the defects induced by the decarboxylation process on the electro-catalytic reaction, the structure–composition–activity relationship of Co@ZIF-8-900 and Co@DMOF-900 were examined (Table S8). Co@ZIF-8-900 with the atomical distribution of Co (Figure S29–30) possesses much higher N and Co contents (N, 9.22 wt%; Co, 3.33 wt%) than Co@DMOF-900 (N, 5.23 wt%; Co, 0.71 wt%) (Table S9). Without the aid of decarboxylation, the defect degree in Co@ZIF-8-900 is significantly lower (Figure S14). It owns a similar $C_{dl}$ (52.9 mF cm⁻²) (Figure 3b) and Tafel slope (69.2 mV dec⁻¹) to those of Co@DMOF-900 ($C_{dl}$, 53.2 mF cm⁻²; Tafel slope, 66.2 mV dec⁻¹) (Figure S31), which means that their catalytic kinetics are similar. The deduced mass activity $(j_{m})$ of Co@DMOF-900 (3.30 A mg⁻¹) with less Co content is almost ninefold of that of Co@ZIF-8-900 (0.39 A mg⁻¹), revealing a much higher intrinsic activity excluding the influence of mass transfer can be found in Co@DMOF-900 (Figure 3g). $^{[21]}$ The significantly improved intrinsic activity is further evidenced by the turnover frequency (TOF) analyses. The TOF of Co@DMOF-900 (2.015 e s⁻¹ site⁻¹) is eight times higher than that of Co@ZIF-8-900 (0.238 e s⁻¹ site⁻¹) (Figure 3h).

XPS (Figure S32), elemental analyses (Table S9), PXRD (Figure S33), Raman (Figure S34), AC HADDF-STEM images and EELS mapping images (Figure S35) of Co@IRMOF-3-900 with randomly distributed morphology (Figure S36) exhibit similar results for Co@DMOF-900. Co@IRMOF-3-900 shows similar catalytic performance as Co@DMOF-900 (Figure S37), demonstrating the good applicability of the decarboxylation-induced defect strategy.

DFT calculations were carried out along the typical $4e^-$ pathway, in which the adsorbed $O_2$ is first reduced to *OOH and further transforms to *O and *OH. $^{[22]}$ Combined with the above structural characterizations, four possible models of Co-N₄ site with defect were constructed (Figure 4a). The pristine Co-N₄ without defect and defective N-doped graphene (Figure S38) was also constructed to identify the crucial synergistic effect between Co-N₄ site and defect in accelerating the ORR energetics. The free energy model suggested by Nørskov is employed to investigate the thermodynamic activity. It is found that Co-N₄-6r-c2 shows the highest $U$ (working potential, V vs. SHE) (Figure S39), closer to the equilibrium potential of 1.23 V, suggesting that it will possess the highest activity for ORR. The free energy curves (Figure 4b) of Co-N₄-6r-c2 and Co-N₄ indicate that the rate-determining step is $O_2 \to OOH$. For the $U = 0.83$ V on Co-N₄, the elementary reaction of $O_2 \to OOH$ is thermodynamic equilibrium, while it is still downhill on Co-N₄-6r-c2 ($U = 0.83$ V) (Figure 4c). This suggests that the synergistic effect of Co-N₄ site and defect can accelerate the ORR. For comparison, there is no stable adsorbed OOH species on pure graphene and defective N-doped graphene.

In summary, a decarboxylation-induced defect strategy is designed to prepare defect-rich Co SACs by using the mixed-ligand MOF as the precursor. The high surface area, N doping, Co-N₄ sites, and rich defects played the crucial role in improving the electrochemical ORR performance. DFT calculations further

confirmed that defect structure formed near the Co-N₄ site in the defect-rich Co SACs can greatly improve the intrinsic catalytic performance of the Co-N₄ site, reduce the adsorption free energy of OOH on Co-N₄ sites, and thus promoting the electrochemical reaction. The ORR performance and ZAB performance of Co@DMOF-900 rival against those of the best Co SACs (Table S10 and S11). Since carboxylate MOFs provide abundant structural diversities, spatially distributed anchor sites, and rich mass transport channels, this work may offer an avenue for developing highly porous and defect-rich TM SACs with greatly improved intrinsic activity and distinct catalytic routes.
