# High-performance Na-$CO_2$ batteries with $ZnCo_2O_4$@CNT as the cathode catalyst†

Subashchandrabose Thoka, $^{a}$ Zizheng Tong, $^{b}$ Anirudha Jena, $^{bc}$ Tai-Feng Hung, $^{d}$ Ching-Chen Wu, $^{d}$ Wen-Sheng Chang, $^{d}$ Fu-Ming Wang, $^{efgh}$ Xing-Chun Wang, $^{e}$ Li-Chang Yin, $^{*i}$ Ho Chang, $^{*c}$ Shu-Fen Hu $^{*a}$ and Ru-Shi Liu $^{*b}$

Rechargeable Na–$CO_2$ batteries are promising energy storage devices as they not only provide high energy density but also utilize earth-abundant Na and the greenhouse gas $CO_2$. However, Na–$CO_2$ battery development is deterred due to the sluggish electrochemical reactions at the cathode. The cathode catalyst is unable to decompose the insulating discharge product $Na_2CO_3$, thereby leading to increasing charge overpotential and poor cycle performance of the Na–$CO_2$ battery. Herein, we report spinel $ZnCo_2O_4$ porous nanorods with multiwall carbon nanotubes ($ZnCo_2O_4$@CNT) as a cathode composite in the Na–$CO_2$ batteries to effectively decompose $Na_2CO_3$ and improve the cycle performance. The battery shows stable discharge–charge for at least 150 cycles with a limited capacity of 500 mA h $g^{-1}$ at 100 mA $g^{-1}$. Moreover, the battery with the $ZnCo_2O_4$@CNT cathode catalyst exhibits a reversible discharge–charge capacity of 12 475 mA h $g^{-1}$. Theoretical simulations suggest that the adsorption energies of $CO_2$, Na, and $Na_2CO_3$ on the three surfaces of $ZnCo_2O_4$ are favorable for the $CO_2$RR and $CO_2$ER during the charge–discharge of the Na–$CO_2$ battery. The surfaces of $ZnCo_2O_4$ investigated for catalytic activity include the [001] surface, the [111] surface with only exposed Co atoms, and the [111] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms. The density of states calculation results further reveal that Co atoms on these three surfaces are possibly the catalytic active sites for the $CO_2$RR and $CO_2$ER during the charge–discharge of the Na–$CO_2$ battery.

## 1. Introduction
Metal-$CO_2$ batteries have recently received a great deal of attention from researchers because of their high specific energy and ability to recycle the greenhouse gas $CO_2$. $^{1-4}$ The metal-$CO_2$ battery concept was formulated while researching the $CO_2$ contamination effect on Li-$O_2$ batteries. $^{5}$ Xu et al. $^{6}$ introduced the first Li-$CO_2$ batteries. Thereafter, a significant improvement has been observed in the progress of Li-$CO_2$ batteries in the last few years. However, the scarcity and uneven distribution of lithium-containing ores in the Earth's crust increase the processing cost of Li and hamper the commercialization of Li-$CO_2$ batteries. $^{7}$ On this note, Na-$CO_2$ batteries are considered one of the viable storage devices due to the utilization of inexpensive and earth-abundant Na as the anode and still deliver a high energy density ($\sim$1125 W h $kg^{-1}$), which is approximately five times that of the currently commercialized Li-ion batteries. $^{8,9}$

Hu et al. $^{2}$ developed a room-temperature rechargeable Na-$CO_2$ battery that contained an ether-based electrolyte, sodium foil as the anode, and multiwall carbon nanotubes (MCNTs) as the cathode material. The reduced electrochemical polarization of the battery was attributed to the high porosity and the electrical conductivity of the cathode. The good wettability of the electrolyte to the cathode also led to the satisfactory performance of the battery. The reversible reaction of the Na-$CO_2$ battery was demonstrated for the first time with various characterization techniques. The reaction is shown in eqn (1).

The authors² also confirmed the presence of carbon in the discharge products by designing carbon-free Ag nanowires as a cathode electrode. Zhou's group¹⁰ also reported a similar kind of approach to detect the carbon in the discharge products of Li–CO₂ batteries by using a carbon-free platinum net as the cathode material. Chen's group reported the construction of stable quasi-solid state Na–CO₂ batteries with an activated MCNT (a-MCNT) cathode, reduced graphene oxide sodium anode, and composite polymer electrolyte to deal with the leakage of the electrolyte and instability of the Na metal anode.¹¹ However, the thermodynamically stable discharge product, Na₂CO₃, is difficult to decompose during charging to complete the reversible reaction; thus, the Na–CO₂ batteries experience high charge potential and side reactions, which could hamper their practical application.¹² Guo et al.¹³ reported a ruthenium nanoparticle porous Ketjen black carbon composite (Ru@KB) as the cathode material for the Na–CO₂ batteries to improve the decomposition of Na₂CO₃ and increase the reversibility and cycling performance. Low-cost and earth-abundant catalysts should be developed for the commercialization of Na–CO₂ batteries owing to the scarcity and high cost of noble metals.

On this note, transition metal oxide electrocatalysts have received attention from researchers due to their superior catalytic activity and natural abundance.¹⁴⁻¹⁶ Mixed metal oxides have been used as bifunctional cathode catalysts for metal–air batteries.¹⁷,¹⁸ Co₂MnOₓ nanowires decorated on carbon nanofibers have been employed as electrocatalysts in Na–CO₂ batteries to enhance the decomposition of Na₂CO₃.¹⁹ In our recent reports, ZnCo₂O₄ is utilized as the cathode catalyst for the Li–O₂ and Li–CO₂ batteries.²⁰,²¹ Li–CO₂ batteries with porous ZnCo₂O₄ nanorods with MCNTs on a stainless steel substrate as the cathode composite exhibit a high cycle performance of 290 cycles at 100 mA g⁻¹ with a restricted capacity of 500 mA h g⁻¹.

Based on the excellent catalytic activity of the ZnCo₂O₄@CNT composite in a Li–CO₂ battery, the ZnCo₂O₄@CNT on a carbon paper substrate is employed in this study as the cathode electrode in rechargeable Na–CO₂ batteries for the first time. The Na–CO₂ batteries with a ZnCo₂O₄@CNT cathode composite deliver a reversible discharge–charge capacity of 12 475 mA h g⁻¹. The coulombic efficiency reached 100% by charging the battery below 4.1 V. The superior catalytic performance of the cathode and the better cycle performance could be attributed to the synergistic effect of the porous ZnCo₂O₄ nanorods and MCNTs.

## 2. Experimental section
### 2.1. Synthesis of porous ZnCo₂O₄ nanorods
The hydrothermal method was employed to synthesize ZnCo₂O₄ by adapting the method in our previous reports.²⁰,²¹ Appropriate amounts of 0.2 M cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (99%, ACROS), 0.1 M zinc nitrate hexahydrate (99.9%, J. T. Baker), 0.2 M ammonium fluoride (98%, Merck), and 0.5 M urea (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 50 mL deionized water with thorough sonication for approximately 30 min. The resultant solution was properly sealed in a Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment for 7 h at 140 °C. After completion of the stipulated reaction time, the autoclave was removed from the heating oven and allowed to cool down to room temperature. The products were collected from the bottom of the Teflon tube and separated from unreacted residues by the centrifugation method, followed by washing with ethanol and water. After washing several times, the products were dried at 70 °C and then finally calcined at 400 °C for 2 h, thereby yielding porous ZnCo₂O₄ nanorods. Co₃O₄ nanorod structures were synthesized by following a similar procedure but excluding the addition of the zinc nitrate hexahydrate precursor for comparison.

### 2.2. Instrumentation and physical characterization
The phase purity of the as-prepared electrode materials was examined using X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD, Bruker D2 Phaser, Cu Kα radiation, $\lambda = 1.541$ Å). The morphological structures of the electrode materials were investigated with a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JEOL JSM-6700F). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out at beamline 24 A1 of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC) in Hsinchu, Taiwan. The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of the Co and Zn K-edges were collected at the BL 17C1 beamline of the NSRRC. Gas evolution during the Na–CO₂ battery charging process was monitored with a gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer (GC-MS, Q1500).

### 2.3. ZnCo₂O₄@CNT composite air electrode preparation
A similar approach from our previous report was employed to prepare the ZnCo₂O₄@CNT composite electrode.²¹ Initially, 20 mg of multiwall carbon nanotubes and 10 mg of polyvinylidene fluoride were thoroughly dissolved in ~1 mL of N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone solvent with sonication. Then, the as-prepared 20 mg of ZnCo₂O₄ powder was introduced in the above-mentioned mixture while sonicating (~30 min) and then ball-milled for approximately 2 h to generate the homogeneous and well-dispersed ZnCo₂O₄@CNT composite ink. Approximately 10 uL of the electrocatalyst ink was uniformly drop cast on the carbon paper. The carbon paper that holds the electrocatalyst was heated at 80 °C for 12 h under vacuum to finally obtain the ZnCo₂O₄@CNT composite air electrode. A similar approach was followed to prepare the Co₃O₄@CNT composite air electrode by replacing the ZnCo₂O₄ powder with Co₃O₄ powder while preparing the slurry. The mass loading of the electrocatalyst of 0.2 mg was maintained for all the electrochemical measurements. The current densities and capacities were calculated according to the carbon amount in the electrocatalyst. An Ar-filled UNIlab MBraun glovebox was used to store all the electrodes.

### 2.4. Battery assembly
The Na–CO₂ batteries were fabricated in an Ar-filled UNIlab MBraun glovebox by employing conventional CR2032 coin cells

with the above-mentioned $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}@CNT$ electrode as the air cathode, $1\ M\ NaClO_{4}$ in a TEGDME solvent as the electrolyte, a glass fiber as the separator, and sodium metal as the counter electrode. The cathode part of the coin cell was drilled to make a 4 mm hole to ensure that $CO_{2}$ can interact with the air cathode. The assembled coin cell was placed in a specially designed $CO_{2}$-filled gas bottle to perform charge and discharge. The battery performance was examined with an AccuTech cycler.

### 2.5. Computational method
As previously reported, the electronic structure of spinel $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ can be well described by DFT plus Hubbard U correction (DFT+U) with $U = 2$ eV for the Co atom. $^{22}$ The DFT+U ($U = 2$ eV) calculations in this work were carried out using the VASP code. $^{23 - 26}$ Projector augmented-wave pseudopotentials were used to describe the interaction between ions and electrons. $^{27,28}$ The exchange-correlation potential was described by the generalized gradient approximation with the PBE parameterization. $^{29}$ The specific calculation settings were similar to those adopted in our recent work. $^{30}$ The cut-off energy of the plane-wave basis set was fixed at 800 eV. The optimization calculations for bulk $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ were performed with a Gamma-centered $4\times4\times4$ $k$-point mesh. The convergence criteria were set to $1\times10^{-6}$ eV and $0.01$ eV $\mathring{A}^{-1}$ for total energies and residual forces, respectively. The O 2s 2p, Co 3p 4s 3d, and Zn 3d 4p were treated as valence states for all calculations. The van der Waals interactions were considered by using the empirical correction in Grimme's method (DFT+D3). $^{31}$ On the basis of the above-mentioned settings, the lattice constants of bulk $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ were calculated to be $a = b = c = 8.131\ \mathring{A}$, which is very close to the experimental value $(8.105\ \mathring{A}).^{22}$ Three low indexexes, namely [001], [110], and [111] surfaces, were constructed based on the fully relaxed crystal structure of bulk $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$. This task was conducted to study the adsorption behavior of Na atoms, $CO_{2}$ molecules, and $Na_{2}CO_{3}$ clusters on the $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ surface. The [110] and [111] surfaces have two and three possible terminations, respectively, due to the spinel structure of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$. The following six surfaces were considered in this work: [001] (Fig. S4$\dagger$), [110] with only exposed Co atoms and [110] with exposed Co and Zn atoms (Fig. S5$\dagger$), [111] with only exposed Co atoms and [111] with only exposed Zn atoms (Fig. S6$\dagger$), and [111] with exposed Co and Zn atoms (Fig. 4). The bottom three or four atomic layers of all considered surfaces were fixed during the relaxation. The vacuum thickness was set at $15\ \mathring{A}$ for all calculations. The Brillouin zones of the [001], [110], and [111] surfaces were sampled with the Gamma-centered $4\times4\times1$, $4\times3\times1$, and $3\times3\times1$ $k$-point mesh, respectively. The C 2s 2p and Na 2p 3s were treated as the valence states to calculate the adsorption energies of Na atoms, $CO_{2}$ molecules, and $Na_{2}CO_{3}$ clusters on all the considered $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ surfaces. The adsorption energies were calculated based on the following definition: $E_{\text{ad}.}=E_{\text{A}}+E_{\text{S}}-E_{\text{T}}$, where $E_{\text{T}}$ is the DFT total energy of the corresponding system with the specified adsorbates considered in this work (including Na atoms, $CO_{2}$ molecules, and $Na_{2}CO_{3}$ clusters) and $E_{\text{A}}$ and $E_{\text{S}}$ represent the DFT total energies of the isolated specified adsorbates and the corresponding $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ surfaces, respectively. $E_{\text{A}}$ of an isolated Na atom was set at half the total energy of bulk bcc sodium with two Na atoms. This definition indicates that a large positive value of $E_{\text{ad}.}$ means strong adsorption. The charge population and charge transfer were calculated *via* Bader charge analysis. $^{32,33}$

## 3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1a and b represent the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the hydrothermally synthesized $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ porous nanorods. The hydrothermal synthetic approach was adapted from our previous reports. $^{20,21}$ Fig. 1a shows that the crystal phase of the $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ nanorods is confirmed by the XRD measurements. The well-matched peaks at $2\theta$ values of $18.9^{\circ}$, $31.20^{\circ}$, $36.81^{\circ}$, $38.48^{\circ}$, $44.74^{\circ}$, $55.57^{\circ}$, $59.29^{\circ}$, and $65.14^{\circ}$ corresponded to the (111), (220), (311), (220), (400), (422), (511), and (440) diffraction planes of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ with the cubic spinel structure [Fd3m space group, JCPDS # 23-1390], respectively. In the spinel structure, the $Co^{3+}$ ions occupied the octahedral sites, and the $Zn^{2+}$ ions resided at the tetrahedral sites. The broad XRD peaks indicated the presence of nanometer-sized particles. According to the Scherrer equation the grain diameter of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ is calculated to be about 5 nm. The uniform porous nanorod morphology of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ can be observed in the SEM image (Fig. 1b). The particle size distribution histogram obtained from the SEM images presented in Fig S7$\dagger$ also indicates the presence of nanoparticles in $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ nanorods. Fig. 1b shows that the small nanoparticles (20-50 nm) are aggregated to form nanorods with a length ranging from $1.2\ \mu\text{m}$ to $2.7\ \mu\text{m}$. These nanorods were long enough to improve the electron mobility, thereby leading to increased conductivity of the $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}@CNT$ composite. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) characterization was employed to further confirm the porous nature and nanorod structure of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$. The voids created by adjacent particles in the nanorods can be observed in Fig. 1c and d. These voids were responsible for the porous nature of the nanorods, which could play a vital role in $CO_{2}$ diffusion and accommodation of $Na_{2}CO_{3}$ during discharge. The $d$-spacing values of 0.286 and 0.467 nm well matched the corresponding (2$\overline{2}$0) and (111) lattice planes of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$, respectively (Fig. 1e).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out to analyze the binding states of the Zn and Co elements in $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$. The reversible discharge-charge capacity (12 475 mA h g⁻¹) of the $ZnCo_2O_4@CNT$ composite cathode in $Na-CO_2$ batteries is superior to the maximum discharge capacities of recently reported Ru metal and transition metal oxide cathode catalysts.¹³,¹⁹

The leftover $Na_2CO_3$ that accumulated on the cathode during cycles might be the reason behind the increased overpotential as cycles progressed.

The morphology of pristine, discharged, and charged electrodes was investigated by SEM to understand the formation/decomposition behavior of the discharge product during the cycle performance. The SEM images of pristine electrodes in Fig. 3b show that the $ZnCo_2O_4$ porous nanorods adhered to uniformly dispersed MCNTs. The newly formed products validated the XRD results of the discharged electrodes (Fig. 3a), thus confirming the formation of $Na_2CO_3$. The major parts of discharge products disappeared or decomposed during charging, which could be observed in the SEM images of the charged electrodes (Fig. 3b). This phenomenon indicated the electrocatalytic activity of the $ZnCo_2O_4$@CNT composite to decompose $Na_2CO_3$. The $ZnCo_2O_4$ porous rods and MCNTs on the charged electrodes could be observed compared with those on the discharged electrodes due to the decomposition of fully covered $Na_2CO_3$. The SEM images of discharged and charged electrodes contain the $ZnCo_2O_4$ nanorod structures and CNTs, thereby indicating the stability of the composite during the discharge-charge.

However, the $CO_2$ evolution during charging needs to be confirmed. In situ gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) measurements were employed on a homemade $Na-CO_2$ battery with the $ZnCo_2O_4@CNT$ composite as the cathode during charging. Initially, the $Na-CO_2$ battery was discharged under the limited capacity of $500\ \text{mA}\ \text{h}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ at $100\ \text{mA}\ \text{g}^{-1}$ to obtain the discharged electrode. The discharged electrode was utilized to assemble a homemade $Na-CO_2$ battery that was connected to the GC-MS instrument. Fig. 3d represents the subsequent charge curve of the homemade $Na-CO_2$ battery with $ZnCo_2O_4@CNT$ as the cathode. The gas evolution during charging was monitored by GC-MS. The gas evolution curves in Fig. 3d show that the $CO_2$ evolution could be observed during charging. Meanwhile, no apparent $O_2$ evolution was observed. The results indicate the reversible decomposition of $Na_2CO_3$ to $CO_2$ during charging by utilizing the $ZnCo_2O_4@CNT$ electrocatalyst. The fragments of 58, 45, and 15 correspond to the decomposed electrolyte solvents. However, the unobserved fragment 58/45/15 evolution during charging proved the stability of the electrolyte in the presence of the $ZnCo_2O_4@CNT$ cathode. The cyclic voltammetry curves of the $Na-CO_2$ battery with $ZnCo_2O_4@CNT$ presented in Fig. S11† also show no obvious peaks at voltaged above 4 V further confirming the stability of the electrolyte. The TEGDME solvent in the electrolyte was earlier also reported to be stable up to $4.4\ \text{V}.^{10,34,35}$

Furthermore, the XANES profiles of Zn and Co K-edges (Fig. 3e) also indicated the stability and durability of $ZnCo_2O_4$. The absorption energies of the Co and Zn K-edges are located at 7729.5 and 9665.2 eV, respectively. There is no obvious difference that can be observed from the XANES profiles of pristine, discharged, and recharged samples indicating the high electrochemical stability of $ZnCo_2O_4$. The Fourier-transformed (FT) $k^3$-weighted EXAFS spectra of the Co and Zn K-edges can be derived (Fig. 3e). The Co-O and Zn-O bonds with average bond lengths of 1.92(2) and 1.95(0) Å can be obtained from the EXAFS spectra. The physical distance between Zn and Co is 3.35(5) Å (Table S1).†

According to the chemical reaction (eqn (1)) during the Na-$CO_2$ battery operation, the adsorption strength of $Na$, $CO_2$, and $Na_2CO_3$ on the possible catalytic sites of the cathode material is closely related to the kinetics of the $CO_2$ reduction reaction ($CO_2RR$) and $CO_2$ evolution reaction ($CO_2ER$); thus, the electrochemical performance of the Na-$CO_2$ battery can be determined. $^{36,37}$ Density functional theory calculations are carried out to examine the adsorption behavior of Na atoms, $CO_2$ molecules, and $Na_2CO_3$ clusters on six possible exposed surfaces of $ZnCo_2O_4$. The surfaces include the following: the [001] surface (Fig. S4†), the [110] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms (Fig. S5(a-d)†), the [110] surface with only exposed Co atoms (Fig. S5e†), the [111] surface with only exposed Co atoms (Fig. S6(a-e)†), the [111] surface with only exposed Zn atoms (Fig. S6f†), and the [111] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms (Fig. 4a-h). This task is conducted to reveal the catalytic role of $ZnCo_2O_4$ in the performance of the Na-$CO_2$ battery. The stable adsorption of the $CO_2$ molecule at the catalytically active sites is critical for the discharge of the Na-$CO_2$ battery, especially when considering the chemical inertness. The $CO_2$ adsorption on all six considered surfaces has first been examined. The adsorption of $CO_2$ on the considered [110] surfaces and the [111] surface with only exposed Zn atoms is typical physisorption with very low adsorption energies of approximately 0.13-0.36 eV for different adsorption configurations. Concerning the remaining three surfaces, $CO_2$ can be chemically adsorbed with large adsorption energies. The adsorption energies of $CO_2$ on the [001] surface, the [111] surface with only exposed Co atoms, and the [111] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms are calculated to be 1.37, 1.32, and 2.19/1.76 eV, respectively. This finding indicates that these three surfaces might be the active catalytic surfaces for the $CO_2RR$ process during the discharge of the Na-$CO_2$ battery. The strong chemical adsorption of $CO_2$ on these three surfaces forms new chemical bonds (C-Co and/or O-Co and/or O-Zn bonds), which can be further confirmed by the charge population and charge transfer analysis. The charge transfer of $CO_2$ on the [001] surface, the [111] surface with only exposed Co atoms, and the [111] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms is calculated to be 0.83, 0.64, and 0.97/0.81 electrons from $ZnCo_2O_4$ to $CO_2$, respectively. The different adsorption behavior of $CO_2$ on different surfaces can be well understood by the density of states (DOS) calculation. Fig. 4i shows that only the [001], the [111] with exposed Co atoms, and the [111] with exposed Co and Zn atoms show the unoccupied states of surface Co atoms around the Fermi level ($E_F$), thus allowing the surface Co atoms to bond with C or O atoms of $CO_2$. A truly catalytically active surface for the Na-$CO_2$ battery should have strong binding toward Na atoms and $Na_2CO_3$ clusters in addition to the strong adsorption toward the $CO_2$ molecules. On this basis, we further calculate the adsorption of the Na atoms and $Na_2CO_3$ clusters on these three surfaces. These three surfaces show strong binding with $Na_2CO_3$ clusters and Na atom. The adsorption energies of the $Na_2CO_3$ clusters (Na atoms) on the [001] surface (Fig. S4b†), the [111] surface with exposed Co atoms (Fig. S6b†), and the [111] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms (Fig. 4c) are calculated to be 2.66 (1.32), 5.18 (1.24), and 5.09 (1.23) eV, respectively. The coordinate environment of the exposed Co atoms is revealed in the FT $k^{3}$-weighted EXAFS spectra (Fig. 3e). The EXAFS fitting results are summarized in Table S1. $\dagger$ According to the fitting results, Co-O has an average bond length of $1.922\ \mathring{A}$. The average coordinate number of Co is 3.32 in the first shell. The distance between Co and the second shell Co is $2.878\ \mathring{A}$. The second shell coordinate number is 6.76. The $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ showed a decreased coordinate number compared with that of the perfect crystal, where the Co-O first shell coordinate number is 6. The decreased coordinate number can be ascribed to the nanosize of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$. With the decreased coordinate number, more orbitals will be available to coordinate with the $CO_{2}$, $Na_{2}CO_{3}$, and Na-ions, and thus enhanced electrochemical properties can be expected. Furthermore, the coordinate environment of Zn atom has also been investigated by EXAFS. Zn also demonstrates a decreased coordinate number compared with the perfect crystal, where the coordinate number of Zn-O should be 4. So the catalytic properties of the Zn site may also be enhanced. From the EXAFS fitting results, the outstanding electrochemical performance of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ can be ascribed to the nanostructure, which decreased the average coordinate number and promoted the adsorption of $CO_{2}$, $Na_{2}CO_{3}$, and Na on the surface.

## 4. Conclusions
In summary, the hydrothermal method has been employed to synthesize porous $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ nanorods. The bifunctional catalytic activity of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$ nanorods combined with CNTs improves the effective decomposition of $Na_{2}CO_{3}$ and leads to the excellent cycle performance of $Na-CO_{2}$ batteries. The Na-$CO_{2}$ battery with the $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}@CNT$ cathode composite delivers a low charge overpotential of 3.8 V for the initial 100 cycles. Overall, the battery sustained over 150 cycles with a limited capacity of $500\ mA\ h\ g^{-1}$ at $100\ mA\ g^{-1}$. The $Na-CO_{2}$ battery also delivers a reversible discharge-charge capacity as high as $12\ 475\ mA\ h\ g^{-1}$. The discharge plateau is stable at 2.31 V, and the coulombic efficiency reached 100% when charging the battery just below 4.1 V. Theoretical calculations revealed that three surfaces, such as the [001] surface, the [111] surface with only exposed Co atoms, and the [111] surface with exposed Co and Zn atoms of $ZnCo_{2}O_{4}$, possess strong adsorption energies for $CO_{2}$, Na, and $Na_{2}CO_{3}$ and could act as the catalytically active sites for the $Na-CO_{2}$ batteries. Considering the DOS calculation results, the exposed Co atoms on these three surfaces are the true catalytically active sites for the $CO_{2}RR$ and $CO_{2}ER$ during the charge-discharge of the $Na-CO_{2}$ battery.
