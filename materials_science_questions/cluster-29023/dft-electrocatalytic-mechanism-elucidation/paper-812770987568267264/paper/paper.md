# Powerful CO₂ electroreduction performance with N-carbon doped by Ni single atoms

Cheng-Zong Yuan,ⁿ Kuang Liang,ⁿ Xian-Ming Xia,ᵇ Zheng Kun Yang,ⁿ Yi-Fan Jiang,ⁿ Tan Zhao,ⁿ Cong Lin,ⁿ Tuck-Yun Cheang,*^c* Sheng-Liang Zhong*^b* and An-Wu Xu*^a*

Electrocatalytic carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO₂RR) under ambient conditions provides an intriguing blueprint for the conversion of greenhouse gas into valuable chemicals or fuels. However, the CO₂RR mainly relies on high-efficient and low-cost catalysts, and it remains a grand challenge in enabling effective CO₂ reduction on N-doped carbon (N-C) catalysts. Here we report a single-atom dispersed Ni doping strategy to boost the performance of N-C materials for CO₂RR. The N-C nanosheets doped by Ni single atoms catalyst (Ni-N-C) derived from a novel metal-organic complex precursor exhibits significantly enhanced performances for CO₂ conversion in comparison with pure N-C sample, presenting a high CO FE of ~ 91.2 % and a 2.15-fold enhancement of current density at - 0.9 V relative to N-C. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations combined with experimental results reveal that the single-atom dispersed Ni could alter the adsorption of intermediate onto the surface of catalyst, and further suppress the HER and boost CO₂RR performance. The long-term stability tests demonstrate the obtained Ni-N-C sample can be used for 22 h without obvious deactivation under ambient conditions. This work provides the guideline the rationally tuning the performance of cost-effective nanostructured N-C catalysts by this atomic scale metal-doping strategy.

## Introduction

Due to the increasing concentration of environmental CO₂ and decreasing storage of fossil fuels in our earth, conversion greenhouse CO₂ into potential chemicals or fuels using various strategies is becoming a more and more urgent project.¹⁻³ Recently, electrocatalytic CO₂ reduction reaction (CO₂RR) into value-added products operated at moderate temperature and ambient pressure and powered by the renewable electricity, has been considered as one of the most effective solutions to alleviate the problems of environment and energy.⁴⁻⁶ However, the CO₂ molecule is extremely stable, so the high potential is usually required to facilitate the transfer of electron from catalysts to CO₂ molecules, further achieving the efficient CO₂RR process.⁷⁻⁹ Moreover, hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) often takes place at the same operated potential range, leading to low desired product Faraday efficiency (FE) and multiple distribution of side products.¹⁰⁻¹² Therefore, developing highly active and stable catalysts for CO₂RR is very important to catalyze CO₂RR to scale up for practical application.

Since the topping work reported by Hori et al.,¹³,¹⁴ various CO₂RR catalysts have been extensively studied, such as noble metals,¹⁵,¹⁶ transition metal oxides¹⁷,¹⁸ and transition metal chalcogenides¹⁹,²⁰. Among these catalysts, Cu and Cu-based materials are unparalleled catalysts which show excellent catalytic performances for conversion of CO₂ into C₁ – C₃ hydrocarbon products.²¹⁻²⁴ However, there is still much challenging in the practical application of Cu-based catalysts for CO₂ reduction into multi-carbon products, such as high overpotential of activating CO₂ and low selectivity of expected products.²⁵⁻²⁷ Apart from that, noble metals-based material (such as Ag, Au and Pd) is active towards conversion CO₂ into CO with low applied overpotential and high desired selectivity, while these materials suffer from scarcity and high cost.²⁸,²⁹ Therefore, more efforts have to be devoted to develop more powerful low-cost catalysts for catalyzing CO₂ conversion into potential products with low overpotential and high selectivity. Recently, an alternative with great promise to these costly noble metals is nitrogen doped carbon (N-C) materials, because of their good conductivity, strong resistance to corrosion, low cost, tunable surface areas and satisfactory porous structures. In recent studies, experimental and computational results have revealed that the pyridinic type N in N-C catalysts is the active site for CO₂RR, but the content of pyridinic type N in N-C is not so high.³⁰,³¹ Therefore, improving the CO₂RR performance of N-C materials by increasing the

Here, a feasible strategy to tune the performances of the cost-efficient N-C nanosheets towards CO₂RR by single-atom dispersed Ni doping is reported. The Ni-N-C sample was synthesized via direct pyrolysis of rationally prepared Ni, Zn-bidppz coordination polymer precursor. In this synthetic process, a N-rich bridging organic ligand, 11,11'-bis(dipyrido[3,2-a:2',3'-c]phenazinyl) (bidppz), is selected and used as the source of N and C. The one-dimensional precursor [Zn(Ni)-bidppz] is prepared by the coordination of Ni²⁺ (Zn²⁺) and bidppz ligand after solvothermal treatment, which possesses innate isolated metal nodes bonded by bidppz linkers. During the pyrolysis process, organic bidppz ligand is transformed into N-C and Zn is evaporated away, leading to resultant Niᵟ⁺ species anchored into N-C skeleton by N coordination. Due to single atom dispersed Ni doping, the obtained Ni-N-C sample exhibits enhanced activities of catalyzing CO₂ into CO. DFT calculations indicate that the dopant of single-atom dispersed Ni could serve as the dominant CO₂RR sites, weaken the adsorption of H onto the surface of catalyst, hence promote CO₂ electroreduction efficiency and selectivity.

## Results and discussion

The atomically dispersed Ni doped N-C catalysts (Ni-N-C) were synthesized via direct pyrolysis of rationally designed [Zn(Ni)-bidppz] precursors. Firstly, a one-dimensional coordination polymer precursor [Zn(Ni)-bidppz] is obtained via coordination of metal ions and bidppz molecules (see experimental section). In this [Zn(Ni)-bidppz] precursor, the Ni and Zn atoms are effectively isolated by the organic ligand, hindering the agglomeration of metal species during pyrolysis. After the pyrolysis of the as-made designed Zn(Ni)-based coordination polymer precursor at 900 °C, the final Ni-N-C sample was obtained, in which Zn ions were evaporated away and Ni ions were reduced by carbonized organic ligand, leaving atomically dispersed Ni atoms doped into the N-C matrix. For comparison, the pure N-C sample was also prepared via direct pyrolysis of bidppz molecules under the same experimental conditions. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images display that the Ni-N-C sample processes the morphology of nanosheets (Fig. 1a, b), no Ni nanoparticles are detected. The obtained pure N-C shows the irregular morphology (Fig. S1). Selected-area electron-diffraction (SAED) displays a ring-like pattern (Fig. 1c), which suggests the poor crystallinity of the Ni-N-C sample. To directly observe the atomic dispersion of Ni atoms, aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images with sub-angstrom resolution were performed and shown in Fig. 1d. Notably, a good deal of small isolated bright spots in the N-C matrix are clearly detected from Fig. 1e, and the diameters are about 0.1 – 0.2 nm, confirming the single dispersed Ni atoms in Ni-N-C sample. The energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy elemental mappings of Ni-N-C exhibit the uniform distribution of Ni, N, and C elements (Fig. 1f), indicating that single dispersed Ni atoms are homogeneously doped into the entire Ni-N-C material.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were measured to further analyze the crystallographic information of Ni-N-C. As illustrated in Fig. 2a and Fig. S2, two broad diffraction peaks are detected in the range of 20 ° – 30.8 ° and 40 ° – 45.8 ° in the XRD patterns of both Ni-N-C and N-C, which are readily indexed to the (002) and (101) reflections of graphitized carbon respectively. Any peak associated with Ni or Ni-based species is not detected in Ni-N-C sample, because the Ni atoms are atomically dispersed in the N-C matrix, coinciding well with the SAED and HAADF-STEM results. Raman scattering spectrum (Fig. S3) of Ni-N-C shows two peaks located at 1349 and 1583 cm⁻¹, belonging to the D and G bands of carbon materials, respectively. The intensity ratio of D band and G band (I_D/I_G) is calculated to be 0.95, further indicating a high graphitization degree in Ni-N-C sample, which is favorable for electron transfer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum (Fig. S4) reveals the existence of Ni, N and C elements in Ni-N-C sample. High-resolution N 1s spectrum (Fig. 2b) can reveal the peaks of five types of N species, pyridinic type N at 398.5 eV, Ni-Nₓ at 399.1 eV, pyrrolic type N at 400.3 eV, graphitic type N at 401.2 eV, and oxidized N at 402.8 eV.³²⁻³⁵ As shown in Fig. S5, Ni species are not obviously observed in the Ni 2p spectrum, most likely due to the low content of Ni. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) data shows that the actual loading mass of Ni is 1.25 wt% in Ni-N-C, which is consistent with XPS result. High-resolution C 1s XPS spectrum of Ni-N-C (Fig. 2c) shows four characteristics peaks for C–C at 284.5 eV, C–N at 285.4 eV, C–O at 286.2 eV and O–C=O bond at 288.9 eV.³¹,³⁶ The detailed electronic and structural information of Ni-N-C sample was analyzed using X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). From the Ni K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 2d), the energy absorption threshold of Ni-N-C appears between that of Ni metal foil and NiO, confirming the state of positively charged Niᵟ⁺ species in Ni-N-C.³⁷,³⁸ Fourier transform-extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectrum of Ni-N-C at Ni K-edge shows only a main peak

located at around 1.44 Å, attributed to the Ni-N scattering path, and Ni-Ni bond at about 2.13 Å are not detected (Fig. 2e), further proving that N-C was doped by the atomically dispersed Ni. To explore the coordination structure of Ni centers in the Ni-N-C, EXAFS fitting was performed (Fig. 2f and Table S1). The obtained fitting curves for the first shell exhibit that Ni atoms in Ni-N-C are averagely four-coordinated with nitrogen atoms (inset in Fig. 2f). Taken together, these results clearly demonstrate the single atom Ni species were successfully doped into the N-C matrix after the pyrolysis process.

The electrocatalytic CO₂RR experiments were conducted in a two-compartment cell, and to avoid the oxidation of as-generated products, a Nafion-115 proton exchange membrane was used to separate the compartments of the cell. During the test process, continuous CO₂ with a fixed flow rate of 10 mL min⁻¹ was introduced into the cathode compartment. All gas or liquid products formed from CO₂RR were detected using gas chromatography (GC) or ¹H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR). Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) curves of Ni-N-C carried out in N₂ and CO₂-saturated 0.5 M KHCO₃ electrolyte are shown in Fig. 3a, in which a noticeable enhanced reduction current density is observed for the Ni-N-C catalyst in the CO₂-saturated electrolyte, indicating that CO₂ was reduced on the Ni-N-C electrode. Fig. 3b depicts the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves of the as-prepared Ni-N-C and N-C catalysts in CO₂-saturated 0.5 M KHCO₃ electrolyte. It is obvious that the onset potential of Ni-N-C sample for CO₂RR is - 0.566 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (vs. RHE), which is more positive than that of the pure N-C (- 0.58 V). The current density of Ni-N-C at - 0.9 V vs. RHE reaches a high value of 12.75 mA cm⁻², which is 2.15 times higher than that of N-C. The GC and ¹H NMR analyses confirm that the main CO₂RR products catalyzed over Ni-N-C and N-C catalysts are CO and H₂. The Faradaic efficiency (FE) of CO over Ni-N-C at different applied working potential is presented in Fig. 3c. At a potential of - 0.9 V vs. RHE, the FE of CO production over Ni-N-C sample reached up to 91.2 %. Moreover, the calculated total FE of CO and H₂ products is approximately 100 %, suggesting that no by-product reaction occurs on the as-prepared Ni-N-C catalyst in CO₂RR process. From Fig. 3d, we can clearly see that the FE of CO over N-C catalyst is much lower than that over Ni-N-C at the potential range of - 0.6 to - 1.1 V. For exact comparison, the controlled sample using pure Zn-bidppz as precursor was also prepared and related CO₂RR performance is shown in Fig. S6. These results demonstrate that the dopant of atomically dispersed Ni could serve as active sites and tremendously boosts the CO production and selectivity. In addition, Tafel plot for CO₂RR of Ni-N-C was also recorded to explore the dynamic behavior. Previous reports declared that Tafel slopes of 118 and 59 mV dec⁻¹ suggest the activation process and surface reaction.³⁹⁻⁴¹ Tafel slope of Ni-N-C was evaluated to be about 157 mV dec⁻¹ (Fig. 3e), which implies that the initial CO₂ activation step serves as the rate-limiting process over Ni-N-C catalyst. The related Nyquist plots of Ni-N-C and N-C catalysts are shown in Fig. S7. The charge transfer resistance (Rct) of Ni-N-C is smaller than that of N-C, suggesting a favorable Faradaic process by introducing atomically dispersed Ni. Furthermore, Ni-N-C material displays a good long-term durability for 22 h operation, in which obvious current density decay is not observed (Fig. 3f). HAADF-STEM image of Ni-N-C catalyst after electrocatalysis was performed (Fig. S8), further proving its excellent stability for CO₂RR. The striking activity and stability clearly suggest that the prepared Ni-N-C is a promising catalyst of practical CO₂RR application.

To get a deeper insight into the effect of the single atomic Ni dopant on promoted CO₂RR activity with Ni-N-C catalyst, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed. The configurations of N-C and Ni-N-C were firstly optimized, and the resulting structures are shown in Fig. S9. Fig. 4a displays the theoretical simulation of the CO₂ reduction process over Ni-N-C catalyst. Fig. 4b shows the free-energy diagrams for CO₂RR of N-C and Ni-N-C. For Ni-N-C catalyst, the activation step of CO₂ molecule to generate adsorbed *COOH species held the highest energy barrier, suggesting this is the rate-limiting step, which is in line with the Tafel result (Fig. 3e). In contrast, the highest free energy barrier of N-C catalyst is protonating *COOH to generate adsorbed *CO species. As we all known, HER involved H* as the adsorbed intermediate may also occur during the CO₂RR process. Consequently we compared the Gibbs free energy changes for proton reduction reaction on N-C and Ni-N-C catalysts. The free energy diagram of HER (Fig. 4c) suggests that the Ni could reduce the adsorption of *H on the N-C, which could suppress the HER and facilitate adsorption of *COOH and *CO for CO₂RR. Study has shown that the difference between the limiting potential for CO₂RR (UL_CO₂) and HER (UL_H₂) is chosen as a reasonable criterion of CO₂RR selectivity, and more positive UL_CO₂ − UL_H₂ contributes to a higher selectivity of CO₂RR.⁴² As shown in Fig. S10, Ni-N-C exhibits more positive UL_CO₂ − UL_H₂ in comparison with pure N-C sample, which is in agreement with the experimental results (Fig. 3c). It is reasonable to consider that the dopant of single-atom dispersed Ni could act as the dominant CO₂RR sites, weaken the adsorption of H on the surface of catalyst, facilitate the formation of *COOH and *CO, hence suppress the HER and improve the CO₂RR performance.

## Conclusions

In summary, a single-atom Ni doping strategy has been developed to tailor the performance of N-C material for CO₂RR. The obtained Ni-N-C catalyst displays enhanced CO₂RR activity as compared to N-C, exhibiting a high CO FE of ~ 91.2 % and a 2.15-fold enhancement of current density at − 0.9 V vs. RHE. The DFT calculations in combination with experimental results confirm that Ni single atoms in Ni-N-C could act as the CO₂RR active sites, facilitate formation of *COOH and *CO, and finally suppress HER and promote CO₂ electroreduction efficiency and selectivity. More importantly, the long-term stability tests over 22 h without obvious deactivation demonstrate its high potential in practical application of obtained Ni-N-C catalyst. Our work provides a guideline for rationally tuning the CO₂RR performance with low-cost N-C materials by the atomic-scale metal doping strategy.

## Experimental
### Materials
11,11'-bis(dipyrido[3,2-a:2',3'-c]phenazinyl) (bidppz) was bought from Jinan Henghua Sci. & Tec. Co., Ltd. Nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate ($\text{Ni(NO}_3\text{)}_2\text{•6H}_2\text{O}$), Zinc nitrate hexahydrate ($\text{Zn(NO}_3\text{)}_2\text{•6H}_2\text{O}$) and potassium bicarbonate

$(KHCO_3)$ were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used without further purification.

### Preparation of N-C and Ni-N-C catalysts
To synthesize precursor, 0.4 mmol bidppz was added into 70 mL DMF and sonicated to form a homogenous solution, then, 0.36 mmol $Zn(NO_3)_2•6H_2O$ and 0.04 mmol $Ni(NO_3)_2•6H_2O$ were added under vigorous stirring. After stirring for 10 min, the mixture was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless autoclave and heated at 120 ºC for 18 h. The brown precursor was collected by centrifugation, washing with ethanol for several times and dried in vacuum at 60 ºC overnight. Then the as-made precursor was heated to 900 ºC for 3 h at a heating rate of 5 ºC/min under $N_2$ atmosphere. The pyrolyzed product was leached in 3 M hydrochloride (HCl) for 12 h to remove inactive metal species to obtain final Ni-N-C sample. For comparison, the pure N-C was also prepared via direct pyrolysis of the bidppz molecules at the same temperature.

### Characterization
Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the products were measured using a Rigaku/Max-3A X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation ($\lambda$ = 1.54178 Å). Raman scattering spectra were recorded with a Renishaw System 2000 spectrometer using the 514.5 nm line of Ar+ for excitation. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-6701F, JEOL) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM images were taken on a JEM 2010 with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular darkfield scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) measurements were taken on a JEM-ARM200F instrument (University of Science and Technology of China) at 200 keV. The content of Ni was quantified by an Optima 7300 DV inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) experiments were performed at the photoemission endstation in the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL, Hefei, China). The Ni K-edge XAS data were taken at the beamline 1W1B of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF, Beijing) in the fluorescence mode using a Lytle detector.

### Electrochemical measurements
Electrochemical $CO_2$RR performances were measured on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation in a H-cell system separated by Nafion 115 membrane. The Pt sheet and the saturated Ag/AgCl electrode were employed as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The Toray carbon papers coated with the obtained catalysts layer were applied as the working electrodes. All potentials in this work were measured against the Ag/AgCl reference electrode and converted to the RHE reference scale through Nernst equation $E_{RHE} = E_{Ag/AgCl} + 0.0591 × pH + 0.197$ V. $CO_2$RR measurements were performed in $CO_2$-saturated 0.5 M $KHCO_3$ electrolyte at room temperature and atmospheric pressure under continuous stirring at 150 rmp. Before test, $KHCO_3$ electrolyte solution was saturated by $CO_2$ for at least 30 min to remove residual air in the reactor. LSV curves of the N-C and Ni-N-C catalysts were taken in $N_2$-saturated and $CO_2$-saturated 0.5 M $KHCO_3$ at the scan rate of 10 mV s⁻¹ and the controlled potential electrolysis was performed in the potential range from - 1.0 V to - 1.6 V for 40 min at each potential. The gas products of $CO_2$ reduction were monitored by an online micro gas chromatography (GC) (Shimadzu GC-2014) equipped with a TCD detector and Molsieve 5A column. The liquid products were analyzed on a Varian 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. The working electrodes were prepared as follows: 5 mg of obtained catalysts were dispersed into 1 mL of ethanol solvent containing 25 μL of 5 wt % Nafion under sonicating for 30 min to form a homogeneous ink. Then 100 μL of the catalyst ink was loaded onto a carbon paper (1 x 1 cm² area, loading amount: ~ 0.5 mg cm⁻²).

### Density functional theory (DFT) calculations
DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP),⁴³ with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method.⁴⁴ For the electron exchange-correlation energy, we employed the generalized gradient approximation corrected Perdew-Burke-Ernzerh of (GGA-PBE)⁴⁵ functional within the Van der Waals (vdW) correction developed by Grimme (DFT+D3).⁴⁶ The cut-off energies for plane waves were 500 eV. All structures were optimized with a convergence criterion of $1×10^{-5}$ eV for the energy and 0.02 eV/Å for the forces. The Monkhorst-Pack k-mesh of 4×3×1 was adopted for all systems and the spin polarization scheme was adopted for all calculations. The models were calculated in a periodically repeated slab with the vacuum layer of 20 Å to avoid the layer-layer interactions. The single nickel atom was bonded to four pyridinic-N atoms. The free energies of the intermediates at 298.15 K was obtained by $\Delta G = \Delta E + \Delta ZPE - T\Delta S$, according to Rossmeisl's method,⁴⁷ where the $\Delta E$ is the binding energy of adsorption species by DFT, $\Delta ZPE$ and $\Delta S$ are the zero point energy (ZPE) changes and entropy changes, respectively.
