## Abstract
Molecular components with their inherent scalability are expected to be promising supplements for nanoscale electronic devices. Here we report on how to specifically tune the electronic structure of chemisorbed molecules and thus to gain control of molecular transport properties. The electronic structure of our prototype $\pi$-conjugated carboxylic acid anchored on the Cu(110) surface is modified systematically by inserting nitrogen atoms in a six-membered aromatic ring, a carboxylic functional group at the aromatic ring or both. Depending on the specific nature of the substituent, the relative position of the occupied or unoccupied electronic states with respect to the Fermi level can be specifically controlled and thus the transport properties of the studied molecular systems are modified intentionally, as proven by our scanning tunneling spectroscopy measurements. On the basis of the insight gained by our systematic experiment and first-principles calculations we are also able to predict the specific molecular character ($\sigma$ or $\pi$) of the orbitals involved in the transport process of a carboxylate–Cu(110) system, depending on the functionalization pattern employed.

## 1. Introduction
The possibility to develop electronic devices built on organic molecules was suggested for the first time by Aviram and Ratner who theoretically devised a single molecule rectifier [1]. Nowadays, the emerging field of molecular electronics [2, 3] focuses on the possibility to use organic molecules adsorbed on surfaces as basic functional units to construct technologically relevant electronic devices such as single-molecule diodes [4–7], organic field-effect transistors [8–10] or ultra-high-density memory circuits [11]. From the technological point of view, a crucial requirement to obtain a functional molecular electronic device is to precisely control the alignment of the molecular orbitals with respect to the Fermi level of the substrate since this process determines the barrier height for electron or hole injection [12–14].

In light of these considerations, a very appealing feature of molecular devices is the prospect of tuning their physical properties at the atomic scale by a chemical functionalization of the organic molecules adsorbed on a metal surface. A key aspect of this approach is that the electronic transport properties through the combined molecule–metal system are modified by changing the functional groups of the molecule. The basic role of the functional groups is to enable a chemical bond to surface and to selectively modify the geometry and electronic properties of the adsorbate–substrate systems [15]. In this context, experimental and theoretical surface structure studies, aimed at investigating the functionalization of the Cu(110) surface by adsorption of organic molecules, have been recently performed for several carboxylic acids like formic [16, 17], acetic [18, 17], oxalic [19], benzoic [20–22] and terephthalic [23–25] ones. Nevertheless, the correlation between the electronic structure of carboxylic acids chemisorbed on the Cu(110) surface and the transport properties through the corresponding molecule–surface interface is theoretically as well as experimentally less explored in the field of molecular electronics as compared with other adsorbate–substrate systems like thiolates on gold surfaces [13, 26, 27].

In particular, we had chosen nitrogen to functionalize carboxylic molecules since its insertion in the aromatic ring modifies the energetic order of the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ MOs of the resulting molecules in the gas phase and their alignment with respect to the Fermi level when adsorbed on a surface [28]. Finally, on the basis of the physical insight gained from the comparison of the theoretical and experimental data, we predict the nature ($\sigma$ or $\pi$) and the alignment of the frontier molecular orbitals for a specific molecule-$Cu(110)$ system, namely for the (dehydrogenated) pyrazine-2,5-dicarboxylic acid ($C_6H_3N_2O_4$) chemisorbed on this substrate. In order to guide the experiments, we first performed a systematic theoretical study of several carboxylate-type molecules adsorbed on the Cu(110) surface. Since in a previous paper [22] we have shown that the electronic properties of chemisorbed carboxylate molecules are practically independent of coverage, we decided to use in our calculations a single carboxylate molecule adsorbed on the metal surface. Therefore each carboxylate molecule chemisorbed on the Cu(110) substrate was modeled by a periodic slab geometry using the theoretical lattice parameter of the bulk Cu (3.64 Å). In each supercell the slab consists of five atomic layers separated by a vacuum region of $\approx 23$ Å and the carboxylate molecule was adsorbed on one side of the Cu(110) slab. The size of the in-plane surface unit cell was set to $4 \times 5$. For this geometrical set-up, the Kohn–Sham orbitals were expanded over a plane-wave basis set which includes all plane waves up to a cutoff energy $E_{\text{cut}}$ of 500 eV. The Brillouin zone integrations were carried out using the $\Gamma$-point. The geometry of the molecule–Cu(110) system was optimized by relaxing the atomic positions of all molecule atoms and those in two surface layers. The equilibrium geometry of the molecule–Cu(110) surface was obtained when the calculated forces were smaller than $\approx 0.005$ eV Å⁻¹. Finally, our first-principles calculations were dipole-corrected by using a dipole sheet in the middle of the vacuum slab [36].

### 2.2. Experimental methods

From the experimental point of view, in the present study we report on current–voltage ($I/V$) spectroscopy measurements of several of the above-mentioned aromatic carboxylates chemically adsorbed in upright configurations on a single crystal Cu(110) surface obtained by ultra-high vacuum scanning tunneling microscopy (UHV-STS). The molecules on which we report here are BCA, ortho-PyCA, TPAH and PyDCAH. For measurements a JEOL 4500 UHV-STM with homemade electrochemically etched tungsten tips was used at room temperature. The Cu(110) single crystals were cleaned by repeated cycles of Ar⁺ sputtering (1 keV) and annealing to 800 K in the UHV system. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed to prove the cleanliness of the metallic surface and low energy electron diffraction (LEED) measurements were performed to control the [110] orientation of the copper surface. The chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich with a guaranteed purity of $\geqslant 98\%$ and were used for evaporation after further purification in vacuum by freezing–thawing cycles. The powder temperature during sublimation varied from room temperature to 356 K while the substrate was kept at around 400 K. The pressure during deposition was between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-6}$ mbar.

Topographic imaging was performed before and after every series of spectroscopic measurements to make sure that the spectroscopy is done on molecules in an ordered, defect-free structure. An example of a high resolution STM topography scan is shown in figure 2(a) for BCA. The experimental procedure used to perform $I/V$ measurements is as follows: after the feedback loop had been turned off, the bias voltage was swept from $-3.0$ to $+3.0$ V (or in a larger range), while recording the resulting current at steps of 0.01 V. In the next step, after each voltage sweep the feedback loop was turned on again and the tip height was readjusted, before the next voltage sweep was started. This procedure was repeated several times and with different tips and also for positive and negative set point sample bias voltage. In this way an unambiguous assignment of the $I/V$ curves to the electronic structure of the molecule–Cu(110) system is possible. The spectroscopy data shown here were averaged over ten curves

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Chemical functionalization of the Cu(110) surface

We first note that the aromatic molecules which incorporate in their structure a carboxylic functional group like those investigated in our study will chemisorb on a Cu(110) surface upon a dehydrogenation process and they will anchor on this substrate via the resulting carboxylate group with the oxygen atoms on top of the surface copper ones [25, 37, 22]. To be more specific, in the present work we have investigated the benzoic acid ($C_{7}H_{6}O_{2}$, symbolized as BCAH) that will dehydrogenate on Cu(110) yielding benzoate ($C_{7}H_{5}O_{2}$, hereafter denoted as BCA). Similarly, the pyridinecarboxylic acid ($C_{6}H_{5}NO_{2}$ or PyCAH) will become on the copper surface $C_{6}H_{4}NO_{2}$ (hereafter denoted as PyCA) and the terephthalic acid ($C_{8}H_{6}O_{4}$ or $TPAH_{2}$) becomes terephthalate ($C_{8}H_{5}O_{4}$, hereafter denoted as TPAH). Finally, the alternative notations used in this paper for the dehydrogenated pyridine-2,5-dicarboxylic acid ($C_{7}H_{5}NO_{4}$ or $PyDCAH_{2}$) and pyrazine-2,5-dicarboxylic acid ($C_{6}H_{4}N_{2}O_{4}$ or $PzDCAH_{2}$) are PyDCAH and PzDCAH, respectively.

Then, as depicted in figure 1, we conceived of two possible ways to chemically functionalize a single molecule–Cu(110) interface starting from BCA chemisorbed on this surface (see figure 1(a)). One path (denoted by (1) in figure 1) is to replace a CH group in the aromatic ring by a nitrogen atom, resulting in PyCA. In this case three different conformers are possible: ortho-($C_{6}H_{4}N^{(2)}O_{2}$), meta-($C_{6}H_{4}N^{(3)}O_{2}$) and para-($C_{6}H_{4}N^{(4)}O_{2}$) PyCA (see figures 1(b)–(d)). Another scenario to functionalize the BCA–Cu(110) system (denoted by (2) in figure 1) is to substitute an H atom at the aromatic ring by a carboxylic group leading to the formation of TPAH on Cu(110) (see figure 1(e)). Combining paths (1) and (2) opens up a very interesting possibility to further functionalize the TPAH–Cu(110) system by replacing a CH group through an N atom resulting in the PyDCAH–Cu(110) interface (see figure 1(f)) and a similar substitution in the latter adsorbate–surface system leads to the PzDCAH molecule on the Cu(110) surface (see figure 1(g)).

### 3.2. First-principles and experimental results

We first analyze how the electronic structure of the BCA–Cu(110) system is modified by the substitution of a CH group through an N atom (path (1) in figure 1), i.e. BCA $\rightarrow$ PyCA. We note that the calculated local density of states at the molecular site will be used to define the molecular orbitals (MOs) of the molecule adsorbed on the Cu(110) surface. Then the theoretical LDOS can be decomposed into $\sigma$-and $\pi$-like contributions, where a $\sigma$ molecular orbital represents the atomic orbitals in the molecular plane while a $\pi$ MO originates from the p-like atomic orbitals perpendicular to the molecular plane.

The presence of the $\sigma_{2}$ orbital close to the Fermi level and the shift of the $\pi_{1}$ orbital to lower binding energy is intrinsically related to the presence of the N atom in the aromatic ring as shown for pyridine ($C_{5}NH_{5}$) and pyrazine ($C_{4}N_{2}H_{4}$) molecules in the gas phase with respect to the benzene ones ($C_{6}H_{6}$) [28]. Also, by comparing the three different PyCA conformers with N atoms in ortho- , meta- and para-positions relative to the carboxylate group, it is obvious that $\sigma_{1}$ and $\sigma_{1}^{*}$ peaks remain almost at the same energetic positions while the relative positions of the $\sigma_{2}$ and $\pi_{1}$ orbitals with respect to the Fermi level change with the specific geometric position of the N atom in the aromatic ring. Note that this substitution slightly modifies the energetic position of the lowest unoccupied states (LUMOs) near the Fermi level with a $\pi$ character (denoted as $\pi_{1}^{*}$, $\pi_{2}^{*}$ and $\pi_{3}^{*}$ in figures 3(b)-(d)) at the molecular site. More specifically, these states are shifted towards lower energies.

To get a deeper understanding of this chemical functionalization process that can, according to the results presented in this study, be identified by the appearance and the energetic shift of the molecular orbitals in the carboxylate-Cu(110) systems under consideration, we compare our theoretical LDOS with the differential conductance ($dI/dV$) curves obtained from the experimental current-voltage ($I/V$) spectroscopy data. In contrast to organic molecules physisorbed on a metal surface like $C_{60}$ on Au(111) [38], it is important to note that such a comparison is a challenge in our study since the carboxylate molecules are chemisorbed on the Cu(110) surface which leads to mixed molecule-surface states with the molecular orbitals of the gas phase molecules strongly hybridized with the substrate electronic states. Therefore, to correlate our measured $dI/dV$ spectra with the theoretical LDOS, in the following we define the experimental HOMO$^{\text{exp}}$-LUMO$^{\text{exp}}$ gap of the chemisorbed molecule on surface as the difference between the first large peaks in the occupied and unoccupied parts of the experimental $dI/dV$ spectrum with a significant electron density at the molecule-vacuum interface. It is important to stress that our definition of the HOMO$^{\text{exp}}$-LUMO$^{\text{exp}}$ gap does not correspond to that of the fundamental (quasiparticle) HOMO-LUMO gap (see, for instance, [41] and references therein). Note also that, although the precise DFT electronic structure of a molecule-surface system is altered by the self-interaction error, the general trend of the LDOS changes induced by using different molecular substituents at the same molecular core is, at least qualitatively, correctly captured by ab initio DFT calculations as shown, for instance, in [42-45].

As depicted in figure 4 for the PyCA-Cu(110) system, this definition implies that, from the experimental point of view, the HOMO$^{\text{exp}}$ and LUMO$^{\text{exp}}$ will not be assigned to the $\sigma_{1}$ and $\sigma_{1}^{*}$ orbitals but to $\sigma_{2}$ ($\pi_{1}$ for BCA) and $\pi_{1}^{*}$ ones. The rationale behind this operational definition is that the orbitals with a spatial distribution only at the molecule-metal interface like $\sigma_{1}$ and $\sigma_{1}^{*}$ will not be probed by $I/V$ spectroscopy when the STS measurements are performed at normal set points [39]. This observation is also supported by the study of Hu et al [40] who suggested that some molecule-surface hybrid states are difficult to be probed by a W tip since the frontier orbitals in the W tip are spatially localized on the tip. Therefore, the highest $dI/dV$ peaks correspond to the MOs which dominate quantitatively the transport properties of the tip-vacuum-molecule-metal surface system.

In this case, the main difference in the electronic structure with respect to the ground state geometry is a sizable shift of the $\sigma_3$ orbital closer to the Fermi level while the $\pi_1$ peak remains almost unaffected (compare figures 5(c) and (d)).

### 3.3. Outline

To summarize, by analyzing the theoretical LDOS in conjunction with the experimental $dI/dV$ spectra, we have identified three main mechanisms that lead to different electronic and transport properties of a carboxylate–metal surface interface: (1) to employ several different functional groups with respect to a starting carboxylate–surface system, (2) to change the geometric position of a functional group in the host molecule (i.e. to change the configuration of the molecule) and (3) to modify the position of an atom, like H, in a carboxylic (COOH) functional group (i.e. to modify the conformation of the molecule). To be more specific, our systematic *ab initio* and experimental study aimed to selectively tune the electronic properties of several carboxylates chemisorbed on the Cu(110) surface leads to the

following physical picture: starting from the BCA-Cu(110) system, by replacing a CH group through an N atom in the aromatic ring the nature of the HOMO$^\text{exp}$ is modified from $\pi$ to $\sigma$ which also leads to a different value of the HOMO$^\text{exp}$-LUMO$^\text{exp}$ gap of the resulting PyCA-Cu(110) system with respect to the initial one. A further finer tuning of this HOMO$^\text{exp}$-LUMO$^\text{exp}$ gap is possible by varying the position of the N atom in the aromatic ring (i.e. by changing the configuration of the molecule). On the other hand, by replacing an H atom of the CH group through a carboxylic (COOH) one at the aromatic ring, besides changing the nature of the HOMO$^\text{exp}$ from $\pi$ to $\sigma$ as in the previous case, the LUMOs orbitals with a $\pi$ character are shifted remarkably closer to the Fermi level. Furthermore, the specific position of the H atom in the carboxylic group COOH at the vacuum interface can also be used to modify certain features of the molecule–surface electronic structure. Overall, the selective tuning of this gap is expected to modify the transport properties of physical systems under consideration as clearly shown by the $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V$ measurements reported in our study.

On the basis of this picture, we predict that, starting from the TPAH (see figure 1(e)) and replacing two CH groups by two N atoms to obtain PzDCAH (see figure 1(g)), the HOMO$^\text{exp}$-LUMO$^\text{exp}$ gap will be further reduced by a similar effect like in the case of the PyDCAH-Cu(110) system, i.e. the occupied $\sigma_2$ and the unoccupied $\pi_1^*$ and $\pi_2^*$ peaks are moved closer to the Fermi level (see the H-down configuration in figure 5(e)). However, it is important to note that the $\pi_1^*$ and $\pi_2^*$ MOs are closer to the Fermi level for PzDCAH on the Cu(110) surface than for the PyDCAH-Cu(110) one (compare figures 5(c) and (d)). This implies that we can precisely control the shift of the molecular orbitals to the Fermi level by combining the tuning mechanisms outlined above. As previously shown for the PyDCAH-Cu(110) system, by changing the position of the H atom of the carboxylic group in the ground state, the electronic structure of a possible metastable molecule like that depicted in figure 1(g$'$) can be further modified. As presented for the H-up configuration in figure 5(f), the most notable change in this case occurs in the occupied part of the LDOS where a $\sigma_4$, $\pi_1$ and $\sigma_2$ group of MOs is moved closer to the Fermi level.

To conclude, in this study we presented systematic theoretical and experimental results which demonstrate how the electronic structure of carboxylate molecules chemisorbed on the Cu(110) surface can be intentionally tuned by inserting N atoms and/or a carboxylic group in/at the aromatic ring. In particular, our measured STS data point out the presence of specific $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V$ features depending on the detailed chemical structure of the specific carboxylate molecule chemisorbed on Cu(110). The nature ($\sigma$ or $\pi$) of these $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V$ peaks at the molecular site was identified in our first-principles simulations. Therefore our study (i) can serve as a practical recipe how to identify which specific carboxylate molecules are chemisorbed on metal surfaces and (ii) outline a practical scheme to design molecule–metal systems with specific transport properties. This gained knowledge represents a prerequisite for further advances in the direction of constructing functional molecular devices.
