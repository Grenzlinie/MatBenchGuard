# Unzipping Carbon Nanotubes at High Impact

Sehmus Ozden¹, Pedro A. S. Autreto¹,²,‡, Chandra Sekhar Tiwary¹,³‡, Suman Khatiwada¹, Leonardo Machado², Douglas S. Galvao², Robert Vajtai¹, Enrique V. Barrera¹, Pulickel M. Ajayan¹,*\

¹Department of Material Science and NanoEngineering, Rice University, Houston, Texas 77005,USA,
²Applied Physics Department, State University of Campinas, Campinas-SP, 13083-959, Brazil,
³Department of Material Engineering, Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore-560012, India

‡ These authors contributed equally to this work

*Corresponding Author: ajayan@rice.edu

Abstract: The way nanostructures behave and mechanically respond to high impact collision is a topic of intrigue. For anisotropic nanostructures, such as carbon nanotubes, this response will be complicated based on the impact geometry. Here we report the result of hypervelocity impact of nanotubes against solid targets and shows that impact produces large number of defects in the nanotubes, as well as rapid atom evaporation, leading to their unzipping along the nanotube axis. Fully atomistic reactive molecular dynamics simulations are used to gain further insights of the pathways and deformation and fracture mechanisms of nanotubes under high energy mechanical impact. Carbon nanotubes have been unzipped into graphene nanoribbons before using chemical treatments but here the instability of nanotubes against defect formation, fracture and unzipping is revealed purely through mechanical impact.

KEYWORDS: Unzipping, Carbon nanotubes, High Impact, Unzipping Mechanism,

The fracture and unzipping of carbon nanotubes have been extensively studied in recent times. In some specific cases, the axial splitting of nanotubes into a graphene nanoribbons is observed during harsh chemical treatments¹⁻⁴. Although the basis for such unusual fracture mechanism of is not properly understood, it is believed that the unzipping occurs along mechanically strained fault lines imposed by mechano-chemical interactions⁵. The deformation and fracture of individual nanostructures are indeed a complex problem and only very little work exists on this topic. Nanostructures such as carbon nanotubes are even more complicated given the anisotropic nature of the structures and their high aspect ratio. In carbon nanotubes controllably breaking of $C=C$ bonds along a specific direction is very difficult. One of the ways to induce drastic deformation and fracture in such nanostructures could be by providing high energy through impact forces. Although direct observation of impact induced fracture of such nanostructures is not feasible in most cases, analyzing the structures produced before and after the impact can be used for indirectly extracting useful information on deformation and fracture mechanisms. Here, for the first time, we use high velocity impact of nanotubes on solid targets, via hypervelocity jets, to generate localized stresses which can break $C=C$ bonds along preferred orientations resulting in the longitudinal splitting and unzipping of nanotubes. The observation is unexpected, as previous simulations have shown fragmentation of nanotubes under harsh mechanical treatments⁶,⁷.

Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) were prepared using water-assisted chemical vapor deposition (WACVD) on a Si substrate⁸. The obtained MWCNTs were packed manually as pellets and fed into a two-stage light gas guns (LGG). LGG is a specially designed setup mostly used for hypervelocity impact (HVI) experiments (Figure S1a)⁹. The impact chamber is maintained in a high vacuum. The spherically shaped CNT pellets are used as bullets to shoot at

a solid (aluminum) target, at a velocity of $\sim 6.9$ km/s ($\sim 69$ Å/ps). As a consequence of the high impact the spherical CNT samples are dispersed on the target surface (Figure 1). The samples are collected from the target, characterized and contrasted against the pre-impacted CNTs. The Raman intensity ratio of D and G bands ($I_\text{D}$/$I_\text{G}$), related to the density of defects in carbon materials$^{10}$, is used to evaluate the quality (disorder) of the samples$^{10,11}$. The original $I_\text{D}$/$I_\text{G}$ value of CNTs is 0.6. This large initial value is a consequence of using water-assisted chemical vapour deposition followed by the removal of amorphous carbon, which creates CNT defects. After the hypervelocity impacts (HVI), the $I_\text{D}$/$I_\text{G}$ increased to 0.88 (Figure 2a), suggestive that this is a direct consequence of the impacts. The samples before and after HVI experiments were further characterized using transmission electron microscope (TEM), as shown in Figure 2b. The low and high magnification bright field TEM images of CNTs show the CNTs to be multi-walled tubes (MWCNTs) and present in the form of bundles. The bright field TEM images of the samples after impact are shown in Figure 2b. It clearly shows the presence of 2D graphene nanoribbons (GNRs). The inset shows selected area diffraction (SAD) from the nanoribbon. The high-resolution image clearly shows the hexagonal graphene lattice of the GNR. The low magnification images shown in the insets confirm complete unzipping of CNTs. The impacted nanotube product was also characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) as shown in Figure 2c. XPS spectra show that there is no oxygen or any other impurity on the surface of the impacted nanotubes. A XPS peak appeared at 283.49 eV, corresponds to C-C single covalent bonds, forms due to defect generation (Figure 2c). The peak around 285 eV can be attributed to C=O covalent bonds, which is also present in the pristine CNTs.

Our results show GNRs and few deformed MWCNT due to mechanical impact. The post-impact TEM image shows various structures of these (Figure 3). This might be expected,

because the pellets used in our experiments are composed mostly of non-oriented CNT bundles, which causes individual nanotubes to reach the substrate target at different angles. In order to obtain high time resolution event features of the impact process and to understand the role of the impact angle on the final nanoribbon morphology, fully atomistic reactive molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed. In these simulations, single and MWCNTs are collided at 6.0 km/s with a solid target using three demonstrative angles: $0^\circ$ (lateral), $45^\circ$ (diagonal) and $90^\circ$ (normal). Typical simulation results are presented in Figure 3. We observed that impact angles of $90^o$ resulted in tubes deformed along the radial direction, angles of $45^o$ resulted in partially unzipped CNTs and angles of $0^o$ resulted in completely unzipped nanotubes. In all cases we observed the formation of graphene and tube defects, in agreement with the TEM data. From TEM analysis of three set of samples we estimated a 70-80% unzipping efficiency.

To gain insight on the deformation and unzipping mechanisms, we analyzed the dynamics of a lateral impact that resulted in a MWCNT fully converted into nanoribbons. Snapshots of the process, with scale-color von Mises stress values, are presented in Figure 4. At the beginning of the collision processes, the outer CNT wall tends to fold in, which is opposed by the inner wall. As a consequence, high stress is accumulated in the wall regions closer to the target surface, resulting in CNT mechanical (fracture) failure (Figure 4a). The part of the walls immediately close to the substrate continues to be pushed against the collapsing inner wall (Figure 4b), which leads to the flattening of the bottom of the CNT. Subsequently, when the nanotube midsections collide with the target, the outer wall atoms are pushed upwards without inner wall opposition (see arrows in Figure 4c), resulting in the CNT unzipping (Figure 4d). A snapshot of the process and is better visualized in supplementary movie. Considering that the same amount of kinetic energy is being transmitted into stress energy during impact in all cases.

The breaking or unzipping of CNT will depend mainly on two main factors in our simulations: (i) how this stress energy is accumulated and dissipated, and; (ii) the mechanical CNT response, which is impact angle dependent. To evaluate the distribution of the stress, we analyzed the fraction of atoms that were highly stressed and the average stress for these atoms. We considered atoms to be highly stressed when their von Mises stress values were above the fracture strength of carbon nanotubes $(\sim 100\ \text{GPa})^{12,13}$. Since these were the atoms on the impact region, restricting the averaging estimations to them, provide information about the stress levels in this particular region. For lateral impact, the collision involves many atoms (Figure 4e), leading a high fraction of atoms $(\sim 35\%)$ to become stressed in a small amount of time $(\sim 200\ \text{fs})$. Because there were many atoms able to dissipate the stress energy, the average stress level on the collision region is not high (Figure 4e), few atoms were ejected (Figure 4f), and it was possible to obtain an almost perfect nanoribbon in this case. For the case of a frontal collision, the opposite happens: few atoms were involved in the impact, they became highly stressed (Figure 4e), and a large fraction of them $(\sim 12\%)$ ended up ejected from the nanotube. For the most part, the CNT region that collided with the substrate broke. For the diagonal impact case, the number of atoms that collided with the substrate initially was even smaller than for the frontal case (Figure 4e). However, although the stress dissipation was similar to the frontal case (Figure 4e), the mass loss distribution is similar to the one obtained for the lateral case (Figure 4f). To understand this, note that a CNT is more easily deformable along the radial direction than along the axial one$^{14}$. Because of this fewer atoms were ejected in the angular case than in the frontal case, only partial unzipping was observed.

Since the dynamics of the MWCNT unzipping is dependent on the number of layers in the tubes, quite different mechanism might be expected for single-walled carbon nanotubes

(SWCNT). This is true, as can be seen in the MD simulation snapshots for the SWCNT unzipping, presented in Figure 5. We have used again color-coded scale to indicate the high von Mises stress regions. For this case, bottom wall was simply pushed upwards unopposed after the impact with the target (Figure 5a), and the nanotube folds in. Afterwards, the atoms that were at the bottom of the CNT kept moving upward (Figure 5b), until they collided with the atoms at the top (Figure 5c), breaking the $C=C$ bonds there. For SWCNTs, the unzipping starts from the center of the top wall (Figure 5c). A better understanding of these processes can be obtained from the video (S1) in the supplementary materials provided.

The ejected (lost) mass that occurs during the CNT unzipping processes generates a significant number of structural defects. In Figure 5f we present images from theoretical simulation and from (HR-TEM). As seen in the Figure 5f, the obtained structures from the simulations are quite consistent the experimentally data. Figure 5g shows defects that were formed during the peta-seconds (ps) that lead to GNR formation. The kinetic energy imparted to the CNTs is transferred to the carbon atoms during impact. This energy is larger than the threshold value for breaking the chemical bonds and giving rise to reconstructed pentagon-heptagon defects$^{15-17}$.

In conclusion, the combined effort of high velocity impact experiments with detailed atomistic MD simulations has revealed the deformation mechanisms of unzipping CNT under mechanical collisions. The unzip effectiveness is highly dependent on tube impact angle with respect to the target. This is a new and completely general (that can work even for small diameter SWCNTs) way of producing graphene nanoribbons without chemical contaminants.

This is a new method to produce in one-step, chemical-free, clean and high quality GNRs. In this the CNT unzipping is the result of fast process, which allows stress concentration in specific CNT regions, for favourable impact angle values. This induced mechanical high strain favours the crack propagation along the main tube axis²⁰, thus resulting in the unzipped structures. When these conditions are not satisfied, CNT cuttings are favoured, as illustrated in Figure 3.

The current work provides new understanding of the behavior of nanostructures and their mechanical failure under high impact.

### Methods

Preparation of carbon nanotubes: MWCNTs were prepared by using water-assisted chemical vapor deposition (WACVD) on a Si substrate⁸. . A Si wafer, on which 10 nm aluminum layer and 1.5 nm iron catalyst were deposited via electron beam deposition, was put in quartz tube and heated to 775 °C in a buffer $Ar/H_2$ ($15\%H_2$) mixture. Then ethylene gas as the carbon source and water vapor was introduced into quartz tube for 3 hours. Produced MWCNTs were shaped as a sphere and fed in a two stage light gas gun (LGG). LGG is a special designed setup mostly used for hypervelocity impact (HVI) experiments (Figure S1a)⁹. The tube is maintained in vacuum and the spherical CNT is used as a bullet and shot at the target at a measured velocity of 6.9km/s.

Renishaw Raman spectroscopy (633nm laser) was used for Raman characterization. PHI Quantera XPS was used for surface analysis and determine elemental ratio of the material. Field emission gun transmission electron microscope (JEOL 2100) was used to capture the morphology of the material.

The molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out using the NTV ensemble at 300K controlled by the Nose-Hoover thermostat as implemented in LAMMPS¹⁸. For the SWNT simulations we used a 10 nm long (10,10) CNT, while for the DWNT simulations we used a 10 nm long (10,10) CNT as the inner wall and a 10 nm long (15,15) CNT as the outer wall. All simulations used the fully atomistic reactive force field ReaxFF¹⁹. The used shooting velocities are close to the experimental values, ~ 6.0 Km/s, and was set up through the scaling of atomic velocities along the impacting/target direction. We have used time-steps of 0.025 fs. The stress was calculated by computing the forces on each atom, then obtaining the stress tensor. From this tensor, we calculated a quantity known as von Mises stress, the second invariant of the deviatoric

stress tensor. The von Mises yield criterion suggests that yield starts when this stress reaches the yield strength of the material. By calculating the von Mises stress for each atom at every 100 time-steps, we were able to visualize the time evolution of the local stresses and obtain detailed information of the fracturing process. This methodology was successfully used to investigate the mechanical failure of carbon-based nano structures, such as graphene and carbon nanotubes²⁰.
