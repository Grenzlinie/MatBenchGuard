# Role of surface chemistry in hydrogen adsorption in single-wall carbon nanotubes

Manuela Volpe $^{a,b}$, Fabrizio Cleri $^{a,*}$

$^{a}$ Ente Nuove Tecnologie, Energia e Ambiente (ENEA), Unità Materiali e Nuove Tecnologie, Centro Ricerche Casaccia, C.P. 2400, 00100 Roma A.D., Italy
$^{b}$ Dipartimento Scienze e Tecnologie Chimiche, Università 'Tor Vergata', 00133 Roma, Italy

Received 30 September 2002; in final form 7 February 2003

## Abstract
A combination of tight-binding molecular dynamics and grand-canonical-ensemble Monte Carlo is used to model adsorption of molecular hydrogen in chemically modified matrices of single-wall carbon nanotubes. We study the effects of chemical modification of the nanotube surface by including different coverages of chemisorbed atomic hydrogen and surface oxidation by removing islands of missing carbon atoms with H-saturated bonds. Chemisorbed hydrogen reduces the gas storage capabilities of carbon nanotubes, while the opening of cavities by oxidation tends to increase the mass of stored hydrogen gas in terms of both gravimetric and volumetric capacity.

The use of hydrogen as an alternative energy carrier is rapidly developing due to an increasing demand for renewable and environmentally friendly energy sources. A major candidate for using hydrogen in a power supply network is the fuel cell technology, for both automotive and stationary power plant uses. In this context hydrogen storage and transportation is a great technological concern [1,2]. The exceedingly small density (0.09 kg m⁻³) of H₂ gas under normal conditions forces to look for alternative storage and transportation technologies, such as compressed gas, liquefaction at cryogenic temperatures, solid-state metal hydride matrices (in which, however, atomic hydrogen is stored), physisorption in large-specific-surface matrices. Carbon matrices, indeed, have huge specific surface values in the range 500-2000 m²/g and are known to absorb hydrogen up to about 2 wt% and 20-30 kg(H₂) m⁻³ volumetric density, i.e., about one third of the target fixed by the US DOE Hydrogen Plan [3]. Recently, single-wall carbon nanotubes (SWNT) have also been indicated as candidates for hydrogen storage, the most reliable measurements of gas adsorption being in the range 3-4 wt% [4,5].

Molecular hydrogen is physisorbed at graphite-like surfaces at a distance of about $3 \pm 0.3$ Å with a binding energy in the range 20-80 meV [6,7]. The details of H₂ adsorption in SWNT [4,5] depend on a number of factors, among which the adsorption

Experiments have shown that the adsorption potential can be improved by interca- lation of alkali metals [8] (although these results have been recently put in question [9]) or by elec- trochemical charging [10]. Most importantly, however, the surface chemistry of nanotubes should be a major factor influencing $H_{2}$ adsorp tion. As a function of the pressure, temperature and local surface structure (e.g., curvature, defects, residual metal-catalyzer particles) $H_{2}$ can be de composed into atomic hydrogen, as $H_{2} \Leftrightarrow 2 H$ , and eventually chemisorbed. Equilibrium is de- termined by the competing processes of $H_{2}$ disso ciation, atomic H chemisorption with the formation of a surface C-H group, and H ab- straction, by either surface diffusion-collision or by impingement of H atoms from the gas phase. Chemisorption should preferentially take place at graphite defects, such as dangling bonds or nanotube open ends [11,12], vacancies [13], inter- stitial carbon loops between the graphite basal planes [14]. Moreover, such structural defects are also the preferred sites for oxidation reactions, by either $O_{2}, H_{2} O$ and $CO_{2}$ gas. In fact, oxidation at elevated temperatures is often used to obtain clean nanotube samples [15,16]: disordered amorphous carbon nanoparticles and nanotube caps, con- taining reactive pentagonal defects, are quickly removed by reactions with gaseous oxygen species by forming volatile $CO_{2}$ and CO groups, depend ing on the reaction temperature.

Theoretical studies have also contributed to elucidate the key factors leading to a possible ex- cess $H_{2}$ adsorption in SWNT matrices. $H_{2}$ physi sorption in quenched SWNT matrices has been mainly studied by Grand-Canonical ensemble Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations with empirical potentials [17-20]. Such simulations probed the relative influence of various factors on $H_{2}$ physi sorption as a function of pressure and tempera- ture, e.g., nanotube diameter [17,18], inter-tube spacing [18-20], multipolar dispersion forces and quantum effects in $H_{2}$ dynamics [19,20], excess electronic charge [20]. However, no physisorption studies did take into account the effects of surface chemistry on the nanotubes. In fact, in the above works the matrix was always assumed to be composed of perfect, defect-free cylindrical SWNTs.

In this Letter we report a theoretical study of H2 physisorption in SWNT matrices including surface chemistry in the form of both hydroge- nated and oxidized nanotube structures. We use tight-binding molecular dynamics (TBMD) to determine the equilibrium atomic structure of nanotubes, hydrogenated by the formation of surface C-H groups and/or oxidized by the re- moval of islands of C atoms of various sizes. Both kinds of surface modification entail the sub- sequent structural relaxation by TBMD of indi- vidual nanotubes and of the inter-tube distances in the matrix. $H_{2}$ adsorption in such hydrogenated/ oxidized matrices is calculated by GCMC simu- lations with generalized Lennard-Jones (L-J) in- termolecular potentials. We find that the presence of chemisorbed hydrogen generally reduces the gas-storage capability of carbon nanotubes, both as a function of tube diameter and intertube spacing. On the other hand, oxidation results in the opposite effect by increasing the amount of stored hydrogen gas, both in terms of the relative gravimetric fraction and of the absolute volu- metric capacity.

The equilibrium structure of SWNTs, either pure or with chemisorbed H at various levels of coverage and/or partly oxidized by removal of carbon islands of various sizes, was determined by tight-binding molecular dynamics (TBMD) [21]. To describe the interaction among C and H atoms we adopted an orthogonal C-C parametrization[22] and its extension to C-H [23]. The dispersive interactions between SWNTs, both clean and hy- drogenated, and between $H_{2}$ molecules were modeled by means of (12-6) L-J potentials with $\varepsilon=28.2,36.7 K$ , and $\sigma=3.40,2.958 \AA$ for C-C and $H_{2}-H_{2}$ , respectively [24]. The cross-interaction parameters between C and $H_{2}$ are derived by theusual Berthelot rules, i.e., $\sigma_{AB}=(\sigma_{AA}+\sigma_{BB}) / 2$  and $\varepsilon_{AB}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{AA} \varepsilon_{BB}}$ . Concerning the dispersive in teraction between C-H groups and $H_{2}$ molecules, we obtained an effective $H-H_{2}$ interaction in the gas phase from the theoretical curves of Hirschf- elder et al. To compute $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ adsorption we used the standard GCMC method as described, e.g., in [26]. A first series of GCMC simulations was run for the free $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ gas at constant volume, to obtain $(\mu, T)$ and $(\mu, P)$ curves to relate the chemical potential $\mu$ to the free gas density $\rho$. All the GCMC simulations below were conducted at values of $T$ and $\mu$ such that the thermal wavelength of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$, $\Lambda=h / \sqrt{2 \pi m k_{\mathrm{B}} T}$, is so small with respect to the intermolecular distance that quantum effects in $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ dynamics represent a minor correction (less than $10 \%$ for the lowest temperature studied). In our GCMC simulations, we calculate the average number, $\langle N\rangle$, and density, $\langle\rho\rangle=\langle N\rangle / V$, of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ molecules in each matrix configuration at given $\{\mu, V, T\}$, so that several measures of gas adsorption can be given. By defining $m=2.016$ amu the mass of a $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ molecule, $N_{0}$ and $\rho_{0}$ the number and density of the $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ gas at given $P$ and $T$, and $M_{\mathrm{C}}$ the mass of the carbon matrix, respectively, we have: the weight efficiency (or gravimetric capacity), $w=m\langle N\rangle /\left(m\langle N\rangle+M_{\mathrm{C}}\right)$; the volumetric capacity, $\rho_{V}=m\langle\rho\rangle$; the capacity ratio, $R=\langle\rho\rangle / \rho_{0}$. The latter corresponds to the experimentally measured 'usable capacity ratio' [3] when the discharge pressure is equal to $1 \mathrm{~atm}$.

A number of different SWNT-based matrices were prepared for the study of gas adsorption. SNWTs of the type $(n, n)$ with $n=6,9$ and $(n, 0)$ with $n=9,13,15,17,21,25$ were firstly relaxed by TBMD. The dependence of the nanotube strain (or curvature) energy as a function of the diameter with this TB model was already published [27]. We then placed SWNTs in a supercell with fixed $z$ length of $36.89 \AA$ for the $(n, n)$ and $34.08 \AA$ for the $(n, 0)$. The tube axis was parallel to the $z$ direction, and the $x y$ plane was filled with square-lattice symmetry. The lateral dimension $x y$ of the supercell was varied to obtain SWNT matrices with different carbon densities.

A series of GCMC simulations was performed at $T=293 \mathrm{~K}$ and $P=10 \mathrm{MPa}$ on matrices of perfectly cylindrical SWNTs, as a function of nanotube radius and inter-tube distance. In rather good agreement with previous studies despite the difference in the intermolecular potentials [18-20], we find a maximum density of $\mathrm{H}_{2}$ gas of about $\rho=$ $0.0031 \AA^{-3}$, corresponding to $R=1.34$, for SWNT with diameter $d=11.7 \AA$ arranged in a square lattice with a wall-to-wall inter-tube distance $D=7$ $\AA$. Apparently, such gas density is about $35 \%$ larger than the $\rho_{0}$ for the compressed gas in the same $P$ and $T$ conditions. However, when turned into $w=3.4 \mathrm{wt} \%$ and $\rho=10 \mathrm{~kg}\left(\mathrm{H}_{2}\right) \mathrm{m}^{-3}$, the same data appear much less appealing and, moreover, perfectly comparable to the results of [17]. We thus confirm that the promisingly high values of hydrogen storage appeared in the literature [4,5] cannot be explained by pure physisorption at the carbon surface. Other effects, including, e.g., the presence of residual catalyzer particles, amorphous and disordered carbon or, possibly, surface chemistry should thus be investigated.

The heat of adsorption can be estimated from the total energy of the loaded matrix. We find values comprised between -0.85 and $-1 \mathrm{kcal}$ $\mathrm{mol}^{-1}$, with only a minor dependence on the nanotube curvature and gas pressure. Such value is close to that of graphite, and smaller that the value of $-4.7 \mathrm{kcal} \mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ measured for SWNT [4], probably indicating some deficiency of the simple intermolecular potential adopted.

Besides the preferred chemisorption at graphite defects, both experiments [14] and DFT calculations [28] suggest that covalent $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{H}$ bonds can be formed also on perfect graphite sites or at threefold coordinated defects (Stone-Wales, or 5-7 rings). In such cases a 'tetragonalization' of the graphite surface takes place, the $\mathrm{C}$ atom being pulled out of the basal plane by $d=0.28 \AA$ [28] (our TBMD value is $d=0.46 \AA$ ). Atomic $\mathrm{H}$ adsorption sites in

defect-free SWNTs were previously studied by means of first-principles [29] and tight-binding simulations [10]. In both works it was found that H atoms chemisorbed at the outer surface of the nanotube form stable configurations, even at very high coverages, while H atoms chemisorbed at the inner surface are unstable and detach, forming $\text{H}_2$ molecules inside the nanotube.

For the purpose of simulating surface hydrogenation, different numbers of H atoms, corresponding to increasing coverage $c_{\text{H}}$, were placed at random on the outer surface of a relaxed, defect-free (15, 0) SWNT, with diameter $11.7$ Å, and in a few simulations also on a (6, 6) SWNT with diameter $8.4$ Å. The resulting atomic configurations were again relaxed by TBMD at $T=0$ K. The pull-out effect of a single H atom on the nanotube surface closely resembles that of graphite, however with a smaller tetragonalization of $d=0.3$ Å. Fig. 1a shows a perspective view along the $z$-axis of the atomic structure of the (6, 6) SWNT at $c_{\text{H}}=0.5$, the nanotube cylindrical structure being strongly distorted as a consequence of the combined relaxation of the C–H groups. We find that the radius of the nanotube, measured as the average of the positions of all the C atoms projected in the $z=0$ plane, increases steadily with the surface H coverage. By performing TBMD simulations at $T=293$ K we verified that such configurations are very stable in temperature, even at the highest values of coverage.

A second series of GCMC simulations was performed on matrices of (15, 0) and (6, 6) SWNTs at increasing surface coverages of chemisorbed atomic H. We assume that chemisorbed H is irreversibly stored in the SWNT; therefore, its mass is added to the matrix mass $M_{\text{C}}$. In Fig. 2 we report the gas density at $T=293$ K and $P=10$ MPa as a function of H coverage for the (15, 0) and the (6, 6) SWNT, at the intertube distance $D=7$ Å corresponding to the maximum density. For all nanotube sizes, surface hydrogenation is clearly detrimental to the $\text{H}_2$-adsorption capacity of SWNT. For the smaller diameter, the density falls below the compressed gas at $c_{\text{H}} \geqslant 0.2$, thereby reducing the cross-over pressure by a factor of three with respect to the clean surface. The above results can be quantitatively influ- enced by the simplified form of the intermolecular potential we adopted to describe the $H_{2}$-carbon interaction. In particular, the effect of surface curvature has been recently shown [30] to provide a significant increase of the adsorption energy for molecules at the outer nanotube surface (exohe- dral adsorption). However, the qualitative picture should be unchanged, since we already find exo- hedral adsorption to dominate. It is worth noting that the curvature effect was indicated also to im- prove the agreement between the calculated and experimental heat of adsorption [30], which is, indeed, underestimated by our model. Another effect which could somewhat influence our results is the deformation of the carbon matrix following $H_{2}$ intercalation, as observed in ab initio simula tions [31]. However, it should be noted that in GCMC simulations [17-20] the structure of the matrix is an input of the simulation model, and that there is no assessed procedure capable of in- cluding such dynamic effects in the MC averaging procedure.

Our results point, on one hand, to the impor- tance of using well-purified SWNT samples in ex- perimental studies of gas adsorption, since any kind of structural defect and impurity coming, e.g., from the amorphous carbon or catalyzer residualsof the SWNT synthesis process, can lead to $H_{2}$  decomposition and subsequent, detrimental sur- face hydrogenation. On the other hand, oxidation is shown to increase the adsorption capacity via the opening of a larger specific surface. An ideal configuration could thus be envisaged to maximize hydrogen storage, by assembling in the same compact, bundle-like structure half-nanotubes partially burned by oxidation or other means (e.g., electron or ion-beam irradiation), to exploit the combined effect of surface curvature and maxi- mum specific surface exposed to the gas. All such effects, however, cannot explain the anomalously high values of hydrogen storage in carbon na- notubes, claimed in earlier experimental works, on the basis of pure physisorption.
