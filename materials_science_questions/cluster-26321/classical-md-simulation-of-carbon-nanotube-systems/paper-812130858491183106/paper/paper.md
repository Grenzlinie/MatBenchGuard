# Effect of flexibility on hydrophobic behavior of nanotube water channels

Stefan Andreev
Department of Chemistry and Chemical Biology, Harvard University, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02138

David Reichman
Department of Chemistry, Columbia University, New York, New York 10027

Gerhard Hummer${}^{\mathrm{a)}}$
Laboratory of Chemical Physics, National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases,
National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland 20892

(Received 5 July 2005; accepted 12 September 2005; published online 11 November 2005)

Carbon nanotubes can serve as simple nonpolar water channels. Here we report computer simulations exploring the relationship between the mechanical properties of such channels and their interaction with water. We show that on one hand, increasing the flexibility of the carbon nanotubes increases their apparent hydrophobic character, while on the other hand the presence of water inside the channel makes them more resistant to radial collapse. We quantify the effect of increasing flexibility on the hydrophobicity of the nanotube water channel. We also show that flexibility impedes water transport across the nanotube channel by increasing the free-energy barriers to such motion. Conversely, the presence of water inside the nanotube is shown to affect the energetics of radial collapse in a water nanotube, an ostensibly mechanical property. We quantify the magnitude of the effect and show that it arises from the formation of energetically favorable low-dimensional water structures inside the nanotube such as one-dimensional wires and two-dimensional sheets.

## I. INTRODUCTION

In the past decade, a remarkable number of biological channels have been structurally characterized.${}^{1-5}$ It is interesting that a significant portion of these channels contain hydrophobic pore regions, even though they transport polar molecules or ions.${}^{6-8}$ In human tissues, for instance, the protein aquaporin-1 facilitates water transport across cell membranes through four functionally independent and relatively hydrophobic water pores.${}^{9-12}$ Similarly, significant parts of the proton access channels of the biological proton pumps cytochrome c oxidase and bacteriorhodopsin are hydrophobic.${}^{6}$ In general, the mechanisms of transport through membrane channels remain poorly understood. In proteins it is difficult to separate generic causative features of transport from specific molecular and chemical details. For these reasons, it is important to develop model systems which retain some degree of realism while maintaining the simplicity necessary to determine the functional relevance of key structural features. Here, we focus on the interplay between hydrophobicity, thermal shape fluctuations, and mechanical deformations of the channel.

Carbon nanotubes${}^{13,14}$ form structurally simple hydrophobic channels. Computer simulations${}^{15}$ and experiments${}^{16-20}$ suggest that nanotubes can be filled with water at ambient conditions. This makes them an attractive system for the study of water transport through molecular scale hydrophobic pores.${}^{15,21-26}$ Transport across carbon nanotube membranes has been studied computationally${}^{27-29}$ and experimentally.${}^{30-32}$ For the narrowest channels, simulations have shown single-file water transport. Single-file water conduction through filled nanotubes has been explained as a random walk by modeling the motion of water molecules inside the channel as collective “hopping” that occurs in either direction with equal probability.${}^{22,23}$ To study transport through partially filled nanotubes, Chou developed an asymmetric exclusion process model.${}^{33,34}$ The above models have been successfully applied to both biased and unbiased water transport through channels of various lengths.${}^{27}$ Ordered water chains were also shown to mediate fast proton transport.${}^{35,36}$

Computer simulations have indicated interesting phase like phenomena in which the pore fluctuates between water-filled and empty states. It has been shown that there are three filling regimes depending on the strength of the attractive interaction between the water molecules and the channel.${}^{8,15,21}$ For weak interactions, the nanotube stays empty. For a narrow range of intermediate interactions, there is bistable behavior, with the nanotube switching between a filled and an empty state. For strong interactions, the channel is completely filled. Similar effects were seen in other geometries and systems.${}^{37-44}$ Such filling and emptying behaviors have been connected to gating behavior in the ion channels. One recent example where emptying has been observed is the small mechanosensitive channel (MscS) which couples mechanical stress to channel opening.${}^{45,46}$ These results, as well as a recent study of methane transport through nanotubes,${}^{47}$ suggest connections between filling, transport, and geometric deformations of hydrophobic pores.

In this paper, we investigate the relationship between

rigidity and the filling behavior of carbon nanotube water channels. We show that flexibility increases the effective hydrophobicity of the nanotube. With respect to the filling/emptying transition, we find a quantitative correspondence between pore flexibility and water-pore interaction strength. Surprisingly, we also find that nanotubes resist collapse to a greater degree in water than in vacuum through the formation of one- or two-dimensional water structures. However, the presence of these water structures is highly sensitive to the strength of the water-carbon interactions and equivalently to changes in the solvent conditions. Indeed, for reduced water-carbon interactions, we observe the expected behavior of hydrophobically assisted collapse.

The paper is organized as follows. The simulation approach is discussed in Sec. II. In Sec. III A, we study the effect of flexibility on the apparent hydrophobicity of small diameter nanotubes. In Sec. III B, we discuss how the presence of water molecules inside a nanotube affects its effective mechanical properties, such as the energetics of collapse. Conclusions are presented in Sec. IV. Further simulation details can be found in the Appendix.

## II. METHODOLOGY

Our simulations were performed with the AMBER 6 molecular-dynamics program, using the AMBER parm94 force field. $^{48}$ The solvent used was TIP3P water molecules. We studied two different systems, involving a smaller diameter (6,6) and a larger diameter (10,10) carbon nanotubes solvated in water, each composed of six repeated unit cells. Each unit cell consists of a ring of carbon atoms in the armchair configuration. $^{49}$ The electrostatic interactions were handled by the particle-mesh Ewald summation method. $^{50}$

A 144-atom open-ended (6,6) nanotube segment (14.5 Å long and 8 Å in diameter) was solvated in 1034 water molecules in a periodic cubic box with edges approximately 37 Å long. The average temperature and pressure were maintained at $T{=}298$ K and $p{=}1$ atm by using an Andersen thermostat $^{51}$ and a Berendsen barostat $^{52}$ with a velocity update interval of $\tau_{\mathrm{temp}}{=}5$ ps for the temperature and a coupling constant of $\tau_{\mathrm{press}}{=}1$ ps for the pressure, respectively.

The 240-atom open-ended (10,10) carbon nanotube segment (14.5 Å long and 13.4 Å in diameter) was solvated by enough water molecules such that the nanotube would be separated from its periodic images by at least six layers of water. The number of water molecules needed to do that varied between 900 for the collapsed nanotube to 1100 for the open nanotube. The periodic box used was not always cubic. Asymmetry in the box was introduced to reflect the asymmetry of the nanotube when it was harmonically restrained in a collapsed state. The nanotube was prevented from rotating inside the water bath by making two neighboring carbon atoms near the center of the nanotube essentially infinitely heavy.

We introduce flexibility in small diameter (6,6)-type nanotubes by multiplying the angular, torsional, and nonbonding terms from the carbon-carbon interaction potential by a factor $10^{-\phi}$ ($\phi{\geqslant}0$), where $\phi$ is our flexibility parameter. Defined in this way, the parameter $\phi$ correlates well with the magnitude of the fluctuations of the internal diameter $D$ of our model (6,6) nanotube in vacuum, as shown in Fig. 1. The internal diameter is measured as the distance between two carbon atoms in the same unit cell, but sitting opposite each other in the cylindrical minimum energy configuration of the carbon nanotube.

![](./images/812130858491183106_3.jpg)

FIG. 1. Standard deviation $\delta D$ of thermal fluctuations of the internal diameter of the nanotube in vacuum as a function of the flexibility parameter $\phi$.

We measure the degree to which a nanotube is filled by water by counting the number $N_{w}$ of water molecules enclosed by the nanotube. In addition, we introduce a continuous occupancy number $N_{c}$. It is a measure of nanotube filling obtained by weighted counting of water molecules inside the nanotube. Water molecules closer to the center are weighted more relative to those near the nanotube ends in order to provide a continuous and differentiable counting function that can be used to bias the potential in our molecular-dynamics simulations. In particular, we use it as an order parameter for the potential of mean force calculation for the process of nanotube filling. As shown in Fig. 2, $N_{c}$ and $N_{w}$ are highly correlated. The formal definition of $N_{c}$ and $N_{w}$ is discussed in the Appendix.

In their study of carbon nanotubes in water, Waghe *et al.* observe transitions from complete filling to bistable behavior, and finally to complete emptying by reducing this attractive interaction. $^{21}$ The attractive term of the Lennard-Jones interaction potential is multiplied by a factor $\lambda$, so that the nonbonding interaction is given by

$$

By decreasing $\lambda$, the nanotube becomes more hydrophobic as the potential-energy gain from the interaction between the carbons of the nanotube channel and the water molecules inside is decreased. As $\lambda$ is lowered below some value $\lambda_{\text{fb}}$ a transition to bistable behavior is observed. As $\lambda$ is lowered even further below $\lambda_{\text{be}}$, the nanotube is essentially always empty. $^{15}$

We are able to reproduce the same phenomenological changes in behavior by increasing the nanotube flexibility through $\phi$ without changing the energy parameter $\lambda$. As shown in Fig. 3, for small $\phi$ the nanotube is filled, but as the flexibility is increased beyond $\phi$=2.0, the system exhibits bistable behavior and states of incomplete filling are observed more frequently. As the flexibility is increased beyond $\phi$=2.3 the nanotube is always empty.

The filled nanotube configurations get destabilized and bistable behavior occurs, leading to increased population of the empty ($N_c\lesssim0.3$; see Fig. 2) or partially filled states. Moreover, the number of water molecules inside the fully filled state of the nanotube decreases with increasing $\phi$.

The two effects we observe result from changes in the free energy of filling and emptying. In Fig. 5, the PMF for the filling of the nanotube is plotted for $\phi$=0 and $\phi$=2. The PMF for $\phi$=0 is calculated by umbrella sampling $^{54}$ with $N_c$ as the reaction coordinate, while the PMF for $\phi$=2 is calculated from a single simulation in the bistable regime. These free-energy calculations show that the filled state is destabilized relative to the empty state as the flexibility is increased, even though the potential energy of interaction between the water and the nanotube remains unchanged.

For a given type of nanotube and flexibility parameter $\phi$, we can determine the values of $\lambda_{\mathrm{fb}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{be}}$ where the equilibrium filling state changes from filled to bistable and from bistable to empty, respectively. If introducing flexibility increases the effective hydrophobicity of a channel, the values of $\lambda_{\mathrm{fb}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{be}}$ would increase accordingly. Similarly, a decrease in effective hydrophobicity would decrease the values of $\lambda_{\mathrm{fb}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{be}}$.

We have calculated a "phase" diagram $^{55}$ for a (6,6) nanotube six unit cells long, plotting the equilibrium state of the system (filled, bistable, or empty) for various values of $\lambda$ and $\phi$. The lines that separate the filled from the bistable states, and the bistable states from the empty states give the dependence of $\lambda_{\mathrm{fb}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{be}}$ on $\phi$, respectively. The resulting diagram is plotted in Fig. 6. We find that additional flexibility makes the channel effectively more hydrophobic for all values of $\lambda$, as $\lambda_{\mathrm{fb}}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{be}}$ increase monotonically with $\phi$. The detailed nature of the hydrophobicity we observe will be subject to further investigation.

The reasons increased flexibility destabilizes the filled state are both energetic and entropic. Increasing the flexibility restricts the available free volume for water molecules inside the nanotube, which has two destabilizing effects. One is that the zigzag configurations that the single-file water chain can adopt in the stiff nanotube get straightened on average and forced into an energetically less favorable configuration. The second destabilizing effect of increasing the flexibility comes from the nanotube itself. When empty, a flexible nanotube is subject to much larger shape deformations in order to populate its low-frequency modes than a rigid nanotube. The presence of a chain of water inside such a flexible nanotube sterically restricts the accessible nanotube configurations relative to the empty state. Since a rigid nanotube does not vibrate with a large amplitude, the presence of water inside the nanotube would have a much smaller effect on the accessible nanotube configurations. Consequently, the suppression of vibrational modes will have a stronger effect on the filling of a more flexible nanotube.

In Ref. 22 it is shown that a water molecule moving through a filled nanotube encounters a series of shallow free-energy wells spaced at about $2.6\ \mathring{\mathrm{A}}$. A similar pattern of small free-energy wells is observed for $\mathrm{K}^{+}$-ion conduction along the length of the gramicidin channel. $^{5}$ The low-energy barriers between neighboring wells allow the water molecules of single-file chain inside the filled nanotube to "hop" between wells in a concerted fashion $^{56}$ with some characteristic time constant $\tau$.

According to the hopping model, $^{22}$ the number of exit events is proportional to the hopping rate $k=1/\tau$ where $\tau$ is the mean time between two consecutive hopping events. The translocation time is given by $t_{\text{tr}}=k^{-1}\langle N_{w}\rangle(\langle N_{w}\rangle+2)/3$. Using this relationship, we can test the values in Table I for consistency. We find that a reduced hopping rate in the flexible tube, as inferred from the drop in $N_{\text{exit}}$, is compensated in part by a reduction in the number of water molecules $\langle N_{w}\rangle$ in the tube. Correspondingly, the observed ratio of translocation times $t_{\text{tr}}^{\phi=1.3}/t_{\text{tr}}^{\phi=0}=1.08$ is approximately equal to

$$
\frac{N_{\text{exit}}^{\phi=0}}{N_{\text{exit}}^{\phi=1.3}} \frac{N_{w}^{\phi=1.3}(N_{w}^{\phi=1.3}+2)}{N_{w}^{\phi=0}(N_{w}^{\phi=0}+2)}=1.12, \tag{3}

The decrease in the hopping rate is also qualitatively consistent with the somewhat higher free-energy barrier for motion of the "water wire" along the tube axis, as shown in Fig. 7. Quantitatively, however, the observed increase in the barrier cannot fully account for the slowdown. Using the proportionality of $N_{\text{exit}}$ to the reciprocal of the hopping time, we estimate from the values of $N_{\text{exit}}$ in Table I that the hopping time increased by about a factor 1.24 in the flexible tube. By using the formalism of Lifson and Jackson for diffusive dynamics on a periodic free-energy surface, $^{58}$ we estimate that the hopping time between adjacent minima on a sinusoidal free-energy surface at a distance $L$ should depend on the barrier height $\Delta G^{\ddagger}$ as $\tau=(2D)^{-1}L^{2}[I_{0}(\beta\Delta G^{\ddagger}/2)]^{2}$, where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient and $I_{0}$ is the modified Bessel function of order 0. With an effective diffusion coefficient along the nanotube close to that of bulk TIP3P water, this relation gives hopping times in good agreement with those seen in the simulations. $^{22}$ If we define $L$ to be the length of the nanotube divided by the average number of water molecules inside $(N_{w})$, the Lifson-Jackson relation would predict a slowdown by a factor 1.16 for the barrier increase from 0.2 to $0.5\ k_{B}T$ seen in Fig. 7. This slight underestimate of 1.16 versus 1.24 could reflect statistical uncertainties as well as simplifications in the model, but it may also suggest that the reaction coordinate determining hopping is not simply the location of the water wire along the tube axis, but includes additional solvent degrees, such as solvent motions at the tube openings. $^{23}$

The mechanism by which increased flexibility translates into higher hopping barriers is unclear, although it might have to do with the different water environments at the ends of the nanotube. Figure 4 shows that there is a higher local water density near the ends of the $\phi$=0 nanotube than in the $\phi$=1.3 tube. Such structured water at the openings would suppress excursions of the single-file water chain in and out of the tube, and thus lower the frequency of "hopping attempts." There is some evidence of increased water structure near the exits in Fig. 4, although we could not ascertain the nature of these structures by examining the trajectories.

## B. Filling and emptying transitions in larger nanotubes

In this section, we show that the presence of water inside a (10,10) nanotube increases the energy cost for the radial collapse of the nanotube. For sufficiently large diameters, single-walled nanotubes can remain in a collapsed state, especially if they are stabilized by the presence of a surface. $^{59}$ The transition from collapsed to open configurations has been studied before for carbon nanotubes in vacuum. $^{60,61}$ In this section, we investigate the effect of the presence of water on the collapse of nanotubes whose diameter is large enough to provide space for more than a single file of water molecules. In particular, we study the effect the presence of water has on the PMF for nanotube collapse in an opened (10,10) tube.

Water inside an open nanotube is forced out in the process of cross-sectional nanotube collapse. The collapse causes water molecules to rearrange, and it eventually leads to the breaking of the continuous water chain connecting the two ends of the channel. The energetics of water rearrangement and the cost of interface creation introduce changes in the energetics of nanotube collapse in solution relative to collapse in vacuum. We look for these changes by comparing the PMFs for nanotube collapse in solution and in vacuum.

The coordinate we choose to sample is the antipodal distance between the two faces of the collapsed nanotube being brought together (see Appendix). We pull the two faces of the nanotube together by imposing harmonic restraints on the distance between the collapsing walls. We calculate the PMF using the umbrella sampling method by harmonically biasing our coordinate at various values ranging from $3.5\ \text{\AA}$ for a collapsed nanotube to $14\ \text{\AA}$ for an open nanotube. The results are analyzed to give a PMF curve for our coordinate by the multiple histogram method. $^{54}$

A (10,10) nanotube is stiff and resistant to collapse, so the PMF for the collapse is dominated by the elastic deformation energy due to bending the nanotube walls. For such a rigid nanotube, the effects of the presence of water on the energy profile for nanotube collapse cannot be discerned, as they fall within the margins of error of our simulation. To enhance the magnitude of the effect of water solvation relative to the elastic deformation we increase the flexibility of our carbon nanotube by removing the dihedral interactions and reducing the angular and van der Waals interactions among carbon atoms. In this way, we effectively make the energy difference between the collapsed and open states much smaller, allowing for an examination of the features in the PMF due to solvation with much better resolution even for tubes of relatively small diameter.

![](./images/812130858491183106_10.jpg)

FIG. 8. Potential of mean force for the collapse of a (10,10) nanotube. Below the plot are snapshots taken from simulations at various antipodal distances. The approximate antipodal distance of each snapshot is indicated on the plot.

For a (10,10) nanotube of enhanced flexibility, there are clear differences between the PMF for the collapse in solution and in vacuum (Fig. 8). Starting from an open nanotube, more energy is needed to collapse a solvated tube than one in vacuum. The additional energy is used to expel the water from within the nanotube. One notable feature is that when the antipodal distance between opposite walls of the nanotube is close to 7 Å, the resulting configurations of the water inside the nanotube acquire extra stability, evidenced by the dip in the PMF curve relative to the vacuum collapse. The steeper slope of the curve for the smaller distances reflects the increased difficulty in breaking up stable water configurations by further nanotube collapse. In addition, there are sharp changes in the slope of the PMF at antipodal distances near 4.2 and 5.15 Å, indicating qualitative changes in water configurations inside the nanotube.

Close examination of the configurations generated at the above antipodal distances reveals that the regions with extra stability correspond to the formation of a single, highly ordered layer of water molecules that extends through the length of the tube [see Fig. 8(e), at approximately 6 Å antipodal distance]. At an antipodal distance of around 10.5 Å, two complete layers of water occupy the tube [Fig. 8(f)].

The sharp change in the slope of the PMF at an antipodal distance of 5.15 Å is due to a transition between a state where the entire nanotube is filled with water (corresponding to a larger slope) and a state where only half of the tube is filled by water (corresponding to a smaller slope). The two water configurations present qualitatively different resistance to nanotube collapse, which causes the slope of the PMF for collapse to change depending on which water configuration is sampled. Similarly, the change of slope at 4.2 Å is due to a transition between the half-filled and empty states. Such stabilizing water monolayers had been seen before in simulations, trapped between approaching carbon nanotube membranes.

We are able to observe in a single simulation (with the antipodal distance harmonically restrained at 5.125 Å) transitions between the filled and half-empty states of the nanotube. Although the transition from a filled configuration to a half-filled configuration allows for a slight decrease in the antipodal distance, the change is small relative to the amplitude of thermal fluctuations, making the antipodal distance a poor coordinate to describe the transition from a filled to half-filled nanotubes. A better coordinate is the continuous water occupancy number $N_c$ introduced in Sec. II. The chief limitation of using $N_c$ as the coordinate describing the transition from a half-filled to filled nanotubes is that it does not contain any configurational information about the water molecules inside the tube. However, if the transition we observed were simply a process of pushing more water molecules inside the nanotube, $N_c$ would be able to provide a coordinate which separates well the two observed states. In contrast, if the transition state is characterized by complex water configurations inside the nanotube, $N_c$ will not be able to differentiate between various water configurations containing approximately the same number of water molecules.

![](./images/812130858491183106_11.jpg)

FIG. 9. Histograms of the water occupancy number $N_c$ in single simulations where emptying transitions were seen with unmodified C-O interactions (solid line, antipodal distance around 5.12 Å) and the reduced C-O interactions (dashed line, antipodal distance around 9.5 Å).

In our simulations water does assume very ordered configurations with large interfaces such as those depicted in Figs. 8(c) and 8(d). The strength of the carbon-oxygen nonbonding interactions in the AMBER force field stabilizes such water configurations relative to bulk water. By reducing the strength of the C-O interaction to that used in Ref. 21 (thus artificially making the nanotube more hydrophobic), we can energetically penalize the formation of water wires and sheets inside the collapsed tubes. Under such conditions $N_c$ is a more useful coordinate to describe the filling transition.

A histogram of the parameter $N_c$ for a simulation where the transition between half-filled and filled nanotube states occurs several times is plotted in Fig. 9. While it is possible to discern two peaks corresponding to half-filled and filled nanotubes, the system spends a considerable fraction of time in single water wire configurations [such as those depicted in Fig. 8(c)], which are intermediate states. Examining the portions of our simulation trajectory where the system is in a predominantly half-filled state, we see that even though the formation of a single water wire increases $N_c$, it rarely leads to the formation of a second water wire and the filling of the nanotube. Similarly, starting from the configurations with two water wires, the breaking of a single wire rarely leads to the breaking of the other wire. This apparent hysteresis

shows that $N_c$ is not a very good coordinate to describe the filling transition for this system with full water-carbon van der Waals attractions.

Reducing the strength of the carbon-oxygen interactions has a dramatic effect. The features in the PMF for nanotube collapse arising from the presence of water disappear, as shown in Fig. 8. This highly hydrophobic nanotube empties during collapse when the antipodal distance is approximately 9.5 Å. By contrast, the nanotube with unmodified C–O interactions empties when the antipodal distance is around 4 Å. Configurations such as those depicted in Figs. 8(a)–8(d) are energetically too costly and become increasingly rare. The filling transition in this situation is described much better by $N_c$, because the nanotube is either completely filled with many water molecules or nearly empty. The histogram of $N_c$ for this more hydrophobic nanotube with antipodal distance harmonically restrained at 9.5 Å is plotted in Fig. 9. Instead of having a transition from filled to half-filled, and from half-filled to empty nanotubes, a single transition from filled to empty nanotubes is observed. In addition, the simple variable $N_c$ is able to separate the two states with much less ambiguity.

## IV. CONCLUSIONS

We have studied the effect of the flexibility of a hydrophobic water pore on the filling/emptying transition and on water dynamics inside a filled pore, as well as the effect of the presence of water on the free energy of nanotube collapse. We find a nontrivial interplay between the mechanical properties of the tube and the ability of water molecules to enter and diffuse along the length of the pore that should be included in a complete description of flexible hydrophobic water pores.

By modeling the hydrophobic pore as an open-ended segment of a single-walled carbon nanotube channel, we find that increasing its flexibility makes the channel effectively more hydrophobic, in part by reducing its effective diameter. We quantify the increase in hydrophobicity by measuring the dependence of the filling/emptying transition$^{21}$ on the flexibility of the channel and on the strength of the water-channel attractive interaction. By measuring water transport properties we also show that increased flexibility decreases the mobility of water. Evidence is accumulating that biological channels and pores may lie both in “empty” and “filled” regimes, depending on the specific channel structure, amino acid composition and ionization state, opening state and functional cycle, and the channel environment.$^{6,8,45,46,63}$

The presence of water affects various mechanical properties of carbon nanotubes, such as the PMF for radial collapse. Water is able to form highly ordered structures inside the confined geometry of the tube that are stabilized by the strongly directional hydrogen bonds and by the interactions between water and the channel. Consequently, the collapse of a filled water channel goes through various phases characterized by qualitatively different configurations of water molecules inside the channel [such as the single wire configuration shown in Fig. 8(c)].

The relationship between the behavior of water within the channel and its flexibility is strongly affected by changes in the strength of the attractive interaction between the channel and the water molecules. Small decreases in this attractive interaction can induce the emptying of the channel,$^{15,21}$ and can change the qualitative nature of the energy profile for radial collapse, as shown in Fig. 8, by removing the features in the PMF profile for collapse that are due to specific stable water configurations. Most importantly, such small changes in the effective “polarity” of the pore completely change the qualitative effects of water from obstructing collapse (by preferring the water-filled state) to facilitating collapse (by emptying). This sensitivity underscores the limitations of our simulations, since the force fields used to model those interactions are parametrized for the bulk phase of water, and their applicability to highly confined geometries can be questioned. For protein channels, we can expect similar force-field dependences. Nevertheless, our results suggest that the behavior of water inside various hydrophobic channels found in biological systems and the mechanical properties of those channels might depend strongly on the precise interatomic interactions between water and the channel. As a consequence, consideration of the degree of flexibility of the channel is important to determine the microscopic details of its functional dynamics and thermodynamics.

## APPENDIX: SIMULATION METHODS

The PMF for the filling of small (6,6) nanotubes and for the collapse and filling of larger ones were calculated with the multiple histogram method using a harmonic bias to sample the reaction coordinate.$^{54}$ To impose a bias on the number of water molecules inside the tube, we added a potential $U_{\text{water}}$,

$$
U_{\text{water}}=\frac{k_w}{2}\left(N_c-N_{\text{target}}\right)^2, \tag{A1}
$$

where $k_w$ is the strength of the bias applied in our umbrella sampling window and $N_{\text{target}}$ is the target number of water molecules inside the nanotube.

To measure the filling of the carbon nanotube, we use a continuous occupancy number $N_c$. The calculation of $N_c$ takes into account not only whether a molecule is inside the nanotube, but also how far it is from the midpoint of the channel. The variable $N_c$ that we use has the virtue of being continuous and differentiable, and thus suitable for studying umbrella sampling in molecular-dynamics simulations.

The number of water molecules inside the nanotube $N_c$ is counted by the following algorithm. First, the axis along the length of the nanotube $M_{\text{tot}}$ is determined by

$$
M_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{unit}}^{2}} \sum_{i \in K_{1}} \sum_{j \in K_{\max }}\left(R_{j}-R_{i}\right), \tag{A2}
$$

where $N_{\text{unit}}$ is the number of atoms in a unit cell.

A new Cartesian coordinate system with unit vectors $\hat{X}, \hat{Y}$ and $\hat{Z}$ is created such that its origin is at the geometric center of the first unit cell of the nanotube, and $\hat{Z}$ $=M_{\mathrm{tot}} /\left|M_{\mathrm{tot}}\right|$. The $\hat{z}$ coordinate of each water molecule is determined by a coordinate transformation from the original $\{x, y, z\}$ basis to $\{\hat{X}, \hat{Y}, \hat{Z}\}$. $N_{c}$ is given by

$$
N_{c}=\sum_{\alpha \in \text { all water molecules }} f\left(\hat{z}_{\alpha}\right) \delta_{g\left(\hat{x}_{\alpha}, \hat{y}_{\alpha}, \hat{z}_{\alpha}\right)}. \tag{A3}
$$

In Eq. (A3), the radial counting function $g(\hat{x}_{\alpha},\hat{y}_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha})$ returns 1 when water molecule $\alpha$ with position $\hat{z}_{\alpha}$ along the nanotube has coordinates $(\hat{x}_{\alpha},\hat{y}_{\alpha})$ that are inside the polygon defined by the projections on the $(\hat{x},\hat{y})$ plane of the coordinates of the atoms belonging to the nearest unit cell $K_{\alpha}$ of the nanotube [i.e., if the point $(\hat{x}_{\alpha},\hat{y}_{\alpha})$ is inside the polygon $\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}$ $=\{(x_{i}^{\hat{}},y_{i}^{\hat{}}),i \in K_{\alpha}\}]$ and 0 otherwise. To test whether water $\alpha$ is inside, we take advantage of Cauchy's residue formula to define

$$
g(\hat{x}_{\alpha},\hat{y}_{\alpha},\hat{z}_{\alpha})=
\begin{cases}
0, & h(\hat{x}_{\alpha},\hat{y}_{\alpha},\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})>0.2 \\
1, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases} \tag{A4}
$$

$$
h(\hat{x}_{\alpha},\hat{y}_{\alpha},\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2 \pi i} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}_{\alpha}} \frac{c_{i+1}-c_{i}}{c_{\alpha}-c_{i}}, \tag{A5}
$$

and $c_{k}$ is the complex number $\hat{x_{k}}+i \hat{y_{k}}$ for a given $k$.

The axial counting function $f(\hat{z})$ defined in Eq. (A6) is designed to provide a slowly varying differentiable restraint potential,

$$
f(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos \left(\pi \frac{z-z_{\text {half }}}{z_{\text {switch }}+z_{\text {half }}}\right)\right). \tag{A6}
$$

Here $z_{\text{half}}$ is the distance from the ends of the nanotube to its midpoint, so $z_{\text{half}}=|M_{\text{tot}}|/2$. The switching length $z_{\text{switch}}$ is a parameter which governs how far the counting extends past the ends of the nanotube. In order to pull water molecules inside an empty nanotube when we harmonically restrain $N_{c}$, a nonzero value of $z_{\text{switch}}$ must be used. Small values of $z_{\text{switch}}$ will not allow for good equilibration, while larger values of $z_{\text{switch}}$ will give large values for $N_{c}$ even when the nanotube is empty. We find that $z_{\text{switch}}=3.6$ Å offers a good compromise.

For counting purposes, we also calculate $N_{w}$, the number of water molecules strictly inside the nanotube. In Fig. 10, the histograms of the variable $N_{c}$ are plotted for seven different sets of data points corresponding to the seven observed values of $N_{w}$. There is some uncertainty in the calculation of $N_{w}$, since fluctuations of the position of the nanotube atoms near the ends and small displacements of the water molecules at the entrance of the nanotube can cause the value of $N_{w}$ to vary by one. In a filled nanotube, this happens at both ends of the tube, so the fluctuations of $N_{w}$ are even larger. Therefore, the overlap between the histogram for $N_{w}$ =5 and its neighbors is larger.

The PMF for nanotube collapse is also calculated via the umbrella sampling method. To restrain the antipodal distance, it is not sufficient to harmonically restrain a pair of diametrically opposite atoms near the center of the nanotube, because the distance between the pair can remain large even though the nanotube may collapse in another direction. For this reason we restrain two pairs of atoms with harmonic potentials $R1$ and $R2$ as in Fig. 11. The two harmonic potentials have exactly the same target length and force constant. This technique prevents the nanotube from collapsing along alternate directions.

The antipodal distance is defined as the value of the first restrained distance, $d=R1$. While this might not be the same as the average antipodal distance along the nanotube axis, it does not alter the results obtained in the paper. $$
