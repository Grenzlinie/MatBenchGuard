# ON THE FORMATION AND ORDERING OF 'SUPERABUNDANT' VACANCIES IN PALLADIUM DUE TO HYDROGEN ABSORPTION

W.A. Oates and H. Wenzl

Institut für Festkörperforschung,
Forschungszentrum Jülich GmbH,
52425 Jülich,
Federal Republic of Germany.

(Received December 1, 1994)

## Introduction

Fukai and Õkuma (1,2) have recently presented some extremely interesting results concerning the formation of high concentrations of vacancies (□) in Pd and other metals which result from the absorption of hydrogen. In their first paper (1), they showed that when Pd is annealed for long times (hours) at high temperatures $(\approx 800^{\circ} C)$ in high pressures of $H_{2}( g)(≈5 GPa)$ , vacancy concentrations as high as $N_{\square} / N_{Pd} ≈0.2$ can be obtained (1). In their second paper (2), they demonstrated that these vacancies can order when the alloys containing high vacancy concentrations are slowly cooled to lower temperatures (below $\approx 600^{\circ} C$ ).

The hydrogen chemical potential, $\mu_{H}$ , is very high under the conditions used in these experiments. This can be seen in Fig. (1), which shows $\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{H_{2}}-\mu_{H_{2}}^{o}) / R T$ as a function of $H_{2}( g)$ pressure at 1000 K (3). $\mu_{H_{2}}^{o}$ is the ideal gas reference state value at 1 bar and the temperature of interest. It should be appreciated that, in this temperature and pressure range, the curve represents a substantial extrapolation from the available experimental results. Such extrapolations are sensitive to the analytical form chosen for the fluid's equation of state (a modified van der Waals equation in this case) and although the rapid increase in $\mu_{H}$ with $H_{2}( g)$ pressure in the GPa range shown in Fig. (1) is undoubtedly correct, the quantitative aspects of the relation may be questionable.

A brief explanation for the formation of the high vacancy concentrations in terms of a simple statistical model has been given previously (4). In the present note we wish to present a more quantitative confirmation of this model and also demonstrate how it can also explain the ordering of the 'superabundant' vacancies in Pd observed by Fukai and Õkuma (2).

## Two Sublattice Model

The model generally used for metal-hydrogen (M-H) systems is that first used by Lacher in his classic paper on the thermodynamics of the Pd-H system (5). It may be denoted as:

(M)(H, ES)

The metal atoms, M, reside on one sublattice whilst the H atoms occupy a separate sublattice, with ES representing the empty sites on this sublattice. The thermodynamic properties of the system may be

described in terms of this unary lattice gas of H atoms on the octahedral interstitial sites (O-sites). The model can, however, equally well be considered to be one denoted by:

$$(M)(H, \square)$$

where the interstitial sublattice is now regarded as a binary system of H atoms and vacancies. The thermodynamic properties of the unary lattice gas map onto those of the binary H atom/vacancy system, making the two descriptions equivalent (6).

In either form, this model assumes that the principal role of the metal atoms is to provide the interstitial cages for H absorption. At high temperatures, however, the metal sublattice will also be able to achieve vacancy equilibrium with internal and external vacancy sinks. A more appropriate model for the system which is also open to vacancies is one denoted by:

$$(M, \square)(H, \square)$$

It is convenient to discuss the thermodynamics of sublattice systems like this in terms of species chemical potentials (7,8). There are four to consider in this model:

$$\mu_{M_{\alpha}}=\mu_{M_{\alpha}}^{o}+R T \log _{e}\left(y_{M}^{\alpha}\right)+\mu_{M_{\alpha}}^{E} \tag{1}$$

$$\mu_{\square_{\alpha}}=\mu_{\square_{\alpha}}^{o}+R T \log _{e}\left(y_{\square}^{\alpha}\right)+\mu_{\square_{\alpha}}^{E} \tag{2}$$

$$\mu_{H_{\beta}}=\mu_{H_{\beta}}^{o}+R T \log _{e}\left(y_{H}^{\beta}\right)+\mu_{H_{\beta}}^{E} \tag{3}$$

$$\mu_{\square_{\beta}}=\mu_{\square_{\beta}}^{o}+R T \log _{e}\left(y_{\square}^{\beta}\right)+\mu_{\square_{\beta}}^{E} \tag{4}$$

where $y_{P}^{i}$ and $\mu_{P_{i}}^{E}$ refer to the fraction and excess chemical potential of $P$ on sublattice $i$ and the super-scripted properties refer to the standard quantities.

The molar free energy of the system, $G_{m}$, is given by:

$$
G_{m}=\sum_{P, i} \frac{1}{2} y_{P}^{i} \mu_{P_{i}} \tag{5}
$$

where the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ arises from the definition of $v_{P}^{i}$ as the subla. ic? ..action and not the total fraction. In order to obtain the equilibrium state, $G$ must be minimised subj_ct to the constraint that the numbers of species on the two sublattices are related:

$$
N_{M}^{\alpha}+\mathrm{v}_{\square}^{\alpha}=N_{H}^{\beta}+N_{\square}^{\beta} \tag{6}
$$

It is this constraint on the free energy minimisation which gives rise to a coupling of the vacancies on the substitutional and interstitial sublattices leading to the Schottky relation (9):

$$
\mu_{\square_{\alpha}}+\mu_{\square_{\beta}}=0 \tag{7}
$$

The essence of our model can be demonstrated through Eqns. (6) and (7) by using the Schottky-Wagner approximation (10) in which all excess chemical potentials in Eqns. (1-4) are set to zero and the standard vacancy chemical potentials are taken to be such as to give very low vacancy concentrations on the $\alpha$ sublattice at nearly complete filling with M (zero H concentration) and on the $\beta$ sublattice at nearly complete filling with H (zero M concentration). This model may then be used to calculate the vacancy concentrations as a function of the hydrogen concentration. An increase in H concentration on the $\beta$ sublattice corresponds with a decrease in the vacancy concentration on this sublattice. In order to preserve the Schottky equilibrium this decrease has to be accompanied by an increase in the vacancy concentration on the $\alpha$ sublattice when the vacancy concentration on the $\beta$ sublattice approaches zero. The results obtained from such a model are shown in Fig. (2). It can be seen that the vacancy concentration on the metal sublattice remains low until the equi-atomic composition region is reached (where the $\beta$ sublattice is approaching complete filling by H) but then rises sharply, as it must, in order to achieve the required composition. In the case of a real metal-hydrogen system we must, of course, recognise that, at high hydrogen/metal ratios, the metal sublattice will eventually contain so many vacancies that it will collapse.

In order to apply the model in a more quantitative manner to a specific system like the Pd-H one, we require expressions for the contributions to the excess species chemical potentials in Eqns. (1-4). For simplicity, we have assumed that these are totally energetic, *i.e.*, that the configurational and non-configurational excess entropies are zero. A zero excess configurational entropy is equivalent to using the Bragg-Williams approximation.

In our calculations, the nearest neighbour bond energy model has been used for the between-sublattice contributions to the energy of mixing (8):

$$
\Delta U_{m}(1)=3\left(y_{M}^{\alpha} y_{H}^{\beta} W_{M H}+y_{M}^{\alpha} y_{\square}^{\beta} W_{M V}+y_{\square}^{\alpha} y_{H}^{\beta} W_{V H}\right) \tag{8}
$$

where the $W_{i j}$ are the inter-sublattice pair exchange energies.

Further, configuration independent, contributions to the energy of the Pd-H system, usually assumed to originate from lattice expansion (11) and band filling (12), are determined by the hydrogen/metal ratio, $r=y_{H}^{\beta} / y_{M}^{\alpha}$. For the present purposes, it is assumed that their sum may be adequately represented by an equation of the form:

$$
\Delta U_{m}(2)=A_{1} r^{2}+A_{2} r^{4} \tag{9}
$$

Within-sublattice interactions to the mixing energy must also be considered. We have taken that between H and vacancies on the interstitial sublattice to be zero but that between M and vacancies on the metal

Some assumption must also be made on how this vacancy formation energy varies with the metal atom concentration on the metal sublattice and we have assumed that it drops linearly to zero with increasing vacancy concentration on that sublattice. This assumption gives the following contribution to the energy of mixing:

We have used the following values of the parameters in the above equations: $W_{M H} / R T=0.808$ to agree with the Sieverts constant in pure Pd at $800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}(13), W_{M V}=W_{V H}=0, A_{1} / R T=-2.0, A_{2} / R T=+2.0$ to give values for $\mu_{H}^{E}$ as a function of $r, A_{3} / R T=13.816$ to give a vacancy concentration in the pure metal of $10^{-6}$ at $800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, which seems a reasonable value for pure Pd at this temperature (14).

As intimated earlier, the precise values selected for these parameters do not change the essentials of the results already given in Fig. (2) and this is confirmed by the results shown in Fig. (3) for the calculated vacancy concentration on the metal sublattice as a function of $r$. It can be seen that $y_{\square}^{\alpha}$ reaches a value of $\approx 0.17$ at $r=1.2$.

It is important to ascertain whether this hydrogen/metal ratio can be reached under the experimental conditions used by Fukai and Ökuma (1,2). The hydrogen chemical potential can be obtained from the species chemical potentials:

$$
\mu_{H}=\mu_{H_{\beta}}-\mu_{\square_{\beta}} \tag{11}
$$

and the results shown in Fig. (1) may be used to convert from hydrogen potential to $H_2(g)$ pressure. With this procedure, the results given in Fig. (4) show the calculated vacancy concentration on the metal sublattice as a function of the $H_2(g)$ pressure. We estimate that a composition of $r \approx 1.2 \Rightarrow y_{\square}^{\alpha} \approx 0.17$ can be reached at $p_{H_2} \approx 8.7$ GPa, in very good agreement with Fukai and Ökuma's observations. Thus our earlier interpretation of their results (4) receives a more quantitative confirmation. It appears that, under the conditions of their experiments, if we assume that only the O-sites are occupied, they have reached a hydrogen/metal ratio of $\approx 1.2$, a value which can only be obtained by having a large vacancy concentration on the metal sublattice.

### Eight Sublattice Model

In order to account for ordering of the vacancies on the metal sublattice, $\alpha$, it is necessary to subdivide this sublattice into further sublattices and consider interactions between the new sublattices. In view of the observation of Fukai and Ökuma (2) that the vacancy ordering corresponds with that of $(Pd_3\square_1)H_4$ it seems that the metal sublattice should be divided into four, $\alpha1 \dots \alpha4$, as shown in Fig. (5). There, it can be seen that each of the metal atoms on one of the four sublattices has six interstitial sites as nearest neighbours with another two in the simple cubic cell being third nearest neighbours. It is likely, therefore, that any ordering on the metal sublattices will be accompanied by ordering on the almost completely occupied H

sublattice. Accordingly, the interstitial sublattice has also been divided into four, $\beta 1 \dots \beta 4$, resulting in an eight sublattice model denoted by:

$$[(M, \square)_{\alpha 1}(M, \square)_{\alpha 2}(M, \square)_{\alpha 3}(M, \square)_{\alpha 4}]\ [(H, \square)_{\beta 1}(H, \square)_{\beta 2}(H, \square)_{\beta 3}(H, \square)_{\beta 4}]$$

In calculating the equilibrium state of the alloy for this eight sublattice model it was assumed that the expressions for $\Delta U_{m}(2)$ and $\Delta U_{m}(3)$ in Eqns. (9) and (10) are unchanged from the two sublattice model. Equation (8), however, for $\Delta U_{m}(1)$ has to be modified to allow for the extra sublattices and it is necessary to introduce an additional interaction parameter to bring about ordering between the metal sublattices:

$$
\Delta U_{m}(1)=\frac{1}{4} \sum_{i, j}\left(y_{M}^{\alpha i} y_{H}^{\beta j} W_{M H}+y_{M}^{\alpha i} y_{\square}^{\beta j} W_{M V}+y_{\square}^{\alpha i} y_{H}^{\beta j} W_{V H}+2 L_{M V} y_{M}^{\alpha i} y_{\square}^{\alpha j}\right) \tag{12}
$$

where the summations run over the nearest neighbour pairs of sublattices for the the $\alpha / \beta$ interactions and over all pairs of the metal sublattices for the $\alpha / \alpha$ interactions.

The magnitude of the ordering parameter, $L_{M V} / R T=-0.6$, was set by using the observation of Fukai and Ökuma (2) that the order/disorder temperature was $\approx 880$K. $A_{3} / R T$ has simultaneously to be increased by $12 L_{M V} / R T$ over its value in the two-sublattice model so as to maintain the vacancy formation energy at the same value.

The selected path for the model calculation was similar to that used in the experiments of Fukai and Ökuma (2). An isothermal annealing at $800^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in an open system (to $\mathrm{H}$ and vacancies) first produced the equilibrium described under the two-sublattice model, results for which are shown in Figs. (3) and (4). Since the system at this stage is in complete equilibrium, the Schottky equilibrium holds:

$$
\mu_{\square_{\alpha 1}}+\mu_{\square_{\alpha 2}}+\mu_{\square_{\alpha 3}}+\mu_{\square_{\alpha 4}}+\mu_{\square_{\beta 1}}+\mu_{\square_{\beta 2}}+\mu_{\square_{\beta 3}}+\mu_{\square_{\beta 4}}=0 \tag{13}
$$

This system was then made closed and the alloy allowed to cool. Neither the total vacancy concentration on the metal sublattice nor that on the almost completely occupied interstitial sublattice were changed

The results from this constrained equilibrium calculation, where the Schottky equi- librium no longer holds, are best discussed in terms of the long range order parameter for the vacan- cies on the metal sublattices. It was found, that, at $y_{\square}^{\alpha}=0.17$, the ordering is to non-stoichiometric $(Pd, \square)_{3}(Pd, \square)_{1}(H, \square)_{3}(H, \square)_{1}$. Thus three of the four metal and three of the four interstitial sublat- tices are equivalent and the fully ordered structure at the stoichiometric composition (corresponding to $y_{\square}^{\alpha}=0.25)$ would be $(Pd_{3} \square_{1}) H_{4}$. It can be seen that the long range order parameter varies in the manner observed by Fukai and Õkuma (2).

It should be remembered that this ordering calculation has been carried out in the nearest neighbour Bragg-Williams approximation. Under these conditions the phase diagram for the vacancy ordering on the metal sublattices will be identical with that calculated by Shockley for binary alloys in this approximation (15). It is known, however, that the topology of this phase diagram is incorrect and that it is necessary to use a higher order approximation in order to obtain the correct topology. A Cluster Variation Method (16) calculation for this eight sublattice model would give results which are somewhat different in detail from those described above, i.e., the calculated variation of the long range parameter with temperature would be slightly different from that presented in Fig. (6).

### Conclusions

The formation of superabundant vacancies in metals can be driven by hydrogen incorporation on the interstitial sublattice. In order to achieve this, it is essential to have a sufficiently high hydrogen chemical potential so that effectively all the available interstitial sites are occupied. The concomitant formation of high concentrations of vacancies on the metal sublattice is then controlled by the coupling of the vacancy chemical potentials on the metal and interstitial sublattices by the Schottky equilibrium condition. This formation of excessive concentrations of vacancies on the host metal lattice is expected to be a general phenomenon which, if the system can achieve vacancy equilibrium with external or internal sinks, will

occur as soon as the interstitial sublattice approaches complete filling. This means that the hydrogen/metal ratio will exceed that normally expected for a complete filling of the interstitial sites being considered.

Once a high concentration of vacancies has been formed, then an effective repulsive interaction between the vacancies on the metal sublattice may give rise to vacancy ordering at lower temperatures.

This interpretation is in good agreement with the experimental results of Fukai and Õkuma (1,2) for the Pd–H system. We estimate that they have reached a hydrogen/metal ratio of $\approx 1.2$, which we have shown to be quite feasible at the $\mathrm{H}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})$ pressures and temperatures used in their experiments.
