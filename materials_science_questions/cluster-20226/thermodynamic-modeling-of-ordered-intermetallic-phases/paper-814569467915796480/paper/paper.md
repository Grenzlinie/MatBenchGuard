# Colloidal equilibrium in insulating crystals: Possible application to relaxor ferroelectrics

A. G. Khachaturyan
Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Rutgers University, P.O. Box 909, Piscataway, New Jersey 08855-0909

(Received 15 December 1992; revised manuscript received 25 February 1993)

Decomposition in a multicomponent insulating compound with a considerable Coulomb interaction between ions is considered. A specific situation where decomposition occurs due to redistribution of ions of the same sign (either cations or anions) whereas all other atoms form an immobile frame is discussed. Such a situation may occur if a diffusivity of the ions of the same sign is much higher than that of atoms comprising the frame. It is shown that under these conditions the decomposition results in solid-state colloidal equilibrium. The colloidal state is formed by an array of coherent precipitate phase particles imbedded into a parent phase matrix. These particles are of the same stable size. This size, as well the volume fraction of a precipitate phase and the equilibrium composiitons of precipitate and matrix phases, are determined by the balance between electrostatic and interfacial energies. The balance proves to be dependent on overall stoichiometry. This situation drastically differs from the conventional situation where the compositions of coexisting equilibrium phases do not depend on overall stoichiometry of a system (the lever rule is valid) and where the coarsening process always occurs. The proposed model is applied to relaxor ferroelectrics to explain occurrence of a mesoscopic inhomogeneous structure that does not undergo coarsening.

## INTRODUCTION

The Coulomb interaction between ions in ceramic oxides, being infinite in range, imposes severe constraints on a possible phase transformation path. Particularly, decomposition in oxides can occur only by diffusion of electrically neutral anion-cation complexes. Otherwise, decomposition, which always produces composition heterogeneities, would result in violation of the local charge neutrality and would result in a corresponding drastic increase of the electrostatic energy. However, if diffusion mobilities of anions and cations at low temperatures are so different that the "slow" components, for example, anions, are practically frozen, then the diffusion of neutral complexes is impossible and the phase transformation may occur only by means of diffusional redistribution of "fast" cations. If this is the case, decomposition associated only with the cation diffusion proves to be impossible by the conventional mechanism because the precipitate-phase particles, enriched by cations, should be electrically charged, and thus the greater the size of a precipitate, the higher the electrostatic energy of a system.

Competition between three energies, the electrostatic energy stimulating the particle-size reduction, the interfacial energy stimulating the particle coarsening, and the finite-range interaction energy driving the transformation, would result in a very specific heterogeneous state. This state consists of finite-size particles of a precipitate phase. The size and arrangement of these particles are stabilized by an electrostatic interaction. The physical mechanism of this stabilizing is similar to the mechanism, associated with the electrostatic interaction, which results in a stable colloidal dispersion of solid particles in a liquid (or liquid particles in a gas). Because of that, the stable distribution of electrically charged precipitates formed by the solid-state decomposition will be called below a "crystalline colloidal state." Unlike conventional colloidal dispersion, observed in gas and liquid phases, the colloidal dispersion, described in this paper, occurs in a solid phase. The mesoscopic structure of the solid-phase colloidal dispersion provides the absolute minimum of the total free energy which is the sum consisting of the electrostatic, interfacial, and "chemical" (finite-range interaction-induced) energies. This is the reason why any structure change of the "colloidal" phase, including the precipitate particle coarsening, would lead to a free-energy increase and thus would be impossible. This stability presents a major difference which distinguishes this system under investigation from the conventional two-phase system undergoing unlimited coarsening.

The picture described above gives just a rough qualitative idea how decomposition in an insulating system with an electrostatic interaction could occur. The reality proves to be more complicated because of the coupling between the compositions of coexisting phases (they determine the deviation from the charge neutrality) and the two-phase morphology (the volume fraction of a precipitate phase, sizes of precipitates, distances between them, and their mutual arrangement). This coupling, which has no precedent in conventional systems, occurs through the electrostatic interaction which depends on both the composition gap between phases and the morphology. Both characteristics are the internal parameters of a system which are self-adjusted to minimize the total free energy. The external parameters are the temperature and overall stoichiometry of a system. The coupling between compositions, volume fraction of phases, and mesoscopic structure makes inapplicable the starting point of the classical Gibbs thermodynamics, i.e., the assumption that the free energy of a two-phase mixture is merely a sum of the free energies of constituent phases which depends only on their volumes. Our two-


dimensional (2D) numerical simulation¹ of the decomposition kinetics in a system with an electrostatic interaction confirms the qualitative picture given above.

There is a strong indication that the solid-state colloidal equilibrium discussed above is responsible for the relaxor properties of certain ferroelectrics with the perovskite structure. The relaxor ferroelectrics have unique physical properties. Unlike conventional ferroelectrics with a sharp phase transition, they have a diffuse ferroelectric transition extended into the wide temperature range. This effect results in extremely high values of the dielectric constant within a wide temperature range. The mechanism responsible for this phenomenon is still unknown. The material also has a very large electrostrictive effect. An application of the relaxor ferroelectrics in devices such as capacitors, actuators, and transducers is based on these special physical properties.

There is not too much known about the origin of the relaxor behavior. To explain the relaxor electrical properties of these ferroelectrics, Smolenskii and Usupov²⁻⁴ and Cross⁵ proposed a hypothesis relating these properties to internal concentration inhomogeneities. Recently, experiments by Chen, Chan, and Harmer,⁶ Randall and Bhalla,⁷ and Hussan, Chubb, and Morell⁸ confirmed this model. The authors of these works observed the appearance of nanoscale ordered domains in ${\text{Pb(Mg}}_{1/3}{\text{Nb}}_{2/3}{\text{)O}}_{3}$ and similar relaxor ferroelectrics. According to these observations, the composition of domains differs from the composition of a disordered matrix, and unlike the precipitates of the ordered phase in the usual cases of decomposition (but in agreement with the arguments presented above), the precipitates of an ordered phase in relaxor ferroelectrics do not coarsen in spite of the sufficient diffusion mobility of components. It has been shown that the relaxor properties of ${\text{Pb(Mg}}_{1/3}{\text{Nb}}_{2/3}{\text{)O}}_{3}$ and other oxides are mainly determined by the size and degree of order of the precipitates.⁹,¹⁰ Chen, Chan, and Harmer⁶ have emphasized the important role played in this system by the charge balance and electrostatic equilibrium. Particularly, they proposed a hypothesis that the arrest of coarsening is caused by the fact that the ordered particles are charged because their stoichiometry does not provide the charge balance. The results presented in this paper demonstrate that this hypothesis is correct.

The purpose of this work is an attempt to develop the theory of decomposition of a crystalline system with an electrostatic interaction and consider how the electrostatic energy affects the thermodynamics of the two-phase equilibrium. We will show that the addition of the electrostatic energy to the conventional free energy of a two-phase mixture results in a drastic change of the equilibrium state. Instead of the conventional two-phase mixture undergoing unlimited coarsening, a solid-state colloidal equilibrium with the stable mesoscopic morphology is formed.

# TRANSFORMATION MODEL

Formulating the transformation model, we, for certainty, will consider a specific case of an insulating oxide with the perovskite structure. However, the obtained results seem to be applicable to many other structures as well. For simplicity, we assume that the $B$ sites of the perovskite structure, occupied by small-radius cations (such as Ti in ${\text{BaTiO}}_{3}$), are filled by two different species of cations. The $B$ sites form a primitive cubic lattice. The $A$ sites, forming the fcc lattice frame, are occupied by large-radius atoms, oxygen, and large-radius cations (like Ba). These large-radius atoms have a smaller diffusional mobility and are assumed to be immobile during the decomposition. These assumptions considerably simplify the decomposition model. The analysis is reduced to consideration of a diffusional phase transformation in a binary substitutional solid solution with the primitive cubic lattice. As for the interatomic interaction in the system, we assume that cations composing the solid solution have two different charges. Therefore, besides the finite-range ("chemical") interaction driving the system to decomposition, the electrostatic interaction between cations should be also taken into account.

The electrostatic energy is caused by the distribution of charged precipitates whose size and arrangement are determined by the balance between the interfacial and electrostatic energy. Coulomb repulsion between the precipitates should lead to a mesoscopic structure where precipitates tend to be situated as far from each other as possible. Under the condition that the number of these precipitates and their total volume are fixed, the energy minimizing distribution is a periodical close-packed array. The obtained mesoscopic structure is, actually, the colloidal dispersion occurring in the solid state. The corresponding equilibrium is the colloidal equilibrium.

Our recent 2D numerical simulation¹ of the decomposition kinetics of this system confirms this picture. This simulation did not use any *a priori* assumptions imposing constraints on possible structure transformations. The finite equilibrium structure, obtained by numerical solution of the microscopic nonlinear diffusion equation, did correspond to the colloidal equilibrium of dispersed particles of the precipitate phase. The dispersed structure consists of a honeycomb (close-packed) periodical array of circular-shaped precipitates which do not undergo coarsening. All precipitates are of the same size, the size being dependent on the balance between electrostatic and interfacial energies.

The above conclusion is also backed by the stability analysis of the Cahn-Hilliard equation with Coulomb energy contribution. Gupta and Cahn¹¹ and Jang *et al.*¹² have demonstrated that the Coulomb interaction results in appearance of a "soft" concentration wave with the finite wave vector whose length is determined by the balance between the gradient term in the free energy (it is responsible for the interfacial energy) and the electrostatic energy. As is known, the star of "soft" concentration waves may condense into a stable periodical concentration modulation.

# FREE ENERGY OF A SYSTEM WITH ELECTROSTATIC INTERACTION

The electrostatic energy of a periodical distribution of spherical precipitates is given by Eqs. (A14) and (A16).

This energy may be rewritten in the form
$$
E_{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{1}{2} V \frac{4 \pi}{3}\left(\frac{\Delta z}{\sqrt{\epsilon} v_{0}}\right)^{2} \Delta c^{2} \alpha(\omega) \omega R^{2},
\tag{1}
$$
where $V$ is the total volume of the system, $v_{0}$ is the volume of a crystal lattice cell, $\Delta c=c_{2}-c_{1}, \Delta z=z_{2}-z_{1}$, $R$ is the precipitate radius, $\omega$ is the precipitate-phase volume fraction, $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ are compositions of coexisting phases, $z_{1}$ and $z_{2}$ are effective charges of the ions, $\epsilon$ is the dielectric constant and
$$
\alpha(\omega)=4 \pi \frac{R^{3}}{\Omega} \sum_{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}\left(3 \frac{K R \cos K R-\sin K R}{(K R)^{3}}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{(K R)^{2}}
\tag{2}
$$
is the dimensionless constant which is similar to the Madelung constant. In (2), the vectors $\mathbf{K}$ are reciprocal-lattice vectors corresponding to the macrolattice composed of spherical precipitates and $\Omega$ is the volume of the unit cell of this macrolattice. The summation in (2) is carried out over all reciprocal-lattice points. The prime on the sum implies that the term corresponding to $\mathbf{K}=0$ is omitted.

It follows from (1) that, at the fixed compositions $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ and volume fraction $\omega$ of the precipitate phase, the electrostatic energy decreases if the precipitate size $R$ tends to zero. However, the miniaturization of the structure is limited because at $R \rightarrow 0$ the decrease of electrostatic energy is balanced by the increase of the total interfacial energy,
$$
E_{s}=N_{1} \gamma_{s} 4 \pi R^{2}=V \gamma_{s} \frac{3 \omega}{R},
\tag{3}
$$
where $\gamma_{s}$ is the surface tension coefficient and $N_{1}$ is the total number of the precipitates. Finally, the conventional bulk free energy of the two-phase mixture associated with the finite-range interaction (this free energy depends only on the total volumes of coexisting phases) is
$$
F_{\text {bulk }}=V\left[\omega f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)+(1-\omega) f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)\right],
\tag{4}
$$
where $f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)$ and $f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)$ describe the specific free energies of the precipitate phase with composition $c_{2}$ and the matrix phase with composition $c_{1}$, respectively. The free energy (4) determines the stable-phase diagram. The stable diagram describes the mixture of electrically neutral phases, the state which could be formed if all phase components are electrically "discharged." In the relevant case of a solid-state colloid, where the local charge neutrality is not maintained, the total free energy is higher than (4). The sum of the electrostatic energy (1) and surface energy (3) should be added. It gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
F=V & {\left[\frac{1}{2} \frac{4 \pi}{3}\left(\frac{\Delta z}{\sqrt{\epsilon} v_{0}}\right)^{2}(\Delta c)^{2} \alpha(\omega) \omega R^{2}+\gamma_{s} \frac{3 \omega}{R}\right.} \\
& \left.+\left[\omega f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)+(1-\omega) f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)\right]\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

The electrostatic and interfacial energies, the first two terms entering (5), are positive and proportional to the volume. Therefore their contribution affects the thermodynamics describing the stable two-phase equilibrium into thermodynamics of the metastable crystalline colloidal state.

Minimizing (5) with respect to $R$ gives the equilibrium particle radius
$$
R_{0}=\left[\frac{9}{4 \pi} \frac{\gamma_{s} \epsilon v_{0}^{2}}{(\Delta z \Delta c)^{2} \alpha(\omega)}\right]^{1 / 3}.
\tag{6}
$$

Substituting (6) into (5) excludes $R$ from the free energy (5). It gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
F=V & \left[\frac{3}{2}(12 \pi)^{1 / 3}\left(\gamma_{s} \frac{\Delta z \Delta c}{\sqrt{\epsilon} v_{0}}\right)^{2 / 3} \alpha(\omega)^{1 / 3} \omega\right. \\
& \left.+\omega f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)+(1-\omega) f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

Unlike the conventional situation described by the additive Gibbs thermodynamics, the free energy (7) is not an additive function of the volumes of the constituent phases since it is not a linear function of the precipitate-phase volume fraction $\omega$. This circumstance determines the unusual thermodynamic behavior of this system. To illustrate the thermodynamics of the crystalline colloidal state, let us consider the simplest parabolic concentration dependence of the free energies $f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)$ and $f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)$:
$$
f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)=\frac{m}{2}\left(c_{2}-c_{2}^{0}\right)^{2} \text { and } f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)=\frac{m}{2}\left(c_{1}-c_{1}^{0}\right)^{2},
$$
where $m$ is the materials constant (the typical density of the bulk energy in the absence of the Coulomb interaction) and $c_{2}^{0}$ and $c_{1}^{0}$ are constants coinciding with the equilibrium compositions of coexisting phases in the stable system without the electrostatic interaction. It is also convenient to introduce the constant $\omega_{0}$,
$$
\omega_{0}=\frac{\overline{c}-c_{1}^{0}}{\left(c_{2}^{0}-c_{1}^{0}\right)},
\tag{8}
$$
which would be the equilibrium volume fraction of a precipitate phase corresponding to the stable phase diagram (the diagram in absence of the electrostatic interaction) at the overall stoichiometry $\overline{c}$. The dependence of all thermodynamic functions on $\omega_{0}(\overline{c})$ are, actually, the dependence on the stoichiometry $\overline{c}$.

Equation (7) may be rewritten as
$$
\begin{aligned}
F=V & \left[\frac{1}{2} e_{0} \alpha(\omega)^{1 / 3}\left(\frac{\Delta c}{\Delta c_{0}}\right)^{2 / 3} \omega+\omega f_{2}\left(c_{2}\right)\right. \\
& \left.+(1-\omega) f_{1}\left(c_{1}\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$
where $\Delta c_{0}=c_{2}^{0}-c_{1}^{0}$ and
$$
e_{0}=3(12 \pi)^{1 / 3}\left(\gamma_{s} \frac{\Delta z \Delta c_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon} v_{0}}\right)^{2 / 3}
\tag{10}
$$
are the materials constant. The constant $e_{0}$ is a typical specific energy including the electrostatic and interfacial energies. Expressing $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ in terms of $\omega$,
$$
c_{1}=\overline{c}-\omega\left(c_{2}-c_{1}\right), \quad c_{2}=\overline{c}+(1-\omega)\left(c_{2}-c_{1}\right),
\tag{11}
$$


and definition (8) transforms the free energy (9). It gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
F(x, \omega)=V \frac{m \Delta c_{0}^{2}}{2} & \left\{\xi x^{2 / 3} \alpha(\omega)^{1 / 3} \omega\right. \\
& +\omega\left[\left(1-\omega_{0}\right)-(1-\omega) x\right]^{2} \\
& \left.+(1-\omega)\left[\omega_{0}-\omega x\right]^{2}\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$
where $x=\Delta c / \Delta c_{0}$ is the internal thermodynamic parameter describing the miscibility gap in the colloidal state and $\xi=e_{0} /\left(m \Delta c_{0}^{2}\right)$ is the dimensionless constant. This constant is the ratio of the typical sum of the electrostatic and interfacial energies opposing the decomposition process, and the typical transformation driving force. The ratio $\xi$ describes the balance between the energies which determines the main features of the colloidal equilibrium.

## EQUILIBRIUM PHASE DIAGRAM
It is natural to assume that the precipitate phase is a phase whose volume fraction $\omega$ is less than 0.5. For example, if the volume fraction of the phase with composition $c_{2}$ is less than one-half $(\omega<0.5)$, then this phase forms the precipitates, while the other phase with composition $c_{1}$ forms the matrix. Otherwise, if the volume fraction of the phase with composition $c_{2}$ becomes greater than one-half, then the precipitates are formed by the phase with composition $c_{1}$, while the matrix is formed by the phase with composition $c_{2}$. This renaming the precipitate and matrix phases in accordance with their volume fractions does not affect the analytical results presented above. They are still valid within the accuracy of this renaming. The reason for this conclusion is that the electrostatic energy (1) and interfacial energy (3), which are responsible for the appearance of the colloidal phase, are the same, irrespective of the fact which of the phases has composition $c_{2}$ and which composition $c_{1}$. This result is obvious for the interfacial energy because the interfacial boundaries are not affected by the interchange of concentrations of the precipitate and matrix phases. This is also the case for the electrostatic energy because the electrostatic energy depends on the miscibility gap $\Delta c$ rather than on compositions $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ separately.

Therefore, without the loss of generality, the volume fraction of the precipitate phase can be assumed to be within the range $0<\omega<0.5$. The same arguments, applied to the stable phase diagram, give the range for $\omega_{0}$: $0<\omega_{0}<0.5$.

To investigate the metastable phase diagram describing the colloidal equilibrium, we have to analyze the free energy $F(x, \omega)$ in (12) as a function of two internal thermodynamic parameters $x$ and $\omega$. The equilibrium is characterized by the global minimum of this function with respect to $x$ and $\omega$ at given values of the external thermodynamic parameters $\xi$ and $\omega_{0}$. Minimizing $F(x, \omega)$ with respect to the variable $x$ at fixed $\omega$ gives $\partial F(x, \omega) / \partial x=0$ or, with the use of Eq. (12), the equation
$$
\frac{\xi}{3} \frac{\alpha(\omega)^{1 / 3}}{1-\omega}=x^{1 / 3}(1-x). \quad(13)
$$

The solution of the transcendental equation (13) gives the dependence $x=x(\omega)$. Graphical analysis of Eq. (13) shows that there are two branches of solutions, $x_{1}(\omega) \leq \frac{1}{4}$ and $x_{2}(\omega) \geq \frac{1}{4}$. The first branch $x_{1}(\omega)$ corresponds to the free-energy maxima and thus will not be considered. We will analyze only the second branch $x_{2}(\omega)>\frac{1}{4}$, corresponding to the free-energy minima.

The periodical distribution of spherical particles of the same size considered above minimizes the total electrostatic repulsion energy between spherical particles, if the particles are arranged into a close-packed periodical macrolattice (for the close-packed periodical structure, the distances between particles are maximal at the given volume fraction $\omega$ and therefore the Coulomb repulsive energy is minimal). For the relevant 3D case, this is the fcc macrolattice whose sites are occupied by spherical particles. For the fcc macrolattice, the numerical calculation of $\alpha(\omega)$ based on Eq. (2) is presented in Fig. 1. If $R^{3} / \Omega \ll 1(\omega \ll 1)$, the analytical calculation shows that $\alpha(\omega)$ has the asymptotic form
$$
\alpha(\omega) \approx \frac{6}{5}-\sqrt{\pi}(\omega)^{1 / 3}. \quad(14)
$$

Since the right-hand side of Eq. (13) assumes its maximum value $\beta_{c}=3 \times(4)^{-4 / 3} \approx 0.4725$ at $x_{0}=\frac{1}{4}$, Eq. (13), certainly, has no solution if the minimum of the left-hand side of (13) is greater than $\beta_{c}$, i.e., if
$$
\min \left\lfloor\frac{\xi}{3} \frac{\alpha(\omega)^{1 / 3}}{1-\omega}\right\rfloor>\beta_{c}=\frac{3(2)^{1 / 3}}{8}. \quad(15)
$$

For the fcc pattern formed by precipitates, the minimum of $\alpha(\omega)^{1 / 3} /(1-\omega)$ is equal to 0.80628. It falls at $\omega_{c}=0.26337$. Then the inequality (15) may be rewritten as
$$
\xi>\xi_{c} \approx 1.7579. \quad(16)
$$

Therefore, if $\xi>\xi_{c}$, Eq. (13) has no solution and thus the only free-energy minimum which exists is the one at $\omega=0$. It is obvious that if the volume fraction $\omega$ of the

The obtained dependences of $x_{\text{eq}}(\omega_{0})$ and $\omega_{\text{eq}}(\omega_{0})$ on the overall stoichiometry $\bar{c}$ [through the parameter $\omega_{0}(\bar{c})$] indicate that the miscibility gap and the volume fraction of a precipitate phase for the two-phase equilibrium depend on the overall stoichiometry. This is an unusual situation which never occurs in conventional additive Gibbs thermodynamics. Particularly unusual is the result that the equilibrium compositions $c_{1}$ and $c_{1}$ of the precipitate and matrix phases, unlike the case of Gibbs thermodynamics, prove to be dependent on the overall stoichiometry $\bar{c}$. ## DISCUSSION

As has been shown above, even a small Coulomb interaction produces a drastic effect on decomposition process. If the diffusion kinetics is such that the electrical neutrality cannot be maintained, the decomposition results in a new mesoscopic crystalline state corresponding to the "colloidal" equilibrium between the charged particles of a precipitate phase, which, in many respects, is similar to the colloidal equilibrium of a dispersion in gel. Instead of the classical scenario, where the two-phase mixture undergoes unlimited coarsening, the coarsening in this crystalline system does not occur at all (as long as the local charge inhomogeneity is maintained). The particle size is stable in the sense that any variation, increase or decrease, would result in a free-energy increase. This size is determined by competition between the electrostatic interaction and interfacial energy. It is commensurate with the material constant $L$, which has dimensionality of length. The typical length $L$ appears because the electrostatic interaction introduces a new constant, the difference in the volume charge densities of the precipitate and matrix phases caused by violation of the charge balance. This allows one to construct the length parameter from the materials constants. The typical difference between the ion-induced charge densities of constituent phases is $\Delta z \Delta c_{0} / \sqrt{\epsilon} v_{0}$ ($\Delta z \Delta c_{0} / \sqrt{\epsilon}$ is the mismatch between the average charges per ion in the precipitate and matrix phases, $v_{0}$ is the volume per ion, and $\epsilon$ is the dielectric constant). Using this value and the materials constant characterizing the interfacial energy (the interfacial tension coefficient $\gamma_{s}$), we may construct a constant with dimensionality of length:

$$
L=\left[\gamma_{s} \epsilon\left(\frac{v_{0}}{\Delta z \Delta c_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]^{1 / 3}.
\tag{21}
$$

With the accuracy of the dimensionless constant $(9 / 4 \pi)^{1 / 3} \sim 0.9$, which is of the order of unity, the characteristic length $L$ is equal to the length constant $R_{00}$ given by Eq. (20). The length $L$ is the greater, the greater is the interfacial energy and the smaller are the miscibility gap and the difference between the charges of the ion species $\Delta z$. The length parameter $L$ determines the typical size of the precipitates. This, particularly, could be seen from Eq. (19) since $R_{00} \sim L$.

The electrostatic repulsion between the precipitates generates a close-packed periodical array because in this case the separation distances between the precipitates are maximal if the number of precipitates and their total volume are fixed.

A computer simulation $^{1}$ based on a numerical solution of the microscopic nonlinear diffusion equation for the 2D model gives the same result. It showed that the stable equilibrium structure formed by charged precipitates is the honeycomb (close-packed) periodical array of precipitates of the same size. This simulation result is not based on any $a$ priori assumptions or constraints which could affect the predicted morphology of the mesoscopic structure.

The mesoscopic structure was considered above as a mixture of two phases. However, this mixture has very unusual properties. It minimizes the total free energy and thus cannot undergo coarsening. This equilibrium is analogous to the colloidal equilibrium of particles in gas or liquid media. Therefore the more accurate interpretation implies that this two-phase mixture is actually an equilibrium phase with a periodic close-packed structure. As a matter of fact, it could be also interpreted as a single-phase ordered phase. The only insignificant difference which distinguishes the mesoscopic phase from the conventional superstructures is that the period of the mesoscopic phase is macroscopically large. Formation of such long-period phases induced by the electrostatic interaction is a very well-known phenomenon for a 1D system. $^{13}$ The 1D system of electrically charged atoms never undergoes decomposition into a two-phase mixture with a subsequent coarsening. Instead, it forms a so-called devil's-staircase superstructure whose period is adjusted to the current stoichiometry and may be macroscopically large. The mesoscopic structure discussed above is actually a 3D analog of the devil's-staircase structures.

As has been shown in the previous section, the electrostatic interaction also dramatically affects the phase diagram. The two-phase miscibility gap of the stable phase diagram transforms into two subfields shown in Fig. 2. The first subfield describes a single-phase state where the decomposition is prevented by the electrostatic interaction. The second subfield, shown by the hatched area in Fig. 2, describes the range of the external thermodynamic parameters $\zeta$ and $\omega_0(\bar{c})$ where the decomposition does occur, but it produces the colloidal equilibrium of a two-phase dispersion of charged particles. The diagram shown in Fig. 2 is metastable; i.e., it is stable only conditionally, as long as the diffusion of the charge-compensating structural vacancies is hindered.

The system taken in the single-phase field of the metastable diagram in Fig. 2 is expected to have a behavior as interesting as the material in the two-phase field describing the colloidal equilibrium. In the long run, the material in the single-phase field of the metastable phase diagram is unstable because it eventually should find itself in the two-phase field of the stable phase diagram. However, as has been mentioned above, the system is unable to decompose unless a sufficient amount of structural vacancies is available. The vacancies should eventually neutralize the charged phases and thus provide the transition from the metastable to the stable diagram by producing really equilibrium phases with "built-in" structural vacancies restoring the charge balance. Since the process of the transition to the two-phase mixture, dictated by the stable phase diagram, is actually controlled by the rate of generation of structural vacancies, it is extremely slow. Therefore the coarsening of the mesoscopic state, which is possible only after the charge neutrality is restored, is controlled by the "slow" vacancy flux from the external surfaces.

Thus the structural vacancies play the role of the third component without which the decomposition proves to be impossible. The volume fraction of the precipitate phase in this case is determined by the concentration of structural vacancies (which is usually extremely low). Under these circumstances the kinetics of growth of the stable (electrically neutral) precipitate particles will be determined by the transport of structural vacancies from the crystal surfaces. The latter is a very slow process.

This situation is very similar to the situation occurring in a two-phase alloy with a large crystal lattice mismatch if the alloy is within the two-phase field of the stable (stress-free) diagram, but it is within a single-phase field of the coherent metastable phase diagram. $^{14,15}$ The decomposition in such a system is also completely controlled by the vacancy flux from the vacancy sources since the vacancies, lifting the elastic coherency, eliminate the elastic strain and thus provide the transition from the metastable to the stable phase diagram. As a result, an ultrafine dispersion (the $K$ state) consisting of atomic-scale incoherent particles of precipitate phase is formed. As in the relevant case, the coarsening of this structure is controlled by vacancy flux and thus is also extremely slow.

There is another interesting effect that also has a counterpart in coherent two-phase systems with a strong strain-induced interaction caused by the crystal lattice mismatch. This is a violation of the Gibbs lever rule. The lever rule implies that the miscibility gap as well as the equilibrium compositions of both coexisting phases do not depend on the overall stoichiometry $\bar{c}$. As has been shown by Roitburd $^{16}$ and Cahn and Larche, $^{17}$ there are conditions where the lever rule in the two-phase elastically coherent systems is not satisfied. A similar effect, the violation of the lever rule, is predicted here for the material within the "colloidal" region of the metastable diagram in Fig. 2. This effect is illustrated by Fig. 4, where the concentration dependences of the volume fraction $\omega_{\text{eq}}$ of the precipitate phase and the miscibility gap parameter $x_{\text{eq}}$ in the crystalline colloidal dispersion are shown by solid lines, while the dependences corresponding to the lever rule are shown by dashed lines. Unlike the situation in the Gibbs additive thermodynamics, the compositions of precipitate and matrix phases in the colloidal phase prove to be a function of the overall stoichiometry (Fig. 5). This analogy in the behavior of a two-phase material with a strain-induced interaction and material with an electrostatic interaction is actually rooted in the fact that both interactions have an infinite range (the strain-induced interaction is dipole-dipole like; it decays with the separation distance $r$ between the finite elements of the coherent precipitate phase as $1/r^3$).

The conclusions concerning the structure of the colloidal coherent dispersion, obtained above, actually follow from the pure energetic consideration. They do not take into account the kinetic factors. In reality, the slow kinetics may make it difficult to observe the periodical particle array predicted from thermodynamic arguments. Indeed, the nucleation of particles occurs randomly. Although nuclei grow to approximately the same size, commensurate with $L$, they still stay randomly distributed. A further reduction of the electrostatic energy may occur only by a spatial rearrangement of the particles which requires them to have a rigid-body translational movement. This structure refinement is a very sluggish process. It does not produce a driving force as large as the initial one, leading to an initial growth of each particle, because the refinement does not change the precipitate phase volume. Therefore, in the most cases, the initial random distribution of the precipitates with approximately equal size could still be expected.

The theory proposed in this paper assumes a pure Coulomb interaction between ions. This assumption is good for insulating crystals and semiconductors where the density of charge carriers is very low. Then the Debye screening effect can be neglected. If this is not the case, Debye screening may modify the obtained results.

Debye screening introduces the screening length parameter $r_D$. If heterogeneities have a typical length which is substantially greater than $r_D$, the electrostatic interaction can be regarded as a short-range interaction. Being short range, it is just reduced to the bulk and interfacial free energies. Therefore a system with large-scale charged precipitates can coarsen as any conventional two-phase mixture. Otherwise, if heterogeneities have a typical length which is substantially less than $r_D$, the screening effect is negligible. If this is the case, all results

obtained in this paper, and particularly the complete arrest of the particles growth, prove to be valid.

As has been shown above, the typical particle size characterizing the inhomogeneity scale is $R_{00}$. Therefore the condition which has to be met to describe the effects of the colloidal equilibrium is $r_{D} / R_{00} \gg 1$. Otherwise, if $r_{D} / R_{00} \ll 1$, the system behaves in a conventional way; i.e., the two-phase mixture undergoes coarsening.

In the border case $r_{D} / R_{00} \sim 1$, the particle growth may be hindered by the electrostatic interaction, but the electrostatic interaction is not high enough to completely arrest the coarsening. In principle, the electrostatic barrier may be overcome by thermal fluctuations.

Doping of the material may substantially affect the colloidal state. If dopant ions have a mixed valency (such as Ti, Mo, and so on), the elements with mixed valency can adjust their valency to maintain the local neutrality of the system during decomposition. If this is the case, decomposition occurs by the conventional mechanism including the coarsening process, i.e., without the formation of the mesoscopic phase.

## APPLICATION TO THE Pb(Mg, Nb)O₃ SYSTEM

The proposed theoretical model may be used to explain the mechanism of formation of a stable mesoscopic structure in relaxor ferroelectrics and particularly in the oxides with the perovskite structure. Let us consider a typical example of $\mathrm{Pb}\left(\mathrm{Mg}_{1 / 3}, \mathrm{Nb}_{2 / 3}\right) \mathrm{O}_{3}$. The structure transformations in this compound occur within the $B$-sublattice, completely occupied by "small" cations $\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\mathrm{Nb}^{5+}$. The $\mathrm{Mg}_{1 / 3}, \mathrm{Nb}_{2 / 3}$ stoichiometry provides the charge balance of the homogeneous state. Experimental observations for this system $^{6-8}$ have reported the appearance of stable nanoscale particles of the ordered phase with $\left\{\frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{2}\right\}$ order which do not undergo coarsening. This order corresponds to the $\mathrm{Mg}_{1 / 2}, \mathrm{Nb}_{1 / 2}$ stoichiometry. However, the $\mathrm{Mg}_{1 / 2}, \mathrm{Nb}_{1 / 2}$ stoichiometry would violate the charge balance (if the stoichiometries of $\mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{Pb}$ do not change). If the latter is the case, the particles of the $\mathrm{Mg}_{1 / 2}, \mathrm{Nb}_{1 / 2}$ phase (if they appear) should be electrically charged. This is exactly the situation discussed in this paper. Therefore we may expect that the observed stable inhomogeneous state in this system that does not undergo coarsening is actually a crystalline colloidal state. The proposed model allows us to make certain estimates and compare them with observation results.

Assuming the typical values $\Delta z \sim 0.1 e, \Delta c_{0} \sim 0.1$, $\epsilon \sim 10, v_{0} \sim 10^{-23} \mathrm{~cm}^{3}$, and $\gamma_{s} \sim 100 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$, we may estimate from Eq. (21) the length parameter $L \sim R_{00}$ and thus the stable particle size $R_{\text {eq }}$, which is of the order of $L$ [see Eq. (19)]:

$$
L=\left[\gamma_{s} \epsilon\left(\frac{v_{0}}{\Delta z \Delta c_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]^{1 / 3} \sim 10^{-7} \mathrm{~cm}=1 \mathrm{~nm}.
$$

This size is of the same order of magnitude as the size of segregations observed experimentally (2-5 nm) (Refs. 6 and 7) and assumed theoretically $^{3}$ in order to explain the electric properties of these materials.

If our model is applicable to relaxor materials such as $\mathrm{Pb}\left(\mathrm{Mg}_{1 / 3}, \mathrm{Nb}_{2 / 3}\right) \mathrm{O}_{3}$, the following predictions could be made.

(1) The precipitates forming the crystalline colloidal dispersion should be electrically charged. They may become neutral only later when the flux of nonstoichiometry vacancies, generated on the surfaces, restores the local charge balance. After that, conventional growth and coarsening processes could be expected. However, the rate of these processes seems to be very low because it is controlled by the vacancy generation rate. The rate could be higher in thin films where vacancy generation is facilitated.

(2) The stable colloidal state cannot be formed if the $B$ sites are occupied by cations with the same valency. The colloidal state should be expected for cations with different but fixed valencies. The addition of cations with mixed valencies, such as Ti, may completely wipe out the colloidal state since the local charge balance can be always restored by the valence change. Instead, coarsening of precipitates with conventional $\left\{\frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{2}\right\}$ long-range order would occur.

(3) The stable colloidal dispersion could be expected if the screening radius is much higher than $L \sim 10^{-7} \mathrm{~cm}$. Doping, which reduces the screening length, may transform this structure into a conventional two-phase mixture.

## APPENDIX: CALCULATION OF THE ELECTROSTATIC ENERGY

Let us consider a Bravais sublattice of crystal lattice sites completely occupied by two different ions with charges $z_{2}$ and $z_{1}$. The charge distribution on lattice sites of the sublattice is described by the function

$$
q(\mathbf{r})=z_{1}[1-c(\mathbf{r})]+z_{2} c(\mathbf{r}), \quad \text { (A1) }
$$

where $c(\mathbf{r})$ are the occupation numbers equal to 1 , if the site $r$ is occupied by a cation of charge $z_{2}$, and 0 if the site is occupied by a cation of charge $z_{1}$. Equation (A1) may be rewritten as

$$
q(\mathbf{r})=\bar{q}+\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)[c(\mathbf{r})-\bar{c}], \quad \text { (A2) }
$$

where $\bar{q}=z_{1}(1-\bar{c})+z_{2} \bar{c}$ is the average charge per a crystal site and $\bar{c}$ is the overall stoichiometry, the fraction of sites occupied by cations of charge $z_{2}$. By definition, the average charge $\bar{q}$ per site provides the local charge balance of the stable homogeneous state. The deviation of the charge at the site $\mathbf{r}$ from the value $\bar{q}$, providing a local

neutrality, is characterized by the function
$$
\Delta q(\mathbf{r})=\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)[c(\mathbf{r})-\bar{c}], \tag{A3}
$$
where $\Delta c(\mathbf{r})=c(\mathbf{r})-\bar{c}$ is the local variation of the occupation probability. The $k$-space representation of the Coulomb electrostatic energy, caused by a charge inhomogeneity (by a deviation from the local charge neutrality), is determined by the equation
$$
E_{\mathrm{el}}=2 \pi \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime} \frac{|\Delta \tilde{q}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{\epsilon k^{2}},\tag{A4}
$$
where
$$
\Delta \tilde{q}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{r}} \Delta q(\mathbf{r}) \exp (-\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})
$$
is the lattice-site Fourier transform of $\Delta q(\mathbf{r}), \epsilon$ is the dielectric constant, and $V$ the total volume of the system. The summation in (A4) is carried out over all points $\mathbf{k}$ within the first Brillouin zone of the crystal lattice permitted by the periodic boundary conditions, besides the point $\mathbf{k}=0$ (the prime on the sum implies that the term corresponding to $\mathbf{k}=0$ is omitted). The term at $\mathbf{k}=0$ vanishes since, by definition, $\Delta \tilde{q}(0)=\Delta \tilde{c}(0)=0$. Therefore, the electrostatic energy may be rewritten as
$$
E_{\mathrm{el}}=2 \pi\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime} \frac{|\Delta \tilde{c}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{\epsilon k^{2}}.\tag{A5}
$$

Let us specify the general equation (A5) for a specific charge inhomogeneity caused by a set of precipitates of the same radius with composition $c_{2}$ within the matrix of composition $c_{1}$. Neglecting the short-range-order contribution, we may present the average electrostatic energy in the form
$$
\bar{E}_{\mathrm{el}}=2 \pi\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime} \frac{|\langle\Delta \tilde{c}(\mathbf{k})\rangle|^{2}}{\epsilon k^{2}},\tag{A6}
$$
where
$$
\langle\Delta \tilde{c}(\mathbf{k})\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\langle\Delta c(\mathbf{r})\rangle \exp (-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})\tag{A7}
$$
and $\langle\Delta c(\mathbf{r})\rangle=\langle c(\mathbf{r})\rangle-\bar{c}$ is a local average variation of the occupation number $c(\mathbf{r}) ;\langle c(\mathbf{r})\rangle=c_{2}$ if the point $\mathbf{r}$ is within the precipitate phase and $c_{1}$ if it is in the parent phase. Then the function $\langle c(\mathbf{r})\rangle$ can be presented in the form
$$
\langle c(\mathbf{r})\rangle=c_{2} \Theta(\mathbf{r})+c_{1}[1-\Theta(\mathbf{r})],\tag{A8}
$$
where the function $\Theta(\mathbf{r})$ is the shape function. The shape function $\Theta(\mathbf{r})$ is defined to be equal to 1 if the vector $\mathbf{r}$ is within the precipitate phase and zero otherwise. The Fourier transform of (A8) gives
$$
\langle\Delta \tilde{c}(\mathbf{k})\rangle=\left(c_{2}-c_{1}\right) \Theta(\mathbf{k})+c_{1} \delta_{\mathbf{k} 0},\tag{A9}
$$
where $\delta_{\mathbf{k} 0}$ is the function equal to 1 at $\mathbf{k}=0$ and zero otherwise and
$$
\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{r}} \Theta(\mathbf{r}) \exp (-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}).\tag{A10}
$$

Substituting (A9) into (A6) gives
$$
\bar{E}_{\mathrm{el}}=2 \pi\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)^{2}\left(c_{2}-c_{1}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{V} \sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime} \frac{|\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}}{\epsilon k^{2}}.\tag{A11}
$$

For the system of spherical precipitates of equal radius, the function $\Theta(\mathbf{r})$ may be presented as a sum of shape functions of the precipitates:
$$
\Theta(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{j} \Theta\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right)_{0},\tag{A12}
$$
where $\Theta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{j})_{0}$ is the shape function of the $j$ th precipitate whose center is located at the point labeled by the vector $\mathbf{R}_{j}$. It is equal to 1 if $\mathbf{r}$ is within the $j$ th precipitate and zero otherwise. The Fourier transform of (A12) is
$$
\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{k})=\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{k})_{0} \sum_{j} \exp \left(-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{R}_{j}\right).\tag{A13}
$$

For a periodical arrangement of the precipitates, the sum in (A13) (it is a form factor) is equal at $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{K}$ to the total number of precipitates $N_{1}$ and is equal to zero at all other points, with $\mathbf{K}$ being a reciprocal-lattice vector corresponding to the translations of the macrolattice formed by the precipitates. Using this property of the sum in (A13) in substituting (A13) into (A11) gives
$$
\bar{E}_{\mathrm{el}}=2 \pi\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)^{2}\left(c_{2}-c_{1}\right)^{2} \frac{1}{\Omega} \sum_{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime} \frac{\left|\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{K})_{0}\right|^{2}}{\epsilon K^{2}},\quad \text{(A14)}
$$
where $\Omega=V / N_{1}$ is the volume per precipitate and the summation in (A14) is carried out over the reciprocal-lattice sites of the macroscopic lattice of the precipitates other than $\mathbf{K}=0$. If all sizes of a precipitate are substantially larger than the interatomic distances, the Fourier transform $\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{k})_{0}$ may be presented as an integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{k})_{0} & =\sum_{\mathbf{r}} \Theta(\mathbf{r})_{0} \exp (-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}) \\
& \approx \frac{1}{v_{0}} \int d^{3} r \Theta(\mathbf{r})_{0} \exp (-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}),
\end{aligned}\tag{A15}
$$
where $v_{0}$ is the volume per atom. Taking into account (A15), we have
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\Theta}(\mathbf{K})_{0} & \approx \frac{1}{v_{0}} \int \Theta(\mathbf{r})_{0} \exp (-i \mathbf{K} \mathbf{r}) d^{3} r \\
& =\frac{4 \pi R^{3}}{3 v_{0}}\left(3 \frac{K R \cos K R-\sin K R}{(K R)^{3}}\right),
\end{aligned}\tag{A16}
$$
where $R$ is the precipitate radius.
