# Compositional trends in glass transition temperatures in
inorganic network glasses

Richard Kerner *

LPTMC, Université Pierre et Marie Curie - CNRS UMR 7600, Tour 22, 4-ème étage, Boite 142, 4 Place Jussieu, 75005 Paris, France

Available online 26 March 2007

## Abstract

We present a simple mathematical model of glass transition based on the analysis of molecular agglomeration in overcooled liquids. With simple assumptions concerning local configurations and their and bonding energies, and with elementary combinatorics we are able to derive the dependence of the glass transition temperature on chemical composition in non-organic covalent glasses.

The success of the theory of crystalline solid state is based on the use of *dual space* whose symmetry, imposed by the symmetry of crystal itself, enables one to quantize the wave vectors of phonons, to introduce the Brillouin zones, and to derive many thermal, optical and electromag- netic properties of crystals. In structural glasses which can be described by Zachariasen's concept of the *Continuous Random Network* (CRN), the use of dual space is not help- ful anymore because it becomes infinitely chaotic and intri- cate, although locally at least glasses do not differ very much from corresponding crystalline counterparts. For example, regular $Si(O_{\downarrow})_{4}$ tetrahedra are found in both crystalline and amorphous $SiO_{2}$ ; similar structures can be observed in $Ge_{x}Se_{(1-x)}$ glasses. Other regular local struc tures, six-membered boroxol rings or edge-sharing tetrahe- dra, appear in borate or chalcogenide glasses, respectively. This local order is rapidly destroyed in the medium range due to the relative weakness of angular constraints.

The Stochastic Agglomeration Theory (SAT) is based on the analysis of stable local configurations in real space, and modelizes a progressive buildup of random networks during the glass transition process. Taking into account the energy landscape and the entropic factors SAT provides a new definition of glass transition temperature $T_{g}$ relating it with minimal fluctuation condition.

The formulae for $T_{g}$ deduced from the stochastic agglomeration models have found a spectacular confirma- tion in recent years due to the novel experimental tech- niques introduced by Boolchand and collaborators [1,2]. The new approach to the measurements of $T_{g}$ is called *Modulated Differential Scanning Calorimetry* (MDSC). The trends in $T_{g}$ behavior are parameter free and can be measured very reliably from the reversing heat flow by going up and down with $T$. The advantage is that the $T_{g}$ so defined is devoid of kinetic and aging effects. The MDSC method has also made possible the discovery of the *revers- ibility window* in many network glasses i.e. the region of composition in which glassy networks remain isostatic its internal structure evolving keeping the number of con- straints per atom constant.

The model of glass formation [3-7] is based on a detailed description of agglomeration and growth of micro-clusters that already exist in hot melts about to undergo glass transition. In order to predict structural features of the resulting random network, we must have a reasonable model imitating in which way these clusters grew and

A hot melt at constant temperature contains a lot of clusters of different sizes and shapes. Let us denote by $k$ the number of atoms in a cluster. At a given temperature negligibly few ones are bigger than $k_{\max }$. The statistical distribution of cluster sizes and shapes depends on the temperature. At a given $T$ some of the clusters split into smaller units, while some of the smaller ones agglomerate creating larger entities; however, on the average the balance is maintained and the mean distribution of clusters in the space of all possible configurations remains constant.

But let the temperature decrease, and the distribution of probabilities of finding a given number of clusters of size $k$ will also evolve: the higher the temperature, the greater the 'population' of clusters corresponding to lower values of $k$, and vice versa. It is quite obvious that when the temperature slowly decreases, the 'center of gravity' of the overall distribution is displaced slowly towards greater values of $k$; at the same time, $k_{\max }$ is growing, too. We believe that the best way to get more insight into the structural properties of a glassy network is to reconstruct as precisely as possible the very process of its creation, which occurs during the glass transition and stops at the temperature $T$ slightly under $T_{\mathrm{g}}$, and consists essentially in the saturation of all bonds between the micro-clusters that already existed in the hot melt. The whole phenomenon is an off-equilibrium thermodynamical process, during which, starting from some moment, clusters agglomerate with the exponentially growing speed. This results in an exponential growth of viscosity and in a sharp variation of heat capacity $C_{p}$.

We suppose that the energies stored in a given cluster during its agglomeration are additive i.e. the energy is locally conserved. This means that we can evaluate the probability of observing a given cluster by summing up all the agglomeration pathways that lead to its creation from elementary 'singlets', by multiplying the probabilities of each transition by corresponding statistical weights and Boltzmann factors.

Let us take as an example the binary As-Se glass. Consider an average cluster as shown in Fig. 1. There are two possible kinds of sites on its rim: a selenium atom with one unsaturated bond, and an As atom presenting one or two free bonds; these are indicated by $x=\circ-$ and $y=\boldsymbol{\ell}$. There is an intermediate site $z=\bullet-$, but it is bound to transform immediately into an $x$ or into an $y$, so it can be not taken under consideration.

To each site a Se or a As atom can attach itself, reproducing one of the initial configurations, in the specific combinations shown in the second column of Fig. 2. Each agglomeration step is synonymous with complete saturation of all the bonds on the rim, so that only two types of sites (denoted by $x$ and $y$ ) observed, assuming that the growth is of dendritic type (no small rings present).

All possible agglomeration processes are represented in Diagram 1.

The Boltzmann factors depend on the energy barriers characteristic for the three types of bonds, $A-A, A-B$ and $B-B$:

$$
\epsilon=\frac{E_{A A}}{k T}, \quad \eta=\frac{E_{A B}}{k T}, \quad \alpha=\frac{E_{B B}}{k T}. \tag{1}
$$

Diagram 1. States, steps and matrix entries for $m_A=2$, $m_B=3$ (e.g. $\text{As}_c \text{Se}_{(1-c)}$ glass) case.

Consider the dendritic growth with only two types of sites appearing all the time. Given an arbitrary initial state $(p_x,p_y)$, the new state results from taking into account all possible ways of saturating the bonds of the previous state's sites by the available external atoms. The un-normalized probability factors are displayed in the figure. The non-normalized probability factors can be arranged in a matrix

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
2(1-c)\text{e}^{-\epsilon} & 8(1-c)^2\text{e}^{-2\eta}+12c(1-c)\text{e}^{-\eta-\alpha} \\
3c\text{e}^{-\eta} & 12c(1-c)\text{e}^{-\eta-\alpha}+18c^2\text{e}^{-2\alpha}
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{2}
$$

The normalized transition matrix is written as

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}
M_{xx} & M_{xy} \\
M_{yx} & M_{yy}
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}
M_{xx} & 1-M_{yy} \\
1-M_{xx} & M_{yy}
\end{pmatrix}, \tag{3}
$$

where the entries are obtained by normalizing the columns of the matrix (2).

$$
M_{xx}=\frac{2(1-c)\xi}{2(1-c)\xi+3c} \quad \text{and} \quad M_{yy}=\frac{3c\mu}{2(1-c)+3c\mu}, \tag{4}
$$

where we have introduced the abbreviated notation $\xi=\text{e}^{\eta-\epsilon}$ and $\mu=\text{e}^{\eta-\alpha}$. The action of matrix $M$ on the probability distribution $(p_x,p_y)$ symbolizes the new layer creation, with an altered probability distribution of sites $x$ and $y$, $(p_x',p_y')$.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
p_x' \\
p_y'
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}
M_{xx} & M_{xy} \\
M_{yx} & M_{yy}
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
p_x \\
p_y
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{5}
$$

The difference $\vec{p}'-\vec{p}$ can be interpreted as the derivative of the probability distribution with respect to time or another parameter describing the advance in agglomeration, like the average number of layers at a given time. This scheme can be easily generalized to the case of arbitrary valence, say $m_A$ and $m_B$. In that case, the stochastic $2 \times 2$ matrix has the same form as (3), but with the entries given by

$$
M_{xx}=1-M_{yx}=\frac{m_A(1-c)\xi}{m_A(1-c)\xi+m_Bc},
$$

$$
M_{xy}=1-M_{yy}=\frac{m_A(1-c)}{m_A(1-c)+m_Bc\mu}.
$$

The eigenvalues of this matrix are $\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_2=M_{xx}-M_{xy}=M_{yy}-M_{yx}$, with $|\lambda_2|<1$, so that in the long run, after the matrix $M$ was applied many times (creating many consecutive layers), only the first eigenvector will survive. This stationary eigenvector (corresponding to the eigenvalue 1) is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
p_x^\infty \\
p_y^\infty
\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{M_{xy}+M_{yx}}\begin{pmatrix}
M_{xy} \\
M_{yx}
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{6}
$$

On the surface of clusters, when averaged over a great number, $p_x$ is the $A$ concentration and $p_y$ is the $B$ concentration. The high homogeneity exhibited by known glass structures suggests that even in relatively small clusters, deviations from the average modifier concentration $c$ must be negligible. Thus, *in the bulk*, the $B$ concentration should be equal to $c$. Therefore, the condition of minimal fluctuations in the bulk concentration can be interpreted as the glass transition condition. The asymptotic state is thus fixed by the external concentration. It follows that the eigenvector (6) must be equal to the average distribution vector $(1-c,c)$, therefore it is enough to satisfy the following equation:

$$
p_y=c=\frac{M_{yx}}{M_{xy}+M_{yx}}=\frac{m_Am_Bc(1-c)+m_B^2c^2\mu}{m_A^2(1-c)^2\xi+2m_Am_Bc(1-c)+m_B^2c^2\mu}. \tag{7}
$$

In the low concentration limit $c \ll 1$ and we can neglect the quadratic terms $c^2$ in the formula (7), which leads to a much simpler equation

$$
p_y=c \simeq \frac{m_Am_Bc(1-c)}{m_A^2(1-c)^2\xi+2m_Am_Bc(1-c)}. \tag{8}
$$

In the low concentration limit condition (7) leads to the following equation for $c$,

$$
c(1-c)\left[(m_A^2\xi-m_Am_B)-c(m_A^2\xi-2m_Am_B)\right]=0. \tag{9}
$$

Eq. (9) has three solutions: the two obvious ones, $c=0$ and $c=1$, and the third one, which can be identified as the definition of the glass transition temperature:

$$
c=\frac{1-\frac{m_{A}}{m_{B}} \xi}{2-\frac{m_{A}}{m_{B}} \xi}. \tag{10}
$$

In the case when the $A$-component (e.g. selenium) is a glass former, the initial slope of $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ at $c=0$, when the modifier atoms are still scarce, has a particularly simple form. When $c \to 0$ and $T_{\mathrm{g}} \to T_{\mathrm{g} 0}$, (the glass transition temperature for pure selenium) in formula (10), we must have

$$
\left.\xi\right|_{T=T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{E_{A B}-E_{A A}}{k T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}}=\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}. \tag{11}
$$

Taking the logarithm on both sides, we get

$$
\frac{E_{A B}-E_{A A}}{k T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}=\ln \left(\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}\right) \text { or } E_{A B}-E_{A A}-k\left(\ln m_{B}-\ln m_{A}\right)=0 . \tag{12}
$$

Formula (12) can be interpreted as the well known relationship for the free energy minimum, $\delta F=\delta U-T \delta S$. As a matter of fact, when a single modifier atom $(B)$ arrives in the melt at $c=0$, the energy variation $\delta U$ equals $E_{A B}-E_{A A}$. The entropy variation can be evaluated following Boltzmann's definition of configurational entropy, $S=k \ln W$, where $W$ is the number of configurations available to a system. Here two atoms $A A$ could create $m_{A}^{2}$ bonds, whereas the couple of atoms $B$ can create $m_{A} m_{B}$ bonds. The corresponding entropy variation will be

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta S & =k\left[\ln \left(m_{A} m_{B}\right)-\ln \left(m_{A}^{2}\right)\right] \\
& =k\left[\ln m_{B}+\ln m_{A}-2 \ln m_{A}\right]=k\left[\ln m_{B}-\ln m_{A}\right]. \tag{13}
\end{aligned}
$$

However, the interpretation of this formula is quite different than in classical statistical mechanics, instead of configurations we consider different bond creation processes.

The derivative of $c$ with respect to the temperature $T$ is easily found, and its inverse gives the 'magic formula' for $\frac{\mathrm{d} T_{\mathrm{g}}}{\mathrm{d} c}$ at $c=0$ : This defines the slope of the function $T_{\mathrm{g}}(c)$, which is an important measurable quantity:

$$
\left[\frac{\mathrm{d} T_{\mathrm{g}}}{\mathrm{d} c}\right]_{c=0}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}{\ln \left(\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}\right)}, \tag{14}
$$

where we used the fact that $\frac{\mathrm{d} \xi}{\mathrm{d} T}=-\frac{1}{T} \xi \ln \xi$, and $\left.\xi\right|_{c=0}=\left(m_{B} /\right.$ $\left.m_{A}\right)$.

The formula valid for any concentration is more complicated:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} T_{\mathrm{g}}}{\mathrm{d} c}=T_{\mathrm{g}} \frac{\left[\left(1-\frac{m_{A}}{m_{B}} \xi\right)+\left(1-\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}} \mu\right)\right]^{2}}{\left(\frac{m_{A}}{m_{B}}-\mu\right) \xi \ln \xi-\left(\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}-\xi\right) \mu \ln \mu}. \tag{15}
$$

## Discussion

Formula (14) has been checked against the experiment very successfully, in more than 30 different compounds. In some cases the formula does not seem to work well; usually it comes from the change of valence of certain atoms provoked by the influence of the surrounding substrate.

Other interesting cases are shown in Fig. 3.

The singularity in this formula when $m_{A}=m_{B}$ (i.e. a mixture of two different glass formers with the same coordination number) can be easily dealt with as was shown by M. Micoulaut. If the glass transition temperature of the pure glass-former $A$ is $T_{\mathrm{g} 0}$, and that of the pure glass-former $B$ is $T_{\mathrm{g} 1}$, then the 'pure states' $c=0$ or $c=1$ represent stationary solutions of (7) and can be factorized out. The non-trivial condition for the glass forming is given by (7) Now, using the limit conditions at $c \to 0, T_{\mathrm{g}}=T_{\mathrm{g} 0}$ and $c \to 1, T_{\mathrm{g}}=T_{\mathrm{g} 1}$, the expressions $\xi$ and $\mu$ at the arbitrary temperature $T$ can be written as

$$
\xi(T)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{E_{A B}-E_{A A}}{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}} \frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}{T}}=\left(\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}\right)^{\frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}{T}} ; \quad \mu(T)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{E_{A B}-E_{B B}}{T_{\mathrm{g} 1}} \frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 1}}{T}}=\left(\frac{m_{A}}{m_{B}}\right)^{\frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 1}}{T}} . \tag{16}
$$

![](./images/812004052459061250_6.jpg)

Fig. 3. Other examples of the initial slope formula agreement with experiments.

![](./images/812004052459061250_7.jpg)

Fig. 4. $T_{\text{g}}(c)$ curves for S-Se and Te-Se binary glasses.

Substituting these expressions into (14) and taking the limit $c \to 0$, we get

$$
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d} T_{\mathrm{g}}}{\mathrm{d} c}\right|_{c=0}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}\left[1-\left(\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}\right)^{\frac{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}-T_{\mathrm{g} 1}}{T_{\mathrm{g} 0}}}\right]}{\ln \left(\frac{m_{B}}{m_{A}}\right)}.
\tag{17}
$$

Even when $m_A = m_B$, this formula has a well defined limit. Indeed, set $\frac{m_B}{m_A}=1+\epsilon$, and then develop the numerator and the denominator of the above equation in powers of $\epsilon$. In the limit when $\epsilon \to 0$ we arrive at a simple linear dependence which is in agreement with common sense and with experiment as well, namely (see Fig. 4)

$$
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d} T_{\mathrm{g}}}{\mathrm{d} c}\right|_{c=0}=T_{\mathrm{g} 1}-T_{\mathrm{g} 0}.
\tag{18}
$$

This formula is also confirmed by many experiments, e.g. performed on selenium-sulfur mixtures (where $m_A = m_B = 2$). The deviations from the linear law (18) observed in the Se $-$ Te binary glass are explained by the fact of the chemical properties of tellurium, which changes its valence from 2 to 3 in presence of selenium.
