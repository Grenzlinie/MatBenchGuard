# High-temperature Hf-site-interchange chemistry in $\text{LiNbO}_3$ and $\text{LiTaO}_3$

Dunbar P. Birnie, III
Department of Materials Science and Engineering, University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85721

Gary L. Catchen
Department of Nuclear Engineering, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park,
Pennsylvania 16802

(Received 17 September 1992; accepted 3 February 1993)

We explain observed Hf-site interchange in $\text{LiNbO}_3$ and $\text{LiTaO}_3$ at high temperatures using solid-state defect chemistry reactions. The model takes into account fully the effects of nonstoichiometry on the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric phase transition. Specifically, we use this model to interpret the temperature dependence of the Hf-site interchange that we measured using perturbed-angular-correlation (PAC) spectroscopy. In this context, the site interchange is an equilibrium, thermodynamic process that involves the partitioning of Hf ions between Li and group-V (Nb and Ta) sites. The Hf ions replace group-V ions by pushing them from their normal sites to the Li sublattice. Based on the temperature dependence of the site occupancy, this reaction requires approximately 2.2 to 2.3 eV.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Lithium niobate, $\text{LiNbO}_3$, and lithium tantalate, $\text{LiTaO}_3$, are isostructural, electro-optic, and piezo- electric materials that have interesting and important technological applications. These materials are used, for example, to produce optical-waveguide switching devices, $^{1}$ acoustic-optic modulators, $^{2,3}$ and holographic optical recording devices. $^{4}$ For many of these applica tions, dopant atoms are important for determining the final properties of the material. For example, optical waveguides are formed in lithium niobate by diffusing titanium atoms into the surface. $^{5-7}$ Also, for holographic storage applications, lithium niobate is often doped with iron. $^{8}$ Because the dopant atoms strongly affect the material properties, it is necessary to understand the dissolution and transport mechanisms of the dopants in the crystal lattice, which includes understanding the associated temperature variations.

The PAC technique has been used successfully via the $^{181}$Ta probe to investigate phase transitions in several ferroic ternary metal oxides, $^{9-12}$ including both $\text{LiNbO}_3$ and $\text{LiTaO}_3$. $^{13,14}$ Because this probe via its site occu pancy shows some interesting and unusual crystal chem- istry in $\text{LiNbO}_3$ and $\text{LiTaO}_3$, $^{13,14}$ we focus on hafnium ($\text{Hf}^{4+}$) site-occupation and site-interchange chemistry at high temperatures in these two crystals. The defect chemistry of Hf in these materials, for which we provide a model, presents a paradigm for dopant behavior in other ternary metal-oxide crystals. Moreover, investi- gating the solid-state chemistry of Hf specifically in $\text{LiNbO}_3$ and $\text{LiTaO}_3$ may yield a better understanding of the general dopant chemistry in these two materials.

To integrate the subjects of dopant crystal chemistry and PAC spectroscopy, we present a review of the former and a brief description of the latter.

Dopant incorporation into ternary metal-oxide crys- tals often has unforeseeable implications on properties of the crystal. For example, when Ti is diffused into lithium niobate (to increase the refractive index), a concomitant reduction in the ferroelectric Curie temperature ($T_{\text{C}}$) occurs that may cause domain reversal in near-surface diffused layers. $^{15,16}$ Adding the dopant itself does not always directly cause these property changes. Sometimes the associated "charge-balancing" defects actually cause them. $^{17}$ For example, in the case of Ti addition, the reduction in $T_{\text{C}}$ has been directly correlated with the change in cation-vacancy concentration rather than with the concentration of Ti itself. $^{18}$ Thus, a careful analysis of the native defect chemistry is important as a basis for understanding dopant incorporation and site interchange.

Because two different cation sites are available in each structure, the "1+ site" and the "5+ site," dopant substitution may involve several subtle chemical effects acting concurrently. Although these two sites are similar in size and they both have quasi-octahedral symmetry, substitution into either one requires widely different charge compensation, which we discuss further in the background section. Because the size and the symmetry of these two sites are very similar, site interchange of various metal ions may occur between these two available sites. This effect seems to occur for the hafnium dopant. Two distinct signals can be distinguished in the high-temperature PAC

## II. BACKGROUND
This section presents topics that are relevant to understanding the measurement of Hf-site interchange in these two crystals. First, we present a simple description of the crystal structure of these two isostructural materials. Second, we discuss the point-defect chemistry and nonstoichiometry that determines how dopants can be incorporated into the crystal. Third, we analyze the high-temperature defect chemistry as it impacts the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition. This information is important because our measurements have been carried out in the vicinity of the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition temperature, $T_{\text{C}}$. Fourth, we survey the little that is known about dopant incorporation and site interchange in these structures. Finally, we briefly describe the PAC technique.

### A. Crystal-structure description
The crystal structures of lithium niobate and lithium tantalate have identical symmetries, and only slight differences in atom positions.¹⁹⁻²⁴ At low temperatures, the structures can be represented as a hexagonal-close-packed lattice of oxygen atoms in which group-V atoms and Li atoms are distributed among two-thirds of the octahedral sites. The particular occupation of these octahedral sites determines the two possible domain polarizations; going along the $c$-axis direction (vertical to the oxygen close-packed planes), the octahedral sites are filled in the sequence:
$$\text{V, Li, vacancy, V, Li, vacancy, etc}\ldots \tag{1a}$$
for one polarization, and in the sequence:
$$\text{V, vacancy, Li, V, vacancy, Li, etc}\ldots \tag{1b}$$
for the opposite polarization, in which "V" represents the location of the group-V ion ($\text{Nb}^{5+}$ or $\text{Ta}^{5+}$). One interesting aspect of this particular crystal structure is that domain reversal requires only that all lithium atoms jump from their starting sites into each of their adjacent vacant octahedral sites.

### B. Point-defects and nonstoichiometry
This simple crystal-structure description is somewhat idealized, because most lithium niobate and lithium tantalate crystals actually deviate significantly from a true one-to-one metal-atom-ratio stoichiometry. Both materials have rather broad ranges of group-V-element excess solubility in their structures.²⁵⁻³⁷ Of these two materials, only the lithium-niobate defect chemistry has been well characterized, but the dramatic similarity between the two structures and their properties strongly suggests that the lithium tantalate defect chemistry is very similar. Thus, the first crucial assumption built into the present analysis is that both $\text{LiNbO}_3$ and $\text{LiTaO}_3$ have very similar types and concentrations of native defects.

For the lithium niobate, several studies²⁶,²⁷,³⁸,³⁹ have identified that significant numbers of niobium atoms located at lithium sites cause the niobium-rich properties. Using the standard Kröger–Vink notation, these defects are written as $\text{Nb}_{\text{Li}}^{\cdots\cdots}$, in which the subscript denotes the atom's site and the superscript denotes a relative charge, which in this case is four units of charge in excess of the charge associated with the normal occupant atom ($\text{Li}^{1+}$).¹⁷

These positively charged defects must be balanced by some concentration of negatively charged defects to maintain overall charge neutrality. Using precise x-ray diffraction measurements,³⁸ these charge-balancing defects were determined to be niobium vacancies. Later work supported this explanation of the laboratory-temperature findings. These investigators showed that niobium vacancies could occur from defect clustering during cooling, and they suggested that this simple defect clustering effect would not be manifest at higher temperatures.³¹,⁴⁰,⁴¹ At higher temperatures, the most probable charge-neutrality condition requires that the levels of niobium antisite defects should be balanced by the appropriate levels of lithium vacancies:
$$4[\text{Nb}_{\text{Li}}^{\cdots\cdots}] = [V_{\text{Li}}'].\tag{2}$$

This charge-balancing condition is used throughout this paper, because the present measurements were conducted at higher temperatures.

### C. High-temperature defects
The aforementioned defect model is only rigorously correct at temperatures significantly below $T_{\text{C}}$, because point defects cause the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition in these crystals. A successful model of this transition is an order-disorder process, in which the lithium atoms become distributed between their normal sites and the adjacent vacant sites.⁴²⁻⁴⁴ The defect chemical reaction that describes the order-disorder aspect of the transition is:
$$\text{Li}_{\text{Li}}^x \to V_{\text{Li}}' + \text{Li}_{\text{i}}^\cdot.\tag{3}$$

This reaction progresses to a larger and larger extent until the two possible crystal-structure stacking sequences [which Eqs. (1a) and (1b) present] are no longer distinguishable. When the lithium atoms are evenly distributed between the two sites, the sites become structurally

identical, and this situation occurs at the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition temperature, $T_{\mathrm{C}} \cdot{ }^{42-44}$ At this level of disorder, the nonstoichiometry defects are vastly outnumbered by the randomness of the Li-site occupancy. For this reason, care must be used when describing properties of these materials, at temperatures either near or above $T_{\mathrm{C}}$.

The presence of a small concentration of group-V atoms located on the lithium sublattice (the nonstoichiometry defects described above) complicates the phase transition, because these group-V atoms must also become evenly distributed among normal and interstitial $\mathrm{Li}$ sites for the transition to occur. ${ }^{44}$ As temperature increases, this redistribution of the $\mathrm{Nb}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\cdots}$ antisite defects may happen at a different rate than for the $\mathrm{Li}^{1+}$ ions.

For nonstoichiometric crystals that undergo the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition, site numbers and the $\mathrm{Li} /$ (group-V-atom) ratio are completely conserved, and the transition does not involve either any oxygen atoms or any of the properly located group-V atoms. Instead, the Li-sublattice atoms gradually rearrange, whatever their type. One way for representing this conservation of composition is to describe the level of nonstoichiometry using the overall cation vacancy concentration (CV) as the key composition parameter. Earlier, this approach has been used successfully to model the phase transition and other properties. ${ }^{18,44}$ To emphasize the effect of the phase transition on the locations of the $\mathrm{Li}$ and group-V atoms, Tables I and II give the occupation numbers that correspond to perfectly ordered and perfectly disordered distributions of these atoms among the three available sites.

Above $T_{\mathrm{C}}$, the lithium and normally vacant sites become indistinguishable, and the occupations of these two sites must, by symmetry, be equal. However, even these large defect concentrations still conserve the total number of cation vacancies as CV. ### D. Dopant incorporation and site interchange

One of the few impurities that has been confidently assigned to a substitution site is hafnium. ${ }^{46-48}$ These measurements showed that $\mathrm{Hf}^{4+}$ substitutes into lithium sites in lithium niobate at laboratory temperature. Again, because lithium niobate and lithium tantalate have very similar structures, we assume that hafnium also substitutes for lithium in lithium tantalate. This substitution can be represented by an $\mathrm{HfO}_{2}$ solution mechanism of:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{HfO}_{2}+\frac{2}{3} \mathrm{Nb}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\cdots} \rightarrow \mathrm{Hf}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\cdots} & +2 \mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{O}}^{x} \\
& +\frac{2}{3} \mathrm{Nb}_{\mathrm{Nb}}^{x}+\frac{1}{3} \mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\prime}. \quad(5)
\end{aligned}
$$

This reaction describes the hafnium oxide solution in a manner that is consistent with the charge neutrality condition given above as Eq. (2). The reaction stoichiometries indicate that the addition of every three atoms of $\mathrm{Hf}^{4+}$ annihilates two niobium antisite defects and creates a single lithium vacancy. This reaction maintains the lithium-to-niobium ratio. The resulting increase in the cation-vacancy concentration is compatible with the creation of Hf defects that have positive charges and lithium vacancies that have negative charges.

This reaction applies to hafnium dissolving into lithium sites in the crystal at temperatures that are well below $T_{\mathrm{C}}$. Above $T_{\mathrm{C}}$, hafnium becomes disordered between the two possible lithium sites, but, because these sites are symmetrically equivalent, these disordered hafnium probes are thought to give identical PAC signatures that cannot be distinguished. Alternatively, the

high-temperature appearance of two distinct sites for hafnium in the PAC measurements results from partial jumping of the hafnium probes into the niobium sites.

Occupancy of niobium sites by hafnium, at least partly, is consistent with evidence obtained from mea- surements on several other dopant elements, which may experience site interchange in lithium niobate. For ex- ample, titanium diffusion in lithium niobate has been ex- plained in terms of titanium ions interchanging between lithium and niobium sites. $^{49}$ Also, using Mössbauer effect spectroscopy, two temperature-dependent signa- tures were observed for $Fe^{2+}$ ions in lithium niobate at temperatures near $T_{C}$ , which was explained as being associated with the disordering phase transition. $^{50}$  Other studies at laboratory and cryogenic temperatures display only one signature, $^{51,52}$ which the investigators interpreted as corresponding to nuclear quadrupole inter- actions at the niobium site. Together, these studies may indicate that site interchange is thermally activated.

Many other cation dopants have been found to substitute into multiple sites when measured by a variety of techniques at laboratory temperature. These dopants include $Eu^{3+},^{47,53-55} Nd^{3+},^{47,56} Gd^{3+},^{57,58} Cr^{3+},^{59}$ and $Fe^{3+}.^{60}$ The presence of more than one defect site in these cases may be a result of defect clustering, as well as possible multiple-site substitution. Interestingly, a model for substitution of trivalent impurities was suggested where an adjacent $Li-Nb$ site pair would be occupiedby a pair of impurity atoms. $^{61}$

## E. PAC spectroscopy
### 1. General description
During the last decade, PAC spectroscopy has emerged as an important materials characterization technique. $^{62}$ This technique is used to measure hyperfine interactions at the sites of radioactive probe atoms that are substituted into specific, well-defined lattice sites in a particular crystal.

In nonmagnetic materials, the PAC technique can measure the electric-field-gradient (efg) tensor at the lattice site of a radioactive probe ion that was substituted into the crystal. Specifically, the technique measures the interaction of the nuclear-electric quadrupole moment of an excited nuclear level in the probe-ion nucleus with extranuclear efg's produced by all of the electrons and nuclei that surround the probe nucleus. This excited nuclear level is populated by the emission of an initial $\gamma$ -ray, and it is depopulated by the emission of a final y-ray. During the lifetime of the intermediate level, the quadrupole moment of the level can interact with the extranuclear efg. This interaction changes the nuclear orientation prior to the emission of the final $\gamma$ -ray. Because the nuclear orientation changed, the direction in space where the final $\gamma$ -ray is emitted is different from where it would have been had no interaction occurred. To describe this process, we say that the nuclear-quadrupole interaction perturbs the angular correlation, i.e., spatial correlation of the $\gamma-\gamma$ cascade. This effect is indeed measurable, and it is the basis of the PAC technique.

Nuclear-quadrupole interactions fall into two cate- gories, static and time-varying. During a static inter- action, the efg appears experimentally to be essentially a time-averaged quantity, in which atomic vibrational motion in the crystal is not resolved on the PAC experi- mental timescale. Time-varying interactions arise when the efg fluctuates either in magnitude or in symmetry during the intermediate-level lifetime, i.e., motion on a slower timescale. Because detecting the initial $\gamma$ -ray selects nuclei in specific orientations, these fluctuations cause the nuclear-spin orientation of the intermediate level to become random, i.e., to relax. This effect, which can be caused by effects such as ions "hopping" in and out of lattice sites, attenuates the measured angular correlation. In this context, a primary task in a PAC experiment is to distinguish static nuclear-quadrupole interactions from time-varying interactions that cause nuclear-spin relaxation.

## 2. $^{181} Hf \to^{181} Ta$ probe chemistry
The $^{181} Hf \to^{181} Ta$ PAC probe has been used successfully to investigate a variety of complex metal oxides. $^{9-14}$ Because the chemistry of the $Hf^{4+}$ ion is very similar to that of the $Ti^{4+}$ ions and nearly identical to that of the $Zr^{4+}$ ion, the $^{181} Hf \to^{181} Ta$ probe is well suited to investigating the perovskites that contain the group-IV elements, $Ti$ and $Zr$ . The situation is not as clear in the case of the ternary metal oxides that contain the group-V elements, $Nb$ and $Ta$ . Since no group-V PAC probes are available, the question arises as to how well the $^{181} Hf \to^{181} Ta$ probe would work when substituted into compounds that contain group-V elements. From our experience, we know that the $^{181} Hf \to^{181} Ta$ probe can be used to measure nuclear-quadrupole interactions at the $Nb$ sites in the rare-earth niobates, $GdNbO_{4}$ and $NdNbO_{4},^{11}$ and in the ferroelectric perovskite $KNbO_{3}^{63}$ However, in the case of $LiNbO_{3}$ and $LiTaO_{3}$ , the $Hf^{4+}$ ion substitutes (at concentrations $<1$ at. \%) primarily into the Li sites. $^{13,14}$ This result appears to be counter intuitive. The naive expectation is that, because the $Hf^{4+}$ ion is close in charge and size to the group- $V$  ions, $Nb^{5+}$ and $Ta^{5+}$ , it should substitute into the group $V$ sites. At lower temperatures, the opposite is the case, and at sufficiently high temperatures, $Hf^{4+}$ ions can transport between the $Li$ sites and the group-V sites in $LiNbO_{3}$ and $LiTaO_{3}$ . Moreover, this process is reversible. $^{14}$ Thus, the site substitution of the probe depends on the chemistry of the parent nuclear species, in this case $^{181} Hf^{4+}$ ions, which decay by $\beta^{-}$ -emission

and populate excited levels in the the $^{181}$Ta nuclei. However, the actual nuclear-quadrupole interaction involves the daughter nuclear species, in this case $^{181}$Ta$^{5+}$ ions.

### 3. Measurement details
A recent review$^{62}$ describes the general experimental details. Two recent reports$^{13,14}$ describe the specific PAC measurements made on LiNbO$_3$ and LiTaO$_3$. The earlier report primarily describes the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition in LiTaO$_3$ in the context of an order-disorder model. The later report describes high-temperature measurements on LiNbO$_3$ and LiTaO$_3$ in which site interchange of the $^{181}$Hf $\rightarrow$ $^{81}$Ta probe was observed.

The high-temperature measurements on LiNbO$_3$ and LiTaO$_3$ were analyzed successfully using a model of two static nuclear-electric-quadrupole interactions, in which one interaction corresponded to probes located at the Li sites and the other interaction corresponded to probes located at the group-V sites. No evidence was found for any time-varying interactions. The analysis of the measured perturbation functions using this "two-site" model yielded the parameters $A_1$, $\delta_1$, $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$ and $A_2$, $\delta_2$, $\omega_4$, $\omega_5$, and $\omega_6$. At site one, the frequency set $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$ describes the interaction of the nuclear-quadrupole moment with the efg, $A_1$ describes the number of probes at site one, i.e., it is a normalization factor, and $\delta_1$ describes the extent of inhomogeneous line broadening. The other parameters describe the quantities of site two. Thus, the ratio $A_2/A_1$ that is calculated from the two-site-model parameters represents the probe-site-occupancy ratio at a specific temperature. At the lower temperatures, below $\approx$1200 K for LiNbO$_3$ and below $\approx$1350 K for LiTaO$_3$, the probe ions occupy primarily the Li sites. As temperature increases, the probe ions occupy increasing numbers of group-V sites. This process is reversible, and equilibrium is reached rapidly. To determine the thermodynamic quantities that describe the site-interchange process, we plot the logarithmic ratio of the normalization factors ($A_2/A_1$) versus inverse temperature, which Fig. 1 shows. We see that a linear fit provides a good representation of these data. In what follows, we develop a model with which to analyze the slopes and intercepts from these linear fits to the data.

### III. THERMODYNAMIC MODEL
Since all temperatures of measurement for the LiTaO$_3$ samples were well above $T_{\rm C}$, all Li-site atoms were completely disordered, i.e., they were evenly distributed between the two types of Li sites in the crystal. For a small amount of Hf substituted into the crystal, we calculate the Li$^{1+}$ ion concentrations by requiring that the cation-vacancy concentration (CV) remain constant. This constraint is important, because it affects the onset of the ferroelectric-to-paraelectric transition. Adding a small amount of Hf alters slightly the ratio of Li-site occupancy to group-V-site occupancy. For the Li$^{1+}$, Nb$^{5+}$, and Ta$^{5+}$ ions, this specific effect should not be measurable.

## IV. DISCUSSION
The site-interchange reaction described above really represents a mass-action equilibrium condition. It does not represent an actual, simultaneous interchange of the niobium and hafnium atoms as a process. Instead, it shows that the associated Hf, Li, and group-V concen- trations are dependent on each other and influence each other based on the energetics of this balance. The actual mechanisms for this site-interchange process probably depend on the presence of some concentration of vacan- cies in the crystal. These vacancy concentrations, CV, are taken into account in the model that considers these nonstoichiometric materials. These vacancies must also be interchanging sites dynamically to cause the cation- site interchange that is observed. $^{13}$ Since both $\mathrm{Hf}^{4+}$ ions and group-V ions are experiencing site interchange, the following two reactions are important:

$$
\mathrm{Hf}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\cdots}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Nb}}^{\prime \prime \prime \prime} \rightarrow \mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{Hf}_{\mathrm{Nb}}^{\prime},
\tag{12}
$$

$$
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{Nb}_{\mathrm{Nb}}^{\mathrm{x}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Nb}_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\cdots}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Nb}}^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}.
\tag{13}
$$

These two reactions are complementary to a degree; both of them represent vacancies that cause the cation-site interchange. However, the direction of vacancy changing is opposite. In fact, the sum of these reactions is simply the site-interchange reaction, Eq. (6). Although these two equations are important, we do not have enough information to learn about vacancy-site preference in the Hf-Nb site-interchange reaction.

This model is more general than a similar model that Ref. 14 presents, because it takes into account the effects of crystal nonstoichiometry. And, essentially, the effects of nonstoichiometry modify the entropy term in Eq. (11), i.e., $\ln (8 / \mathrm{CV})$ is added to $\Delta S^{\circ} / \mathrm{k}$. The enthalpies were determined by both models using the same data, and the enthalpies differ by a factor of two. This difference arises because the more general model (in this paper) describes site-interchange as a one-step process, Eq. (6), whereas the other model describes site-interchange as a two-step process, Eq. (2d) in Ref. 14. In reality, only one process takes place, but the enthalpies derived from the two different model descriptions include the effects of two distinct physical processes. Hence, no inconsistency arises between the models as a result of this difference.

## V. CONCLUSIONS
We have observed dynamic site redistribution at high temperatures for hafnium dopants dissolved in both lithium niobate and lithium tantalate. We have developed a more general model of the chemical process of hafnium distribution between lithium and group-V sites; the model gives very similar reaction energies for the two materials. This result further emphasizes

the broad similarities between the properties of the two materials. The hafnium-site-interchange mechanism is probably linked to vacancy-jumping processes that result in bulk diffusion, although these vacancy pro- cesses were not visible using the PAC spectroscopic technique. This study shows that PAC spectroscopy using the $^{181} Hf \to ^{181} Ta$ probe can provide important information about chemical transport processes in host crystals. Moreover, this study indicates that the PAC technique could be an effective tool with which to investigate the thermodynamics of site interchange in other complex ternary metal-oxide ceramics.
