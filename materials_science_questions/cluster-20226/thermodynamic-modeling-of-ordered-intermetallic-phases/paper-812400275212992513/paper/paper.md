Abstract—The recent discoveries by Williams of unusual phase diagram features for coherent phase equilibrium are explored in detail for a model that leads to a simple formulation involving only the minimization of a polynomial free energy function that includes an elastic energy term. The elastic energy term in general makes the use of the common tangent construction invalid. It requires one to distinguish between the phase diagram concepts of "field boundaries" where new phases make their appearance and "tie line ends" marking compositions of coexisting phases. The elastic energy term invalidates the phase rule and the theorem that tie line ends lie on field boundaries, and several other theorems about metastable equilibrium. Two-phase coexistence can terminate at a special point, which we call the Williams point, where a ratio of elastic to chemical energy reaches a definite value. Beyond the Williams point a first-order coherent phase transition occurs without phase coexistence or composition difference, and the $T_0$ curve becomes an equilibrium feature of the coherent phase diagram.

## INTRODUCTION

In recent years there has been increasing awareness that more than one form of solid state phase equi- libria are being observed. Two limiting forms, termed coherent and (fully) incoherent occur quite fre- quently. While incoherent phase equilibria seem to obey rules quite similar to those derived for multi- phase fluid equilibria, coherent phase equilibria do not. Such differences were first developed for solid state critical points [1]. Recently in major papers, Williams [2, 3] has developed the field extensively and has pointed out several important additional differences between coherent and incoherent equi- librium. In this paper we present the simplest possible model of coherent equilibrium to illustrate as clearly as possible that new features are part of coherent equilibria.

Phases coexist in incoherent equilibrium when each phase is subjected to the same hydrostatic pressure and when chemical potentials are constant for each component. This occurs for instance when the par- ticles of each phase are elastically separated as they would be if they were imbedded in a fluid. If a multiphase solid can be converted into a single undistorted crystal by a deformation in which the

displacements are continuous, then we consider the phase to be in coherent contact. The actual state is elastically distorted because lattice parameters of the various phases are different. The coherency is defined by the requirement that the displacement from the single crystal state to the actual state is continuous across all interfaces [4].

For incoherent equilibrium the appropriate free energy must be invariant to transfers of atoms from one phase to any other. Pressures remain unchanged. At equilibrium chemical potentials are defined and constant throughout the system. The equilibrium conditions and phase diagram constructions are the same whether the phases are fluid or solid.

Coherent equilibrium is equilibrium subject to what we have called the network constraint. This constraint does not permit discontinuities in displace- ment (sliding) along the interface. Coherent coexistence of two phases with different (stress-free) lattice parameters leads to internal stresses that de- pend on the shape and size of the particles of the two phases. There are two fundamental reasons why the network constraint would occur in real systems. Because incoherent interfaces have higher interfacial free energies than coherent interfaces, coherent equi- librium is actually the stable equilibrium at small enough particle size. But even when the particle size exceeds the crossover size where loss of coherence could occur thermodynamically, there is metasta- bility because most coherence loss mechanisms begin with steps that increase the energy [5].

For coherent equilibrium the variational statement for equilibrium [4] takes into account that transfers of atoms from one phase to the other changes the stress fields. Equilibrium conditions specify equations for both stress and composition fields. Equilibrium can occur with inhomogenous non- hydrostatic stresses and inhomogenous phase compositions.

Phase equilibrium is calculated from a min- imization of free energy. The common tangent method due to Gibbs is an elegant graphical con- struction for finding this minimum for mixtures of phases whose free energies are the sums of the free energies of the individual phases. We will show that the addition of an elastic term that depends on the properties and amounts of all phases present in general makes the usual derivation of the common tangent rule invalid. Nonetheless in the vicinity of a critical point it was possible to show [1] that adding an elastic term to the free energy curve led to a special new free energy that could be used with a common tangent construction to find coherent equilibria in exactly the same way that the ordinary free energy is used for incoherent or fluid equilibrium. But it was also noted that this could not be general. Far from the critical point where the phases have quite different elastic constants compositions of coexisting phases depended on whether the matrix was the harder or softer phase [1].

In the first Williams paper [2] an assertion was made that a modification of the tangent construction was used. It has since become clear that the suggested modification was not a valid method for finding the minimum.

Since the minimization of a function is a well known procedure of differential calculus or in numer- ical procedures there is no need to resort to a graphical method. Where a graphical method is derivable and is useful for heuristic purposes it will be pointed out. The role of an elastic energy term in invalidating general theorems of fluid phase equi- librium becomes obvious even with quite simple formulations of the problem. Williams' second paper deals with a large number of cases, but by no means all. In this paper we explore a model that shows some of the unusual behavior, but which has so simple a formulation that ordinary differential calculus of polynomials is all that is needed to derive the proper- ties of the phase equilibrium and illustrate what can happen when an elastic energy term due to coherency is present.

## THE MODEL

Consider a two-component infinite single crystal which can decompose into two coherent phases $\alpha$ and $\beta$. We wish to examine the state of lowest free energy, which could be one or more phases. We ignore interfacial contributions to the energy. It is beyond the scope of this paper to permit phases with in- homogenous stress and composition fields in order to prove that the minimum energy consists of homoge- neous phases. Instead we will examine the case where each phase is compositionally homogeneous. The problem is to minimize
$$F=z F_{\alpha}+(1-z) F_{\beta}+F_{e} \tag{1}$$
with respect to variations in the compositions $C_{\alpha}, C_{\beta}$ of the phases, and the mole fraction of $\alpha, z$, subject to a constant overall composition $C$
$$C=z C_{\alpha}+(1-z) C_{\beta} \tag{2}$$
and to a constraint on mole fraction
$$0 \leqslant z \leqslant 1 \tag{3}$$
where $F_{\alpha}$ and $F_{\beta}$ are the molar free energies of the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ phases which depends respectively on $C_{\alpha}$ and $C_{\beta}$ alone. $F_{e}$ is the elastic energy due to coherency which in general is not only a function of $z$ and both $C_{\alpha}$ and $C_{\beta}$ because both elastic constant and lattice parameters are functions of composition, but also a function of the phase morphology, i.e. the shape and distribution of the two phases. Under certain condi- tions the morphology that minimizes $F_{e}$ is known. Under some very special conditions both Bitter and Crum have shown that $F_{e}$ depends only on $z$ and is independent of morphology. To simplify the problem we assume conditions that make the Bitter-Crum theorem valid [6-8]. Let the crystal be infinite in size.

Let both phases have the same linear isotropic elastic constants. Assume that in the absence of a stress the unit cell constants of the two phases are each independent of composition and differ from each other by a small pure dilation $\varepsilon$. Under these conditions the Bitter-Crum theorem states that the elastic energy of a coherent dispersion of the two phases is independent of the geometry and depends only on the mole fraction $z$. The elastic energy $F_{e}$ per mole of the crystal is (to second order in $\varepsilon$)
$$F_{e}=z(1-z) V E \varepsilon^{2} /(1-v) \tag{4}$$
where $E$ is Young's modulus, $v$ is Poisson's ratio, $\varepsilon$ is the linear mismatch between the phases, $V$ is the molar volume in the reference state and $z$ is the mole fraction of the $\alpha$ phase.

We further assume that the free energy $(F_{\alpha}, F_{\beta})$ per mole of each phase is a parabolic function of composition with the same parabola constant $F''$
$$F_{\alpha}=\mu_{1}^{c}\left(1-C_{\alpha}\right)+\mu_{2}^{c} C_{\alpha}+F^{\prime \prime}\left(C_{\alpha}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right)^{2} \tag{5}$$
$$F_{\beta}=\mu_{1}^{c}\left(1-C_{\beta}\right)+\mu_{2}^{c} C_{\beta}+F^{\prime \prime}\left(C_{\beta}-C_{\beta}^{c}\right)^{2} \tag{6}$$
where $C_{\alpha}$ and $C_{\beta}$ are the actual compositions of the two phases; $C_{\alpha}^{c}$ and $C_{\beta}^{c}$ their values at incoherent equilibrium; $\mu_{1}^{c}$ and $\mu_{2}^{c}$ the chemical potentials of the two species during incoherent equilibrium.

The requirement that $F''$ be the same for both phases ensures that the free energy curves intersect only once. The only difficulty encountered if we permit the $F''$ to differ is that the curves intersect twice. As a result multiple solutions are obtained to account for two separate phase changes at different compositions. No great difficulty is encountered with realistic free energies, except algebraic complexity, and the possibility of additional effects. We further define reduced compositions
$$x=1-2\left(C_{\alpha}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right) /\left(C_{\beta}^{c}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right) \tag{7}$$
$$y=1-2\left(C_{\beta}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right) /\left(C_{\beta}^{c}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right) \tag{8}$$
$$w=1-2\left(C-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right) /\left(C_{\beta}^{c}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right). \tag{9}$$

Combining equations (1)-(9) and rearranging we obtain an expression for a dimensionless free energy
$$\phi=z(1-x)^{2}+(1-z)(1+y)^{2}+A z(1-z)(10)$$
where
$$\begin{gathered}
\phi=4\left\{F-\mu_{1}^{c}(1-C)+\mu_{2}^{c} C\right\} / F^{\prime \prime}\left(C_{\beta}^{c}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right)^{2}(11) \\
=\text { const }_{1} F+\text { const }_{2}
\end{gathered}$$
and
$$A=\frac{4 V E \varepsilon^{2}}{(1-v) F^{\prime \prime}\left(C_{\beta}^{c}-C_{\alpha}^{c}\right)^{2}}. \tag{12}$$

The mass conservation equation becomes
$$0=w-z x-(1-z) y. \tag{13}$$

We next minimize $\phi$ with respect to $x, y$ and $z$, holding $w$ and temperature (and hence $A$) constant and subject to conditions (3) and (13). One method is to solve equation (13) for $z$ and use this result to eliminate $z$ from equation (10). We prefer to use the method of Lagrange multipliers because it is simpler and gives identical results. Multiplying the Lagrange multiplier $L$ with (13), subtracting this from (10) and setting the derivatives of this with respect to $x, y$ and $z$ to zero, we obtain
$$2(1-x) z-L z=0$$
$$2(1+y)(1-z)+L(1-z)=0$$
$$\begin{aligned}
(1-x)^{2}-(1+y)^{2}+A(1-2 z)+L(x-y) & =0. \\
(14) &
\end{aligned}$$

Equations (13)-(14) are four algebraic equations in four unknowns ($x, y, z$ and $L$). The solution for $z \neq 0,1$ is
$$x=1-\frac{A w}{4-A} \tag{15}$$
$$y=-1-\frac{A w}{4-A} \tag{16}$$
$$z=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2 w}{4-A} \tag{17}$$
and gives the extrema in $\phi$ subject to (13).

For this solution
$$\phi=\frac{A}{4}-\frac{A w^{2}}{4-A}. \tag{18}$$

The compositional derivative of $\phi$ is
$$\mathrm{d} \phi / \mathrm{d} w=-2 A w /(4-A). \tag{19}$$

We next apply the inequality (3) to equation (17). For $A<4$ this places a limit on the range of compositions $w$
$$-1+A / 4<w<1-A / 4. \tag{20}$$

For $A>4$ the range becomes $1-A / 4<w<A / 4-1$ but it is easily shown this solution no longer minimizes $\phi$.

Inequalities like equation (3) give rise to extrema at the ends of the range of $z$, whether the derivatives are zero or not. We must therefore consider two additional extrema, one with $z$ set to 0, the other for $z=1$. These are
$$z=0, \quad y=w, \quad \phi=(1+w)^{2} ; \tag{21}$$
$$z=1, \quad x=w, \quad \phi=(1-w)^{2} \tag{22}$$
and correspond to single phases.

We next explore which of the three extrema (18), (21) or (22) give the absolute minimum in $\phi$. The result is plotted in Fig. 1 as a diagram which shows the field over which a particular solution is the minimum, bounded by lines where two minima are equal and a point where all three minima are equal. We propose to call such phase diagrams "field diagrams." They indicate the range of conditions over which the same mix of phases coexist in equilibrium. All classical phase diagrams are field diagrams. In Fig. 1 the axes are $w$ and $A$, but by use of equations

(9) and (12) this diagram would map into a diagram with temperature and composition as axes.

Three fields are shown in Fig. 1. Within the triangle described by equation (20), two phases coexist coher- ently and the properties of the minimum is given by equations (15)-(19). Outside this triangle, two phases cannot coexist in coherent equilibrium and we have minima given by either equation (21) or (22).

When $A=0$, there is no elastic term in the energy. The equations (15)-(22) for this situation exactly reproduces the standard results of fluid and incoherent phase equilibrium. One aspect is depicted in Fig. 2. The two-phase region spans the range $-1<w<1$ (or $C_{\alpha}^{e} \leqslant C \leqslant C_{\beta}^{e}$). Coexisting compositions for each $w$ are plotted. Tie lines are vertical line segments whose ends are the coexisting compositions. Throughout the two-phase region the two phases retain constant composition given by the ends of the range namely $x=1, y=-1$. The coexisting compositions coincide with the limits of the two- phase field. The mole fraction $z$ rises linearly with $w$ in agreement with the lever rule. The compositional derivative of $\phi$ is constant as the free energy follows a tangent that is common to the two parabolas that represent the free energies of the two phases. This compositional derivative is proportional to $\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}$ and must be constant if chemical potentials are constant.

When $0<A<4$, many of these standard fluid phase equilibrium principles cease to hold for coher- ent equilibrium. Although the two phase-region exists between the composition $-1+A / 4$ and $1-A / 4$ the compositions of the phases given by equations (15) and (16) and shown in Fig. 3, are no longer indepen- dent of the overall composition $w$, and only approach the field boundaries in the limit of unit volume fraction. Subtracting equation (16) from (15) gives

$$x-y=2. \tag{23}$$

The above results indicate that the phase rule does not apply to a coherent system. According to this rule a two-phase system at constant temperature and applied pressure would have no degrees of freedom. The compositions of the phases and the chemical potentials would be independent of the overall composition. In this case it would mean that $x$, $y$ and $\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}w$ would be independent of $w$. As equations (15), (16) and (19) indicate, this is not true unless $A=0$.

According to equation (18) in the two-phase field, the free energy $\phi$ is not a linear function of composition. Chemical potential differences, proportional to $\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}w$, are no longer independent of volume fraction. When you consider that the stress on a phase depends on its volume fraction it is unreasonable to expect chemical potentials to remain constant through the two-phase field.

We next examine the free energy $\phi$ and its compositional derivatives. For fluids stability requires that $\phi(w)$ be concave upward. A negative $\mathrm{d}^2\phi/\mathrm{d}w^2$ leads to spinodal decomposition. With the coherency constraint, $\mathrm{d}^2F/\mathrm{d}c^2$ can be negative for a single solid phase without instability in the field between the coherent and incoherent spinodal. In this model, according to equation (18), $\mathrm{d}^2\phi/\mathrm{d}w^2$ is negative, in this case for a two-phase coherent alloy. Closer examination reveals that within the coherency constraint equation (18) does represent the minimum in $\phi$ with respect to all variations and thus must represent a stable equilibrium.

Comparing equations (18) with (21) and (22) we see that at the two kinds of field boundaries, $\phi$ behaves differently. When $A<4$ in this model $\phi$ and $\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}w$ are continuous at the field boundaries, but $\mathrm{d}^2\phi/\mathrm{d}w^2$ suffers a discontinuity changing from 2 to $-A/4$. This is shown in Figs 4 and 5. When $A=0$, we have the same singular behavior of $\phi(w)$ going from parabola to tangent line. When $A\geqslant4$, $\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}w$ and hence the chemical potentials suffers a discontinuity. The manner in which the discontinuity arises is shown in Fig. 5. The discontinuity in $\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}w$ is that which occurs at all $T_0$ curves and partitionless phase changes.

When we compare equations (17) with (21) and (22) we see that when $A\geqslant4$, $z$ is continuous but $\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}w$ has a discontinuity at the field boundary. This is like the behavior for incoherent binary phase diagrams with two-phase regions. However, when $A\leqslant4$, $z$ is discontinuous. The transition from continuous to discontinuous develops in the same way as in Fig. 5.

$$

The field boundaries equally well give a lever rule. Rearranging equation (17) we have

$$
z=\frac{w-(-1+A/4)}{(1-A/4)-(-1+A/4)}. \tag{25}
$$

This second lever rule is the result of the simplifications in this model, and will not hold gener- ally for coherent equilibria [3].

When $A \geqslant 4$, there is no two-phase equilibrium. The coherent energy is single phase $\alpha$ if $w \geqslant 0$ or single phase $\beta$ if $w \leqslant 0$. The condition of two phases with equal free energy at constant composition defines the $T_{0}$ line on incoherent phase diagrams. For incoherent phase changes it does not represent equi- librium except at congruent points where there are no composition changes. The $T_{0}$ line of the incoherent phase diagram becomes a field boundary of the equilibrium coherent diagram. Even though we per- mit composition differences between phases, the equi- librium coherent phase change will occur without composition change at the $T_{0}$ line. It is a first order phase change without a composition change having a finite volume change and a latent heat.

The point $(0,4)$ in Fig. 1 has no counterpart in fluid phase diagrams, or incoherent diagram. Since it does not represent a higher order transition it would be confusing to call it a critical or tricritical point. Since only two phases are involved it is not a triple point. We propose to call it a Williams point.

## A MORE GENERAL PROBLEM

The problem of coherent equilibrium involves finding a minimum of a system free energy that is formulated to depend not only on the shapes of the particles but on compositions and stresses that need not be homogeneous within each phase. If it can be proven as Gibbs did for the fluid case that each phase will be compositionally homogeneous the reduced free energy will in general be of the form

$$\phi=z f_{\alpha}(x)+(1-z) f_{\beta}(y)+A z(1-z) g(x, y, z) \quad(26)$$

where $A=0$ represents the fluid or incoherent case and $f_{\alpha}, f_{\beta}$ are reduced free energies that depend only on the composition of that phase. The last term represents the general case's elastic energy in which $g$ is finite and continuous, but need not have con- tinuous derivatives. Combining (26) and (13) and introducing the Lagrange multiplier $L$ we obtain instead of (14)

$$\begin{gathered}
d f_{\alpha} / d x-L=-A(1-z)(\partial g / \partial x) \\
d f_{\beta} / d y-L=A z(\partial g / \partial y) \\
f_{\alpha}-f_{\beta}-L(x-y)=-A[(1-2 z) g \\
+z(1-z)(\partial g / \partial z)]. \quad(27)
\end{gathered}$$

If $A=0$ we obtain the familiar common tangent equation

$$\partial f_{\alpha} / \partial x=\partial f_{\beta} / \partial y=\left(f_{\alpha}-f_{\beta}\right) /(x-y)=L.$$

In general the numerical solution of (27) and (13) for $x, y$ and $z$ is straightforward given $f_{\alpha}, f_{\beta}$ and $\mathrm{Ag}$, but general properties of such solutions have not yet been deduced. Williams [3] has examined several models.

## DISCUSSION

Although it has been known for more than 20 years that coherent phase diagrams differed qualitatively and quantitatively from incoherent ones, the recent work of Williams has uncovered a large number of features in simple models. In this paper we have developed a particularly simple model in detail to display some of those phenomena. The model in- cludes as a limit the case where there is no elastic energy due to coherency $(A=0)$, so that the qual itative and quantitative effects of this energy are displayed. Because the model is an idealization, quan- titative results are approximate, but the qualitative differences between coherent and incoherent equi- librium are model independent. With more complex models these features displayed here will persist, except for accidental cancellations where the phe- nomenological parameters just happen to give no effect. Williams [3] has shown that more complex models will give additional features.

The important parameter $A$ is defined in equation (12). It is proportional to the ratio of the coherency energy to the difference between the energy of a single phase at the $T_{0}$ point and that of the same system after it forms an equilibrium incoherent mixture of phases. For example, $A$ is large if there is much mismatch or if there is a small difference between the equilibrium compositions in the incoherent diagram. If $A$ is larger than 4, no two-phase coherent co- existence is possible. Under the coherency constraint,an equilibrium phase change occurs along the $T_{0}$  curve, equilibrium occurring only when our infinite crystal is entirely composed of one phase. When $A$ is less than 4, two-phase coexist coherently over a range of alloy compositions that is narrower than and enclosed within the range of incoherent two-phase coexistence. The compositions of each of the two coexisting coherent phases do not coincide with the ends of either range. They may lie outside them and will shift with volume fraction. Only as $A$ approaches zero do the ranges of coherent and incoherent two- phase regions coincide and coincide in turn with the ends of the tie lines that in the limit become indepen- dent of volume fraction.

The shifting about of tie-line ends shows that the phase rule does not apply to coherent phase equi- libria. Surprisingly above the Williams point the phase rule correctly gives the number of degrees of freedom at the $T_{0}$ transition point, but this is surely accidental. In the region of two-phase coexistence below the Williams point there is one more degree of freedom than the phase rule allows. Standard deri- vations of the phase rule assume the same pressure in all coexisting phases. When this condition is not met whether resulting from capillarity [9] or coherency the phase rule can not be derived.

One of the important theorems about fluid equilibria is that the extent on the phase diagram of the stable single phase field is entirely enclosed within

the metastable single phase field. Metastability always expands a single phase field. As a corollary, when tie lines end on field boundaries, compositions of phases coexisting in metastable equilibrium cannot lie within the stable single phase field of that phase. For coherent equilibrium this is no longer true. For our example in the entire range of two phase coexistence $0<A<4$ the minor phase $(w \neq 0)$ composition is always within that of the single phase incoherent stable equilibrium field. Our model by counter-example disproves the corrolary.

The approximate theoretical mapping of the canonical phase diagram of Fig. 1 onto a real phase diagram is done in Fig. 6. We start with the incoherent field boundaries, which become $w=-1$ and $w=1$. We then interpolate and extrapolate between these curves to locate $w$ in the range from -2 to +2 . We next estimate $A$. Even though real systems do not obey our assumption we can use averaged $E /(1-v)$ and $F^{\prime \prime}$ to evaluate $A$ from equation (12) for each temperature where the two related phases appear in an incoherent stable or metastable equilibrium. From equation (20) we then estimate the ratio of the coherent to the incoherent phase composition difference. If $A<4$

$$(\Delta C)_{\mathrm{coh}} /(\Delta C)_{\mathrm{inc}}=1-A / 4$$

and the coherent two-phase region is drawn to straddle the $T_{0}$ curve. If $A \geqslant 4$ there is no two-phase region, and the transition occurs on the $T_{0}$ curve.

The Williams point is a special point that arises with coherent phase diagrams. It is likely to exist for phase diagram with congruent points. As the congruent point is approached, $A$ rises to infinity since the composition difference in the denominator of equation (12) tends to zero. Thus for some range about the congruent point two phase coherent coexistence is impossible. Before two-phase coexistence can occur a Williams point must be crossed. Williams gives evidence that such a point occurs in the CuAu system [2].

The theorems that the ends of tie lines lie on field boundaries of phase diagrams and thus are independent of volume fraction are very important to our determination of phase diagrams and in our subsequent use of them. That these theorems are not valid for coherent diagrams is the most important result of Williams' work. It was already discovered [1] twenty years ago for the special case where the two phases differ in elastic constants, but the effect survives in the present model where the elastic constants are the same.

Because of the belief in the validity of the theorem that tie ends lie on field boundaries, and that volume fraction is linear in composition, data from different methods of coherent phase diagram determinations should have been treated differently. Tie end com- position data should not have been used to construct coherent field diagrams. Many discrepancies between diagrams undoubtedly are due to this cause.

In the simple model presented here, only algebraic equations and simple calculus is involved. The anal- ysis is so straight forward, that it may not seem necessary to explain what caused these unexpected results. Basically the finding of a minimum of three term without unduly raising the other terms. Since the elastic term in this model depends only on volume fraction, reducing the volume fraction of the minor phase reduces the elastic energy. When $A$ is large, eliminating the minor phase leads to the minimum. With smaller $A$ shifting the phase compositions reduces this volume fraction, as can be seen rising the lever rule. The exact amount of shift is a balance between chemical and elastic energy.

Consolute critical points are not fit by the present model for two reasons: (1) the free energy is not fit by two intersecting parabolas and (2) the mismatch instead of remaining constant depends on the com- position difference. $F_{e}$ can be lowered in two ways, reducing volume fraction or reducing composition difference. The analysis of this situation was given before and has been verified in many experiments.

Williams [3] has treated six different cases of a more complicated model, some of which reveal addi- tional features. A completely general treatment is feasible. The experimental task of sorting out old, apparently discrepant, data and testing these ideas with new data seems most immediate.

It is important to note the coherent phase equi- libria have been shown to be different from other metastable phase equilibria. Incoherent metastable equilibria obey the same thermodynamic laws as stable phase equilibria [10-12] because the free energy can be expressed entirely with terms like the first two in equation (1). They are determined experimentally in many of the same ways as stable equilibria. Often they can be obtained by extrapolation of stable phase equilibria into metastable regions. They can be calcu- lated from models in the same ways as stable equi- libria.

By contrast this model has shown that additional information is needed to characterize coherent equi- libria. This requires additional experiments, or the information can be calculated from elastic and ther- modynamic data on the individual phases.

The phenomena set forth in this paper have un- doubtedly been a source of many discrepancies in phase diagram data, but they may also be a factor in many properties of coherent two phase alloys that depend on volume fraction, phase compositions and mismatch. The existence of one more degree of freedom than the phase rule allows must surely have consequences for alloy design.

## CONCLUSION

General theorems of thermodynamics, that have benn derived for fluid phase equilibrium, do not have validity when applied to coherent phase equilibrium. The model presented here although quite simple is plausible. It presents a counterexample to many of these theorems and therefore proves that they cannot be generally valid for coherent phase equilibrium. We believe that many discrepancies among solid state

phase diagram determinations are the result of inter- pretation of data using invalid theorems.
