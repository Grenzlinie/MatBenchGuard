# Resonance Interaction between Impurities
and Crystal Molecules
## Application to Benzselenophene in Naphthalene

By
I. V. BROVCHENKO (a), N. I. OSTAPENKO (a), M. T. SHPAK (a),
L. L. VALKŪNAS (b), and G. B. VEKTARIS (b)

Theoretical and experimental investigations of resonance hybridization between impurity and local states are carried out in naphthalene with benzselenophene impurities. The analysis of the vibron regions of the luminescence and absorption spectra of this system shows that the hybridization effect really takes part in such a system and there are two centres with impurities being in non-equiv- alent positions. Moreover each impurity perturbs two molecules of the crystal. The matrix ele- ments of the resonance interaction between impurity and crystal molecules are given.

Теоретически и экспериментально рассмотрен эффект резонансного смешивания примесных и локальных состояний в системе кристалл нафталина-примесь бензсе- ленофена. Из анализа вибронных спектров люминесценции и поглощения не- посредственно следует, что эффект смешивания действительно имеет место и что в кристалле имеются два центра, в которых молекула примеси возмущает по две молекулы кристалла. Оценены матричные элементы резонансного взаимодействия между молекулами примеси и кристалла.

## 1. Introduction

Heterocyclic impurities (thyonaphthene, indole, benzofurane) in the naphthalene crystals cause the appearance of local states related to the optical transition of crystal molecules perturbed by the impurity [1]. In the case of benzselenophene as impurity, the features of the local states differ basically [2].

Firstly, three bands instead of one band are observed in the pure electronic region of the absorption spectrum. This can be explained by the assumption that three crystal molecules are being simultaneously perturbed by the impurity. Secondly, anomalous polarization is inherent in the local states (three bands, situated at distances of $-73$, $-47$, and $-21\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ from the bottom of the A-Davydov component, polarized mainly along the crystal $a$-axis, though usually the local bands are polarized along the crystal $b$-axis at distances of $\approx 30\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ [1]). Bearing in mind the facts that in this case 0-0 transitions of the impurity and the crystal molecules are situated at a smaller distance $(\approx 800\ \text{cm}^{-1}$, in comparison with $(2\ \text{to}\ 4)\times 10^{3}\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ for other impurities), and the integral intensity of the impurity band is by three orders of magnitude larger than the integral intensity of the A-Davydov component, the anomalous polarization of the local bands has been thought to be the result of the effects caused by mixing of local and impurity states [2].

The present paper is devoted to further investigation of the hybridization effects between the impurity and defect states in naphthalene with benzselenophene impurity. The luminescence transitions from local states to the impurity vibrational sublevels of the ground electronic state make such a phenomenon evident. The analysis of the luminescence spectra gives additional information on the structure of the impurity centre as well. The investigation of the vibronic absorption spectra can also serve as a supplementary confirmation of the proposed model.

## 2. Experimental
Luminescence and absorption spectra of the naphthalene crystal with benzselenophene impurity ($T = 4.2$ K) were examined on a device with back dispersion $2$ Å/mm. The vibronic region of the luminescence spectrum of the naphthalene monocrystal with benzselenophene impurity ($C \leqq 0.01$ wt $\%$, $C$ is the concentration of the impurity) is shown in Fig. 1. Two additional long-wavelength series ($\text{N}_1$ and $\text{N}_2$) can be mentioned to be present in the luminescence spectrum, the intensities of the bands of the $\text{N}_2$ series being weaker than those of $\text{N}_1$ series. The initial bands of these series ($31404$ and $31430$ cm$^{-1}$) coincide with the corresponding bands in the absorption spectrum. It is noteworthy that in the absorption spectrum three series can be observed [2]. The main parameters of the spectrum are presented in Table 1.

The vibration frequencies of the $\text{N}_1$ and $\text{N}_2$ series coincide with the frequencies of intramolecular vibrations of naphthalene, whereas in $\text{N}_1$ and the exciton series, the most strong vibrations of the benzselenophene molecules as well as vibrations of the naphthalene molecules take part (Table 2). This fact is observed for the first time while considering local states. At high impurity concentrations only the $\text{N}_1$ series remains in the luminescence spectrum.

### 3. Local States in the Electronic Spectrum

The new series appearing in the absorption spectrum are related to optical transitions to local exciton states [2]. The presence of two long-wavelength series in the luminescence spectrum testifies the fact that these series correspond to two spatially separated local centres. The heterocyclic impurity having no inversion centre may be found in two non-equivalent positions in the naphthalene crystal (Fig. 4). The same situation can be observed when introducing naphthalene derivatives into naphthalene crystals [4].

Let us now assume that every impurity perturbs two crystal molecules, e.g. 1,4 and 2,3 (Fig. 4). Then the model of the local centre could be as follows: two separate types of impurity centres simultaneously influence two crystal molecules. The fact that in the absorption spectrum only three series can be observed testifies that the perturbation of two molecules is the same or that one of the molecules is perturbed very little so the corresponding electronic level does not become apparent in the spectrum. Really, a weak maximum at $15\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ from the bottom of the exciton band can be observed in the electronic region of the absorption spectrum.

The Hamiltonian of the exciton states of such a centre in Heitler-London approximation is as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
H_{\mathrm{ex}}= & \varepsilon_{0} \sum_{n \alpha} B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{n \alpha}+\sum_{n \alpha, n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}} M_{n \alpha, n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}} B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}}+ \\
& +\sum_{n \alpha} \Delta_{n \alpha} B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{n \alpha}+\sum_{n \alpha} \delta M_{0 \alpha_{0}, n \alpha}\left(B_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{+} B_{n \alpha}+B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{0 \alpha_{0}}\right),
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$
$n\alpha$ being the coordinate of the cell and the number of the molecule in the cell, $B_{n\alpha}^{+}(B_{n\alpha})$ the creation (annihilation) operator of the electronic excitation of molecule $n\alpha$, $\varepsilon_{0}$ the excitation energy of the crystal moleclues, $M_{n\alpha,m\beta}$ the resonance interaction between naphthalene molecules $n\alpha$ and $m\beta$, $\delta M_{0\alpha_{0},n\alpha}=M_{0\alpha_{0},n\alpha}^{\text{imp}}-M_{0\alpha_{0},n\alpha}$ the change of the resonance interaction of impurity and surrounding molecules, $\Delta_{n\alpha}$ the change of the excitation energy of molecule $n\alpha$ related with the impurity. The wave function $|l\rangle$ with the effect of state hybridization can be presented thus as
$$
|l\rangle=\sum_{n \alpha} a_{n \alpha}^{l} B_{n \alpha}^{+}|0\rangle=\sum_{n \alpha\left(\neq 0 \alpha_{0}\right)} a_{n \alpha}^{l} B_{n \alpha}^{+}|0\rangle+a_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{l} B_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{+}|0\rangle, \quad (2)
$$
$|0\rangle$ being the ground state of the crystal, $a_{n\alpha}^{l}$ are expansion factors determined from the Schrödinger equation with Hamiltonian (1). The precise solution for an analogous system was presented in [5, 6]. Taking into account that the impurity level is situated far from the exciton band (i.e. $\Delta_{0\alpha_{0}}\gg\Delta_{n\alpha}$, $M_{n\alpha,m\beta}$ and $\Delta_{0\alpha_{0}}\gg(M_{l}^{\text{imp}})^{2}/\Delta_{n\alpha}$ as $\Delta_{0\alpha_{0}}=$

$$

are expressed through the matrix elements of the Green function of the pure crystal [7],

$$
G_{n \alpha, m \beta}=\frac{1}{N} \sum_{\mu k} \frac{a_{\alpha}^{\mu} a_{\beta}^{\mu} \mathrm{e}^{i k(n \alpha-m \beta)}}{\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{\mu}(k)}.
$$

Here we suppose $\tilde{G}_{i j}=\tilde{G}_{n_{i} \alpha_{i} m_{j} \beta_{j}} ; \varepsilon_{\mu}(k)$ and $(1 / \sqrt{N}) a_{\alpha}^{\mu} \mathrm{e}^{i k n \alpha}$ are the dispersion law and the wave function of the exciton, respectively. The wave function expansion factors $a_{n \alpha}^{l}$ satisfy the following equations:

$$
a_{n \alpha}^{l}=\sum_{n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}} \tilde{G}_{n \alpha, n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}} \Delta_{n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}} a_{n^{\prime} \alpha^{\prime}}^{l},
$$

$$
a_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{l}=-\frac{1}{\Delta_{0 \alpha_{0}}} \sum_{n \alpha} M_{0 \alpha_{0}, n \alpha}^{\mathrm{imp}} a_{n \alpha}^{l}.
$$

The intensity of the bands in the luminescence and absorption spectra are determined by the wave function of the corresponding state, i.e. are proportional to the square of the dipole moment of the optical transition. Taking into account the state hybridization between the impurity and the crystal molecules the moment of the crystal optical transition to the $l$-state can be expressed as

$$
p_{l}=\sum_{n \alpha} a_{n \alpha}^{l} p_{\alpha}^{-}+a_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{l} p_{0},
$$

$p_{\alpha}$ being the dipole moment of the optical transition of the crystal molecules, $p_{0}$ that of the impurity.

When substituting (6) and (7) into (8) the polarization ratio can be easily obtained [2] as

$$
p^{l}(a / b)=\frac{\left|p_{a}\right|^{2}}{\left|p_{b}\right|^{2}} \frac{\left[\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{b}(0)\right]^{2}}{\left[\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{a}(0)\right]^{2}} F_{l},
$$

$$
F_{l}=\frac{\left\{A_{a}\left(\varepsilon_{l}\right)-\frac{M_{l}^{\mathrm{imp}}}{\Delta_{0} \Delta_{l}}\left[\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{a}(0)\right] \frac{\left|p_{0}\right|}{\left|p_{a}\right|}\right\}^{2}}{\left\{A_{b}\left(\varepsilon_{l}\right)-\frac{M_{l}^{\mathrm{imp}}}{\Delta_{0} \Delta_{l}}\left[\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{b}(0)\right] \frac{\left|p_{0}\right|}{\left|p_{b}\right|}\right\}^{2}},
$$

$$
A_{\mu}\left(\varepsilon_{l}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[1+(-1)^{\mu} \frac{G_{01}}{G_{00}}\right]\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{5-l} \tilde{G}_{14}}{1-\Delta_{5-l} \tilde{G}_{11}}\right),
$$

$\mu=1$ being the $a$-component, $\mu=2$ the $b$-component, $\left|p_{a}\right|^{2} /\left|p_{b}\right|^{2}$ [8] the polarization ratio of the exciton band.

The matrix elements of the resonance interaction between the crystal molecules and the impurity, when substituting the values from Table 1 into (9), (10), and (11) are obtained: $M_{1}^{\mathrm{imp}}=60 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}, M_{2}^{\mathrm{imp}}=50 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}, M_{3}^{\mathrm{imp}}=49 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. we can assume that: a) $\Delta_{3}=\Delta_{4}$ and consequently $M_{3}^{\text {imp }}=M_{4}^{\text {imp }}$; b) $\Delta_{4}=-55 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$,
i.e. the position of the local state in the NTS vibronic region coincides with the band
of pure crystal at $31918 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ (Fig. 2). In this case the precise value of $M_{4}^{\text {imp }}$ cannot
be obtained supposing only $M_{4}^{\text {imp }}=50 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ for calculations.

The transition dipole moment from the $l$-state of the crystal to the vibrational
sublevel of the electron ground state of the impurity and perturbed molecule is equal to

$$
p_{l}^{\nu}=p_{m \beta}^{\nu} a_{m \beta}^{l},
\tag{12}
$$

$$
p_{m \beta}^{\nu}=\left\langle m_{\beta}\left|\hat{p}\right| \varphi_{m \beta}^{\nu}\right\rangle, \quad\left|\varphi_{m \beta}^{\nu}\right\rangle=\beta_{m \beta}^{+}|0\rangle, \quad\left|m_{\beta}\right\rangle=B_{m \beta}^{+}|0\rangle,
\tag{13}
$$
$\beta_{m \beta}^{+}$ is the creation operator of a vibration on molecule $m \beta$, depending on the value
of $m \beta$ it corresponds to the creation of the vibration on impurity or crystal molecules,
$p_{m \beta}^{\nu}$ is the corresponding transition moment in molecule $m \beta$.

Consequently, using (6), (7), (12), (13), for the deep levels the ratio of the fluores-
cence bands corresponding to the transition from the local state to the vibrational
sublevel of the naphthalene molecule $I_{\text {def }}$ and the impurity $I_{\text {imp }}$ can be expressed as

$$
\frac{I_{\mathrm{imp}}}{I_{\mathrm{def}}}=\left(\frac{M_{l}^{\mathrm{imp}}}{\Delta_{0 \alpha_{0}}}\right)^{2} \frac{\left|p_{0}^{\nu}\right|^{2}}{\left|p^{\nu}\right|^{2}},
\tag{14}
$$

with the abbreviations $p_{0}^{\nu}=p_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{\nu_{\text {imp }}} ; p^{\nu}=p_{n l \alpha_{l}}^{\nu_{0}}$, where $\nu_{\text {imp }}$ and $\nu_{0}$ are the vibrations
of impurity and crystal molecules, respectively.

By comparing (14) with experimental data the value of $M_{l}^{\text {imp }}$ can be determined.
The experiment shows that $I_{\text {imp }} / I_{\text {def }}=0.1$ when $\nu_{0}=590 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and $\nu_{\text {imp }}=278 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$.
At present the exact value $|p_{0}^{\nu}|^{2} /|p^{\nu}|^{2}$ is not known, assuming $p_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{\nu_{\text {imp }}} \approx\langle 0| \hat{p} B_{m \beta}^{+} \beta_{m \beta}^{+}|0\rangle$
this ratio equals 22 according to the absorption spectrum. Then (14) directly gives
$M^{\text {imp }}=52 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ in agreement with previous calculations from the polarization ratio.

### 4. Local States in the Vibronic Spectrum

Calculations of the local states in the vibronic spectrum may be done by means of
the dynamic theory of vibronic states [9,10]. The vibronic Hamiltonian can be
presented as

$$
H=H_{\mathrm{ex}}+H_{\mathrm{ph}}+H_{\mathrm{ex}-\mathrm{ph}}^{(1)}+H_{\mathrm{ex}-\mathrm{ph}}^{(2)}+H_{\mathrm{ex}-\mathrm{ph}-\mathrm{imp}},
\tag{15}
$$
$H_{\text {ex }}$ being expressed by (1),

$$
H_{\mathrm{ph}}=\nu_{0} \sum_{n \alpha} \beta_{n \alpha}^{+} \beta_{n \alpha},
\tag{16}
$$

$$
H_{\mathrm{ex}-\mathrm{ph}}^{(1)}=\Delta_{\nu} \sum_{n \alpha} B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{n \alpha} \beta_{n \alpha}^{+} \beta_{n \alpha},
\tag{17}
$$

$$
H_{\mathrm{ex}-\mathrm{ph}}^{(2)}=\gamma^{2} \sum_{n \alpha, m \beta} M_{n \alpha, m \beta} B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{m \beta}\left(\beta_{n \alpha}^{+} \beta_{m \beta}+\beta_{m \beta}^{+} \beta_{n \alpha}-\beta_{n \alpha}^{+} \beta_{n \alpha}-\beta_{m \beta}^{+} \beta_{m \beta}\right),
\tag{18}
$$

$$
H_{\mathrm{ex}-\mathrm{ph}-\mathrm{imp}}=\gamma \sum_{n \alpha} M_{0 \alpha_{0}, n \alpha}^{\mathrm{imp}}\left(B_{0 \alpha_{0}}^{+} B_{n \alpha} \beta_{n \alpha}+B_{n \alpha}^{+} B_{0 \alpha_{0}} \beta_{n \alpha}^{+}\right),
\tag{19}
$$

$\nu_{0}$ is the vibrational frequency, $\Delta_{v}$ the change of the frequency under molecular
electronic excitation, $\gamma^{2}$ the dimensionless constant of the linear electron-phonon
interaction. Term (19) of the Hamiltonian has a rather unusual form and it will be
discussed further. In the case of a NTS vibron $\gamma=0$ and the calculations come to
the consideration of the local states of the new centre where vibrationally excited
molecules are situated near the perturbed molecule.

The configuration when the perturbed molecules are excited vibrationally can be considered by (3) in which $\varepsilon_{l}$ should be changed to $\varepsilon_{l}-v_{0}$ and $\Delta_{l}$ to $\Delta_{l}+\Delta_{\nu}$. The parameters $\Delta_{l}$ are just determined by the position of these levels. Changing the distance between vibrational excitation and perturbed molecule causes a change of the local state energy [10], therefore the vibronic spectrum of these states must be structural. Calculations of the energy levels and the intensities of the fine structure in case of the vibration $v_{0}=509 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}\left(\Delta_{\nu}=-89 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}\right)$ are carried out as in [11,12]. It is worthwhile noting here that the oscillator strength of the M-band is of the same order as that of the impurity. That is why the effects of the hybridization are absent in this case. The results of the calculations in comparison with the experimental data are presented in Table 3. The following notations to classify the local states have been introduced: ED is an exciton being localised on the defect (perturbed molecule), EP an exciton on the vibrationally excited molecule (phonon).

For TS vibrons both $H_{\text{ex-ph}}^{(1)}$ and $H_{\text{ex-ph}}^{(2)}$ should be taken into account. The case without hybridization effect was investigated in [3] and shows an expected coincidence with experimental data for indole, thionaphthene, and benzofurane impurities. However, the electronic transition of the benzselenophene impurity is situated in the two-particle TS vibron region [2]. That is why the hybridization effects may be essential when analysing the spectrum of the vibronic transition $^1\text{A}_{1\text{g}} \rightarrow ^1\text{B}_{3\text{u}}\ \text{a}_{\text{g}}$. The Hamiltonian $H_{\text{ex-ph-imp}}$ determines the creation of an electronic excitation on the impurity with simultaneous annihilation of an exciton and a phonon near the impurity as well as the reverse process. It is noteworthy that the resonance interaction now is $\gamma M_{l}^{\text{imp}}$, which distinguishes it from that in the electronic region where it equals $M_{l}^{\text{imp}}$. Taking into accounce that $\gamma<1$ (in case of naphthalene $\gamma^{2}=0.2$) it is quite understandable that the resonance hybridization effect in the vibronic spectrum will appear to be $\gamma$ times weaker than that in the exciton spectrum.

The Hamiltonian (15) (as well as in [3]) has been diagonalized in two stages. First of all the diagonalization was carried out with $H_{\text{ex-ph}}$ and $H_{\text{ex-ph-imp}}$ being neglected (the case of the NTS vibron). Then this solution was used in the computer simulation on the two-dimensional matrix with the naphthalene crystal parameters and taking into account nearest-neighbour interaction and terms $H_{\text{ex-ph}}$ and $H_{\text{ex-ph-imp}}$ as well [14].

Then the vibron function can be expressed by wave functions $|n\alpha\rangle$, which proceed the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian in the first stage leading to the solution $E_{n\alpha}$. Expansion factors $A_{m\beta,j}$ and corresponding vibronic levels $E_j$ are defined by the set of equations

$$
\sum_{m \beta}\left[\left\langle n \alpha\left|H_{\text {ex-ph }}^{(2)}\right| m \beta\right\rangle+\left\langle n \alpha\left|H_{\text {ex-ph-imp }}\right| m \beta\right\rangle+\delta_{n \alpha, m \beta}\left(E_{n \alpha}-E_{j}\right)\right] A_{m \beta, j}=0,
\tag{20}
$$

where $j$ numbers the vibronic levels.

Now the transition dipole moment can be calculated [14]. Thus there is no undetermined parameter in the calculation scheme. It must be mentioned that the basis of the wave functions taken is convenient because the $\gamma^2$-parameter is small. The resonance interaction between the impurity and the crystal molecules may be included into the consideration by the renormalizing parameter $\gamma$ in $H_{\text{ex-ph-imp}}$ [13].

## 5. Discussion

The presence of local states combined with impurity vibrations in the luminescence spectrum shows that the hybridization effect takes place in the given system. The appearance of additional impurity bands in the exciton series of naphthalene can be related with the scattering processes of excitons on impurities.

This fact is witnessed by the appearance of the intensive local band polarized along the crystal $a$-axis in the TS vibron region of the absorption spectrum. The hybridization effect mostly influences position and intensity of the EDP band, because the main contribution to the wave function of this state is given by the bound configuration of exciton defect and phonon. Nevertheless, these bands cannot be observed in the experiment, as they enter the two-particle band of the NTS vibron. The energy positions of ED and EP states are practically independent of the hybridization strength. But the intensities and the polarization ratio of these bands become obvious in the change of the state mixing parameters. This may be witnessed by the appearance of a very strong band $E_{j}=-9 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ polarized along the crystal $a$-axis which coincides in position as well as other parameters with the calculated EP band (see Table 5). When this band gets into the $\mathrm{A}_{2}$-band its observation is possible, as it is situated near the bottom of $\mathrm{A}_{2}$-band and therefore its width is small [3]. The levels $E_{j}=-4$ and $-5 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ are situated a bit further from the bottom of the $\mathrm{A}_{2^{-}}$ band. Being too broad they do not appear in the experiment. Our calculation method gives the position of the $\mathrm{A}_{2}$-band, therefore the calculated levels $E_{j}=-11 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ and $-12 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ can be correlated with the $\mathrm{A}_{2}$-band (in experiment $-13 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$).

The intensity and polarization ratio of the band $E_{\exp }=-26 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ (polarization along the crystal $a$-axis) in the TS vibron spectrum are related with the hybridization effect. The corresponding ED band is situated at $E_{j}=-20 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. The other cal culated bands are considerably less in intensity and in spite of this get into the charac- teristic maxima of the absorption spectrum of the pure naphthalene crystal and thus cannot be resolved.

It has already been mentioned that the hybridization effects do not appear in the M-band region. This makes the feature of the local states in the NTS vibron region similar to that of the thionaphtene, indole, and benzofurane impurities. The local bands $E_{j}=-69,-43$, and $-44 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ cannot be observed because of their very weak intensity (an order less than others) and as regards the bands from $-21$ to $-28 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$, they serve to broaden the M-band. The band $-6 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ can be found in the calculations when proposing $\Delta_{4}=-55 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$. This value of $\Delta_{4}$ is obtained supposing that the corresponding EDP level coincides with the band of the pure crystal. The corresponding electronic local level is situated $10 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ below the bottom of the $\mathrm{A}_{2^{-}}$ band. In fact, some structure of the $\varepsilon_{3}$-band is obtained (in Table 1 we bring the possible position of the $\varepsilon_{4}$-band). However, the weak intensity of this band does not allow to define the precise polarization relation and other parameters of this band. Thus the hybridization effect of crystal and impurity states in the absorption and luminescence spectra can be explained by the parameters of resonance interaction of the two-centre impurity model.
