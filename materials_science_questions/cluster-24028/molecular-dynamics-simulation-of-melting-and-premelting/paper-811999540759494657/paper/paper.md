# Eutectic Interface Configurations During Melting

C.A. NORLUND and R. TRIVEDI

Experimental studies are presented on the melting of a lamellar eutectic structure in a transparent model alloy system. Steady-state morphologies have been characterized for different dissolution rates. Both coupled and noncoupled melting of eutectic are observed. A diffusive model of coupled eutectic melting has been developed and compared with the experimental results. Because the eutectic spacing is fixed, the system is shown to select the shape so that the average position of the interface for the two phases remains isothermal. A comparison of the predicted shapes with the experimental data shows that the higher melting point phase adjusts its curvature to equate the average temperature of the two phases when the experimental conditions are in the coupled melting regime. The conditions for a coupled melting to be unstable are described for which one of the phases extends further into the liquid. When this extension is significant, a spherodization of the leading phase is observed.

## I. INTRODUCTION

EXTENSIVE research has been performed in the field of solidification, including a considerable amount on eutectic solidification. In comparison, very little research has been conducted on melting at the microstructural level, with no analytical work on eutectic melting. With the widespread use of eutectic alloys as solders and brazes, the melting characteristics can become an important issue. The melting structure can also play a crucial role in the soldering, weld- ing, or laser processing of materials. For example, during the remelting process in welding, significantly different dis- solution rates of the two lamellae can lead to spherodization of the leading phase and give rise to an undesirable micro- structure during the solidification of welds.

For eutectic dissolution, several critical questions need to be investigated. First, to determine the conditions for cou- pling of the diffusion field between the two phases during melting, and second, to investigate whether this coupling leads to a melting interface that is macroscopically planar or nonplanar. Also, because the spacing is fixed during eutectic growth, it is important to establish the characteristic that the system can adjust to achieve stable steady-state melting. In order to examine these ideas, experiments were conducted using a transparent organic eutectic alloy in which the solid- liquid interface could be observed *in situ*. The microstructure was characterized for various dissolution velocities and com- positions near the eutectic. The melting microstructure was seen to differ significantly from a solidifying microstructure. Further, several unique dynamic processes and unstable interfaces have been observed under melting conditions.

Eutectic melting is analyzed by solving the steady-state diffusion equation in the liquid. The interfacial temperature and morphology are determined by examining the role of solute diffusion and surface energy. Because the lamellar spacing is determined by the prior solidification process, the model lacks a mechanism to select stable diffusive coupling.

However, interface shapes can be adjusted, and we shall show that the shapes adjust such that the average position of the interface remains isothermal to allow for changes in melt velocity and composition. The predicted interface morphology is then shown to match with experimental obser- vations. The regime of coupled melting is also evaluated with respect to constitutional supercooling, and the general behavior predicted by constitutional supercooling is then compared with experimental observations of interfacial instability.

## II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

The primary objective of this research was to gain insight into the melting behavior of eutectics. The melting behavior can be observed *in situ* by using a transparent, organic alloy. The carbon tetrabromide-hexachloroethane $(CBr_{4}-C_{2}Cl_{6})$ eutectic system was chosen because the phase diagram and material properties of this system have been well character- ized by Kaukler, $^{[5]}$ and the eutectic structure can be easily observed experimentally because the spacings are relatively large due to a small difference in solute compositions between the two solid phases.

The as-received materials, $CBr_{4}$ and $C_{2}Cl_{6}$, were not suffi ciently pure. If impurity concentrations were too high, regu- lar lamellar structures could not be obtained. Due to the high vapor pressures of both materials, purification was accomplished by sublimation under a slight vacuum.

In order to observe the melting process, a glass cell was constructed to contain the alloy. The cell consisted of two glass plates separated by a gap and fused together along the edges. The gap thickness did not exceed $150\ \mu\text{m}$ to avoid convection and three-dimensional growth phenomena. $^{[1]}$ The thickness was maintained above $50\ \mu\text{m}$ to prevent significant effects on the solidification/melting process due to the alloy/ cell surface tension. A reservoir was constructed at one end by the use of an epoxy on the lower protruding slide. The cell was filled by placing the cell horizontally on a hot plate, melting the alloy separately, and filling a reservoir, allowing the alloy to fill the cell by capillary. The cell filling was conducted in a dry box back filled with nitrogen to reduce the amount of moisture and oxygen dissolved in the alloy. One cell was constructed with a calibrated thermocouple to measure the thermal gradient.

The experiments were conducted on a directional solidification/melting apparatus that allowed in situ observation of the interface. A more complete description of the experimental apparatus is given by Mason and Eshelman.[²] The cell was directionally melted by pulling the cell from the cold chamber to the hot chamber. Solidification was accomplished in a reverse manner. The hot and cold chambers were maintained at a constant temperature using a temperature bath controller. The thermal gradient was established by separating the chambers with a Lexan block 0.250-in. thick. The velocity was controlled by a computer-driven electric stepper motor, turning a ball screw mechanism attached to the cell. A light source was transmitted from beneath, through the Lexan and cell. A microscope and video camera were mounted above for observation of the experiment. The experiment was recorded on a time-lapse SVHS VCR, and the interface morphology and spacing were analyzed using a computer equipped with image analysis software and image acquisition hardware. The image analysis software was calibrated using the same experimental setup and an optical micrometer.

To adequately test the theory, experiments were conducted over a range of melt velocities, 0.25 to 5 µm/s, with a fixed solidification velocity of 0.5 µm/s. The fixed solidification velocity provided a fairly constant lamellar spacing. The thermal gradient was kept constant for all experiments at 2.0 K/mm.

The focus of the experiments was to observe the effect of velocity and composition on the interface morphology. A small variation in alloy composition due to the cell-filling procedure provided a second variable affecting the morphology, causing differences in the volume fractions of each phase. The experimental duration was maintained as long as possible, in an effort to obtain a steady-state condition.

The experiments can be assessed by measuring key features of the interface. The diffusion characteristics at the interface will depend on the microstructural lengths. These can be determined by measuring the lamellar spacing and volume fraction. Another characteristic feature of the interface is the location of the lamellar tips of each phase, where one phase extends further into the liquid. This feature will be termed the phase lead, which characterizes the distance between the tip positions of the two phases. The phase lead is defined as positive if the β phase leads. The interface position can also be measured relative to a fixed position on the viewing field, indicating the position of the interface relative to a fixed isotherm. The measurements were taken periodically to establish when the steady state occurred.

The lamellar spacing varied slightly for the experiment because, during growth, a distribution in spacing is selected.[³,⁴] To account for this, the lamellar spacing was measured over at least four spacings and averaged for each image. To consistently measure the volume fraction, the image analysis software was programmed to mark the center of the dark band between the two solid phases using the luminance pattern. Based on observations at the triple point (where both solid phases and the liquid phase form a point), the center of the dark band represents the most accurate basis for establishing volume fraction. The volume fraction was measured over several lamellae and averaged similar to the spacing measurements. The phase lead was measured as the distance between the lamellar tips of the two phases parallel to the growth direction. Because the phase lead was constant across the screen for each image, the phase lead was measured for one pair of lamellae in the center of the screen. When the phase lead is measured in this manner, it represents differences in interface temperatures at the tips of the lamellae. The interface position was measured as a function of time to ascertain when solutal and thermal steady states were achieved. This simplifies the analysis by determining steady-state conditions with a constant velocity. The interface position was used in conjunction with the phase lead, and a steady state declared when both values reached a constant value. A steady-state time interval was established, and the spacing, volume fraction, and phase lead averaged over that time. These values are then used in comparison with the steady-state theory developed in the next section.

Another dynamical effect observed during melting occurs with large phase leads. The lead phase is observed to oscillate in width along its length (Figure 5(a)). This instability is similar to the instability during the transition from cellular growth to dendritic growth, where perturbations occur during growth. For melting, the perturbations lead to spherodization. Note that the volume fraction of the $\beta$ phase has increased significantly in the two-phase region compared to that in the solid region.

## IV. THEORY

To improve our understanding of the melting of a eutectic structure, an analysis of the solute concentration profile is necessary. This analysis was carried out for eutectic solidification by Jackson and Hunt.[⁷] A similar procedure will now be followed for the melting of eutectic structures for conditions of coupled melting in which the phase lead is small.

\frac{\partial^{2} C}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2} C}{\partial z^{2}}-\left[\frac{V}{D}\right] \frac{\partial C}{\partial z}=0 \tag{1a}

$$
\begin{aligned}
C= & C_{E}+C_{\infty}+B_{0} e^{-V z / D} \\
& +\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_{n} \cos \left(\frac{n \pi x}{S_{\alpha}+S_{\beta}}\right) e^{-n \pi z /\left(S_{\alpha}+S_{\beta}\right)}
\end{aligned} \tag{2}

$$

and

$$
\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}\right)_{z=0}=-\frac{V C_{0}^{\beta}}{D}, \quad S_{\alpha}<x \leq S_{\alpha}+S_{\beta} \tag{3b}
$$

### B. Interfacial Temperature

The interface temperature will deviate from $T_E$ due to solute diffusion and surface energy (Figure 7(b)). The interface must be superheated in order to achieve a composition gradient to drive the diffusion process. The composition gradient creates a corresponding melting point variation along the interface. The compositional superheat for each phase may be written as

$$
\Delta T_{c}=m\left[C(x)-C_{E}\right] \tag{5}
$$

where $m$ is the liquidus slope (negative for alpha and positive for beta). To achieve a near-isothermal interface, the interface becomes curved to balance the solutal superheat along the interface. The resulting curvature lowers the equilibrium melting temperature. The curvature undercooling may be written as

$$
\Delta T_{\sigma}=\Gamma \kappa(x) \tag{6}
$$

where the capillary constant, $\Gamma$, is given by the Gibbs-Thomson relationship, and $\kappa(x)$ is the local curvature at position $x$ on the interface. The kinetics of atom attachment and detachment provide a third term to the interfacial temperature. For simplification, the kinetic term will be ignored for the present analysis.

![](./images/811999540759494657_15.jpg)

Fig. 7—A eutectic diagram. (a) Solute rejection notation and (b) solutal, capillary, and total undercooling during melting and the change in composition for each phase.

The average temperature of the interface due to the solutal and curvature effects can be used in the analysis of the interface morphology. The average temperature is the sum of the eutectic temperature, the solutal superheat, and the curvature undercooling, written as

$$
\overline{T}=T_{E}+m\left(\overline{C}-C_{E}\right)-\Gamma\langle\kappa\rangle \tag{7}
$$

where $\overline{C}$ and $\langle\kappa\rangle$ are the average composition and average curvature, respectively. The equation is written separately for each phase.

The average composition of the interface will be the same as that derived by Jackson and Hunt, except opposite in sign due to the negative velocity terms. Similarly, the average curvature undercoolings are the same as derived by Jackson and Hunt. The resulting average temperatures of the interface for each phase are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{T}_{\alpha} &=T_{E}+m_{\alpha}\left[C_{\infty}+B_{0}+\frac{2 V C_{0}\left(S_{\alpha}+S_{\beta}\right)^{2} P}{S_{\alpha}}\right] \\
&-\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}^{L} \sin \theta_{\alpha}^{L}}{S_{\alpha}}
\end{aligned} \tag{8a}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{T}_{\beta} &=T_{E}+m_{\beta}\left[-C_{\infty}-B_{0}+\frac{2 V C_{0}\left(S_{\alpha}+S_{\beta}\right)^{2} P}{S_{\beta}}\right] \\
&-\frac{\Gamma_{\beta}^{L} \sin \theta_{\beta}^{L}}{S_{\beta}}
\end{aligned} \tag{8b}
$$

with

P = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(n \pi)^3} \sin^2 \left( \frac{n \pi S_{\alpha}}{S_{\alpha} + S_{\beta}} \right) \tag{8c}

An important distinction between solidification and melting is apparent in Eq. [8]. The lamellar widths are not variables in melting, as they were fixed during the prior solidification step. Consequently, the average interface temperatures in the two phases are fixed at a given velocity.

### C. Interface Shape

The interface shape (Figure 8(a)) may be calculated by determining the local curvature along the interface. Neglecting the effect of the thermal gradient, the interface of each phase is assumed to be isothermal at its average interface temperature value. The solute profile (Figure 8(b)) can be converted to a temperature profile, $\Delta T_c(x)$, as in (Figure 8(c)). The interface melts isothermally by balancing the temperature due to the solute profile and the local curvature (arrows, Figure 8(c)). Given the average interface temperature (Eq. [8]) and the solute profile (Eqs. [2] and [4]), the local curvature may be solved for as

$$
\Gamma \kappa(x) = m[C(x) - C_E] - \overline{\Delta T} \tag{9}
$$

Approximating the curvature with $d^2 z/dx^2$ and integrating twice with respect to $x$, the interface shape, $z(x)$, may be obtained as

$$
z(x) = \int \left\{ \int \frac{1}{\Gamma} [m\{C(x) - C_E\} - \overline{\Delta T}] dx \right\} dx \tag{10}
$$

## V. DISCUSSION

The derivation of the solute profile in the previous section was based on a flat interface. This condition allows diffusion to be coupled between the two phases. The diffusion distance is on the lamellar scale, where the sinusoidal solute profile decays exponentially from the interface with a characteristic distance of $(\lambda/2 \pi)$, as in Eq. [2]. For the theory to accurately predict the experimental solute profile and, consequently, the interfacial temperature and morphology, the observed interfaces must be planar on a lamellar spacing scale.

Experiments 2 through 4 and 6 through 8 exhibited phase leads of the same order of magnitude as the lamellar spacing (Table I). The model for these experiments should be in agreement within experimental accuracy. Experiments 1 and 5 exhibit phase leads of 230 and $308 \mu \text{m}$, respectively, which are an order of magnitude larger than the lamellar spacings. For these experiments, the solute profile will differ significantly from the predicted profile. Because the leading phase (extended farthest into the liquid) (Figure 2) is not influenced by the diffusive field of the trailing phase, its melting characteristics will differ from that for a planar eutectic interface. The lead phase is more closely approximated as cellular melting but on a smaller microstructural scale. This case corresponds to melting conditions that are out of the coupled melting regime. No model has been developed for cellular melting, which leaves a possibility for future research, both for single-phase and multiple-phase microstructures. Also, to understand the loss of coupling, a theoretical model that investigates the instability of a planar front eutectic needs to be developed. We will, therefore, discuss only results that are relevant to the coupled growth.

separately from Eq. [8]. The average temperature of the interface is a function of velocity, lamellar spacing, volume fraction, and material constants. Generally, the average temperature of the alpha phase is going to differ from the beta phase. This difference is plotted in Figure 9 for the $CBr_{4}$-$C_{2}Cl_{6}$ alloy as a function of lamellar spacing at several velocities. It is seen that the melting temperature difference increases with increasing velocity and lamellar spacing. The volume fraction has only a slight effect on the temperature difference.

The majority of experiments exhibited a near-planar front with both phases melting at approximately the same temperature, contrary to the theoretical predictions of different average melting temperatures of the two phases. The mechanism available to achieve this isothermal interface is the interface morphology. This is different than eutectic solidification where the interface adjusts by changes in lamellar spacing.

The next step is determining the average interface temperature because, theoretically, $\overline{\Delta T_{\alpha}} \neq \overline{\Delta T_{\beta}}$. Since the system can adjust the shape of the interface, we shall consider that the system selects the shape that gives equal undercooling for the two phases. Given two different melting temperatures, the interface may adjust by (1) the lower melting phase reducing its curvature to melt at the melting temperature of the other phase, (2) the higher melting phase increasing its curvature to melt at the melting temperature of the other phase, or (3) some combination of the previous two criteria.

Comparison with the experimental results shows that the system selects the second process. Experimental interface shapes were digitized and then plotted against the predicted interface shapes, as shown in Figure 10. The predicted shapes closely fit the experimental shapes, verifying that each phase melts at the average temperature of the lower melting phase. The only discrepancy seen in the comparison is that the experimental interface has a rounder tip than the predicted interface. This is a likely result of the model neglecting temperature gradient effects. Traveling along the interface in the $z$ direction increases the temperature, thus, reducing the curvature necessary to achieve an isothermal interface. This results in the tip region, being at a higher temperature, becoming more rounded.

The interface melts isothermally by adjusting the interface morphology. However, the average melting temperatures for each phase were seen to be fixed (Eq. [8]). For the average temperatures of both phases to be equal, Eq. [8] must possess a variable or be incorrect in their derivation. Examining the experimental interfaces of Figure 1, it appears that the dynamic contact angles, $\theta_{\alpha}$ and $\theta_{\beta}$, are changing at the microstructural scale with changes in velocity that will affect the surface energy contributions in Eq. [8]. However, the surface energy contribution is negligible at velocities greater than $\sim 1\ \mu$m/s. With a maximal difference of contact angles of 0 and 90 deg, the surface energy cannot compensate for the differences in average temperatures seen in Figure 9. The average temperatures must then equate by changes in the average composition.

The exact composition profile and average composition were determined for a planar eutectic interface. Therefore, the average composition must be altered due to the slight nonplanarity seen in Figures 1(b) and (c). Examining Figure 9, differences in melting temperatures are significant at the higher velocities, and the beta phase has the higher melting temperature. Experimentally, the alpha phase leads the beta phase, melting at a higher temperature (Figures 1(b) and (c)). The lead alpha phase affects the composition profile of the beta phase. As the tip region of the alpha phase melts, the liquid composition between the alpha tips is reduced. This solute concentration reduction effectively produces a boundary layer for the melting beta phase. The effect of this boundary layer is that the $C_{\infty}$ term of Eq. [8b] is increased without affecting the $B_{o}$ term (the volume fraction is unaltered). As the velocity increases, the alpha tips are narrower (Figures 1(b) and (c)) more solid has been melted, and the $C_{\infty}$ term is increased. This is necessary to adjust for greater melting temperature differences at higher velocities. The alteration of the solute concentration between the alpha tips is similar to the formation of an interdendritic eutectic during solidification due to the solute rejection by the dendrite tips.

## VI. CONCLUSIONS

Directional solidification experiments have been carried out in a model transparent system to examine the process of eutectic melting. In contrast to eutectic growth, eutectic melting lacks the mechanism of lamellar spacing adjustment. Instead, diffusive coupling and a macroscopic planar interface are maintained by adjustment of the interface morphology. Increased curvature lowers the melting temperature of one phase sufficiently to allow isothermal melting of both phases. A theoretical model of eutectic melting is developed, and it is shown that the system selects the interface shapes such that the higher melting phase increases its curvature so that both phases have the same average temperature.

Both coupled and noncoupled melting have been observed. In the noncoupled region, one of the phases can melt at a significantly higher rate so that the other phase protrudes significantly, which can lead to the spherodization of this phase. The basic ideas developed in this article in a model transparent system should be applicable to metallic systems. In processes such as welding, the spherodization during the remelting of eutectic can give rise to the presence of single-phase particles and can lead to eutectic grains around these particles with different orientations. This effect could be significant in high speed melting such as in laser welding. Also, in soldering, if the solder is not completely melted, the leading single phase can reduce the fluidity of the solder.
