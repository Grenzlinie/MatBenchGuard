A combination of first-principles calculations based on density-functional theory, pseudopotentials, and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements is used in order to study Au segregation in $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}(111)$ surfaces. Our theoretical results suggest Au compositions from 50% to 75% in the topmost layer, depending on the chemical potentials of the atomic species. This strong Au segregation is restricted to the topmost surface plane and it is supported by the XPS measurements in a semiquantitative manner.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$ alloy has been widely studied as a model for binary alloys. This alloy has often been selected for thermodynamic studies of order-disorder transitions; one of the main reasons is that, in contrast with other $L1_2$ compounds, the $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$ suffers first order transition at a temperature of 663 K, well below the melting point.$^{1}$ In particular, the surface order-disorder transition temperature is different from the bulk, and this transition has been extensively studied as well.$^{2-5}$

Besides showing surface disorder effects, often in binary alloys one atomic species segregates to the surface,$^{6,7}$ modifying the surface composition and, hence, its properties.$^{8}$ Segregation can be studied by analyzing the energy balance at zero temperature. Along the [111] direction, all atomic planes of $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$ are composed of 25% Au and 75% Cu atoms, so that would be the composition of the unsegregated (111) surface. Theoretical and experimental studies of $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}(111)$ are scarce compared to those of the (100) surface, and they have mainly focused on the order-disorder transition.$^{9-18}$ Concerning specifically Au segregation to the surface, apparently there are conflicting results, both for experimental and theoretical work. McDavid and Fain$^{19}$ found 64% Au enrichment in the topmost surface layer using Auger spectroscopy. Huang *et al.*$^{20}$ used reflection electron microscopy to find evidence of gold enrichment at the surface as well. Stierle *et al.*$^{21}$ and Renner *et al.*$^{22}$ found by synchrotron x-ray diffraction and crystal truncation rod scattering that the alloy is $L1_2$ ordered and unsegregated up to the topmost surface layer. Theoretical papers using Monte Carlo simulations in combination with classical interatomic potentials (in which the electronic degrees of freedom are not explicitly included) also report Au segregation to the (111) surface. While Hayoun *et al.*$^{7}$ found a concentration of 53% of Au in the first surface layer and only 20% of Au in the second layer, Hou and El Azzaoui.$^{23}$ found 40% Au in the first surface layer and that segregation occurs only in the topmost layer.

Larger amounts of Au segregation can be achieved not in ultra-high vacuum (UHV) conditions, but by using electrochemical driving forces. The presence of an electrolyte cannot only enhance mobility but also effectively change the relative chemical potential of the Cu-Au system beyond the limits imposed by UHV conditions (to be described in Sec. III). In this way, copper can be extracted much more efficiently from the surface causing dealloying. Under those conditions, complete Au segregation to the topmost atomic layer has been observed.$^{24}$ Interestingly, the Au monolayer observed in this situation is not epitaxial to the underlying $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$, but expands to an in-plane lattice constant between those of Au and $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$.

In this work we use a combination of theoretical and experimental techniques to study Au segregation at the (111) surface of $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$. Our theoretical approach is based on

Our results clearly indicate the occurrence of Au segregation at zero temperature. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements support, in semiquantitative manner, the theoretical findings.

## II. THEORETICAL AND EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGIES

We use the framework of DFT$^{25,26}$ with the exchange-correlation potential in a generalized gradient approximation parameterized by Perdew *et al.*,$^{27}$ and use periodic boundary conditions. Ultrasoft pseudopotentials$^{28}$ are employed and a plane wave basis with maximum kinetic energy of 50 Ry is used, as implemented by the plane-wave self-consistent field code.$^{29}$ The $k$-point meshes for the Brillouin zone sampling are constructed by using the Monkhorst–Pack scheme$^{30}$ with $7\times7\times1$ $k$-point samplings (25 irreducible $k$-points).

We use a slab geometry with nine layers (Fig. 1) and four atoms per layer, which allow us to vary each layer's composition among five different values (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% Au concentration). Along the axis perpendicular to the surface, a large 2 nm lattice constant is used to provide a substantial vacuum region. For the three inner layers we fix the atomic positions, and we fully relax the three outermost layers of each end of the slab to relax until atomic forces are below 0.001 Ry/bohr.

XPS studies on the $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}(111)$ single crystal were performed at LAFES (Surface and Thin Films Labortatory)—INMETRO. The experiments were performed in an ESCA-plus P surface science system (OMICRON Nanotechnology) equipped with an XM 1000 MkII x-ray source and a monochromated AlK$\alpha$ radiation (1486.6 eV) as excitation source operating at 300 W. Broad scan survey spectra with pass energy of 50 eV were performed on the sample in order to identify and quantify the Cu and Au elements. One spectrum was collected for the rough sample, i.e., right after being placed in the equipment. Then argon was sputtered, annealed at 680 K for 1 h and allowed to cool down for 1 h. After that, another spectrum was collected. Spectra were analyzed using CasaXPS software (version 2.3.12).$^{31}$ Peaks Cu $2p_{3/2}$, O $1s$, Au $4f$, and C $1s$ were analyzed and quantifications were carried out by correcting the peak areas with sensitivity factors obtained from Scofield.$^{32}$ A Voigt function was applied to fit the peaks, and the Shirley background correction$^{33}$ was used for all spectra.

## III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

### A. Theoretical results

In the bulk, all $\langle 111 \rangle$ planes of $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$ direction have three Cu atoms and one Au atom per unit cell, so the nonsegregated (111) surface has proportion 3/1 of Cu/Au atoms, or 25% Au composition. Figure 1 shows the nonsegregated slab model. At the outermost surface plane, the Au atom suffers a small upward relaxation (0.10 Å above the Cu atoms). This value is very similar to the one reported by Dias *et al.*$^{34}$ and references therein, in their study of the $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}(100)$ surface. As usual in metal surfaces, the top layer undergoes an inward relaxation. The interlayer spacing between the first and the second layer (using the Cu atoms as reference) is reduced by 2.3% (0.05 Å) with respect to the bulk value. Stierle *et al.*$^{21}$ measured the same 2.3% reduction in the surface interlayer spacing to the (111) surface over the bulk. Notice that theoretical lattice constant from our calculations value is 3.79 Å, 1.01% larger than the experimental value (3.75 Å).$^{35}$

We then study the energetic stability of different segregated surfaces by changing the atomic concentration of Cu and Au atoms in the first and second surface layers. To compare the energy of surfaces with different stoichiometries, we use the methodology described in Dias *et al.*$^{34}$ The surface energy $\gamma_{\text{surf}}$ is written as

$$2\gamma_{\text{surf}}A = E_{\text{slab}} - \sum_{i} \mu_i N_i, \tag{1}$$

where $A$ is the surface area and we approximate the Gibbs free energy of the surface by the total energy $E_{\text{slab}}$ of a slab (therefore neglecting the $TS$ and $pV$ terms). This is well-known to be a good approximation solid-state systems, mostly because of the partial cancellation of vibrational free energies of the alloy surface and bulk systems, and for sufficiently low temperatures (well below the order-disorder transition temperature), so that the effects of configurational entropy can be neglected.$^{36}$ Furthermore, the results obtained at zero temperature constitute the basis for understanding finite-temperature properties. The factor of 2 appears in the expression because we use a symmetric slab with two identical surfaces on each side. Equation (1) assumes that the surface is in equilibrium with reservoirs of Cu and Au, acting as sources and sinks of atoms of each species. The reservoir

chemical potential and the number of atoms for each species $i$ are given by $\mu_{i}$ and $N_{i}$, respectively. As described in detail by Dias *et al.*, $^{34}$ the chemical potential of Au and Cu are related by imposing equilibrium with $\text{Cu}_{3}\text{Au}$ bulk, and therefore the surface energy can be written as a function of the chemical potential of one species, that we choose to be $\mu_{\text{Cu}}$, which can be varied between an upper and a lower bound

$$
\mu_{\text{Cu}}^{\text{bulk}} - \frac{\Delta H_{f}}{3} < \mu_{\text{Cu}} < \mu_{\text{Cu}}^{\text{bulk}}, \tag{2}
$$

where $\Delta H_{f}=0.142$ eV is the ordered alloy formation energy per formula unit with respect to the bulk species. The inequality (2) is obtained by imposing that Au and Cu chemical potentials have to be smaller than the corresponding bulk values (total energies per atom in the bulk phase).

Figure 2 shows the values of surface energies as a function of $\mu_{\text{Cu}}$ calculated for the various surfaces. The notation in the figure means Au composition in the 1st and 2nd layers, i.e., 50/25 means 50% Au in the top layer and 25% in the 2nd layer. Our calculations show that, for most of the values of chemical potential, the most stable structure is 50/25, and only for very low values of the Cu chemical potential (Au-rich conditions) the stable structure is 75/25. This shows a substantial Au segregation effect, but limited to the outermost layer.

The two most stable structures are shown in Fig. 3. In the 50/25 structure (left panel), the surface layer has an interplanar distance of $2.17\,\text{\AA}$, slightly higher than in the unsegregated surface, and the two Au atoms are $0.10\,\text{\AA}$ and $0.21\,\text{\AA}$ above Cu atoms in the same layer. In the 75/25 structure (right panel), the interplanar distance is the same as the bulk interplanar distance $(2.19\,\text{\AA})$ and Au atoms are $0.08\,\text{\AA}$, $0.24\,\text{\AA}$, and $0.24\,\text{\AA}$ above the Cu atom.

### B. Experimental results

Figure 4 shows the XPS spectrum for the annealed sample (which is likely more representative of an equilibrium state). From the ratio of $\text{Cu}(2p_{3/2})$ and $\text{Au}(4f)$ peak areas, an effective Cu/Au composition ratio $\overline{R}=(1-\bar{x})/\bar{x}=1.7$ is found, where $\bar{x}$ is an effective Au concentration. This value is lower than the ideal 3/1 ratio, clearly indicating Au segregation to the surface. However, it cannot be directly related to the Au composition of the first layer since its determination assumes the sample to be homogeneous (nonsegregated), and XPS will probe photoelectrons coming from many atomic layers into the bulk, with varying composition, as determined by the electron mean free path.

The mean free paths of $\text{Cu}(2p_{3/2})$and $\text{Au}(4f)$ photoelectrons in $\text{Cu}_{3}\text{Au}$ can be obtained by considering the bulk density and the model by Seah and Dench, $^{37}$ which gives $l_{\text{Cu}}=0.77$ nm and $l_{\text{Au}}=1.96$ nm. Then, a rough estimate of the surface composition can be obtained by considering the following simple model. For a homogeneous sample, the peak area ratio $A_{\text{Cu}}/A_{\text{Au}}$ can be related to the effective composition ratio $\overline{R}$ by

$$
\frac{A_{\text{Cu}}}{A_{\text{Au}}}=\overline{R}\frac{f_{\text{Cu}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-nd/l_{\text{Cu}}}}{f_{\text{Au}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-nd/l_{\text{Au}}}}, \tag{3}
$$

where $n$ indexes the atomic planes starting from the outermost surface plane $(n=0)$, $d=0.217$ nm is the interplanar

FIG. 4. Top: Survey scan XPS spectrum for Cu₃Au(111) after annealing. Middle and bottom: Zooming the survey scan near the Cu $2p_{3/2}$ and Au $4f$ peaks, respectively. Continuous and dashed lines show the peak and background curve fittings, respectively.

distance in the [111] direction and $f_j$ is a factor taking into account the atomic cross section and detection efficiency for Cu($2p_{3/2}$) and Au($4f$) photoelectrons. Each term of the sum $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-nd/l_j}$ represents the contribution of photoelectrons coming from different atomic planes at distances $nd$ from the surface; the contributions die-off exponentially with the distance from the surface divided by the mean-free-path. For homogeneous samples, the sums can be performed analytically and, in the limit $l_j \gg d$, the quantity $f_j\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-nd/l_j}=f_jl_j/d$ is usually associated with the atomic sensitivity factor $S_j$.³⁶ For nonhomogeneous samples, Eq. (3) can be generalized

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{A_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{A_{\mathrm{Au}}} & =\frac{f_{\mathrm{Cu}} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1-x_{n}\right) e^{-n d / l_{\mathrm{Cu}}}}{f_{\mathrm{Au}} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x_{n} e^{-n d / l_{\mathrm{Au}}}} \\
& =\frac{f_{\mathrm{Cu}}\left[\left(1-x_{0}\right)+\frac{3}{4} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-n d / l_{\mathrm{Cu}}}\right]}{f_{\mathrm{Au}}\left[x_{0}+\frac{1}{4} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-n d / l_{\mathrm{Au}}}\right]},
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

where it is assumed, as suggested by the theoretical results, that $x_n$ (the Au composition for layer $n$) is only modified from its bulk value of 1/4 in the first layer, where it takes the value $x_0$. Combining Eqs. (3) and (4), we obtain $x_0$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{0} & =\frac{\left(1+\Sigma_{\mathrm{Au}}\right)\left(1+\frac{3}{4} \Sigma_{\mathrm{Cu}}\right)-\frac{1}{4} \bar{R} \Sigma_{\mathrm{Au}}\left(1+\Sigma_{\mathrm{Cu}}\right)}{\left(1+\Sigma_{\mathrm{Au}}\right)+\bar{R}\left(1+\Sigma_{\mathrm{Cu}}\right)} \\
& =1.0 \pm 0.2,
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

where $\Sigma_j = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} e^{-nd/l_j}$ and the error is estimated by considering 10% uncertainties in the mean-free paths and in $\bar{R}$. This value of $x_0$ is larger than the one predicted by theory, even in conditions of high Au chemical potential ($x_0=0.75$), but considering the many simplifications of the model and the semiquantitative nature of composition measurements by XPS, we consider it as giving a fair support to the theoretical findings and a clear indication of Au segregation in Cu₃Au(111).

## IV. CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we performed first-principles calculations and XPS measurements to investigate Au segregation near the Cu₃Au(111) surface. Our theoretical findings support a fairly strong segregation effect, between 50% and 75% Au composition, depending on chemical potentials. Since previous theoretical and experimental studies of this effect are scarce and report very scattered values,⁷,¹⁹,²⁰,²³ our state-of-the-art calculations pinpoint a reliable and quantitative value for the amount of Au segregation. XPS measurements provide extra semiquantitative support for a strong Au segregation effect in this surface.
