# First-principle study on the surface and interface properties of the half-metallic ferromagnet of rocksalt structural BaC

## ABSTRACT

Recent study showed that rocksalt BaC exhibits half-metallic ferromagnetism (Dong and Zhao, 2011). Here we use the first-principles calculations to investigate the electronic and magnetic properties of the BaC (001) and (111) surfaces and the interfaces with the SnSe (111) substrate. The obtained results reveal that the half-metallicity verified in bulk BaC is preserved at the (001) and (111) surfaces. Unfortunately, interfacial configuration shows that the half-metallicity of bulk rocksalt BaC is destroyed for all possible configurations of C-Se, C-Sn, Ba-Se, and Ba-Sn. Moreover, by computing the interface adhesion energies, we evaluate the interfacial adhesive strength for the four possible interfacial structures. We also discuss the changes of the atomic magnetic moments at the (001) and (111) surfaces and the interfaces layers with respect to the corresponding bulk values.

## 1. Introduction

In the past thirty years, a great deal of attention has been paid to the expansion of spin electronic devices because of their potential implementation to the next production of electronic devices. The Heusler alloys exhibit the characteristics of metal and semiconductor in the spin-up and spin-down channels, respectively. This characteristic was given the name of half-metallic (HM) magnetism. The notion of HM ferromagnet was offered by de Groot and collaborators in 1983 [1]. Thereafter, other renowned materials have also been found to be HM ferromagnets [2–12], e.g., the double perovskite, the alkaline-earth pnictides and carbides, the europium chalcogenides, the diluted magnetic semiconductors, the pyrite structure, the transition-metal pnictides and chalcogenides, the rutile and spinel structural compounds. Interestingly, in some alkaline-earth pnictides and carbides [13,14], the magnetic moments are formed mainly by the anion p electrons, and they are named sp HM ferromagnets. Differently, in the zinc-blende (ZB) CrAs, CrSb and CrTe the anion p electrons are completely loaded, therefore the magnetic moments originate from the spin polarization of the $3d$ electrons [15]. Usually, the magnetic moments for ZB HM transition-metal pnictides or chalcogenides are larger than those of ZB sp HM ferromagnets [16,17].

Theoretically, BaC has the structure of rocksalt (RS) with lattice constants of $a = 6.00$ Å [18]. Above room temperature, RS BaC shows a HM ferromagnetism. In addition, it is found that the RS-type structure is energetically most stable among the different four structures: RS, CsCl, ZB, and NiAs [18]. By using first-principles calculations, the bulk compound BaC in zinc blende, RS, and wurtzite structures was predicted to be HM [5,18,19]. In 2011, Dong and Zhao [20] predicted that RS MC (M = Ca, Sr, and Ba) under small pressure belongs to the group of HM ferromagnets.

Because the surface and interface usually affect and even destroy the half-metallicity of the bulk [21–24], it is very important to examine the surface properties and their interface with semiconductors for the practical spintronic implementations. In this paper, by using the first-principles calculations, we extend the previous study on sp HM ferromagnet of RS BaC [20] to the BaC (111) and (001) surfaces and the BaC/SnSe (111) interface. It is found that the bulk HM property is kept at both the (111) and (001) surfaces. Unfortunately, the four possible configurations of the BaC/SnSe (111) interface destroy the HM characteristic.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. The electronic structure and magnetic properties of (1 1 1) and (0 0 1) surfaces

To simulate the (1 1 1) and (0 0 1) surfaces properties of RS BaC, the bulk equilibrium lattice constant of $6.003$ Å [20] is used, which is almost the same as the theoretical value of $6.00$ Å [18]. We adopt a slab model containing 13 atomic layers. To avoid the interactions of neighboring slabs, we add a $15$ Å vacuum above the surfaces. The (1 1 1) surfaces of BaC are built of alternating Ba and C layers normal to the surface direction. Consequently, either a completely Ba or a completely C terminates the surface, and they are called Ba- and C-terminated (1 1 1) surfaces, respectively. For the (0 0 1) surface, there is only one type of termination with both Ba and C, where each layer comprises Ba and C together. We examine different thick slabs containing 7-15 layers and different k meshes of more than $13 \times 13 \times 1$. We discover that the changes of the calculated magnetic moments $(0.001\ \mu_{\text{B}})$ of surface atoms and the bond length between surface and subsurface atoms $(0.0018$ Å) are very small. Therefore, the thickness of the slab of thirteen layers and the k meshes of $12 \times 12 \times 1$ we used is thick appropriate to study the surface properties. Previous study also indicated that the slab of thirteen layers is adequate to study the characteristics of the surface in some RS materials [27]. The relaxation of the top five atomic layers of the BaC surface is performed by the total energy and atomic force calculations. After relaxation, there is a decrease in the Ba-C length of the bond between the layers of the surface and subsurface. Before relaxation, the bond length is $3.00$ Å for both surfaces while after relaxation, the values decrease to $2.76$ and $2.77$ Å for Ba- and C-terminated (1 1 1) surfaces, respectively. Furthermore, at the (0 0 1) surface, the bond length value decreases to $2.89$ Å.

To explain HM properties of BaC surfaces, we shown in Figs. 1 and 2 the calculated surface atomic density of states (DOS) of RS BaC. The minority spins are factored by $-1$ and the Fermi levels are set to zero. It is very clear that both the (0 0 1) and the Ba- and C-terminated (1 1 1) surfaces of RS BaC are HM, because the spin-up and spin-down channels have semiconducting and metallic properties, respectively. Therefore, both the (0 0 1) and (1 1 1) surfaces preserve the half-metallicity of bulk RS BaC, For comparison, the DOS of Ba and C atoms in the bulk RS BaC are also presented, which are drawn in the black lines. In addition, all the sub-surfaces also reveal a clear HM property. We noted that for CaC with ZB structure the half-metallicity is destroyed at both the Ca- and C-terminated (0 0 1) surfaces [28]. We believe the reason is that the Ba-C bond at the (0 0 1) surface of ZB BaC is broken. However, for RS BaC, Ba-C bond at the (0 0 1) surface is kept. The Ba and C atoms have four nearest-neighboring atoms in the bulk ZB BaC. After the forfeit of two neighboring atoms compared to that of bulk BaC, the (0 0 1) surface has two nearest neighboring atoms, and the Ba-C bond is broken at the (0 0 1) surface, which makes the surface properties greatly differed from the bulk case. While the (0 0 1) surface with the RS structure loses only one nearest-neighboring atom compared to the bulk case, there are five nearest-neighboring ones for the surface atoms, and the Ba-C bond is kept at the surface, which makes the surface effect slight.

Comparing the surface partial DOS with those in the bulk RS BaC, we discover that both the energy gaps of spin-up channels of the (0 0 1) surface atoms of Ba and C (1.63-1.71 eV) are increased compared to those in the bulk BaC (1.39-1.55 eV) as presented in Fig. 2. While, the energy gap of C (Ba) atom increases (decreases) at the BaC (1 1 1) surface as indicated in Fig. 1.

Clearly, the value of Ba magnetic moment at the (1 1 1) surface is reduced by 50% compared to that in the bulk RS BaC because of the weakening of spin split of Ba DOS as shown in Fig. 1. ### 3.2. Interfacial half-metallicity and interfacial adhesive strength

To understand the electronic properties and magnetism of the interface studied here, we select semiconductor SnSe because SnSe has the same structure (space group No. 225) and similar lattice constant (6.02 Å) [29] to RS BaC. Because the SnSe (1 1 1) surfaces have also two kinds of terminations, the BaC/SnSe (1 1 1) interfaces have four possible configurations: (1) the Ba-Sn configuration, it means that the BaC (1 1 1) surface is terminated with Ba, and the SnSe (1 1 1) surface is terminated with Sn. Similarly, the other three configurations are (2) Ba-Se, (3) C-Sn, and (4) C-Se. A slab of thirteen atomic layers of HM BaC and nine atomic layers of the semiconductor SnSe is constructed, which is enough to discuss the interfacial properties. Firstly, we carry out structural relaxations for the four configurations of the BaC/SnSe (1 1 1) interfaces. After that, the optimized bond distances between the interfacial atoms are listed in Table 2.

We further calculate the interfacial adhesion energies in order to compare the adhesive strength among all the possible different configurations for the relaxed interface of BaC/SnSe (1 1 1). The interfacial adhesion energy is definite as the work per interfacial area segregates the interface into two surfaces, and thus it can be considered as a measure of the strength of the adhesion. For the interface of BaC/SnSe (1 1 1), the interfacial adhesion energy $\gamma$ per interface area can be written as.

$$
\gamma=\left(E_{\mathrm{BaC}}+E_{\mathrm{SnSe}}-E_{\mathrm{BaC} / \mathrm{SnSe}}\right) / A, \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{BaC}}, E_{\mathrm{SnSe}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{BaC} / \mathrm{SnSe}}$ are the total energies of the relaxed BaC (1 1 1) slab, SnSe (1 1 1) slab and BaC/SnSe (1 1 1) slab with the slab area $A$, respectively. The calculated adhesion energies for four possible different configurations of BaC/SnSe (1 1 1) interface are listed in Table 2. From Table 2, we can notice that the adhesion energy becomes gradually smaller from C-Sn $(2.76 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~m}^{-2})$ to Ba-Se $(1.52 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~m}^{-2})$, Ba-Sn $(0.51 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~m}^{-2})$, and C-Se $(0.35 \mathrm{~J} \mathrm{~m}^{-2})$ configurations, which means that the C-Sn configuration is most stable among the four possible configurations. We also shown in Fig. 3 the interface atomic DOS of the relaxed BaC/SnSe (1 1 1) interfaces. From Fig. 3, one can perceive that for four possible configurations the up and down spin electrons show metallic properties. This means that the halfmetallicity confirmed in the bulk BaC and at the (1 1 1) and (001) surfaces is destroyed at the four interface configurations of Ba-Sn, Ba-Se, C-Sn, and C-Se, because the interfacial states are found around the Fermi level in the majority spin DOS.

Finally, we also calculate the atomic magnetic moments at the BaC/SnSe (1 1 1) interface, and at the central layer atoms of the half-metal and the semiconductor slabs. The results are listed in Table 3. Clearly, the spin magnetic moments at the central layer atoms of the half-metal slabs and the semiconductor slabs are very close to those in the bulk, which denotes that adequate thicknesses of the half-metal and semiconductor slabs are used to study the interface properties. ## 4. Conclusion and summary

We have used the first-principles calculations based on density functional theory to expand the previous study on the half-metallicity of RS BaC to the BaC (1 1 1) and (0 0 1) surfaces and the interfaces of BaC/SnSe (1 1 1). It is found that both (0 0 1) and Ba- and C- terminated (1 1 1) surfaces and subsurfaces maintain the HM properties of the bulk RS BaC. In addition, from the atomic DOS of interfacial studies, it is concluded that the half metallicity of the bulk is lost at the Ba-Sn, Ba-Se, C-Sn, and C-Se configurations, because the interfacial states are found around the Fermi level in the majority-spin DOS. Moreover, the surface magnetic moments are increased (decreased) at the C (Ba)-terminated (1 1 1) surface compared to the bulk values, while for the case of the (0 0 1) surface the change is very small.
