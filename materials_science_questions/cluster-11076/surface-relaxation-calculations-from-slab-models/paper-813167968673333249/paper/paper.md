## A density functional theory-based study on the dissociation of NO on a CuO(110) surface

Joaquin Lorenzo Valmoria Moreno, $^{a}$ Allan Abraham Bustria Padama $^{a}$ and Hideaki Kasai $^{*ab}$

NO adsorption and dissociation on a CuO(110) surface are investigated using spin-polarized density functional theory calculations. We found that the dissociation of molecularly adsorbed NO is activated, with the transition state below the reference energy, indicating the possibility of dissociation. The interlayer distance between the two topmost layers contracted, indicating that the surface Cu atoms stabilize themselves with reference to the O atoms present in the subsurface. This leads to a modification of the electronic structure of the surface Cu atoms of Cu(110) which greatly affects the adsorption and dissociation of NO. Based on the results, CuO(110) shows similar characteristics to $Cu_{2}O(110)$. Thus, we can generally say that copper oxide can be an efficient catalyst for NO dissociation regardless of whether the oxide is cuprous or cupric.

### 1 Introduction

Atmospheric pollution is one of the main environmental concerns of our society at present. The majority of the pollutants originate from vehicle exhausts due to fossil fuel combustion. This is a crucial issue for automobile industries and researchers alike. One of the emitted gases is nitrogen oxide $(NO_{x})$ that can form nitric acid in the air, which is the main reason for acid rain and smog. Thus, limiting or totally eradicating the emission of $NO_{x}$ is needed to address this concern.

Understanding the reduction process of $NO_{x}$ on surfaces to form less hazardous gases is of utmost importance and consequently has been widely studied. $^{1-9}$ Generally, the process involves the dissociation of the molecule with the aid of a catalyst to form other less harmful gases. Due to the large amount of energy required to break the N-O bond, NO dissociation is the rate-limiting step. It is well known that precious metals (such as Rh, Pd and Pt) used in three-way exhaust catalysts work well to reduce the emission of $NO_{x}$, but their high cost is a road block to a more prevalent use. Therefore, a more readily available and inexpensive material with comparable, if not better, catalytic performance is needed.

Using density functional theory, we have previously investigated the dissociation of NO on Cu(111) and $Cu_{2}O(111)$ surfaces. $^{10-12}$ In the case of Cu(111), we found that a high activation barrier hinders the dissociation of NO, thus desorption is more likely to occur. On the other hand, for $Cu_{2}O(111)$, the reaction path for NO dissociation is exothermic in nature, thus no additional energy is required for dissociation (with reference to the energetics of an isolated NO molecule and a bare $Cu_{2}O(111)$ surface). $^{10}$ The reaction path of NO on $Cu_{2}O(111)$ is comparable to the case of Rh(111). $^{13}$ The high activation barrier on Cu(111) is due to the inability of Cu atoms, having a filled d orbital, to interact with NO. In the case of the O-terminated $Cu_{2}O(111)$ surface, NO adsorption is possible but dissociated N and O atoms are found to be unstable due to the passivation of the active sites by the O atoms present on the surface. On the other hand, for the Cu-terminated $Cu_{2}O(111)$ surface, states are present around the Fermi level, thus Cu atoms can participate in the charge donation process that weakens the N-O bond. Together with the presence of available hollow sites, O can easily dissociate from the NO molecule. $^{11}$

The results we have obtained were also found to be in good agreement with our initial experimental findings. $^{11}$ A high $NO_{x}$ conversion rate of 98.3% was obtained on $Cu_{2}O(111)$ under a reducing atmosphere, whereas only 5.7-23.6% was attained for other oxide surfaces. Thus, all our previous studies support the high reactivity of the $Cu_{2}O$ surface towards $NO_{x}$ reduction. However, this leads us to speculate whether or not the other form of copper oxide, CuO, will be comparable with $Cu_{2}O$ for $NO_{x}$ reduction.

CuO nanocrystals have recently gained attention for their interesting catalytic properties. $^{14-16}$ Thus, we find it necessary to investigate the catalytic activity of CuO for $NO_{x}$ reduction. In this study, we provide a detailed investigation on the dissociation of NO on a CuO(110) surface. The (110) facet was chosen due to the presence of active sites on its Cu-terminated surface. $^{17}$ Moreover, X-ray diffraction characterization of CuO

## 2 Computational method

Spin-polarized calculations based on density functional theory¹⁸,¹⁹ are performed using the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP).²⁰,²¹ Generalized gradient approximation (GGA) based on the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional²² is used for the exchange-correlation energy. The projector-augmented wave method²³ is used to describe the ionic core pseudopotential. The Kohn–Sham one-electron valence states are expanded on a basis of plane waves with an energy cutoff of 400 eV.

CuO has a monoclinic structure with space group *C2/c1* ($a = 4.690$ Å, $b = 3.420$ Å, $c = 5.131$ Å and $\beta = 99.540^\circ$).²⁴ The unit cell is shown in Fig. 1. The calculated structural parameters (Cu–O bond lengths: $d_1 = 1.972$ Å, $d_2 = 1.968$ Å; and O–Cu–O angle $= 82.14^\circ$) are in good agreement with the experimental results.²⁴,²⁵ The CuO(110) surface is modeled by four atomic layers in a $2 \times 2$ unit cell. The Brillouin zone is sampled using $6 \times 6 \times 1$ Monkhorst-Pack $k$-points,²⁶ the accuracy of which was tested through a convergence test. CuO(110) can be terminated by a layer of Cu atoms (Cu-terminated) or a layer of Cu and O atoms with twice as much O atoms (CuO₂-terminated). Surface relaxation is considered by optimizing the two topmost layers of the surface and keeping the other layers in their bulk parameters. Most of the surface relaxation occurred along the $z$-direction as the inter-layer distance between the topmost and second layers showed some contraction for both surface terminations. The Cu-terminated surface contracted by 0.11 Å while the CuO₂-terminated surface contracted by 0.08 Å, which are consistent with a previous theoretical study by Xiang *et al*.¹⁷

The adsorption geometries and binding energies of NO on different symmetry sites (shown in Fig. 2) of the surface are determined as it has been previously found that NO is molecularly adsorbed on surfaces.¹³ The coadsorption of N and O atoms is also calculated to determine the possibility of NO dissociation. Using the molecularly adsorbed NO and coadsorbed N and O atoms as initial and final states, respectively, the climbing image-nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method²⁷,²⁸ is used to determine the minimum energy path for NO dissociation. The path is identified using four intermediate images between the initial and final states. The calculations are allowed to converge until the minimum force acting on each atom is 0.03 eV Å⁻¹. The transition state is then calculated by obtaining the difference between the maximum value in the minimum energy path and the initial state. The Fermi level for density of states (DOS) calculations is defined with respect to the vacuum level.

Fig. 4 shows the local density of states (LDOS) of the d orbitals of surface Cu atoms and the molecularly adsorbed NO before and after the adsorption process. The strong adsorption energy of NO on CuO(110) is explained by the excellent hybridization between the NO orbitals and Cu d orbitals in the region between -3 eV and the Fermi energy, as can be clearly seen in Fig. 4c. Moreover, the elongation of the N-O bond after adsorption can be explained by the strength of the sigma bond for the NO molecule. In Fig. 4b, the second peak (at around -11.5 eV) corresponds to the 5σ molecular orbital of the NO molecule. After adsorption, the 5σ peak is at around -7.5 eV, corresponding to a shift towards the Fermi energy and making the sigma bond relatively unstable as compared with NO in the gas phase. This weakens considerably the strength of the N-O bond, thus making dissociation easier.

We further explain the weakening of the N-O bond and the dissociation process through Bader analysis of the charge distribution. After molecular adsorption, the total charge on the adsorbed NO molecule is 11.51|e|, or 4.94|e| in the N atom and 6.57|e| in the O atom. This means that the NO molecule has gained 0.51|e| transferred from the surface. Examination of the partial charge density of the NO molecule for the region right below the Fermi level (-0.5 eV to 0 eV) revealed that the extra charge transferred from the surface occupies the previously unoccupied anti-bonding states of NO, as can be seen from the second inset in Fig. 4c, and therefore weakens the N-O bond. After dissociation, the total charge of the coadsorbed N and O atoms is 12.45|e|, or 5.71|e| in the N atom and 6.74|e| in the O atom. This corresponds to a gain of 0.94|e| relative to the initial state before dissociation. Thus, greater charge redistribution between NO and the surface occurs as NO undergoes the dissociation process. In particular, the large charge gain is responsible for the weakening of the N-O bond making the dissociation process easier.

The reaction path for the dissociation of NO on the Cu-terminated CuO(110) surface is shown in Fig. 5 together with the paths for Cu(111) and Cu-terminated Cu₂O(111) from our previous study.¹⁰ The reaction paths for CuO(110) and Cu₂O(111) surfaces show similar features. Both reactions are exothermic, in contrast with the Cu(111) surface. The adsorption energies and energy barriers of the two copper oxides are also similar. Moreover, the transition states lie below the reference energy in case of copper oxides, which is similar to the reaction path of NO dissociation on Rh(111).¹³ The only distinct difference between the copper oxides is that the adsorption energy for the coadsorbed N and O atoms is higher than the adsorption energy for NO molecular adsorption on the CuO(110) surface. This may be favorable for the formation of CO₂ or NCO after interaction with a subsequent adsorbing CO molecule. This will be explored further in a future study.

The NO molecule exhibits better interaction with the copper oxides as shown by their higher adsorption energies as compared with the pure copper surface. It is evident that the molecularly adsorbed NO on the copper oxides was more elongated as compared with pure copper, indicating a weaker N–O bond for the adsorbed NO molecule on copper oxides. The shifted d-orbitals of the surface Cu atoms of the copper oxides enable the surface Cu atoms to participate in the charge donation process that weakens the N–O bond, thus further supporting the lower energy barriers. ## 4 Conclusions
We investigated the adsorption and dissociation of NO on a CuO(110) surface using spin-polarized density functional theory calculations. The inter-layer distance between the two topmost layers contracted after surface relaxation as the surface Cu atoms find stability with the O atoms present in the subsurface. This relaxation leads to a modification of the electronic structure of the Cu atoms in the CuO(110) surface in comparison to the surface atoms of Cu(111). NO is molecularly adsorbed with an N-end configuration on the hollow site of the Cu-terminated CuO(110) surface. The N–O bond is weakened due to the upward shift of the sigma bond and the transfer of charge from the surface to the anti-bonding states of the NO molecule. The reaction path for NO dissociation on CuO(110) is similar to that of $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}(111)$, wherein NO dissociation is activated from its molecularly adsorbed state. However, the transition state lies below the reference energy, making the reaction path comparable to that of Rh(111). The LDOS of the d orbitals of the surface Cu atoms of CuO(110) is shifted closer to the Fermi energy, just like in the case of $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}(111)$, which is a consequence of the interaction with O atoms in the subsurface. This provides the surface Cu atoms of the copper oxides better reactivity with the molecularly adsorbed NO as evidenced by the elongated N–O bond length and lower dissociation energy barrier. In general, we can say that copper oxides are good catalysts for NO dissociation and can be a promising alternative non-precious metal catalyst for the purification of automobile exhausts.
