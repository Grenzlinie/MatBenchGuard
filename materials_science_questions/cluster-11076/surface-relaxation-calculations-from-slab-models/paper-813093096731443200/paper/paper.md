# Theoretical study of the adsorption of lithium fluoride molecules at the (111) surface of $CaF_2$

## H. Dabringhaus *

Mineralogisch-Petrologisches Institut, Universität Bonn, Poppelsdorfer Schloss, D-53115 Bonn, Germany

Received 7 September 1998; accepted for publication 28 April 2000

### Abstract

The adsorption of different lithium fluoride molecules at the (111) surface of $CaF_2$ was studied theoretically. Equilibrium adsorption positions and energies on the relaxed terrace and at the relaxed monomolecular $\langle 110\rangle$ step of type I were calculated considering Coulomb, Born-Mayer and van der Waals interactions including an additional relaxation of crystal ions in the vicinity of the adsorbed molecules. The adsorption energies of the monomer LiF, the linear and the rhombic dimer $Li_2F_2$ on the terrace result as $-1.72$, $-1.11$, and $-1.96$ eV and as $-2.68$, $-2.86$, and $-3.06$ eV at the $\langle 110\rangle$-I step. The monomer adsorption energy at the step is in good agreement with the value of $-2.70\pm 0.17$ eV obtained recently by experimental investigations of the adsorption by desorption transients. Also, the experimentally observed dissociation of the lithium fluoride dimers into monomers at the steps can be understood on the basis of the present theoretical results. The linear dimer, which is metastable in the vapor but proved to be stable on the terrace and at the steps of $CaF_2$(111), may be considered as the intermediate stage of the dimer dissociation. © 2000 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

**Keywords**: Adsorption kinetics; Alkali halides; Computer simulations; Insulating surfaces; Surface relaxation and reconstruction

## 1. Introduction

Reversible adsorption at high substrate temperatures and low fluxes to the surface may be considered as a pre-stage of irreversible growth occurring at lower temperatures and/or higher fluxes. Adsorption may determine the nucleation and influence, in this way, the epitaxial relationship between growing guest and underlying crystal. On the other hand, the investigation of early growth stages may yield additional information about adsorption, e.g. about the surface sites where adsorption takes place. On that score, the study of adsorption and of growth may lead to complementing results and to a better understanding of the principal physical processes occurring in the inspected system.

Adsorption, as well as epitaxial and non-epitaxial layers on surfaces of ionic crystals, is of current scientific and technological interest [1]. In recent papers [2,3] we reported on experimental studies of the adsorption of the lithium fluoride monomer LiF and dimer $Li_2F_2$, which are the major constituents of the lithium fluoride vapor [4], at the (111) surface of $CaF_2$ crystals. In an additional paper [5] the epitaxial growth of lithium fluoride on $CaF_2$(111) was investigated. The aim of these studies was to determine the fundamental principles in the adsorption and growth behavior of ionic molecules crystallizing in the rock salt struc-

These studies followed earlier investigations of pure alkali halide systems, see e.g. [6,7], and of systems with alkaline earth halide molecules interacting with alkali halide surfaces [8–10].

The results of the adsorption experiments in the system $LiF/CaF_2$ were interpreted by an adsorption of monomers and dimers at the monomolecular steps on the $CaF_2$ surface. Also, dissociation and formation of dimers into and out of monomers were attributed to processes at the steps. In addition, early growth of lithium fluoride crystallites was found to occur at the steps. For the most part, growth of LiF crystallites in the epitaxial relationship $LiF(20\overline{1})[010]$ parallel to $CaF_2(111)[\overline{1}01]$ was observed.

One argument for the interpretation by an adsorption at step sites was the rough correspon- dence of the experimental adsorption energy of the monomer $E_{\text{ad}}^{\text{m,ex}}=-2.70\pm 0.17$ eV with a theoretical value $E_{\text{ad}}^{\text{m,th}}=-2.55$ eV calculated for an adsorption at an $\langle 110\rangle$ monostep of type I [11], which is the predominant step type on $CaF_2(111)$ [12]. In this theoretical calculation [5], however, a relax- ation of the $CaF_2$ surface, as well as of crystal ions in the vicinity of the adsorbed molecules, which should increase the adsorption energies consider- ably [13], was ignored. To arrive at more realistic values, these calculations were repeated taking into account a general relaxation of the surface layer of $CaF_2(111)$ and of the steps themselves, as well as an additional relaxation of the ions of the crystal lattice due to the adsorbed molecules. A good correspondence of experimental and theoretical adsorption energies of the monomer at the steps would lead to a further confirmation of the proposed adsorption model [2]. The results may also be of interest for the general case of molecules interacting with Coulomb potentials with surfaces of ionic crystals and complement earlier investigations of the adsorption of alkali halide molecules on surfaces of ionic crystals with rock salt structure; compare references quoted in Ref. [13].

## 2. Calculational conditions

The $CaF_2$ structure (space group: $Fm\overline{3}m$; lattice constant $a_0=5.463\,05$ Å [14]) may be considered as a sequence of $F^--Ca^{2+}-F^-$ triple layers (TL) stacking in the [111] direction at a spacing of $a_0/\sqrt{3}\approx 3.154$ Å. In the bulk, the distance of the upper and the lower fluorine to the intermediate calcium layer amounts to $a_0/(4\sqrt{3})\approx 0.789$ Å. Cleavage occurs parallel to the (111) plane between adjacent fluorine layers so that the surface exposes a fluorine layer to the ambient. Low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) studies of $CaF_2(111)$ show that the threefold symmetry is preserved at the surface [15]. This is confirmed by atomic force microscopic investigations of the cleavage face [12,16,17]. Relaxation of the perfect (111) surface is therefore expected only perpendicular to the surface and should concern complete fluorine and calcium layers parallel to it.

Steps on the thermally annealed $CaF_2(111)$ surface have usually monomolecular height [12]. They run essentially in $\langle 110\rangle$ directions and are of the same type, probably of the so-called type I. For this step type, which can be characterized by a calcium row running between two fluorine rows parallel to the step edge, see Fig. 1, a smaller step energy was calculated than for type II [11]. Because the $\{110\}$ planes perpendicular to the steps are planes of symmetry in the fluorite structure, at the steps only relaxations within these planes are expected. These relaxations should then concern complete calcium and fluorine ion rows parallel to the step.

The most important contributions to the interaction energy in the current system of LiF and $Li_2F_2$ on $CaF_2(111)$ are made by the long-range Coulomb interaction. The Coulomb sum due to the point charges over a semi-infinite ionic crystal is only conditionally convergent, e.g. see Refs. [18,19]. To evade this problem, and to speed up the convergence of the summation, the Ewald method [20,21] is usually employed. In the present case, a special formulation appropriate to two-dimensional slabs [22,23] was implemented into a corresponding Fortran program. A slab consisting of four $F^--Ca^{2+}-F^-$ TL parallel to the surface was used for the calculations. A doubling of the thickness of the slab would change the calculated Coulomb energies by values of $\sim 10^{-5}$ eV only.

## 3. Relaxation of the (111) surface and of the $\langle 110\rangle$-I step

The determination of adsorption positions and energies of admolecules on the $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}(111)$ surface requires knowledge of the relaxation of the 'pure' $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}(111)$ surface and of the steps. Calculations of the surface relaxation of $\mathrm{CaF}_{2}$ performed by other authors (e.g. see Ref. [32]; compare also Ref. [33]) show that, in essence, only the outermost triple layer parallel to the surface is affected. Therefore, the present calculations were restricted to this first TL. These calculations, which were performed with the above-mentioned potentials, yielded the following relatively small displacements from corresponding bulk values: upper fluorine layers $\Delta z=-0.077 \AA$, calcium layer $\Delta z=$ $-0.018 \AA$, lower fluorine layer $\Delta z=-0.010 \AA$. Correspondingly, the normalized $z$-coordinates of these layers result as: $0.1302,-0.0033$, and $-0.1461$; see Table 4 . The relaxation can be described by a contraction of the first TL and an overall shift towards the underlying bulk of the crystal. The above values lie between data given in Ref. [32] and those stated in Ref. [33].

Relaxations calculated by different authors differ somewhat from each other. This seems to be caused, in effect, by the usage of different short-range interactions.

For a calculation of the relaxation of ions at a [10$\overline{1}$] step of type I the relaxations calculated for the first TL were taken into account. That is, both the uppermost half triple layers of the lower and of the upper terrace beside the step were assumed to be relaxed, whereas for the second half layer of the upper terrace, i.e. for the continuation of the first half layer of the lower terrace, the ions were assumed to possess coordinates corresponding to the bulk values. For these three half layers the Coulomb energy was obtained by direct summation of regions comprising each 400 and 300 ion rows in the $x$- and $y$-directions (see Fig. 2), whereas for the remaining bulk (three TL) the Ewald method was used. According to results obtained by Puchin et al. [33], it was assumed that the relaxation of the step concerns, essentially, four ion rows, one $Ca^{2+}$ and three $F^{-}$ rows at the outer step edge, indicated as $Ca$, $F_1$, $F_2$, and $F_3$ in Figs. 1 and 2b. As was mentioned above, only relaxations in the $y$- and $z$-directions were taken into account.

The resulting relaxations of the four ion rows at the step edge are given in Table 2. They are in qualitative agreement with results from Ref. [33]. The relaxations are also represented in Fig. 1 in a sectioning view perpendicular to the [10$\overline{1}$] step. Here, the dotted circles indicate ion positions at the unrelaxed step, and the full circles give the relaxed positions. The relaxations can be described as a contraction of the fluorine ion rows 1 and 2 towards the intermediate calcium row and as a small total shift of the step edge away from the lower terrace. As a consequence of this shift a gap opens at the bottom of the step between the $F_2$ and $F_3$ rows.

Fig. 2a gives a contour plot of the potential energy surface from the lower to the upper terrace at a relaxed [10$\overline{1}$]-I step. This potential energy surface was calculated for a positive unit point charge at $z=0.5$ above the lower and at $z=0.5+1/\sqrt{3}$ above the upper terrace. At the step edge a linear increase from the lower to the higher $z$ was assumed, see broken line in Fig. 1; compare also the gray backed region in Fig. 2b. These $z$-values correspond nearly to heights at which a $Li^{+}$ ion would, in a shift parallel to the surface, just touch the periphery of the upper fluorine ions. The comparison of the potential energy surface in Fig. 2a with the schematic structure scheme in Fig. 2b shows that the positions above ions of the upper fluorine layer (larger, filled circles) are good adsorption sites for positively charged ions, for example for $Li^{+}$, and that positions above the calcium ions (small circles) are good adsorption sites for negatively charged, i.e. for fluorine ions of the adsorbed lithium fluoride molecules. The latter concerns, especially, the calcium row at the step edge, for which the largest adsorption energy is found. Here, however, the adsorption of an isolated ion should occur in a [101] direction; see Fig. 1. At the site above an ion of the lower fluorine layer an adsorbed negative ion, i.e. fluorine ion, feels a smaller attractive energy. These results are also understandable in view of the structure of the $CaF_2$ crystal. It should be remarked that the potential energy above the (111) surface decreases rapidly with increasing $z=1$, i.e. about two TL above the actual surface.

The number of crystal ions of these ‘adregions’ ranged from 10 to 16, depending on the size and shape of the admolecule; compare Tables 3–5. In correspondence to the calculations of the relaxation of the surface without adsorption, only ions of the first TL were included in the adregions.

For the search of the positions with the mini- mum total adsorption energy $E_{\text{ad}}$ (maximum of $|E_{\text{ad}}|$) of the respective ion cluster, i.e. of admolecule plus adregion, the coordinates of the corresponding ions were varied by stepwise decreasing amounts $\delta a$ until the variation became less than $10^{-6}$. The resulting positions and adsorption energies were taken as the equilibrium values. The total adsorption energy $E_{\text{ad}}$ was determined by summing the change of the intramolecular energy $E_{\text{mol}}^{\text{rel}}$ of the respective lithium fluoride molecule compared with its free state (see Table 1), the interaction energy of the admolecule with the crystal including the adregion $E_{\text{int}}$, and the change of the energy of the adregion $E_{\text{reg}}^{\text{rel}}$ with respect to its state without adsorption. In the case of the linear dimer, because of its instability in vapor, in $E_{\text{mol}}^{\text{rel}}$ the energy of the free rhombic dimer was taken as vacuum reference. A corresponding specification of energies was performed in Ref. [13].

To test the correctness of the results, in some cases the calculations were repeated with modified initial positions of the ions of the respective admolecule. In doing so, always the same final adsorption positions and energies were found. In particular, the resulting adsorption energies were reproducible to a total of six digits. In addition, because the final minimal configurations are in each case meaningful from a physical point of view, the calculated positions seem to reflect indeed the positions with the minimum adsorption energy.

## 5. Results for adsorption on the terrace

Energy values obtained for the equilibrium adsorption positions of the lithium fluoride monomer and the linear and rhombic dimers on the terrace are given in Table 3. As an example, in Table 4, for the lithium fluoride monomer on the terrace, starting and final equilibrium coordinates of ions of the admolecule and of the adregion are compiled.¹ Apart from symmetrically equivalent orientations, the given positions are the only stable adsorption configurations found in the present calculations.

It is seen from Figs. 3–5 that for the three admolecules the general conclusions drawn from the contour plot, namely that the $\text{Li}^+$ ions should adsorb above a $\text{F}_\text{u}$ of the upper fluorine layer and the $\text{F}^-$ above a Ca, are essentially maintained. Because of the strong Coulomb interactions within the admolecules, which lead to strong decreases of the initial intramolecular distances, the final adsorption sites are not perpendicular above these surface ions. Total restoration of the shapes of the corresponding free molecules, however, are because of the interaction with the substrate, not attained. Correspondingly, positive values of the molecular relaxation energies $E_{\text{mol}}^{\text{rel}}$ result; see Table 3. For the adsorbed monomer, the $\text{Li}^+-\text{F}^-$ distance is enlarged by $\sim 7\%$ compared with its free state; compare Tables 1 and 4. The rhombic dimer is no longer planar, which can be seen from the larger distances of the $\text{Li}^+$ ions to the surface compared with the $\text{F}^-$ ions; see Fig. 4. Also, the linear dimer is not a straight chain; see Fig. 5.

## 6. Results for adsorption at a $[10\overline{1}]$ step

At a $[10\overline{1}]$ step several adsorption positions with different adsorption energies exist. Here, only those with the highest adsorption energies were studied in greater detail. In correspondence with the discussion in Section 3, preferred adsorption sites for fluorine ions of admolecules are positions close to ions of the calcium rows at the step edge and for lithium ions those close to ions of the upper fluorine ions at the lower terrace (Ca and $\text{F}_3$ respectively in Figs. 1 and 2).

The calculated adsorption positions of the monomer, the rhombic and the linear dimer are shown in Figs. 6–8.² The corresponding adsorption energies are listed in Table 5. The respective adsorption energies of $-2.68$ eV, $-3.06$ eV, and $-2.86$ eV are considerably larger than those for an adsorption on the terrace; compare Tables 3 and 5. Correspondingly, admolecules show stronger deviations from their free states. The $\text{Li}^+-\text{F}^-$ distance in the monomer, for example, is increased by about 15% compared with 7% on the terrace. Also here, the relaxation energy of the adregion is lower for the rhombic dimer than for the monomer and the linear dimer. Again, the largest relaxations of the crystal ions are observed for the fluorine ions of the adregion close to the adsorbed lithium ion(s); see Fig. 6.

The most remarkable result of these calculations is that the theoretical adsorption energy of $-2.68$ eV of the monomer at the steps shows good correspondence to the experimental value of $-2.70\pm0.17$ eV obtained from an investigation of the adsorption behavior of lithium fluoride molecules at $\text{CaF}_2(111)$ [2,3] by the molecular beam transient method. The experimental value of the adsorption energy was derived from the temperature dependence of desorption times of the LiF monomers at low coverages. For such conditions the adsorption was found to be independent of the impinging lithium fluoride flux. It may, therefore, be attributed to an adsorption of isolated monomers, which is also the prerequisite of the present calculations.

### 7. Conclusions

The equilibrium adsorption configuration and energies of the lithium fluoride monomer and the rhombic and linear dimers on the terrace of the (111) surface of $CaF_{2}$ and at the $[10\overline{1}]$ of type I were calculated theoretically. These calculations confirm an adsorption model that was developed for the interpretation of results of an experimental study of the adsorption behavior in this system [2,3]; see Section 1. This concerns, above all, the good correspondence of theoretical and experimen- tal adsorption energies of the monomers at the steps. Also, the complete dissociation of the dimers at low coverages, which in the model was assumed to occur at the steps and not on the terrace, is, in principle, supported by the present calculations. For a more conclusive confirmation of this model, however, a complete molecular dynamics investiga- tion, e.g. see Refs. [34,35], of the dimer diffusion, dissociation, and desorption at the steps and on the terrace should be performed.

In the mentioned experimental study, with increasing coverage, a decrease of the desorption times of the monomers and with it a decrease of the absolute values of the adsorption energies from 2.70 to 2.44 eV was found. This effect was attrib- uted to a reversible adsorption in a ‘second row’ along the steps. This second row should comprise a maximum of $2\times 10^{13}$ sites cm$^{-2}$, whereas an estimation of adsorption sites in the ‘first row’ at the steps yielded a ten times lower value of $2\times 10^{12}$ cm$^{-2}$. Sites for adsorption in the second row may be supplied, on the one hand, by sites at the steps with smaller adsorption energies (see Section 6) and, on the other hand, by sites at already adsorbed lithium fluoride molecules in the first row. Here, for a more thorough understand- ing, the study of the adsorption of different lithium fluoride clusters at the steps would be advisable, e.g. as was performed by Shluger et al. [13] in a theoretical investigation of the adsorption of sodium chloride clusters on the (100) surface of MgO. Such a study, which could be supported by corresponding atomic force microscopic investiga- tions, would contribute also to a deeper under- standing of the nucleation and of the early growth stages in the present system. By the present equilib- rium positions of admolecules, which do not differ considerably from those found without relaxation, already the epitaxial growth of lithium fluoride with the $(20\overline{1})$ face and not with the (110), as was concluded in an earlier study [36], could be explained [5]. As was shown in this paper, only slight shifts of the adsorbed ions were necessary to achieve an ordering corresponding to $LiF(20\overline{1})$.

In summary, the different approaches to the problems connected with the adsorption and growth behavior of lithium fluoride on $CaF_{2}(111)$, namely the study of the structure

and topography of thermally annealed pure $CaF_2(111)$ surfaces [12], the experimental investigations of adsorption [2,3] and epitaxial growth [5], as well as the theoretical calculations of this paper lead to complementary results. This not only yields an already far-reaching understanding of this system, but it may also contribute to a more general insight into the adsorption and growth of ionic molecules/clusters crystallizing in the rock salt structure on surfaces of ionic crystals with the fluorite structure.
