## A B S T R A C T

The surface energies and work functions of Mg, Ca, and MgCa surfaces are derived by means of first principles calculation, and it is found that the Ca-terminated B2 MgCa surfaces have much lower surface energies than corresponding Mg-terminated surfaces. Moreover, calculations reveal that the adsorption energy of Cl atom on Ca (111) surface is much lower than that on Mg (0001) surface due to a stronger Ca–Cl bond than Mg–Cl, and that for MgCa (110) surface, various possible adsorption of Cl atoms are investigated and the most energetically preferred site is found. In addition, the magnitude of adsorption energies suggest that the corrosion resistance of MgCa (110) surface against Cl atom would be located between those of Mg (0001) and Ca (111) surfaces. The relative stability of various adsorption sites is discussed by means of electronic structures, and the present calculated results are in good agreement with experimental results in the literature.

### 1. Introduction

Mg alloys have been regarded as superior biodegradable implant materials during the past decades, promising to replace the traditional implant candidates such as stainless steel, titanium alloys, etc. [1,2]. It is well known that both Mg and Ca are essential elements in human body, and therefore show good biocompatibility and no toxicity with human tissues [2–6]. This could be considered as a unique quality of MgCa alloys over other Mg alloys such as Mg–Al and Mg–RE (refractory metal) alloys, as all elements except Mg and Ca are trace elements in human body and serious problems could happen in case their concentrations go beyond a certain level [3,4]. Moreover, the densities of Mg and Ca are 1.74 and 1.55 g/cm³ [1,4], respectively, which are very close to the density (1.8–2.1 g/cm³) of human bones [1] and would be helpful to the healing of human bones [4,7]. In addition, both Mg and Ca are abundant natural resources and seem much cheaper than other implant materials such as titanium and stainless steel [8–10].

Mechanical property is well believed as one of the major properties to be controlled for biodegradable implant materials such as MgCa [4,5,11]. An ideal implant should have mechanical properties very close to human bones, which could benefit the growth of new bone tissues and therefore minimize the so-called stress shielding effects [12] that usually appeared in current metallic implants due to the mismatch of their mechanical properties with natural bone [1,6]. Fortunately, our recent study revealed that the MgCa alloys with a Mg composition of less than 50 at.% would be good candidates as degradable bone materials in terms of Young's modulus and ductility [13].

Another critical property of MgCa as biodegradable implant materials is corrosion resistance, which requires that the implant corrodes at physiological environments with such an appropriate speed that the mechanical integrity of the implant will not be degraded until the tissue has sufficiently healed [1,11]. In this respect, there are several experimental studies regarding corrosion behaviors of MgCa in the literature [5,14–18], while these results are mainly focused on the alloy compositions with only a very small percentage of Ca (0.4–5 wt.%) and there is no any theoretical study about the corrosion resistance of MgCa alloys so far in the literature.

As a first step to explore the corrosion resistance of MgCa alloys theoretically, the present study is aimed, by means of first principles calculation, to investigate the surface properties of Mg, Ca, and MgCa phases, as well as the chlorine (Cl) adsorption on Mg, Ca, and MgCa surfaces. The atom of Cl is purposely selected as it is one of the major elements for aggressive corrosion in human body, and is believed to play an important role in the corrosion process of Mg alloys [1,17–19]. Specifically, the MgCa phase with the B2 structure and equal compositions of Mg and Ca is chosen as a typical example for the surface study of MgCa, since its mechanical properties are very close to those of human bones revealed in our recent study [13].

### 2. Computational methods

The first-principles calculation based on density functional theory (DFT) is performed by using Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [20], and the calculation is conducted in a plane-wave basis, using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [21]. The exchange and correlation items are described by generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew et al. For $k$ space integration, the temperature smearing method of Methfessel and Paxton [23] is used for relaxation calculation and the modified tetrahedron method of Blöchl-Jepsen-Andersen [24] is performed for static calculation.

The present calculations of clean surfaces are focused on low-index orientations, i.e., (100), (110), and (111) planes of FCC Ca, (0001) surface of HCP Mg, as well as (100), (110), and (111) surfaces of MgCa with the B2 structure. The optimized bulk lattice constants (i.e., Ca: 5.52 Å; MgCa: 3.96 Å; Mg: $a=3.19$ Å, and $c/a=1.632$) from our previous study [13] are used in the present surface calculations. Accordingly, a $1\times2$ surface unit cell is selected for (100) and (110) surfaces of Ca as well as (110) surfaces of MgCa, and a $2\times2$ surface unit cell is chosen for (0001) surface of Mg, (111) surfaces of Ca, as well as (100) and (111) surfaces of MgCa. It should be noted that in (100) and (111) MgCa surfaces, the Mg and Ca atoms are completely separated as layers alternating with each other, and the surface terminated with Ca or Mg atoms is named type I or II, respectively.

At the early stage of this study, we did a series of test calculations, such as the $k$-point convergence test, the number of surface layers test, and the number of vacuum layers test. As a result, the Gamma centered $k$-grid is adopted for all the calculations. For Ca (100), Ca (110), and MgCa (110) surfaces, the $k$-meshes of $13\times5\times1$, $9\times5\times1$ and $13\times5\times1$ are used in relaxation calculations, respectively, while $15\times7\times1$, $11\times7\times1$ and $15\times7\times1$ are used in static calculations, respectively. As to the surfaces of Mg (0001), Ca (111), as well as MgCa (100) and (111), the $k$-meshes of $5\times5\times1$ and $7\times7\times1$ are selected for relaxation and static calculations, respectively. After the layer test, the numbers of surface and vacuum layers are set as follows: $7+11$ for Ca (100) and MgCa (110), $5+13$ for Ca (110), $5+9$ for Ca (111), as well as $7+13$ for Mg (0001), MgCa (100) and MgCa (111).

For the adsorption of chlorine atoms, the present calculations are concentrated on the (0001) surface of Mg, the (111) surface of Ca, and the (110) surface of MgCa due to their most closed-packed features. In each calculation, the atoms of the central layer are kept fixed and other atoms are allowed for relaxation. The two Cl atoms are put at both sides of the slab in a symmetric way, and Fig. 1 shows the schematic illustrations of various adsorption positions of Cl, i.e., the top, bridge, fcc, and hcp sites for HCP (0001) or FCC (111) surface, as well as the long, short, bridge, top Mg, and top Ca sites for B2 (110) surface. It should be pointed out that after the relaxation the Cl atom at the bridge site of MgCa (110) surface will be moved to the site of 'long' shown in Fig. 1.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Clean surfaces of Mg, Ca, and MgCa

We first derive the surface energies and work functions of Mg, Ca, and MgCa surfaces. Fundamentally, the surface energy is the energy needed to split an infinite bulk into two semi-infinite crystals, and the work function is the minimum energy to remove an electron from the surface. In the present study, the surface energy ($\sigma$) and work function ($\Phi$) are calculated by means of the following formulas:

$$
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{tot}^{slab}-NE_{tot}^{bulk}\right),\tag{1}
$$

$$
\Phi=V_{vac}-E_{f},\tag{2}
$$

where $E_{tot}^{slab}$ is the total energy of a slab with N atoms, $E_{tot}^{bulk}$ is the total energy per atom of the bulk, $V_{vac}$ is the electrostatic potential in the vacuum region, $E_{f}$ is the Fermi energy of the slab system, and the factor 1/2 in Eq. (1) is due to two equivalent surfaces in the slab.

We now investigate the expansion of the Mg (0001) surface, which seems unusual and different from the contractions of most metal sur- faces [31–34]. In the present study, the change of the interlayer spacing is presented by:

$$
\Delta d_{ij}=\left(d_{ij}-d_{0}\right)/d_{0}, \tag{3}
$$

where $d_{0}$ is the distance between two layers of the bulk and $d_{ij}$ is the dis- tance between two surface layers of $i$ and $j$ after relaxation. Accordingly, the derived $\Delta d_{12}$ value of Mg (0001) surface from the present calcula- tion is 1.933%, which agrees well with the corresponding experimental value of $1.9 \pm 0.3\%$ in the literature [32].

For the B2 MgCa phase, it can be noticed from Table 1 that the surface energies of the Ca-terminated (100)-I and (111)-I surfaces are 0.388 and $0.498\ \text{J/m}^2$, respectively, which are much lower than the corresponding values of 0.906 and $0.796\ \text{J/m}^2$ for the Mg-terminated (100)-II and (111)-II surfaces, respectively, suggesting that the Ca-terminated sur- face should be more stable in real situations. Such a big difference of sur- face energy between Mg- and Ca-terminated MgCa surfaces is due to the facts that pure Mg surface has higher surface energy than pure Ca surface, and that Mg atoms have much more strains than Ca atoms in MgCa surfaces. One could also see clearly from Table 1 that the Ca-terminated (100)-I and (111)-I surfaces have smaller work functions than the Mg-terminated (100)-II and (111)-II surfaces, respectively. In addition, it is of interest to see that the surface energy and work function of the MgCa (110) surface are just located between those of the Ca- and Mg-terminated (100) and (111) surfaces, respectively. To our knowl- edge, there is no study of MgCa surfaces so far in the literature, and we hope that the present calculation could stimulate some experimen- tal researches regarding MgCa surfaces in the future.

### 3.2. Chlorine adsorption on Mg and Ca surfaces

To find out the stability of various adsorption sites, the adsorption energy ($E_{\text{ads}}$) of Cl atom is derived according to the following definition:

$$
E_{\text{ads}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{\text{Cl}/sur}-E_{sur}-2E_{\text{Cl}}\right), \tag{4}
$$

where $E_{\text{Cl}/sur}$ and $E_{sur}$ are total energies of the slab with and without Cl adsorption, respectively, $E_{\text{Cl}}$ is total energy per atom in the $\text{Cl}_{2}$ mole- cule, and the factor 1/2 in Eq. (4) is due to two equivalent adsorption positions of each site in the slab. After a series of calculations, the ad- sorption energy of Cl atom is calculated for the top, bridge, fcc, and hcp sites of Mg (0001) and Ca (111) surfaces, respectively, and the re- sults are listed in Table 2. In addition, the work functions of the sur- faces after the Cl adsorption are also derived and summarized in Table 2. It should be pointed out that the bond length of Cl–Cl bond in the $\text{Cl}_{2}$ molecule is $2.002\ \text{\AA}$ from the present calculation, which matches well with experimental value of $1.989\ \text{\AA}$ in the literature [35].

For Cl adsorption on Mg (0001) and Ca (111) surfaces, several characteristics could be deduced from Table 2. First, the top and bridge sites of Ca (111) as well as the bridge site of Mg (0001) seem unstable, as the Cl atom will relax to the corresponding hcp site shown in Fig. 1. Second, all the listed adsorption energies are negative values, indicating that the Cl adsorption on various sites of Mg (0001) and Ca (111) surfaces should be energetically favorable.

Third, for Mg (0001) surface, the hcp and fcc sites should be the energetically preferred adsorption positions of Cl atoms due to their lower adsorption energies than the top site. Forth, the adsorption energies of Cl atoms on hcp and fcc sites of Ca (111) are −5.326 and −5.299 eV, respectively, which are much lower than the corresponding values of −4.147 and −4.156 eV for hcp and fcc sites of Mg (0001), respectively. Such a difference of adsorption energy suggests that the corrosion of Ca by Cl atoms should be much easier than that of Mg, which is in good agreement with experimental observations that the chemical potential (−2.87 eV) of Ca is lower than that (−2.37 eV) of Mg [35].

It is of importance to have a deep understanding of the Cl adsorption on Mg (0001) and Ca (111) surfaces at an electronic scale. Accordingly, Fig. 3 shows the comparison of total density of states (DOSs) of Mg atom in clean Mg (0001) surface and Mg (0001) surface after Cl adsorption at the fcc site. It can be observed from this figure that compared with clean Mg (0001) surface, several high DOS peaks appear at the positions around −5.3 eV below $E_f$ in Mg (0001) surface after Cl adsorption at the fcc site. Such DOS peaks imply that a strong chemical bonding should be formed at about −5.3 eV below $E_f$ in the fcc site of Mg (0001) due to the Cl adsorption, which could give a reasonable explanation to the high value (−4.156 eV) of adsorption energy shown in Table 2.

One sees clearly from Fig. 4 that the DOS peak of the hcp site has higher binding energy (5.2 eV below $E_f$) than the corresponding value (2.8 eV below $E_f$) of the top site, and that the height of DOS peak of the hcp site is also much bigger. To understand the difference of adsorption energy between Mg and Ca, the DOSs of the Cl atom are derived after adsorption at the hcp sites of Mg (0001) and Ca (111) surfaces, respectively, and the two curves are summarized in Fig. 5. It could be detected from this figure that the Cl atom in Ca (111) surface has much higher DOS peak and bigger bind energy than those in Mg (0001), implying that the Ca–Cl bond should be much stronger than the Mg–Cl bond, which would therefore bring about a deep understanding of the bigger adsorption energy of Cl on Ca (111) surface as related before.

As shown in Table 2, it should be pointed out that the adsorption energy of the top Mg site $(-4.237$ eV) is a little bit lower than that $(-4.218$ eV) of the top Ca site. Such an observation seems contradictory to our previous statement that the $Ca-Cl$ bond should be much stronger than the $Mg-Cl$ bond, and it is of interest to find out the fundamental reason of this unusual observation. To begin with, the bond features of the $Mg-Cl$ and $Ca-Cl$ dimer systems are derived and shown in Table 3 as well as in Fig. 6. It could be seen from Table 3 that the calculated bond energies of $Mg-Cl$ and $Ca-Cl$ dimmers from the present study are 308.1 and 429.1 kJ/mol, respectively, which are in good agreement with corresponding experimental values of 327.6 and 409 kJ/mol [35], respectively. One can also observe from Fig. 6 that the bond energy of $Ca-Cl$ is lower than that of $Mg-Cl$ within a wide range of bond length. These observations from Table 3 and Fig. 6 imply that the $Ca-Cl$ bond should be generally stronger than the $Mg-Cl$ bond, which agrees well with the lower adsorption energy of Cl atoms on Ca (111) surface as well as the stronger chemical bonding of $Ca-Cl$ related before.

To draw a clear comparison between top Mg and Ca sites, Table 4 lists the number of bond and bond length of $Mg-Cl$ and $Ca-Cl$ bonds after Cl adsorption at the top Mg and Ca sites of MgCa (110) surface, respectively. In addition, each bond length shown in Table 4 is used to find out, in Fig. 6, the corresponding bond energy, the absolute value of which is regarded as the bond strength $(E_{b}^{i})$ of each $Mg-Cl$ and $Ca-Cl$ bond. The total strength $(E_{t})$ of top Mg or Ca site is calculated as a sum of all the bond strength:

$$
E_{t}=\sum_{i}\left(n_{i} \cdot E_{b}^{i}\right), \quad (5)
$$

where $n_{i}$ is the number of bond and $E_{b}^{i}$ is the bond strength of each bond. Accordingly, the derived bond strength and total strength are also shown in Table 4. One sees clearly from Table 4 that the total strength of the top Mg site is a little bit bigger than that of the top Ca site, and this difference of total strength is mainly due to the contribution of more $Ca-Cl$ bonds in the top Mg site. Such an observation could bring about a deep understanding to the slightly lower adsorption energy of the top Mg site than that of the top Ca site as related before, and also confirms that the $Ca-Cl$ bond should play a more important role in stability of Cl adsorption than the $Mg-Cl$ bond. It should be pointed out that only the bonds up to the third-nearest neighbors of Cl atom are considered in the present study, and that the above calculated strength and total strength are just estimated values to the real situation, while such approximate strength is relevant to reflect the intrinsic feature of Cl adsorption on MgCa (110) surface.

It is of interest to compare the electronic structures of various adsorption sites of MgCa (110) surface. As a typical example, Fig. 7 shows total DOSs of Ca atom in MgCa (110) surface after Cl adsorption at the top Ca and long sites, respectively. ## 4. Concluding remarks

In the present study, first principles calculation has been conducted to investigate the surface energy and work function of Mg, Ca, and MgCa surfaces, as well as the Cl adsorption on Mg (0001), Ca (111), and MgCa (110) surfaces. It is found that the de- rived surface properties are in good agreement with experimental re- sults in the literature, and that the electronic structures from the present calculation would give a deeper understanding of various surface properties of Mg, Ca, as well as MgCa. It is also revealed that the adsorption energy of Cl atoms on MgCa surface is lower and higher than those on Mg and Ca surfaces, respectively, suggesting that the descending sequence of corrosion resistance against Cl atoms is as follows: $Mg \to MgCa \to Ca$.
