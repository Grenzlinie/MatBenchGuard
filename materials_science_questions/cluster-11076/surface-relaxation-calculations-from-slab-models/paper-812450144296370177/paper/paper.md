# Periodic ab initio study of the hydrogenated rutile TiO₂(110) surface

J. Leconte $^{a}$, A. Markovits $^{a}$, M.K. Skalli $^{a,b}$, C. Minot $^{a,*}$, A. Belmajdoub $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Laboratoire de Chimie théorique, Universite P. et M. Curie, Boite 53, 7616 CNRS, Bat. F 642 4, place Jussieu, 75252 Paris Cédex 5, France
$^{b}$ Département de Chimie, Université Sidi Mohamed Ben Abdellah, F.S.T.Fès Saïss, Fès, Morocco
$^{c}$ Département de Chimie, Faculté des Sciences Dhar El Mehraz, Université Sidi Mohamed Ben Abdellah, Fès, Morocco

Received 28 May 2001; accepted for publication 25 July 2001

## Abstract

We have performed periodic ab initio calculations to study the adsorption of hydrogen atoms on TiO₂(110) rutile surface. Two adsorption modes have been considered on slabs. Even if the presence of atomic hydrogen on the surface does not arise from the H₂ dissociation, we will refer to the H-H cleavage for the analysis. Hence, in the H mode, the heterolytic cleavage of H₂ yields to H⁺ and H⁻. The proton interacts with the most basic oxygen atoms of the surface, while H⁻ adsorbs on a metal cation which is the Lewis acidic site. In the R mode, two H⁺ fix on top of the outer surface anions and two Ti(IV) are reduced to Ti(III). Our calculated adsorption energies values show that the R mode is the most favorable. The surface reduction is only apparent through the spin polarization and does not lead to a full electron charge transfer from the hydrogen atoms to the surface. The change of the electronic state and of the adsorption strength with the slab thickness is presented. Using NH₃ as a probe molecule to test the surface acidity, we show that the Ti reduction modifies its reactivity. Surface relaxation significantly contributes to increase the adsorption strengths; however it does not modify the qualitative conclusions. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Density functional calculations; Hydrogen atom; Chemisorption; Titanium oxide

## 1. Introduction

Studies on titanium oxide have been motivated since it is a very important material for a long range of applications [1], such as coating of metals, adhesion, microelectronic devices and photovolt- taic cells. The interaction of metal atoms with supports such as TiO₂ leads to a very efficient catalyst; this is known as strong metal support interaction (SISI) [2].

In the case of heterogeneous catalysis, the solid surfaces of rutile or anatase are well known [3,4]. Among them, the (110) surface of the rutile structure is the most studied since this surface is at the same time stable and reactive [5]. On the clean surfaces, the molecules, when they adsorb without dissociation, generally bind as bases to Ti⁴⁺; the adsorbates that bind to O²⁻ are a minority: metal atoms or metal clusters. facilitates the cleavage of the weakest bonds in the molecule, the repartition of the fragments at the surface sites corresponds to a heterolytic cleavage. The basic fragments bind to $Ti^{4+}$ while the acidic fragments are adsorbed on $O^{2-}$. Thus, water [6,7] produces protons which adsorb on the $O^{2-}$ surface atoms and $OH^{-}$ which adsorb on the $Ti^{4+}$ ions and the surface become covered by hydroxyl groups. Note that without oxidation or reduction, the hydroxylation process results from an hydration and not from the hydrogenation of surface oxygen atoms.

$H_2$ being a covalent homonuclear molecule, the heterolytic cleavage is not very favorable. When the oxide is not reducible, proton-type hydrogen, $H^{+}/O^{2-}$, equilibrates hydride-type hydrogen, $H^{-}/$ $Ti^{4+}$ [8]; the orientation resulting from an heterolytic cleavage with $H^{-}$ adsorbed on the metal and $H^{+}$ on the oxygen atom is obtained on MgO [9] and ZnO [10] even if surfaces are not reactive toward $H_2$ dissociation. Experimentally, Fourier Transformed IR spectroscopy shows the vibrations of M-H bonds [11].

In the case of reducible oxides, there is a better possibility with all the hydrogen on the sites of the same kind. Since the O-H bond is stronger than the M-H bond, hydrogen atoms are more likely to be adsorbed as protons. The excess of electrons should be transferred on the oxide, reducing the $Ti^{4+}$ to $Ti^{3+}$. The opposite choice, hydrogenating only the surface metal cations, has been found much less favorable [12]; indeed, in addition of the weakness of the M-H bond, the formation of $H^{-}$ would mean that the substrate should be oxidized which implies, preserving the stoichiometry, a conversion of $O^{2-}$ to $O^{-}$ that is unlikely. The adsorption of H on the oxygen atoms with no compensation with adsorption on the metal atoms have been found on $V_2O_5$ [13-15].

This adsorption mode is very similar to the adsorption of alkali metals. The adsorption of alkali metals similarly takes place with a charge transfer inducing a reduction of a metal atom. It occurs selectively on the penta-coordinated Ti centers [16,20]. For $Na/TiO_2(110)$, the adsorption site is above the protruded ridges [16,17] or capping three oxygen atoms (two from the ridge and one basal oxygen) [18-20]; the electron is transferred to an hexa-coordinated titanium atom from the sublayer that are below the penta-coordinated ones at the surface [20-22]. For $K/TiO_2(110)$ these two sites are found at different coverage [23,24]. The formation of reduced $Ti^{3+}$ ions is associated more with the spin localization than with a real change in the net atomic charge [23]. Transition metals adsorb similarly. The reduction is clearly seen from the spin localization. However, in this case, it is not accompanied by a large charge transfer.

Dihydrogen does not dissociate [1,25] on $TiO_2$ except on defect sites [26]. H may come from other sources; $H_2$ may dissociate on a metal and then migrate to the oxide surface (spillover from the metal crystallites to the reducible support such as $TiO_2$ or $CeO_2$ [5]). Hydrogenation is enhanced by the presence of $TiO_2$ as support [27]. Dihydrogen is also formed by the decomposition of carboxylic acids [28,29] (CO or $CO_2 + H_2$). Whereas protons are adsorbed forming surface hydroxyl groups, it is not clear that the hydrogen atoms ($H^{-}$-like) arising from these decompositions are adsorbed during the process [30-33].

Our goal is not to study the adsorption-dissociation of $H_2$ on $TiO_2$. We want to find the most stable structure of the hydrogenated $TiO_2(110)$ surface, whatever the source of hydrogen should be.

This paper shows that the H adsorption on $TiO_2$, and more generally on reducible oxides, resembles to that of alkali metals and proceeds through a reductive process. All the H atoms bind to surface oxygen generating hydroxyl groups.

## 2. The computational features

### 2.1. The method of calculations

We have carried out ab initio periodic calculations with the **CRYSTAL 98** [34] code and the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package code (VASP 4.4.4) [35-37]. In the VASP program, the KohnSham equations are solved with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) proposed by Perdew and Wang [38,39]. Ultrasoft pseudopotentials have been used [40,41] together with plane wave basis sets. The integrations in the Brillouin Zone

are performed on a grid of $5 \times 5 \times 1$ for the geometry optimizations. In order to obtain accurate DOS and energies, we have repeated the calculation of the optimized with a grid of $16 \times 16 \times 1$ Monkhorst-Pack special k-points. Then, we have checked that the energy difference was less than 1 meV. We have also used the CRYSTAL 98 program with the UHF and DFT Hamiltonians choosing the B3LYP functional which combines the Becke three-parameter exchange [42]. In CRYSTAL 98, the local part of the correlation follows the work by Vosko et al. [43]. However, one should notice that in CRYSTAL 98, the parameters used are those fitted by Vosko et al. for the essentially exact Ceperly-Alder calculations (Eq. V in Ref. [43]) while in other codes, like GAUSSIAN 98 [44], the parameters are those fitted to the random phase approximation (RPA) expression, (Eq. III in Ref. [43]). Unfortunately, these two possible choices of the LDA correlation functional lead to two different B3LYP functionals whose results cannot be directly compared. The B3LYP method correctly describes the adsorption on metal oxides [45]. The comparison between DFT and HF results will allow us to evaluate the role of the correlation. We have used for all the atoms effective core pseudopotential, those from Durand and Barthelat. For the oxygen and nitrogen atoms, the basis sets are the PS-31G [46]. For the titanium atom, the basis set consists of d functions contracted to the (4/1) basis set and a single sp shell with an exponent of 0.484 for polarization purposes [47]. Bulk $TiO_2$ calculations for rutile [47] and anatase [48], the adsorption of $H_2O$ [6] on $TiO_2(110)$, of CO [49], of $CO_2$ [50], $NH_3$ [51] and HOOC-COOH [52] have already been calculated with the same basis sets.

### 2.2. The $TiO_2(110)$ surface

In the CRYSTAL program, we define an unit cell which is periodically repeated in two dimensions. A slab of different thickness, made of a finite number of atomic layers, represents the solid. In the VASP program, the calculations are performed in three dimensions. We have defined a cell vector perpendicular to the surface plane of $15\ \text{\AA}$. This is enough to leave an empty space between successive slabs larger than $6.2\ \text{\AA}$ (in the case of the nine layer (9 L) slab, the thickest one).

### 3. The adsorption modes

TiO₂ is an amphoteric and reducible metal oxide. The surface metallic cations are Lewis acidic sites and the surface oxygen atoms are the Lewis basic sites. This is ideal for adsorption of charged fragments issued from an heterolytic cleavage. This adsorption mode is the only possibility for the irreducible oxides such as MgO. The reduction Ti(IV) to Ti(III) for the ions overcompensates the loss of electron of an hydrogen atom (43.2 vs. 13.6 eV). The TiO₂(1 1 0) surface (see Fig. 1) is the most stable rutile surface; it is built of alternating polymers [6]. There is an electrodonation from the horizontal polymer to the vertical polymer: the surface Ti (from the horizontal polymer) become more acidic and the outer O (from the vertical polymer) more basic. Thus, although stable, this surface is specially reactive.

Considering an heterolytic cleavage of H₂, H⁻ adsorbs on Ti and the proton reacts with the basic O. For a coverage $\theta = 1$ with one H per outer surface O, this adsorption mode leads to two possible arrangements sketched on Fig. 2. In this paper, we will refer to these two arrangements as H1 and H2; these two arrangements are in singlet states. A third possibility is to adsorb the two hydrogen atoms on two surface O, we will refer to this possibility as the R arrangement; the double

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Adsorption energies

The adsorption energies are referred to two H atoms according to the expression:

$$E_{\text{ads}}=2E(\text{H})+E(\text{TiO}_2)-E(2\text{H}/\text{TiO}_2)$$

where $E(\text{H})$ is the energy of the hydrogen atom, $E(\text{TiO}_2)$ is the energy of the bare $TiO_2$ slab with the three top layers relaxed along the $z$ direction perpendicular to the surface and $E(2\text{H}/\text{TiO}_2)$ is the energy of the optimized cell containing two hydrogen atoms. We have refereed to the unit cells with two hydrogen for all the adsorption modes in order to have comparable adsorption energies. The reference is the same so that the $E_{\text{ads}}$ reflects the stability of the different H distributions. In the case of the adsorption of $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{H}^-$, $E_{\text{ads}}$ includes the energy of the transfer of one electron from one hydrogen to the other: $E_{\text{ads}}=E_{\text{ads}}(\text{H}^+)E_{\text{ads}}(\text{H}^-)+E(2\text{H}\rightarrow\text{H}^++\text{H}^-)$. The coverage is $\theta=1$: in the unit cell, there is one hydrogen atom per bridging oxygen atom from the top layer. The results are shown in Table 2. As shown in Fig. 2, the primitive cell for the H1 and H2 models is twice that for the R model since it corresponds to a half coverage for the protonation of the bridging oxygen atoms. As mentioned above, we have considered a double cell for the R mode in order to compare with the H modes. The most favorable adsorption mode is clearly the model R where the adsorption is followed by a reduction of one titanium atom per adsorbed hydrogen. In addition to the binding of the adsorbate, there is a substitution of Ti(IV) for Ti(III) that may concern another atom than the surface atom with low coordination. At variance with the clean surface, relaxation is less important and is chiefly limited to the O atom that is protonated. For the three slabs (3, 6 and 9 L) the increase of the adsorption energy due to this single atom relaxation represents 0.12 – 0.26 eV. These values are weak and thus the trends that one would draw ignoring the relaxation would remain valid.

The UHF calculations with crystal 98 give smaller adsorption energies than the DFT calculations using the B3LYP functional. This is probably due to the role of the correlation that is not included in Hartree–Fock (HF) calculations; both results, however, provide the same trends as those obtained with the VASP program (see Table 5). The best adsorption mode is the R arrangement and the adsorption energies of the two other models are weaker and nearly identical. We will see also in the next section the similarities between DFT and HF for the spin localization. Finally, for the 6 L slab, the VASP (PW91) adsorption energy is higher than the crystal (B3LYP). The same tendency [57,58] has already been noticed.

### 4.2. The electronic structure: Ti reduction and spin localization

This section is devoted to the electronic structure of the R model, which is the energetically most favorable adsorption mode. Formally, we consider an electron transfer; the charges do not express this transfer since the binding (donation from an oxygen electron pair to a proton) partially compensates it, restoring some electronic density on H. The analysis of the spin population on the contrary reveals a formal transfer of an electron onto the titanium atoms. Such analysis helps us to better understand the adsorption mode, the trends of the adsorption energies according to the method used, HF or B3LYP and according the slab thickness.

For the 3 L slab (Table 6), the charge transfer from the hydrogen to the slab is rather weak; the electron density on H remains above 0.6 e. However, there is no spin left on H. The electron density of the titanium atom on which the spin is located does not vary by much under hydrogenation contrary to the spin density. UHF and B3LYP give the same trends even if the spin is more delocalized with B3LYP. For the VASP results, we have drawn the projected density of states (DOS) onto the different titanium atoms (Fig. 3). We have chosen the range of energy near the Fermi level which corresponds to the unpaired electrons. Hence, the projected DOS give an idea of the spin localization. The Fermi level has been taken as the origin of the energies. The spin is located on the titanium atoms at the surface: on the unsaturated titanium atom or on the hexa-coordinated one. For thick slabs, the bulk-like titanium atoms of the central layer are not spin polarized. The localization on the hydrogenated surface is larger than on the clean one (the opposite face of the slab).

Several factors seems to compete for the localization of the spin: the proximity to the OH group, the coordination, the amplitudes for the bottom of the conduction band.

The proximity of the OH group is in favor of the hexa-coordinated titanium atom, a substitution $Ti^{4+} \rightarrow Ti^{3+}$ balancing the substitution $O^{2-} \rightarrow OH^{-}$. If one would decompose the rutile structure into polymers by cleaving all the apical Ti–O bonds, he would localize the $Ti^{4+}$ atoms on the polymer that is hydrogenated, i.e. on the atoms that are hexa-coordinated in the slab. The localization on the unsaturated titanium center would imply an electron transfer between the polymers at the surface, from the vertical polymer to the horizontal one. Let us note, however, that considering the charged layers, the localization of the charge on one titanium atom or the other is a internal shift of the density within the same plane (the second layer that contains both the hexa-coordinated Ti and the unsaturated one).

The coordination is in favor of the reduction of the unsaturated atom. According to our calculations, the localization of the spin for $Cl_{3}Ti(\mu ^{2}Cl)_{3}TiCl$ with $C_{3v}$ symmetry is on the tetracoordinated atom. We have added an extra electron to the clean slabs in the VASP calculations. For the 3 L and 6 L slabs, the spin is on the unsaturated surface Ti(2,tetra or penta) (shared on equivalent sites on opposite faces for the 6 L slab). For the 9 L slab, the spin is more delocalized; it is also predominantly on the surface (74%) and lies again on the unsaturated Ti(2,penta). The spin on the hexa-coordinated atom at the surface, Ti(2,hexa), is comparatively negligible, 7% of that on

The spin localizes on the most reactive acidic sites of the unreduced oxide; these sites are the less coordinated one so the two criteria are similar: the conjugation with the oxygen atoms introduces some antibonding character and shift the titanium energy level upwards. The largest amplitude for the bottom of the conduction band lies on the surface atom that has the lowest coordination. For the 3 L slab, the unsaturated surface Ti is tetra-coordinated and coordination is the dominant factor. VASP results show that all the spin is

For the 6 L slab, the unsaturated surface Ti is penta-coordinated and coordination is no more the predominant factor. The spin is now delocalized on the two faces (63% on the hydrogenated surface, 37% on the opposite one with a larger component on the Ti(2,penta)). DFT results, CRYSTAL (B3LYP) and VASP (PW91) all find a large spin delocalization (Fig. 3B and Table 6). On the contrary, the (CRYSTAL) UHF results conclude that the spin is exclusively on the Ti(2,hexa) atom. A recent work by Pacchioni et al. [59] concerning the hole localization in quartz shows that DFT artificially spreads up the spin. It is noticeable that in Table 6, the sum of the spin computed exceeds one, the number of unpaired per cell. This might be due to the partition of the spin population of the Mulliken population analysis. The number of the unpaired electron is imposed in the cell. There might be a repulsion between the electrons $\alpha$ (spin up) and consequently an excess of electrons $\beta$ (spin down) on the oxygen atoms due to partition of the population analysis. Indeed, the Mulliken population analysis shows an excess of electrons $\beta$ on all the oxygen atoms which compensates the excess of electrons $\alpha$ on the titanium atoms. Finally, for the 9 L slab, the spin is delocalized onto the titanium surface atoms, (the contribution of the bulk-like atoms is small); the contribution on the hydrogenated surface is larger than on the bare surface that is opposite; the spin population on Ti(5,penta) and Ti(2,hexa) atoms is about the same (Fig. 3C).

### 4.3. Reactivity at the reduced titanium

Knowing that the site of reduction varies with the thickness of the slab, we raise in this section the question of the consequences for the catalytic activity of TiO₂. The main feature of the reactivity of anhydrous metal oxides is the acidity of the Lewis acid sites at the surface. We consider ammonia adsorption (Fig. 4) to evaluate the variation of $E_{\text{ads}}(\text{NH}_3)$ with a reduction of the adsorption site; this leads us to study the co-adsorption of NH₃ with H. We have chosen a coverage of one for both NH₃ and atomic H: one H per outer O and one NH₃ per unsaturated surface Ti. This corresponds to the saturation of the surface; however, in the case of H adsorbate, the H–NH₃ adsorbate–adsorbate repulsion should remain weak. We have carried out the VASP PW91 and CRYSTAL HF calculations on the hydrogenated 3 L slab, where the Ti(2,tetra) interacting with NH₃ is predominantly reduced, and on the 6 L slab where the reduction at this site is much less exclusive. It is not easy to distinguish the effect of the spin polarization from the effect of the slab thickness. A thick slab is more stable and less reactive than a thin one and hence it should provide a lower adsorption energy. This effect can mask that of the spin. In order to have references with the same thickness, we have also computed the adsorption energies of NH₃ on TiO₂ without coadsorption with hydrogen atoms. Thus, the differences appear due to the coordination effects. The results are shown in Table 7. Our first comment on the results is that the $E_{\text{ads}}$ decrease due to hydrogenation is more pronounced in the case of the 3 L slab than in the case of the 6 L one. For the 3 L slab, $E_{\text{ads}}$ is reduced by a factor 8 whereas the reducing factor is 3.5 for the 6 L slab (VASP PW91) and by a factor 1.5 for the 6 L slab (CRYSTAL HF). This phenomenon should be attributed to modification of the oxidation state of the surface titanium atom. The reduction of Ti induces a decrease in its reactivity. ## 5. Conclusions

We have presented the adsorption modes for atomic hydrogen on the TiO₂(1 1 0) rutile surface using periodic HF and DFT calculations. The mode that is obtained on irreducible metal oxide surfaces is not the best. Formally, in this mode, H₂ would undergo an heterolytic cleavage and the resulting fragments would adsorb differently, the proton onto the outer oxygen and the hydride-type ion onto the surface metal atoms. In the most favorable mode, all the hydrogen atoms are adsorbed as protons onto outer oxygen atoms; this is possible since in parallel some Ti(IV) atoms are reduced to Ti(III). This adsorption mode is similar to the adsorption mode of alkali atoms. This reduction does not induce a significant charge transfer; it is however characterized by the spin distribution. Finally, using NH₃ to test the acidity of the surface Ti atom, we have shown that the reduction of the oxide under hydrogenation induces modifications for its reactivity.
