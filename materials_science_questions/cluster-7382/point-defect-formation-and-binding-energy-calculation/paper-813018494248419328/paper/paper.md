# Towards understanding the influence of Re on H dissolution and retention in W by investigating the interaction between dispersed/aggregated-Re and H

Fang-Fei Ma$^{1,2}$, Wenwen Wang$^{1,2}$, Yu-Hao Li$^{1,2}$, Hong-Bo Zhou$^{1,2}$ and Guang-Hong Lu$^{1,2}$

## Abstract
We have investigated the effects of Re on the behaviors of H in W as well as their interaction with point defects using a first-principles method in combination with thermodynamic models. It is clearly found that the influence of Re on H is directly related to the distribution of Re in W. For the state of Re dispersed distribution, both a single Re and its complexes with point defects have very slight effect on H dissolution and retention in W. However, the influence of Re clusters (for the state of Re aggregation) on H is extraordinary stronger than that of a single Re. The retention of H in W can be significantly decreased by Re clusters, and their influences will be enhanced with the increasing of the number of Re atoms. This can be attributed to the electronic interaction between H and Re/W, in which the repulsion of Re–H is stronger than that of W–H. We have further examined the retention of H at different temperature in W based on the Polanyi–Wigner equation. It is found that a Re₄-vacancy complex can only accommodate 4 H atoms at room temperature, ~33% lower than the maximum number of H atoms in a mono-vacancy. These results give a reasonable interpretation for the experimentally observed D retention significantly decreasing in damaged W–Re alloy in comparison with that in damaged pure W. Consequently, our calculations provide a good reference for evaluating the influence of Re on the performance of W-PFM.

Keywords: dissolution and retention of hydrogen, rhenium–hydrogen interaction, tungsten

## 1. Introduction

Fusion energy, as an environmentally clean, efficient and infinite energy source, is a possible solution to the energy crisis in the future. The final application of nuclear fusion energy is mainly dependent on the development of key materials in the thermonuclear fusion device ‘Tokamak’, in which the choice of the plasma facing materials (PFMs) is one of the critical issues [1–3]. Due to excellent thermal properties, high sputtering threshold and low hydrogen (H) isotope retention, tungsten (W) and W alloys have been considered as one of the most promising candidates for PFMs in future fusion reactors [4]. However, as a PFM, W will be exposed to low energy high fluxes of H isotope and helium ions as well as excessive heat flux. Such extreme irradiation condition will significantly influence the properties and performance of materials. For example, H isotope ions irradiation will lead to the blister formation and surface sputtering, which severely degrade the mechanical and thermal properties of W-PFM [5]. Therefore, the behaviors of W under H isotope ions irradiation are some

of the key concerns. So far, in order to improve the perfor- mance of W under H isotope ions irradiation, the dissolution, diffusion, retention and nucleation behaviors of H in W have been intensively investigated both experimentally and compu- tationally [6-10].

Defects (such as vacancies, grain boundaries, and dislo- cations) are generally considered as the main region for H nucleation in W [9-12], and the existing of alloying elements/ impurities have significant effect on the H behaviors in W [13-15]. Rhenium (Re) is an interesting element for W mat- erials, which is both an alloying element and an impurity in W. On the one hand, Re is one of the typically alloying elements in W to increase low-temperature ductility and improve high- temperature strength and plasticity [16, 17]. On the other hand, Re is the main production of transmutation W under neutron irradiation [18]. W-PFM will be irradiated by high energy (14 MeV) neutrons in fusion reactors, leading to the transmu- tation of W and producing transmutation elements [18-21]. Recent study suggested that the content of Re can reach up to 3.80 at.% after five years of power plant condition [18]. The presence of Re will not only influence the mechanical properties and microstructure evolution of W under neutron irradiation, but also have significant effect on the retention and blistering of H isotopes in W [22-25].

It is found that effect of Re on H in W is directly related with the status of materials (damaged-free or damaged). The H behavior in normal (damaged-free) W-Re alloys is similar with that in pure W. For example, Golubeva et al [22] investi- gated the deuterium (D) retention in pure W and W-Re alloys under 200 eV D ions irradiation $(10^{22}-10^{24} D m^{-2})$ at 300 K by means of the thermodesorption spectra (TDS). It is found that although the variation of D retention is slight with the pres- ence of Re, an additional high-temperature desorption peak of D at 850 K appeared in W-Re. This indicates that the presence of Re provide a new trapping site for H in W. Further, Alimov et al [23] studied the effects of exposure temperature on D retention in pure W and W-5%Re alloy. The D retention in pure W and W-5%Re reach its maximum at 523 K and 463 K, respectively, while the total D retention in pure W is close to that in W-5%Re. Interestingly, it is found the D retention in damaged W-Re alloys is much lower than that in damaged pure W, indicating that Re plays a key role on H behavior in damaged W-Re alloys. In a comparison of D retention in pre- irradiated W-Re and W by 20 MeV W ions, Tyburska-Puschel et al [24] reported that the D retention in damaged W-Re is lower by at least $30 \%$ at exposure temperatures below 650 K than that in damaged W. Surprisingly, D concentration is reduced by two orders of magnitude in damaged W-Re alloys in comparison with damaged pure W, which is also observed by Hatano et al [25]. These may be attributed to the interac- tion between Re and radiation-induced defects. Above exper- imental studies clearly demonstrate that the presence of Re will significantly influence the behaviors of H isotopes in W, while its physical origin remains to be elucidated.

In order to explore the physical origin for the effects of Re on the behaviors of H in W, great efforts have been made to investigate the behaviors of Re as well as Re-H interac- tion in W. A single Re atom energetically prefer to occupy the substitutional site [26, 27]. Due to the strong attractive inter- action between Re and vacancy/self-interstitial atom (SIA)[26-29], both vacancy and SIA can serve as strong trapping centers for Re and facilitate the nucleation of Re clusters in W. Meanwhile, the large binding energy between Re and SIA also suggest that the presence of Re can suppress the fast one- dimension diffusion of SIA and thus contribute to the recom- bination of Frenkel pair [27, 30]. Further, some studies also investigate the interaction between Re and H in W directly. The interaction between substitutional Re and interstitial H is shown to be weak repulsive, while that between interstitial Re and interstitial H is strong attractive [14, 31]. Recently, Ren et al [15] investigated the dissolution and diffusion of H in W-Re alloys. It is found that the presence of Re slightly raise the H solution energy and decrease the H diffusion energy bar- rier in W. So far, the individual behaviors of Re in W have been well identified, while the influence of Re on H has been little studied, especially for Re clusters.

In present work, in order to explore the intrinsic mechanism for the effect of Re on H behaviors in W, we systematically investigate the interaction between dispersed/aggregated Re and H via a first-principles method in combination with ther- modynamic models. It is found that a single Re atom only has little effects on H retention in W, while Re clusters can signifi- cantly suppress the retention of H. These results give a good interpretation for the experimentally observed D retention sig- nificantly decreasing in damaged W-Re alloy compared with that in damaged pure W. Our calculations will provide a good reference for developing W materials as a PFM.

Since H is the lightest element in the periodic table, the zero-point energy (ZPE) cannot be neglected. The ZPE cor- rections are calculated by summing up the vibrational energies of the normal modes by the expression: $E_{\text{ZPE}}={^1/_2}\sum \hbar \nu_i$, where $\hbar$ and $\nu_i$ is the Plank’s constant and the normal vibra- tion frequencies. According to the Einstein approximation, the vibrational energies of the W/Re atoms are assumed to be much smaller than that of H atoms due to the different mass. Thus, only the contribution of H to the ZPE has been taken into account, namely, the W/Re atoms are fixed and the ZPE is calculated from Hessian matrix by allowing only the har- monic vibrational modes of H. The ZPE of one H atom in $\text{H}_2$ is calculated to be ~0.135 eV, which is in good agreement with previous results [13, 39]. Unless otherwise stated, the ZPE correction has been considered in the following sections.

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Interaction of a single H and Re atom in bulk W

In order to investigate the effect of Re on the H dissolution in bulk W, we examine the solution energy of H in the vicinity of Re in W. In the previous studies [40, 41], it has been demon- strated that a single Re atom energetically prefers to occupy the substitutional site instead of the interstitial site in bulk W, because the atomic radius of Re is comparable with that of W. As for a single H atom, it is favorable sitting at tetrahedral interstitial site (TIS) [13, 15]. Therefore, one Re atom is set at a substitutional site in W, while the H atom occupies a series of TIS with the different Re–H distance. The solution energy of H can be obtained by

$$
E_{\text{H}}^{\text{Sol}} = E_{127\text{W},1\text{Re,H}} - E_{127\text{W},1\text{Re}} - E^{\text{ref}}, \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{127\text{W},1\text{Re,H}}$ and $E_{127\text{W},1\text{Re}}$ is the total energy of the system containing $(N-1)$ W atoms, a substitutional Re atom with and without a TIS H atom, respectively. The third term is the reference energy for H, which is chosen to be the one- half of the energy of a $\text{H}_2$ molecule. Correspondingly, the calculated binding energy is 4.56 eV for the $\text{H}_2$ molecule, in agreement with experimental data 4.75 eV [42] and previous theoretical result 4.58 eV [43].

As shown in figure 1, we display the H solution energy as a function of the Re–H distance with and without ZPE cor- rection in 127W–1Re, and the solution energy of H at site A (corresponding to the first nearest Re–H distance) is also listed in table 1. It can be found that although the ZPE cor- rection has some effects on the H solution energy, especially for its absolute value, it almost not influences the interaction between H and Re. Obviously, the presence of Re slightly increase the H solution energy surrounding Re in W. When H occupy the site A with the Re–H distance of $1.82\ \mathring{A}$, the H solution energy is 1.10 eV (with ZPE) or 0.99 eV (without ZPE), which is 0.07 eV larger than that at TIS in bulk W. Then, the H solution energy slightly decreases with the increasing of Re–H distance and converges to that in pure W. These results indicate the weak repulsive interaction between H and Re in W, which is consistent with previous studies [14, 15].

To shed light on the effects of Re on the stability of H in W, the H solution energies have been decomposed into two contributions. One is deformation energy induced by the embedded H atom, defined as the energy release during the course of W/Re lattice relaxation after H is removed, and called the mechanical contribution (MC). The other is the electronic interaction between H and W/Re atoms, obtained by subtracting the MC from solution energy, and called the electronic contribution (EC). As listed in table 1, the EC is much larger than MC for the solution energy of H, and it takes up to 80% and 76% for the H solution energy in bulk W and 127W–1Re system (corresponding to site A in figure 1), respectively. This suggests that the strong electronic interac- tion between H and W/Re atoms is largely responsible for the poor solubility of H in pure W and 127W–1Re system. Further, it can be found that the MC of H in bulk W is 0.06 eV lower than that in 127W–1Re, while the EC difference of H in bulk W and 127W–1Re system is only the 0.02 eV. Thus,

the MC dominates the repulsive interaction between intersti- tial H and substitutional Re in W. This may be attributed to that the presence of Re weakens the interatomic interaction of W atoms surrounding Re, and thus the embedded H atom in W-Re may result in the large lattice distortion and the high MC in comparison with that in pure W.

We further investigate the deformation of supercell volume and atomic configurations induced by embedded H atom. The volume variations ($\Delta V$) of the supercell induced by a substi- tutional Re atom and an interstitial H atom can be obtained by $\Delta V_{\mathrm{Re}}=V_{(N-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{Re}}-V_{\mathrm{NW}}$ and $\Delta V_{\mathrm{H}}=V_{\mathrm{H}+\text { matrix }}-V_{\text {matrix }}$, respectively. The $V_{(N-1) \mathrm{W}+\mathrm{Re}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{NW}}$ are the total volume of bcc W with and without a substitutional Re atom, respec- tively. The $V_{\mathrm{H}+\text { matrix }}$ and $V_{\text {matrix }}$ are the total volume of the matrix system (i.e. pure W or 127W-1Re) with and without an interstitial H atom, respectively. It is found that the volume variation induced by a substitutional Re atom is $-0.83 \mathring{A}^{3}$. Negative value suggests that the occupied volume of a Re atom is slightly lower than that of a host W atom, leading to the shrink of the supercell volume. Further, the volume variation induced by an interstitial H at TIS in pure W is $3.15 \mathring{A}^{3}$, which almost equals to that for H at site A in 127W- 1Re. However, the local deformations induced by embedded H atom in pure W and 127W-1Re are significantly different. In pure W, the atomic distance between TIS H and the nearest neighbor W extends to $1.837 \mathring{A}$ as compared with $1.774 \mathring{A}$ for the case without H. As for H at site $A$ in 127W-1Re system (see figure 1), the average distance between H and the nearest neighbor Re/W atoms can reach up to $1.852 \mathring{A}$. Furthermore, the average distances between these neighboring W (or Re) atoms increase by $\sim 0.031 \mathring{A}$ due to the presence of H at the TIS center in bulk W, while that increase by $\sim 0.123 \mathring{A}$ due to the presence of H at the site $A$ in 127W-1Re. These results demonstrate that the embedded H atom in 127W-1Re will lead to the larger local deformation in comparison with that in pure W, and thus result in the larger MC for H solution energy.

Further, the solution energy of H in bcc Re has also been examined, as listed in table 1. It can be clearly found that the solution energy of H in bcc Re is 1.40 eV and 1.47 eV without and with ZPE correction, respectively, which is much larger than that in bcc W and 127W-1Re system. Such significant increase of H solution energy can also be attributed to the MC, which is ~4 times and ~3 times higher than the that in bcc W and 127W-1Re alloy, respectively. This is corresponding to the significant reduction of cohesive energy. The cohe- sive energy of $(128-m) W-m Re$ system can be obtained by $E_{\text {cohesive }}=\left[(128-m) E_{\mathrm{W}}+m E_{\mathrm{Re}}-E_{(128-m) \mathrm{W}+m \mathrm{Re}}\right] / 128$, where the $E_{\mathrm{W}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{Re}}$ is the energy of an isolated W and Re atom in vacuum with spin-polarization, respectively. The third term is the total energy of the system containing $(128-m) W$ atoms and m substitutional Re atoms. We can get the cohe- sive energy of bcc W, 127W-1Re, and bcc Re with m of 0, 1, and 128, respectively. The cohesive energy is calculated to be 8.59 eV, 8.58 eV and 7.69 eV for bcc W, 127W-1Re and bcc Re, respectively, and that of bcc W is in good agreement with previous studies [44, 45]. The H solution energies in hcp Re have also been calculated. Surprisingly, the lowest solution energy of H in hcp Re is 0.67 eV at TIS, which is 0.37 eV lower than that in bulk W. Therefore, the solubility of H in bcc W will be decreased by Re in bcc structure, and this influence becomes stronger with higher Re concentration. However, as Re concentration increases, the W-Re system will transform to other structure, including $\sigma$, $\chi$ and eventually the hcp phase, leading to the difference influences on H dissolution.

### 3.2. Interaction between H and Re-vacancy clusters

#### 3.2.1. Single H at a $Re_1$-V complex in W.
As the typical radiation-induced defect in materials, vacancy can serve as a strong trapping center for H in W. It is found that a single H atom prefers to occupy a close-to-octahedral interstitial site (OIS) surrounding a mono-vacancy in W with trapping energy of $-1.36 \mathrm{eV}$ to $-1.26 \mathrm{eV}$ [11, 46-51]. Further, the stable sites of H will shift from OIS toward TIS with the increasing of H number trapped by vacancy [11, 46, 49]. It has been dem- onstrated that a mono-vacancy in W can accommodate 12 H atoms in W [11, 46, 47, 49, 50]. It is found that vacancy can also facilitate the nucleation of Re clusters in W due to the attractive interaction of Re-vacancy (Re-V) [26]. Therefore, in order to investigate the synergistic behaviors of H and Re at vacancy in W, we calculate the trapping energy of a single H at a $Re_1$-V complex firstly. The trapping energy can be expressed as
$$
E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{trap}}=E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{1}-\mathrm{V}-\mathrm{H}_{n}}-E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{1}-\mathrm{V}-\mathrm{H}_{n-1}}-\left(E_{\mathrm{W}, \mathrm{H}(\mathrm{TIS})}-E_{\mathrm{W}}\right),
\tag{2}
$$
where $E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{1}-\mathrm{V}-\mathrm{H}_{n}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{1}-\mathrm{V}-\mathrm{H}_{n-1}}$ are the total energy of the $Re_1$-V system with n H atoms and $(n-1)$ H atoms, $E_{\mathrm{W}, \mathrm{H}(\mathrm{TIS})}$ and $E_{\mathrm{W}}$ are the total energy of pure W system with and without an TIS H atom. Negative values mean energy gain when a single H atom is trapped by $Re_1$-V complex relative to an isolated H atom at TIS.

Figure 2 shows the most stable sites for a single H atom in the vicinity of a mono-vacancy and a $Re_1$-V complex in W. For H in mono-vacancy, as shown in figure 2(a), the energy minimization finds the most stable site for H to be at a position of $1.27 \mathring{A}$ off the vacancy center close to an OIS with a trap- ping energy of $-1.301 \mathrm{eV}$, which is in consistent with previous

studies ($-1.318$eV in [11], $-1.36$eV in [47] and $-1.26$eV in [50]). This can be attributed to that the vacancy can lower the electron density in its vicinity by providing an isosurface of optimal electron density, and H energetically prefers to bind onto such isosurface surrounding the vacancy [9, 52]. Similar with Re-free vacancy, as shown in figure $2(b)$, $\text{Re}_1$-V complex can also provide an isosurface of low electron density and a single H atom in the vicinity of $\text{Re}_1$-V complex also prefers to occupy an off-vacancy-center position close to an OIS. However, due to the presence of substitutional Re, the symmetry is broken and there are two different off-vacancy-center OISs, namely, OIS-I and OIS-II, as illustrated in figure $2(b)$. Present calculations show that the trapping energy of a single H atom occupies an off-vacancy-center position close to OIS-I ($\sim1.27$ Å from the vacancy center) and OIS-II ($\sim1.33$ Å from the vacancy center) is $-1.12$eV and $-1.17$eV, respectively, which is slightly higher than that in Re-free vacancy.

The dissolution of H in a metal can be understood approximately in terms of the H solution energy in a homogeneous electron gas [53, 54]. The optimal electron density of H in homogenous electron gas is $n_0 \approx 0.018$ electron $\text{Å}^{-3}$ (e $\text{Å}^{-3}$) corresponding to the minimum H solution energy. Since the electron density everywhere in the W is much higher than $n_0$, H generally has the lowest solution energy where the electron density is the lowest. In a mono-vacancy in W, H prefers to bind onto an isosurface of the same electron density (0.11 e $\text{Å}^{-3}$) surrounding the vacancy [9]. However, the outermost electrons of Re is one more than that of W, which will lead to the increasing of local charge density surrounding Re atom. The electron density for H-occupied position close to OIS-I is 0.12 e $\text{Å}^{-3}$, slight larger than that in pure vacancy. This should be responsible for the slight increasing of H trapping energy at OIS-I. Further, there is repulsive interaction between H and Re in W (as mentioned in section 3.1), and thus the larger the distance between H-site and Re in vacancy, the lower is the H trapping energy. So, the H trapping energy close to OIS-II is lower than that close to OIS-I.

3.2.2. Single H at multi-Re and V ($\text{Re}_m$-V) complexes in W. Previous studies [26, 28, 29] reported that vacancy can serve as a strong trapping center for Re atoms, leading to the nucleation and clustering of Re in W. It is found that the total binding energies of $\text{Re}_m$-vacancy complexes will increase with the increasing of Re number, from $\text{Re}_1$-vacancy to $\text{Re}_8$-vacancy, while the total binding energy of $\text{Re}_9$-vacancy is less than that of $\text{Re}_8$-vacancy, suggesting that a mono-vacancy can bind up to 8 Re atoms. Therefore, we further investigate the behaviors of single H surrounding $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes in W. The ground states of $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes are obtained from our previous calculations [26], and the subsequent H atom occupies different sites in the vicinity of $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes. Figures $3(a)$–$(h)$ show the most stable configurations of $\text{H-Re}_m$-V ($m=1$–8) complexes. It can be found that the most stable site for H is still an off-vacancy-center position close to an OIS. Meanwhile, the H atom stays as far away from Re atoms as possible, indicating the repulsive interaction between H and Re. Then, we calculate the trapping energy of H in $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes, which is defined as

$$
E_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm{trap}}=E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{m}-\mathrm{V}-H}-E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{m}-\mathrm{V}}-\left(E_{\mathrm{W}, \mathrm{H}(\mathrm{TIS})}-E_{\mathrm{W}}\right), \quad (3)
$$

where $E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{m}-\mathrm{V}-H}$ and $E_{\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{Re}_{m}-\mathrm{V}}$ are the total energy of $\text{Re}_m$-V system with and without a single H atom.

The trapping energies of H in $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes are illustrated in figure $3(i)$. It can be found that the trapping energies of H are negative for all cases, suggesting that the $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes can serve as strong trapping centers for H in W. For $\text{Re}_1$-V and $\text{Re}_2$-V case, their trapping energies of H would not change with the increasing number of Re and are the same with that in mono-vacancy ($\sim-1.18$eV), which can be attributed to the large distance ($\sim3.62$ Å) between Re and H, and thus the effects of Re on the dissolution of H is almost negligible. After that, the trapping energies of H monotonically increase with the increasing the number of Re atoms from $-1.11$eV in $\text{Re}_3$-V to $-0.79$eV in $\text{Re}_8$-V, which is larger than that in mono-vacancy. This is due to the short distance ($\sim2.24$–$2.55$ Å) between H and closest Re atom, and therefore the repulsion of Re–H is significant. Further, as shown in figure $3(i)$, the H trapping energies in $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes are almost proportional to the number of Re–H bonds, indicating that Re–H interaction should be mainly responsible for the H dissolution. Consequently, the presence of Re in the vicinity of vacancy will increase the H trapping energy and weaken the trapping capability of vacancy for H, and the larger the number of Re atoms in vacancy, the larger is the effects of Re.

To further shed light on the physical mechanism responsible for the effects of Re on the dissolution of H in vacancy, the solution energies of H in $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes can also be decomposed into MC and EC (see more detail in section 3.1). As shown in figure $4(a)$, both MC and EC increase with the increasing the number of Re atoms, similar to the H solution energy in $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes. However, it should be noted that the variation of MC (from 0.018eV in $\text{Re}_1$-V to 0.058eV in $\text{Re}_8$-V) for H solution energy is much lower than that of EC (from $-0.308$eV in $\text{Re}_1$-V to 0.032eV in $\text{Re}_8$-V), indicating that the EC is the dominating contribution for the variation of H solution in $\text{Re}_m$-V complexes. In order to obtain a realistic evolution of electronic interaction between H and W/Re in W, we calculate the differential electron density. Figure $4(b)$ displays the difference between the electron density of the H surrounding $\text{Re}_m$-V in W, and the density of isolated H plus that of $\text{Re}_m$-V in W. It can be found that the electron density differences between W/Re and H are negative for all cases, suggesting the repulsive interaction of Re/W–H in W. More importantly, the electron density difference between Re and H is much lower than that between W and H, which means that the repulsion of Re–H is stronger than that of W–H. Therefore, replacing W by Re will suppress the dissolution of H and result in an increase of H trapping energy in vacancy.

### 3.4. Effect of Re on H retention in W
Both vacancy and SIA can serve as strong trapping centers for H. However, the presence of Re will weak the H trapping capability of them. Most importantly, the larger the number of Re atoms, the trapping capability of them for H is weaker. This suggests that the presence of Re will significantly influence the trapping and retention of H in W. To verify this speculation, we determined the temperature with the maximum H de-trapping rate, $T_{\text{max}}$, which can be compared with the actual H de-trapping temperature from TDS measurement. For vacancy cases, similar with previous studies [31, 50], the $T_{\text{max}}$ is inserted into the Polanyi-Wigner equations, being expressed by

$$
\frac{E_{\text{de-trap}}}{k T_{\text{max}}^{2}}=\frac{\nu}{\beta} \exp \left(\frac{-E_{\text{de-trap}}}{k T_{\text{max}}}\right),\tag{6}
$$

where $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, $\beta$ is the linear heating rate, we selected $1\ \text{K s}^{-1}$ and $5\ \text{K s}^{-1}$, respectively, and $\nu$ is the attempt frequency of de-trap. The heating rate has effect on the maximum release temperature of H in W, as shown in equation (6). Indeed, according to our calculation, the H release temperature can be reduced by about 30 K when the heating rate decreases by an order of magnitude. Here, in

The maximum number of H atoms trapped by a mono-vacancy in W at RT has been investigated in previous studies. For example, Heinola et al reported that a mono-vacancy in W can accommodate 5 H atoms at RT [31], while Fernandez et al found the maximal number of H atoms that can be trapped by a mono-vacancy is 6 at RT [50]. Besides, You et al examined the concentration of $\text{V-H}_n$ complexes in W and found that the concentration of $\text{V-H}_6$ is much larger than other $\text{V-H}_n$ complexes at RT [68], indicating that the number of H atoms in a mono-vacancy in W is 6. Further, Sun et al determined an average number of 6.5 H atoms in a mono-vacancy in W at RT via molecular dynamic simulations [52]. In our calculation, the maximum number of H atoms in a mono-vacancy in W at RT is around 6, which is in good agreement with Fernandez et al [50], You et al [68] and Sun et al [52], but slight larger than that obtained by Heinola et al [31]. More importantly, the maximum number of H atoms in a mono-vacancy at RT is around 6 (see figures 8(c) and (d)), which is consistent with previous studies of H in a mono-vacancy [50, 52, 68]. As for $\text{Re}_1$-V and $\text{Re}_4$-V, it can only accommodate 5 and 4 H atoms at RT (see figures 8(c) and (d)), which is 16.7% and 33.3% less than that of mono-vacancy, respectively.

Not only in the complexes of Re and vacancy, the maximum release temperature of H in $\text{Re}_1$-SIA complexes has also been determined using equation(6). It is important to note that due to low diffusion energy barrier of SIA and Re-W dumbbell,

de-trapping energy is calculated by $E^{\text{de-trap}} = E^{\text{trap}} + E^{\text{dif}}$, where $E^{\text{dif}}$ is the diffusion energy barrier of SIA and IS-Re (0.005 eV for SIA [31] and 0.12 eV for Re-W dumbbell [27, 58]), respectively. It is found that the maximum release temperature of first H in SIA-H and $\text{Re}_1$-SIA-H complexes is 135 K and 155 K, respectively. This implies that the trapped H in SIA and $\text{Re}_1$-SIA complexes will be released at RT, and thus both SIA and $\text{Re}_1$-SIA complexes cannot act as an effective trapping center for H.

From experiments, many TDS measurements have been used to determine the H de-trapping energies [23-25, 61, 62]. For example, Quastel *et al* [61] and Poon *et al* [62] observed four different peaks of desorption temperature, near 900 K, 640 K, 520 K and 400 K, which is corresponding to the H trapping in void ($\sim 2.10 \pm 0.05$ eV), first H trapping in a mono-vacancy ($\sim 1.34 \pm 0.03$ eV), second H trapping in a mono-vacancy ($\sim 1.07 \pm 0.03$ eV) and a shallow H trapping ($\sim 0.65$ eV). Based on previous study [50], the value of 1.34 eV and 1.07 eV approximately equal to the mean value of de-trapping energy of H in $\text{V-H}_n$ ($n=1-3$) and $\text{V-H}_n$ ($n=4-6$). Our results are in good agreement with previous study [50]. These results suggest that vacancy type defects could dominate the H trapping in W. Alimov *et al* [23] investigated the D retention in pure W and W-5%Re alloys under D plasma irradiation. It is found that although the total D retention in pure W is almost same as that in W-Re, the presence of Re will reduce the maximum release temperature of H from 523 K for pure W to 463 K for W-Re alloys. This can be attributed to the formation of $\text{Re}_1$-V complex, which has a slightly lower H release temperature than a mono-vacancy (see figures 8(c) and (d)). Furthermore, Tyburska-Puschel *et al* [24] and Hatano *et al* [25] investigated the D retention in damaged W and W-Re alloys (initially irradiated by heavy ions and then exposed to D plasma), and reported that the total D retention in damaged W-Re alloys is much lower than that in damaged W. Because the Re atoms will aggregation and clustering under heavy ions irradiation [69, 70], the significant reduction of D retention in damaged materials can be attributed to the formation of multi-Re and vacancy complexes, which weaken the trapping capability of vacancy for H (see figures 8(c) and (d)), leading to the reduction of total D retention. Consequently, our calculations demonstrate that the interaction between Re and vacancy plays a key role on the H retention in W-Re alloys, especially under pre-damaged condition, which provides a good reference for understanding the influence of Re on H retention in W.

## 4. Conclusion

Using a first-principles method in combination with thermodynamic models, we investigate the effects of Re on the behaviors of H in W as well as their interaction with point defects. The presence of Re can slightly increase the solution energy of H nearby, suggesting that Re can somewhat suppress the H dissolution in bulk W. This can be attributed to that Re can weaken the interatomic interaction of W atoms nearby, and thus the embedded H atom in W-Re system result in the large lattice distortion and the high H solution energy in comparison with that in bulk W. Further, similar with Re-free defects, the complexes of Re with point defects (vacancy and SIA) can serve as trapping centers for H in W. More importantly, although the influences of single Re on the dissolution and retention of H surrounding defects is negligible, the Re clusters can substaintially increase the trapping energy of H, leading to the reduction of H retention in W. For example, the trapping energy of first H in $\text{Re}_1$-vacancy ($-1.17$ eV) almost equals to that of mono-vacancy ($-1.18$ eV), while it much lower than that in $\text{Re}_4$-V complex ($-1.03$ eV). Moreover, based on the Polanyi-Wigner equation, the maximum release temperature of H in W and W-Re alloy has been examined. It is found that the maximum number of H atoms in a mono-vacancy at RT is around 6, while a $\text{Re}_1$-V and $\text{Re}_4$-V complex can only accommodate 5 and 4 H atoms at RT, respectively. These results give a good interpretation for the recent experiments and provide a better understanding on the influence of Re on the behaviors of H in W.
