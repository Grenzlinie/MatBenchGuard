# Effective formation energies of atomic defects in D03-Fe₃Al: an ab-initio study

J. Mayer, B. Meyer, J. S. Oehrens, G. Bester, N. Börnsen & M. Fähnle*

Max-Planck-Institut für Metallforschung, Heisenbergstr. 1, D-70569 Stuttgart, Germany

(Received 10 March 1997; revised version received 17 April 1997; accepted 18 April 1997)

The effective formation energies of atomic defects in $D0_{3}-Fe_{3}Al$ are determined within the framework of a grand-canonical statistical method. The grand-canonical defect formation energies entering this method are calculated by the ab-initio mixed-basis pseudopotential method. Thermal vacancies appear on the $\alpha$ sublattice of the Fe atoms with an effective formation energy of 1.25 eV and on the Al sublattice (about 1.4 eV). The structural defects are Al antisite atoms on the $\gamma$ sublattice of the Fe atoms or Fe antisite atoms on the Al sublattice. © 1997 Elsevier Science Limited

Keywords: A. iron aluminides (based on $Fe_{3}Al$), B. defects: point defects, E. ab-initio calculations.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Some intermetallic compounds are promising candidates as structural materials for high-temperature applications. The diffusion processes occurring at high temperatures are determined by atomic defects, especially by vacancies. For the discussion of diffusion mechanisms thereby the following questions arise:

1. What is the concentration of vacancies in thermal equilibrium? In various intermetallic compounds vacancy concentrations of several atomic percent are found $^{1-3}$ which are much larger than in pure metals.
2. For which of the constituents do the vacancies arise? For instance, in B2-FeAl it has been demonstrated by the ab-initio electron theory $^{4-6}$ that vacancies occur nearly exclusively for the Fe sites.
3. If there are various crystallographically inequivalent sublattices for the same constituent, on which one are the vacancies?
4. Are there structural vacancies, i.e. those which survive when cooling down to $T \to 0 K$ in order to maintain deviations from the stoichiometric composition, or are there only thermally excited vacancies (in this case the non-stoichiometry would be maintained by antisite atoms)? For instance, the ab-initio calculations predict $^{4}$ that no structural vacancies occur in FeAl. Experiments predict $^{2}$ that in $Ni_{3}Sb$ the large vacancy concentration is structural in nature, whereas in $Fe_{3}Si$ the vacancies are thermal.

In this paper we report on ab-initio calculations of the formation properties of atomic defects in iron-rich Fe-Al systems near the stoichiometric compound $Fe_{3}Al$ , which exhibit a variety of phase transitions from the low-temperature ordered $D0_{3}$ structure to other phases (partially disordered B2 structure, disordered high-temperature A2 structure, and the two-phase regions at intermediate temperatures). The $D0_{3}$ structure is derived from the bcc lattice and may be represented by four interpenetrating fcc lattices (Fig. 1). In the stoichiometric compound the Fe atoms occupy the two crystallographically equivalent $\alpha$ sublattices (in the following denoted as ' $\alpha$ sublattice') and the $\gamma$ sublattice, whereas the Al atoms occupy the $\beta$ sublattice. The Fe atoms on the $\alpha$ sublattice have four Fe atoms and four Al atoms as nearest neighbours, whereas the Fe atoms on the $\gamma$ sublattice and the Al atoms on the $\beta$ sublattice have eight Fe atoms as nearest neighbours. Measurements $^{3}$ of the mean positron lifetime $\bar{\tau}$ in $Fe_{76.3}Al_{23.7}$ at $20^{\circ}C$ demonstrated that there are no structural vacancies for this composition. ## 2. CALCULATION METHOD

For the calculation of the concentration of atomic defects in $Fe_xAl_{100-x}$ we use the generalized grand-canonical formalism described, for instance, in Refs 4, 5, 8 which works for systems at or close to the stoichiometric composition (i.e. for $x\approx75$ in the present case). Wc consider only single atomic defects on the various sublattices and do not take into account bound defect complexes. Starting from the ordered stoichiometric compound we define grand-canonical excitations, i.e. antisite atoms on the various sublattices and 'grandcanonical' vacancies which are generated by removing atoms from the sublattices and separating them from the system instead of inserting them at surface sites (which would be the corresponding procedure in a canonical statistical ensemble). These excitations are characterized by defect formation parameters which describe the change in energy, entropy and volume due to a single grandcanonical excitation, respectively. In the present paper we neglect the defect formation entropies (which affects only the absolute values of the concentrations but should have only a weak influence on the effective formation energies) and we fix the volume of the system. We then evaluate the grand-canonical potential $J$ as function of the concentrations of the various atomic defects for given temperature $T$, volume $V$, composition $x$, and chemical potentials $\mu_{Fe}$, $\mu_{Al}$, and we obtain the equilibrium concentrations by minimizing $J$. Finally, the chemical potentials which enter the equilibrium concentrations are determined from the thermodynamic relation $J=0$ (for pressure $p=0$) and from the demand that the correct composition of the compound is obtained, i.e. $N_{Fe}/N_{Al}=x/(100-x)$. The resulting concentrations depend on the defect formation energies of all considered defects, and they depend on the temperature in a very complicated manner. However, if the temperature dependences of the chemical potentials are very weak (which is the case for $Fe_3Al$) or can be linearized$^{4,8}$ then we obtain in very good approximation a simple Arrhenius behaviour with temperature-independent effective formation energies for the various defects, which again do not depend just on the defect formation parameters of the respective defect but on the defect formation energies of all considered defects. The structural defect can be identified as the one for which the effective formation energy is zero.

The defect formation energies are calculated by the ab-initio pseudopotential method with a mixed basis of plane waves and five localized $d$-orbitals per Fe atom.$^4$ The effect of the spin polarization is neglected in accordance with the experimental observation$^9$ for the $D0_3$-compound $Fe_3Si$ where the development of ferromagnetic order below the Curie temperature $T_c$ changes the effective vacancy formation energy by a few hundredths of eV only. This has to do with the fact that the magnetic moments on the Fe atoms remain rather stable when introducing atomic defects (see Section 3) so that basically the inter-atomic exchange interactions (which are comparatively weak) rather than the stronger intra-atomic exchange interactions are modified. We fully take into account the structural relaxation of the atoms surrounding the defect. A supercell geometry is adopted, i.e. we consider large unit cells with one defect, respectively, and we repeat these supercells periodically because of computational reasons. ## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Table 1 represents our results for the effective formation energies. It becomes obvious that the structural relaxation has a major effect on the effective vacancy formation energies and this effect is larger than the differences for these energies between the calculations for $N=16$ and $N=32$, respectively. We therefore consider the data obtained for the relaxed $N=16$ supercell, which reveal the following results:

1. In contrast to the behaviour⁴ of B2-FeₓAl₁₀₀₋ₓ near $x=50$, the effective formation energies vary only slightly with composition.

2. The Fe vacancies occur mainly on the $\alpha$ sublattice. This result is in conflict with the conclusions of Jirásková *et al.*¹⁰ who investigated the Mössbauer spectra of a Fe₇₂Al₂₈ compound and concluded that the Fe vacancies appear on the $\gamma$ sublattice. They analyzed their data with the assumption that for systems with overstoichiometric Al concentrations (i.e. with Al antisite atoms on the $\gamma$ sublattice) the introduction of Fe vacancies on the $\gamma$ sublattice leads to a strong reduction of the mean Fe hyperfine field, whereas an Fe vacancy on the $\alpha$ sublattice is assumed to have a minor effect only. To check this hypothesis we have calculated with the linear-muffin-tin-orbital method in atomic-sphere approximation¹¹ the zero-temperature magnetic contact hyperfine fields along the lines described in Ref. 12. We found by supercell calculations for various concentrations $69 \leq x \leq 75$ that the hyperfine fields on the various Fe sites remain rather stable and depend only slightly on $x$ (and on the lattice constant used for the calculation), irrespective on which sublattice the Fe vacancies are introduced. The hypothesis of Jirásková *et al.*¹⁰ thus could not be confirmed by our calculations.

3. There are in addition to the Fe vacancies also Al vacancies with comparable effective formation energy. In contrast, for B2-FeₓAl₁₀₀₋ₓ near or at $x=50$ there are nearly no Al vacancies.⁴⁻⁶It therefore might well be that for increasing Fe concentration the effective formation energy for Al vacancies decreases so that in strongly Fe-rich B2-samples (for which our grandcanonical formalism does not hold) the Fe self-diffusion may be mediated by Al vacancies,¹³ whereas close to $x=50$ this mechanism must be excluded.

4. Our value for the effective formation energy of an Fe vacancy on the $\alpha$ sublattice (1·25 eV) agrees well with the result obtained by Schaefer *et al.*³ from positron annihilation experiments ($1.18 \pm 0.04$ eV).

5. As⁴ in B2-FeₓAl₁₀₀₋ₓ there are no structural vacancies for the D0₃ phase. For the Al-rich system the structural defect is the Al antisite atom on the $\gamma$ sublattice (whereas the Fe vacancy is formed on the $\alpha$ sublattice). For the Fe-rich system there are Fe antisite atoms on the Al sublattice. The effective formation energies for these defects are both zero or close to zero and are more or less independent of the detailed composition. This indicates that the preferential ordering on the $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ sublattices is not very strong, which is compatible with the small ordering temperatures of the D0₃ phase.

Our present results can be compared with those of an empirical Bragg-Williams calculation.¹⁴ The advantage of the latter method is that it can be used not only at or close to stoichiometry but for arbitrary defect concentrations. The disadvantage is⁸ that it is based on the notion of interaction energies between pairs of neighboring atoms (obtained by fitting the theoretical results to experimental data) which are assumed to be independent on the local environment, which is certainly only a rough approximation in intermetallic compounds.⁵ In both calculations the Fe vacancies appear predominantly on the $\alpha$ sublattice. The most important difference is that our calculations predict a comparable concentration of Al vacancies, whereas in the Bragg-Williams calculation there are nearly no Al vacancies.

For the self-diffusion of Fe atoms the existence of Al vacancies means that there is a further channel for nearest-neighbour jumps of Fe atoms. Without Al vacancies the nearest-neighbour jumps of Fe atoms occur exclusively by interchanges between $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ sites (Note that $\gamma$ sites are denoted as $\beta$ sites in Ref. 14 and vice versa). For the diffusion of the Al atoms it means that it is possible by third-nearest neighbour jumps on their own sublattice without involving the Fe sublattices. Alternatively, it may migrate via nearest-neighbour or second-nearest neighbour jumps to vacancies on the $\alpha$ or $\gamma$ sublattice, respectively.
