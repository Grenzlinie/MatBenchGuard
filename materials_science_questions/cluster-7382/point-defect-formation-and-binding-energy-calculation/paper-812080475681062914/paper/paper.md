# Simple Theoretical Estimates of the Schottky Constants and Virtual-Enthalpies of Single Vacancy Formation in Zinc-Blende and Wurtzite Type Semiconductors

J. A. Van Vechten*,1

Bell Laboratories, Murray Hill, New Jersey 07974

## ABSTRACT

Phillips and Van Vechten have shown that, because of the high valence electron density in diamond type semiconductors, their vacancies may properly be treated as macroscopic cavities. With this model one may estimate the enthalpy of neutral vacancy formation, $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}})$, as simply the surface energy of the cavity. Values of $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}})$ are known for $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{Ge}$ and are $10\%$ less than the theoretical estimates. Here this model is extended to zinc-blende and wurtzite type crystals. The virtual-enthalpies of formation $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}})$ and $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}})$ are calculated for 17 common semiconductors. Difficulties in analyzing empirical data which arise from the ionization of the vacancies and their interaction with other defects are noted. However, for CdS, where the data are adequate, the empirical values are about $12\%$ less than those calculated.

Phillips and Van Vechten have noted $(1,^{2}2)$ that the Wigner-Seitz atomic radius, $r_{\mathrm{w}}$, is much greater than the inverse of the Thomas-Fermi screening wave number, $k_{\mathrm{s}}$, in the diamond type semiconductors

$$k_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathrm{Si}) r_{\mathrm{w}}(\mathrm{Si})=3.5, \quad k_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathrm{Ge}) r_{\mathrm{w}}(\mathrm{Ge})=3.6 \tag{1}$$

Moreover, the surface energy of a metal, $E_{\mathrm{S}}$, results from the spilling of electronic charge density into the vacuum, where it is not compensated by the positive charge of the ion cores, because the electronic wave functions cannot be abruptly terminated by a finite potential $(3,4)$. This spilled charge distribution decays as $\rho_{0}\exp(-r/k_{\mathrm{s}})$. One expects the range of effects which produce the surface energy of a semiconductor to be similar to or slightly shorter than in the case of a metal (1). Furthermore, because all the lattice sites of a diamond type crystal may be treated as equivalent sites, the macroscopic volume to be associated with a single atomic vacancy is exactly one atomic volume, $\Omega$

$$\Omega=a^{3}/8=4\pi r_{\mathrm{w}}^{3}/3 \tag{2}$$

where $a$ is the lattice constant. Therefore, if the single vacancy is regarded as a macroscopic cavity of volume $\Omega$, the range over which the surfaces of this cavity would seem poorly defined in a microscopic sense is small compared with the total dimensions of the vacancy. Thus, it was concluded that a single atomic vacancy in a diamond type semiconductor may properly be treated as a macroscopic cavity and the enthalpy of neutral vacancy formation, $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}})$, may be estimated as its surface area times its surface energy

$$\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}})=\hat{\Omega}E_{\mathrm{S}} \tag{3}$$

To evaluate $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}})$, one should note that the equilibrium shape of a cavity in a diamond type crystal is not a sphere, but an octahedron with only (111) surfaces, and that $E_{\mathrm{S}}$ is not isotropic $(2,5,6)$. The enthalpy or internal energy per unit area of the (111) surfaces, $E_{\mathrm{S}}(111)$, is less than that of any other surface. Thus, if the vacancy has the same equilibrium shape as a macroscopic cavity in the same material, we have

$$\Delta H(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}})=\hat{\Omega}_{8}E_{\mathrm{S}}(111) \tag{4}$$

where $\hat{\Omega}_{8}$ is the surface area of an octahedron of volume $\Omega$

$$\hat{\Omega}_{8}=1.1826 \hat{\Omega}_{x} \tag{5}$$

and $\hat{\Omega}_{x}=4\pi r_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}$ is the area of the Wigner-Seitz sphere.

It has been noted (2) that the observed Jahn-Teller distortion of $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Si}}{}^{+}$ corroborates the assumption that its macroscopic shape is octahedral.

Gilman obtained an empirical estimate for $E_{\mathrm{S}}(111)$ of $\mathrm{Si}$ from an irreversible crack-propagation experiment (7). However, similar experiments have not been reported for other semiconductors so it is necessary to make theoretical estimates. It was proposed $(1,2)$ that

$$E_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{ijk})=E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}(\mathrm{ijk})+E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{b}}(\mathrm{ijk}) \tag{6}$$

where $E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}(\mathrm{ijk})$ is the contribution from long-ranged, metallic forces $(3,4)$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{b}}(\mathrm{ijk})$ is the contribution from short-ranged, directional covalent forces (8). [Note that the energy of the covalent bond is here reckoned as the extra cohesive energy of the covalent phase relative to the metallic phase of the same composition rather than as the total cohesive energy relative to free atoms (8). This covalent energy is calculated in the manner of Hume-Rothery by comparing the electronic band structures of the semiconducting and metallic phases.]

Because both theoretical and experimental values are available for the surface tension of liquid metals $(3,4)$, it seems wise to relate $E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}$ to the surface tension and to test the proposed relation with the case of $\mathrm{Si}$. It is assumed that $E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}(111)$ is just the surface enthalpy per unit area, $H_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}$, of the metallic liquid phase of $\mathrm{Si}$ corrected for difference in density between the solid and liquid phases. The value of $H_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}$ is obtained from measurements of the surface tension, $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}$, of the liquid, which is indeed the surface free energy per unit area

$$F_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}=H_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}-TS_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}} \tag{7}$$

where $T$ denotes temperature and $S_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}$ is the surface entropy per unit area, $S_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}=-dF_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}/dT$. The generally accepted values for $\mathrm{Si}$ at $T^{\mathrm{F}}=1685^{\circ}\mathrm{K}$ are $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}(\mathrm{Si},T^{\mathrm{F}})=734\ \mathrm{erg/cm^{2}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}(\mathrm{Si},T^{\mathrm{F}})=0.09\ \mathrm{erg/cm^{2}}$ (9-11) so that $H_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}(\mathrm{Si},1685^{\circ}\mathrm{K})=887\ \mathrm{erg/cm^{2}}$. Taking the density of the liquid phase to be $15.3\%$ greater than the solid (8) and the theoretical result that $H_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}$ vary as the $-5/6$ power with density, one estimates

$$E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}(111,\mathrm{Si})=H_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{l}}/(1.153)^{5/6}=788\ \mathrm{erg/cm^{2}} \tag{8}$$

Therefore, the agreement with theory is considered to be acceptable.

From the above considerations, one may estimate $\Delta H(\mathrm{~V})$ for diamond type crystals as $^{3}$
$$\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}}, T\right)=\mathscr{R}_{8} E_{\mathrm{S}}{ }^{\mathrm{m}}(111)+4 E_{\mathrm{b}}(T) \tag{12}$$
if empirical values for $E_{\mathrm{S}}{ }^{1}$ are known as they are for $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{Ge}$ (9-11). [Note that the factor 4 follows from the fact that there are four broken bonds about the vacancy and the full energy of the bonds must be ascribed to $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$. One should also note that $S_{\mathrm{S}}{ }^{1}$ does not vary greatly from one liquid metal to another (10, 11).] For $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{Ge}$ at their respective melting points one calculates
$$\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{Si}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}, T^{\mathrm{F}}\right)=2.66 \mathrm{eV}, \Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{Ga}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}, T^{\mathrm{F}}\right)=2.21 \mathrm{eV} \tag{13}$$

Empirical values may be obtained from quenching experiments (12-14). However, one should take proper account of the ionization state of the vacancies at high temperatures and of the finite quenching rate when these experiments are analyzed (15-17). The empirical values are
$$\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{Si}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)=2.4 \pm 0.2 \mathrm{eV}, \Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{Ga}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)=2.0 \pm 0.2 \mathrm{eV} \tag{14}$$
so that this macroscopic model seems to overestimate the true values of $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ by about $10 \% .^{4}$ One should expect such an overestimate to occur because the charge density spilling into the vacancy cavity will overlap along the edges and at the apexes. Thus, these portions of the surface should have a lower energy than the corresponding free surfaces at infinite separation.

This is far more than the observed values which generally run between 0.5 and 1.0 times the heat of atomization for most materials. See the discussion of the Jahn-Teller distortion in Ref. (2).

# Extension of Theory to Zinc-Blende and Wurtzite Crystals

The lattice constants of GaAs, ZnSe, and CuBr are nearly the same as that of Ge because all these elements occur in the same row of the periodic table (18). For this reason they have nearly the same valence electron density, bulk, and surface plasmon frequencies. Therefore, the use of the macroscopic model to treat vacancies in zinc-blende or wurtzite type crystals is as well justified as its use with diamond type crystals.

Because zinc-blende and wurtzite crystals will contain both anion and cation vacancies, $V_{B}$ and $V_{A}$, one must establish some criterion for the relative size of these two species. It is here assumed that the dimensions of a vacancy cavity are proportional to the tetrahedral covalent radius, $r_{\mathrm{c}}$, of the relevant element according to the table of Van Vechten and Phillips (18). Then by simple geometry, one finds the effective Wigner-Seitz radius of $A$ is
$$r_{\mathrm{w}}(A)=(16 / \pi \sqrt{3})^{1 / 3} r_{\mathrm{c}}(A)=1.433 r_{\mathrm{c}}(A) \tag{15}$$

Then the area of, for example, an octahedral cavity of appropriate size may be calculated using Eq. [5].

At this point we may make a qualitative observation about the agreement of theory with experiment. Consider the relative energy of neutral cation and anion vacancies which may be discussed in terms of their virtual-enthalpies of formation, $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ and $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$. [We recognize that only the Schottky constant enthalpy
$$H_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{AB})=\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)+\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right) \tag{16}$$
is a true enthalpy defined in terms of a true chemical potential, but that it is both permissible and convenient to calculate the virtual-enthalpies of vacancies in these compound crystals in a manner analogous to that used with elemental crystals. These virtual-enthalpies are then used in the proper structure element analysis of the thermodynamics of compounds developed by Kröger, Stieltjes, and Vink (19). $]^{5}$ Because there is only one type of bond in these AB crystals, the short-range or broken bond contribution is the same to $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ as to $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$. Therefore, the difference between the two is only in the long-range contribution, $\mathscr{R}_{8} E_{\mathrm{S}}{ }^{\mathrm{m}}(111)$ of Eq. [12], which is proportional to $r_{\mathrm{w}}{ }^{2}$. Thus, the present theory predicts that $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ will be larger for the component with the larger $r_{\mathrm{c}}$.

If differences in the enthalpies of ionization, $\Delta H_{\mathrm{I}}\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{-}\right)$and $\Delta H_{\mathrm{I}}\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{+}\right)$, and of the activities in the liquid or vapor phase are neglected, then the ionized vacancies of the smaller element should be more numerous at the maximum of the solidus. Thus, by considering only vacancy contributions to the defect induced extrinsic carrier concentration of undoped material, one predicts the material to be n-type if $r_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{A})>r_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{B})$ and to be p-type if $r_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{A})<r_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{B})$. It is commonly believed that the carrier type that may be obtained in these compounds is limited by a vacancy self-compensation mechanism, although antistructure defects, $\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{-}$or $\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{+}$, may also be important. [See the accompanying paper (23).] In any event, the comparison in Table I shows perfect agreement between prediction and observation. $^{6}$ The same correlation occurs in III-V crystals although most of these can be doped to either type with impurities; undoped GaSb is p-type, while GaP and GaN are n-type. The author knows of no exception to this rule.

In order to make quantitative estimates of $\Delta H(\mathrm{V}^{\mathrm{x}})$, one must determine the shape of the vacancy cavity and the value of the appropriate surface energy. In contrast to diamond, Si and Ge, the equilibrium shape of zinc-blende type crystals is in general the rhombohedral-dodecahedron, which has only (110) surfaces. It is commonly assumed that this distinction results from the fact that the unreconstructed (110) surface has an equal number of anion and cation surface atoms whereas unreconstructed (111) [and (100)] surfaces have only one type of surface atom. Therefore, it seems likely that the distinction between diamond and zinc-blende equilibrium shapes results from Coulomb interactions across several unit cells of surface atoms that would not be operative on the surfaces of a neutral vacancy. Furthermore, the author is unaware of any determination of the Jahn-Teller distortion about $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ or $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ in a zinc-blende crystal which could serve to settle the question of the shape of the cavity, but the geometry of the lattice seems to favor the octahedral shape of vacancies in Si and Ge (2). Finally, there seems to be no measurements of $E_{\mathrm{S}}(110)$ for zincblende crystals like those of Gilman for Si $E_{\mathrm{S}}(111)$ (7), so that it is not possible to test any proposed relation between $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}}(110)$ as was done for $E_{\mathrm{S}}(111)$ in Eq. [11]. Due to the above considerations, it is here assumed that $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ have the same octahedral shape as $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Si}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Ge}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ and that the same relation holds between the surface tension of the stoichiometric liquid, $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}\left(\mathrm{~A}_{0.5} \mathrm{~B}_{0.5}\right)$, and the effective $E_{\mathrm{S}}(111, \mathrm{AB})$ on the surface of the $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ or $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}$ cavity.

Thus, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)=\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{A}) E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}(111)+4 E_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathrm{AB}) \\
& \Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)=\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{B}) E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}(111)+4 E_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathrm{AB})
\end{aligned} \quad[17]
$$

where $\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{A})$ and $\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{B})$ are defined in terms of $r_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{A})$ and $r_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathrm{B})$ by Eq. [5] and [15]. Values of $E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}$ are here calculated using the theory of Schmit and Lucas to calculate $H_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}$ with the same parameters for the surface plasmon cutoff $(3,4)$ and of the change in density upon melting and $S_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}$ as are observed for Si. (Schmit and Lucas calculated $H_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}$ for 53 metals within $15 \%$ of experiment.) The resulting values are shown in Table II.

In Table II, calculated values are also shown for the surface tension of the stoichiometric liquid at $T^{\mathrm{F}}$, $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}\left(\mathrm{~A}_{0.5} \mathrm{~B}_{0.5}, T^{\mathrm{F}}\right)$. Although it would seem to be easily possible to measure $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}$ using the method of Sugden $(26,27)$, only a few measurements have been reported $(28,29)$ and these generally refer to a nonstoichiometric liquid which is in equilibrium with the solid under some convenient growth condition. Thus, for example, the calculated value for $F_{\mathrm{S}}^{1}\left(\mathrm{Al}_{0.5} \mathrm{Sb}_{0.5}, 1353^{\circ} \mathrm{K}\right)=531$ $\mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}$ cannot be compared with the reported value, $480 \mathrm{erg} / \mathrm{cm}^{2}(28)$, until more information about the experiment is supplied.

Although the values in Table II are appropriate for the crystal at $T^{\mathrm{F}}$, the variation of the calculated values with temperature is not large. This is because the coefficients of thermal expansion of these crystals are so small that the electron density varies less than $2 \%$. Thus, the dominant term, $\hat{\Omega} E_{\mathrm{S}}^{\mathrm{m}}$, varies less than $2 \%$. The temperature variation of the covalent term, $4 E_{\mathrm{b}}(T)$, is given in Ref. (8).

### Comparison with Experiment
The analysis required to determine $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ and $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ from experimental data is rather more involved than that required to determine $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ for elemental semiconductors (15-17). As with Si and Ge, one must take account of the ionization state of both types of vacancies at the high temperature, $T_{\mathrm{h}}$, at which an equilibrium is established and of the escape and agglomeration of these vacancies during the finite time required to cool the sample from $T_{\mathrm{h}}$ to the temperature where measurements are made. As with $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Si}}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{Ge}}$, both $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}$ may be expected to form complexes with impurities, $\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{B}}$, during the quench so that any ionization energies observed at low temperature must be ascribed to $\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{B}}$ rather than to $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}$. [The ionization energies $\Delta H_{\mathrm{I}}\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{-}\right)$and $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{+}\right)$, etc., can only be determined if simple, single vacancies can be produced near $T=0^{\circ} \mathrm{K}$ as by electron irradiation.]

The problem of analyzing the data for AB crystals is worse than that with elemental semiconductors because one should expect the vacancies to interact with antisite defects, $\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{B}}$ and $\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{A}}(23)$. If the crystal is grown off-stoichiometry, say with an excess of $\mathrm{A}$, then the excess may be accommodated in the lattice as $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}$ or $\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{B}}$ or interstitial $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{i}}$. It seems that the concentration of interstitials $\left[\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{i}}\right]$ is significant only for elements of valence $Z_{\mathrm{A}}=1$, i.e., the alkali and noble metal impurities. Elements with $Z_{\mathrm{A}}=2$ may occur as interstitials in a concentration sufficient to affect atomic diffusion but they are more commonly found on substitutional sites(30). Except for cases like O in Si where a compound is formed, $\left[\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{i}}\right]$ is negligible when $Z_{\mathrm{A}}>2$. However, the virtual enthalpies of formation of antisite defects, $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~A}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ or $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~B}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$, are comparable with $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ and $\Delta H\left(\mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{x}}\right)$ (23). The charge compensation of $V_B{^+}$ with $A_B{^-}$ or $A_B{^{-2}}$ will occur not only at $T_h$ but also as the sample cools. At $T_h$ the equilibrium concentrations of neutral vacancies $[V_B{^x}]$ and neutral antisite defects $[A_B{^x}]$ will be different functions of the partial vapor pressures, $P_A$ and $P_B$, over the sample (31). However, as the sample is cooled the equilibrium with the vapor is broken and vacancies and antisite defects may be interchanged by reactions of the type

$$
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{\mathrm{x}}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{+}=\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{B}}{ }^{-2}+\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{A}}{ }^{-}+4 e^{+}
\tag{18}
$$

which will occur every time a vacancy migrates to a nearest neighbor site.

$[V_B{^+}]$ and $[A_B{^-}]$, $[A_B{^{-2}}]$, etc., will always be affected by the position of the Fermi level, $E_F$. They may interact through the charge compensation mechanism to keep the $E_F$ in the gap even though the deviation from stoichiometry may be significant. The author proposes that this effect is the resolution of the paradox that deviations from stoichiometry in GaP produce electrically active "shunt-path killer centers" although they have little effect on the carrier concentration either at $T_h$ or at room temperature (32).

To the author's knowledge, the only case for which adequate information has been obtained to determine $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_A{^x}})$ and $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_B{^x}})$ properly is that of CdS (33). Kumar and Kröger have concluded that $\Delta H_S(\text{CdS}) \simeq$ 5.6 eV while an analysis of their data similar to that illustrated for GaP in the following paper (23) implies $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_{Cd}{^x}}) \simeq 3.15$ eV and $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_S{^x}}) \simeq 2.45$ eV. These values may be compared with the present theory, Table II, $\Delta H_S(\text{CdS}) = 6.25$ eV, $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_{Cd}{^x}}) =$ 3.56 eV and $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_S{^x}}) = 2.69$ eV. We see that the theoretical values are about 12% greater than the empirical values in each case. This compares very favorably with the 10% theoretical overestimate found for $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_{Si}{^x}})$ and $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_{Ge}{^x}})$ in Eq. [13] and [14].

We should expect all the theoretical estimates in Table II to be comparably reliable, i.e., 5-20% overestimates, and far more reliable than estimates made by fitting data with a naive van't Hoff plot that neglects ionization states, escape, and interactions.

## Added Note

After completion of this work the author was made aware of detailed studies of the surface tension of liquid GaSb (34) and liquid InSb (35) as a function of composition and temperature. For the 50% Ga-50% Sb liquid $10^\circ$K above $T^F$, the apparent values are: $F_S{^l} = 454$ erg/cm²; $S_S{^l} = 0.112$ erg/cm² deg.; $H_S{^l} = 566$ erg/cm². These values differ somewhat from those assumed in the present calculation and would imply $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_{Ga}{^x}}) = 1.79$ eV and $\Delta H(\mathrm{V_{Sb}{^x}}) = 2.25$ eV, which are 10% less than the values in Table II.

Within the Schmit-Lucas theory (3, 4), one may expect $H_S{^l}$ to be less for a partially ionized liquid than for an elemental liquid of the same valence electron density because interband transitions would be more effective in cutting off the plasmons. However, the magnitude of this effect may not be as large (10%) as the apparent experimental values suggest. It was found that $F_S{^l}$ varies a factor of 2 across the Ga-Sb composition range so that one should expect the composition of the surface to deviate from that of the bulk in such a way as to minimize the free energy of the system. Furthermore, an unexplained peak in $F_S{^l}(x)$ is found at the 50% composition.

The situation in liquid InSb is similar to that in GaSb and the discrepancy between apparent values of $F_S{^l}$ and Table II are somewhat less. However, the data on the temperature variation, which is required to obtain $S_S{^l}$ and $H_S{^l}$, is not sufficiently accurate to discuss a correction.
