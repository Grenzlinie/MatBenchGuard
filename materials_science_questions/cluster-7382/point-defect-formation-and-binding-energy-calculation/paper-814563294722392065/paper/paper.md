# Correlation between crystallinity and oxygen vacancy formation in In–Ga–Zn oxide

Tomoki Hiramatsu*, Motoki Nakashima, Erumu Kikuchi, Noritaka Ishihara, Masashi Tsubuku, Koji Dairiki, and Shunpei Yamazaki

Semiconductor Energy Laboratory Co., Ltd., Atugi, Kanagawa 243-0036, Japan
*E-mail: th1322@sel.co.jp

Received July 17, 2015; accepted November 18, 2015; published online January 21, 2016

We study the effect of indium–gallium–zinc oxide (IGZO) crystallinity on oxygen vacancies that play an important role in the characteristics of IGZO-based devices. Optical and electrical measurements revealed that deep defect levels due to oxygen vacancies are largely eliminated in c-axis-aligned crystal IGZO (CAAC-IGZO), which has increased crystallinity without clear grain boundaries. In this study, the correlation between crystallinity and oxygen vacancy formation has been examined by first-principles calculations to investigate the effect of oxygen vacancies in IGZO. Furthermore, the likelihood of oxygen vacancy formation at an edge portion of single-crystal IGZO has been verified by observations of oxygen atoms at the edge region of the IGZO film by annular bright-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (ABF-STEM). Experimental and calculation results show that the high crystallinity of IGZO is important for the inhibition of oxygen vacancies.

## 1. Introduction

Indium–gallium–zinc oxide (IGZO) was synthesized for the first time in 1985 by Kimizuka and Mohri, and has been widely researched as a semiconductor material.¹) We previously discovered that various IGZO crystal morphologies are formed in addition to amorphous and single-crystal reported by other research institutions. The discovered c-axis-aligned crystal IGZO (CAAC-IGZO) and nanocrystalline IGZO (nc-IGZO) morphologies showed significant differences from single-crystal and polycrystalline structures. Highly reliable devices were fabricated using field-effect transistors (FETs) with active layers containing CAAC-IGZO or nc-IGZO.²⁴) In particular, CAAC-IGZO is an entirely novel crystal morphology that exhibits distortions and does not show clear grain boundaries despite having a high degree of layered crystallinity.⁵) The high crystallinity and the lack of clear grain boundaries are advantageous features of CAAC-IGZO and contribute to the high reliability of a CAAC-IGZO FET. Furthermore, nc-IGZO exhibits high homogeneity and has nanometer-sized crystals that are oriented in different directions.⁶) The ability to form CAAC-IGZO at low temperatures with high reliability is particularly advantageous for applications in mass-produced devices.⁷) In addition to application to currently mass-produced displays, the application of IGZO FETs to large-scale integration circuits (LSIs) is expected; however, this requires further improvement in CAAC-IGZO FET reliability.⁸⁻¹²)

A negative shift in threshold voltage (negative-bias photodegradation) is a widely known example of IGZO FET degradation modes and occurs when a negative bias is applied to the gate electrode under light irradiation.¹³) Our previous research revealed that the use of CAAC-IGZO as an active layer of an IGZO FET largely eliminates negative-bias photodegradation.⁴) Negative-bias photodegradation is thought to be due to the presence of deep defect levels in the band gap, which are derived from oxygen vacancies ($V_O$) in IGZO thin films.¹⁴,¹⁵) We have quantified the deep defect levels due to oxygen vacancies in IGZO thin films by measurements of the absorption coefficient using a constant photocurrent method (CPM) and found that an improvement in crystallinity decreases the absorption coefficient derived from deep defect levels.¹⁶) These studies showed that the effectiveness of CAAC-IGZO with high crystallinity in decreasing negative-bias photodegradation arises from its physical properties. However, the relationship between crystallinity and oxygen vacancy formation has not been well studied theoretically. Therefore, in this study, the correlation between crystallinity and oxygen vacancy formation in an IGZO film is investigated by first-principles calculations, along with the experimental and theoretical investigations of oxygen vacancies at the edge of the film.

## 2. Crystallinity and oxygen vacancies

To evaluate the structure and total energy, first-principles calculations were performed using Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was used for the exchange–correlation functional, and the projector augmented wave method was used to represent the nuclei and core electrons, with In, Ga, Zn 3d or 4d electrons considered as valence.¹⁷⁻¹⁹) The cutoff energy was 800 eV, and a single $k$-point at $\Gamma$ was used in ionic relaxations. A crystalline IGZO model includes 112 atoms on the basis of an $\mathrm{InGaO_3(ZnO)_m}$ ($m=1$) unit cell.²⁰) The lattice constants of the optimized crystalline IGZO model are $a=11.53$ Å, $b=13.38$ Å, $c=8.87$ Å, $\alpha=90.0^\circ$, $\beta=77.9^\circ$, and $\gamma=90.0^\circ$. It was reported that the calculated lattice constants converted into the values per unit cell are compatible with the lattice constants of single-crystal IGZO obtained by experiments.¹)

In a previous study, we performed first-principles calculations for amorphous and crystalline IGZO models to examine the formation of an oxygen vacancy.²¹) The calculation results indicate that oxygen vacancies form deep defect levels below the conduction band in IGZO, suggesting that oxygen vacancies in IGZO can be regarded as deep defect levels measured by CPM. A comparison of oxygen vacancy formation energy between crystalline and amorphous IGZO models shows that oxygen vacancy has a greater tendency to form in the amorphous IGZO model than in the crystalline IGZO model. However, different reference energies were used for the calculation of oxygen vacancy formation energies in the amorphous and crystalline IGZO models. Therefore, the total energies of the models obtained by first-principles calculations could not be directly compared. In this study, we used a model of a complex of crystalline and

amorphous IGZOs to enable a direct comparison of total energies and oxygen vacancy formation energies.

The amorphous IGZO model structure was prepared using ab initio quantum mechanical molecular dynamics (MD) calculations as follows. $^{22,23)}$ NVT ensemble simulations were first performed using a 112-atom crystalline IGZO model structure at 3500 K for 7 ps to obtain a molten state, and the system was then quenched stepwise at a rate of 500 K/ps. The ionic coordinates and cell parameters of the quenched model structure were then optimized with respect to the total energy. The density of the optimized model was $6.16\ \text{g/cm}^3$.

The IGZO complex model (Fig. 1) was set up as follows. $^{24)}$ The crystalline and amorphous IGZO models were connected in a direction parallel to the $b$-axis of the crystalline IGZO model such that the distances between atoms were not shorter than $1.4\mathring{\text{A}}$. The ionic coordinates were then relaxed. Ten different complex model structures were prepared. The model structure with the lowest total energy was used to study the formation of an oxygen vacancy by the removal of an oxygen atom followed by structural optimization. Ab initio quantum mechanical MD calculation is sometimes used after two models are connected to form an interface structure in the above method. $^{25)}$ However, we did not employ ab initio quantum mechanical MD calculation because this calculation focuses on the difference in crystallinity. A calculation of the density of states in this complex model indicated that neither interface states nor defect states were formed, showing that a relatively stable interface was formed by the above method.

The oxygen vacancy formation energy was calculated using the total energy of the optimized model structure. The model subjected to the calculation in this study was the most stable complex model, while the calculation for a different model gave a similar result.

The resulting IGZO complex model structure is shown in Fig. 1, with a 112-atom crystalline region on the left and a 112-atom amorphous region on the right. Two interfaces are present because of periodic boundary conditions. The interface shown in the center of Fig. 1 is referred to as Interface A, and the other interface is referred to as Interface B. For convenience, the distance from Interface A to an atom in the crystalline region is considered negative and the distance to an atom in the amorphous region is considered positive.

In general, the formation energy of defect D with charge $q$ $[E_{\text{form}}(\text{D}, q)]$ is given by $^{26)}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{form}}(\text{D}, q) &= E(\text{D}, q) - E(0,0) \\
&\quad -\sum_{i} \Delta n_{i} \mu_{i} + q(E_{\text{F}} + E_{\text{VBM}}), \tag{1}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $E(\text{D}, q)$ is the total energy of the model containing the defect with charge $q$, $E(0,0)$ is the total energy of a chargeneutral model with no defect, $\Delta n_{i}$ is the number of atoms $i$ removed from or added to the model, $\mu_{i}$ is the chemical potential of an atom $i$ removed from or added to the model, $E_{\text{F}}$ is the Fermi energy, and $E_{\text{VBM}}$ is the energy of the model with no defect at the valence band maximum. Since the IGZO film tends to have n-type conductivity, the Fermi level of IGZO probably lies closer to the conduction band. It has been reported that when the Fermi level is positioned closer to the conduction band, the most stable $\text{V}_{\text{O}}$ has neutral charge. $^{20,27)}$ In this work, charge is neutral in the calculation of the oxygen vacancy formation energy, and Eq. (1) can be simplified as

$$
E_{\text{form}} = E_{\text{tot}}(\text{V}_{\text{O}}) - E_{\text{tot}}(\text{no defect}) + \mu_{\text{O}}, \tag{2}
$$

where $E_{\text{tot}}(\text{no defect})$ is the total energy of the model with no defect, $E_{\text{tot}}(\text{V}_{\text{O}})$ is the total energy of the model with an oxygen vacancy, and $\mu_{\text{O}}$ is the chemical potential of an oxygen atom, which is equal to half of the total energy of an oxygen molecule. This state corresponds to O-rich conditions. A smaller oxygen vacancy formation energy calculated via Eq. (2) means an easier formation of the vacancy.

Fig. 2. Oxygen vacancy formation energy versus distance from Interface A.

A model with an oxygen vacancy was made by removing an oxygen atom from the IGZO complex model. All the atoms in the model were relaxed by first-principles calculations. $E_{\text{tot}}(\text{V}_{\text{O}})$ was calculated with the relaxed model. The complex model has 128 oxygen atoms, and thus 128 models with an oxygen vacancy were prepared. Each oxygen vacancy formation energy of the 128 models with an oxygen vacancy was calculated. Figure 2 shows the relationship between the formation energy and the distance of the vacancy site from Interface A. A crystalline region with a distance of approximately $-7\ \mathring{\text{A}}$ from Interface A shows an average oxygen vacancy formation energy of $2.6\text{eV}$. A positive formation energy means that an oxygen vacancy tends not to form spontaneously. In contrast, the oxygen vacancy formation energy is lower near the interface than in the crystalline region away from the interface and is negative in some cases. Therefore, oxygen vacancies tend to form closer to the interface. When an oxygen vacancy is formed in the crystalline region near the interface and then all the atoms in the complex model are relaxed, an oxygen atom in the amorphous region migrates in some cases to fill the oxygen vacancy. On the other hand, in the amorphous region, although there are some sites with formation energies that are the same as that in the crystalline region away from the interface, the average formation energy is $0.2\text{eV}$, which is lower than that in the crystalline region. Therefore, oxygen vacancies have a greater tendency to be generated in the amorphous region than in the crystalline region.

![](./images/814563294722392065_3.jpg)

Fig. 3. (Color online) Atomic displacement due to oxygen vacancy formation versus distance from Interface A. A closed rhombus indicates the displacement of an atom excluding an oxygen atom with a distance of $-7.8\,\text{Å}$ from Interface A, and an open square indicates the displacement of an atom excluding an oxygen atom with a distance of $6.2\,\text{Å}$ from Interface A.

To understand this finding, the displacement of each atom caused by atomic relaxation was calculated. Figure 3 shows a plot of the displacement of an atom caused by atomic relaxation versus the distance from Interface A to an atom. Each symbol in Fig. 3 corresponds to one atom in the model with an oxygen vacancy. A closed rhombus indicates the displacement caused by atomic relaxation for the model with an oxygen vacancy in the crystalline region with a distance of $-7.8\,\text{Å}$ from Interface A, and an open square indicates the displacement caused by atomic relaxation for the model with an oxygen vacancy in the amorphous region with a distance of $6.2\,\text{Å}$ from Interface A. A large displacement means that the atomic position is significantly changed by the oxygen vacancy formation. When an oxygen vacancy is formed in the crystalline region, the atomic displacement near the oxygen vacancy is approximately $0.2\,\text{Å}$, whereas the atomic displacement away from the oxygen vacancy is negligible. In contrast, the atomic displacement near the oxygen vacancy in the amorphous region is significantly larger than that in the crystalline region. Moreover, the atomic displacement for any atom in the amorphous region is equal to or larger than that in the crystalline region. Thus, hardly any atomic relaxation occurs when an oxygen vacancy is formed in the crystalline region. In contrast, relatively large atomic relaxations are present when an oxygen vacancy is formed in the amorphous region.

To analyze the correlation between the atomic relaxation and the distance from Interface A to the $j$-th oxygen vacancy site, the root sum square of displacements caused by atomic relaxation $(D_j)$ was calculated as

$$
D_j = \sqrt{\sum_{i\neq j}(\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{a},i} - \boldsymbol{r}_{\text{b},i})^2}, \tag{3}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{b},i}$ and $\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{a},i}$ are the position vectors of the $i$-th atom before and after relaxation, respectively. Larger $D_j$ indicates larger relaxation. Calculation for the data shown by closed rhombuses in Fig. 3 results in $D_j=0.9\,\text{Å}$, and calculation for the data shown by open squares in Fig. 3 results in $D_j=5.3\,\text{Å}$, underscoring the much larger relaxation in the amorphous region.

Figure 4 shows the relationship between the root sum square of displacements $(D_j)$ caused by atomic relaxation due to oxygen vacancy formation and the distance from Interface A to the oxygen vacancy site. The displacement is small in the crystalline region for a distance of approximately $-7\,\text{Å}$ from Interface A, and the displacement magnitude increases with decreasing distance to the interface. On the other hand, many sites with large displacements are present in the amorphous region away from the interface. This is probably because atomic relaxation tends not to occur in the crystalline region where atoms are located at positions with optimal bonding in a global minimum with a steep potential energy well, thus precluding large displacements. However, atomic relaxation tends to occur in the amorphous region (i.e., a region with low crystallinity) where the atoms are located in a local minimum with less optimized bonding because of a shallower potential energy surface, allowing for large atomic displacements.

Figure 5 shows the correlation between the displacement due to atomic relaxation and the formation energy. An oxygen vacancy site with a large displacement has a low formation energy. Thus, an oxygen vacancy tends not to form in a region with higher crystallinity where the system lacks the flexibility to accommodate changes in bonding owing to the removal of the oxygen atom.

Our results show that oxygen vacancies tend not to form in the crystalline region with high crystallinity but tend to form in the amorphous region with low crystallinity because atomic relaxation tends to occur in a region with low crystallinity.

### 3. Examination results and consideration of IGZO edge portion

The above-mentioned first-principles calculations for an oxygen vacancy reveal the tendency of an oxygen vacancy not to form in a film with high crystallinity. In this section, we investigate the existence of oxygen vacancies in an IGZO film by measurement. Note that an oxygen vacancy, however, cannot be directly observed because an oxygen vacancy means the absence of an oxygen atom. Therefore, we observed oxygen atoms in an IGZO film to verify the existence of oxygen vacancies.

![](./images/814563294722392065_6.jpg)

Fig. 6. (Color online) Observations of IGZO/SiOₓ interface. (a) HAADF-STEM and (b) ABF-STEM images.

Our previous report suggested the existence of oxygen vacancies in the bulk of single-crystal IGZO.²⁸) In this work, oxygen atoms at the film edge were observed to verify the existence of oxygen vacancies. To prepare the IGZO film edge, an IGZO film was deposited on an yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) substrate at 300 °C, crystallized by baking at 1200 °C, and then processed by dry etching into an island-shaped IGZO film. The edge of the film was then capped with SiOₓ and examined by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and annular bright-field STEM (ABF-STEM), with the results shown in Fig. 6. The intensity in HAADF-STEM [Fig. 6(a)] depends on the square of the atomic number; thus, indium exhibits the highest intensity, gallium and zinc exhibit the second highest intensity, and oxygen exhibits the lowest intensity. On the basis of its atomic number, the luminance of oxygen is estimated to be approximately 3% of that of indium; therefore, oxygen is difficult to observe. In contrast, because ABF-STEM [Fig. 6(b)] has a higher electron transmittance than HAADF-STEM, it is suitable for the observation of a light element, enabling the direct observation of oxygen atoms.²⁹)

In this study, the luminances of indium and oxygen over and below the indium layer in the part of the film from the center to the side edge of one layer were estimated using the ABF-STEM image shown in Fig. 6(b). The estimation procedure is as follows. The luminance profile of a certain indium layer in the ABF-STEM image of Fig. 6(b) was obtained, and the dip height in each column was derived from the luminance profile.³⁰) For each point with a corresponding distance from the interface between IGZO and SiOₓ shown in the graph, the luminance of oxygen around indium is normalized using the luminance of a nearby indium. The results are shown in Fig. 7. Although the reliability of the results is poor because of the effect of disordering at the interface between IGZO and SiOₓ, Fig. 7 shows that the reduction in the luminance of oxygen toward the interface is larger than that in the luminance of indium, suggesting the generation of oxygen vacancies. Consequently, oxygen vacancies tend to form at the side edge of the thin film than in the highly crystalline central region of the film.

### 4. Calculation results and consideration of IGZO edge portion

To explain the above results, the formation of oxygen vacancies in the surface and bulk regions of IGZO was investigated by first-principles calculations. A (010) plane and a (100) plane of a 112-atom crystalline IGZO model structure were extracted for use as model crystalline surfaces. Figure 8 shows the vicinity of the surface in each crystalline surface model. Both models have 224 atoms, and the thickness of the vacuum layer is 10 Å or more.

For both surface models, the oxygen vacancy formation energy gradually decreases from the bulk region toward the surface, showing that oxygen vacancies tend to form near the surface. In particular, in the (100) plane, a 2 eV oxygen vacancy formation energy difference is observed between the bulk and the surface regions. Although these findings reveal that oxygen vacancies tend to form in the surface region than in the bulk region in IGZO, the oxygen vacancy formation energy in the surface of the crystal is larger than 0.2 eV of the average formation energy in the amorphous region calculated using the IGZO complex model. Therefore, the formation of oxygen vacancies at the surface also tends to be greatly affected by crystallinity.

## 5. Conclusions
For the purpose of decreasing deep defect levels in an IGZO thin film, oxygen vacancies in an IGZO crystal were examined with first-principles calculations and observed by ABF-STEM. First, the relationship between crystallinity and oxygen vacancy formation in IGZO was examined by first-principles calculations. The results show that higher crystallinity inhibits vacancy formation due to the lesser structural flexibility and smaller atomic relaxation in response to the introduction of the vacancy. To investigate the actual distribution of oxygen vacancies, oxygen atoms at an edge portion of IGZO were directly observed by ABF-STEM. The results suggest that oxygen vacancies tend to form at smaller distances to the surface, indicating a tendency similar to that obtained from the first-principles calculation results.

Consequently, we conclude that oxygen vacancies tend not to form in CAAC-IGZO films that exhibit high crystallinity and can be fabricated on a large substrate at a low temperature; therefore, CAAC-IGZO films are suitable for use in displays, memories, and LSIs.
