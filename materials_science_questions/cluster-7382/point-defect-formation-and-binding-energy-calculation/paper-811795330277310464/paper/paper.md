# Connection between the formation volume and formation Gibbs energy in noble-gas solids

P. Varotsos and K. Alexopoulos
Department of Physics, University of Athens, Knossou Street 36,
Ano Glyfada, Athens, Greece
(Received 8 May 1984)

In the past the authors advanced a macroscopic model that connects defect parameters of solids with the bulk modulus. Recently, the formation volume of vacancies was measured by Macrander in solid krypton. The value agrees with that calculated from the macroscopic model in conjunction with the experimental enthalpy measured by Losee and Simmons and thus provides a rigorous test of the model.

In 1967, Losee and Simmons¹ determined the concentration of vacancies in Kr by simultaneously measuring a macroscopic length and the x-ray lattice parameter and applying the usual relation

$$
\frac{n}{N}=3 \frac{\Delta l}{l_{0}}-3 \frac{\Delta a}{a_{0}}=\exp \left(-\frac{g}{k T}\right), \quad(1)
$$

where $n$ is the number of vacant sites and $g$ the Gibbs formation energy per vacancy. In this experiment the directly measured quantity is the vacancy concentration $n/N$ and hence the value of $g$. From the temperature dependence of $g$ the following values of the formation enthalpy $h$ and the formation entropy $s$ were obtained:

$$
h=0.077 \pm 0.0087 \mathrm{\ eV}, \quad s=2.0_{-1.3}^{+1.9} k.
$$

The same values for $g$ were found by Korpiun and Coufal.² Macrander³ has recently carried out a very accurate constant-volume x-ray study of solid krypton. His results in conjunction with the measurements of Korpiun and Coufal have shown that within the experimental error the vacancy formation volume $v$ is equal to the mean volume per atom $\Omega$:

$$
v / \Omega=1.08 \pm 0.12.
$$

This means that the relaxation near the vacancy is very small unless it is cancelled by large relaxations of next-nearest-neighbor shells.

The above two independent experimental data allow a direct check of the validity of a model proposed by the authors⁴,⁵ that connects the formation Gibbs energy $g$ to bulk quantities

$$
g=c B \Omega \quad(2)
$$

where $B$ is the macroscopic isothermal bulk modulus, and $c$ is a constant independent of temperature and pressure. The derivation of Eq. (2) is based on the following two approximations which are both theoretically and experimentally justified.
(1) For a vacancy produced under constant (external) volume of the bulk crystal the frequencies of the neighboring atoms before $[\omega_{i}(V)]$ and after $[\omega_{i}'(V)]$ the vacancy creation are different; these frequencies, mainly depending only on the volume, do not change significantly with temperature (for $V=$ const) so that the corresponding isochoric formation entropy can be considered temperature independent.
(2) The formation volume $v$ may vary with pressure more than the bulk volume $V$; however, we can assume that the compressibility of the volume $v$ cannot exceed the bulk compressibility by more than a few times. A direct differentiation of Eq. (2), in respect to pressure, gives⁶

$$
v=c\left(\frac{d B}{d P}-1\right) \Omega. \quad(3)
$$

Therefore a combination of Eqs. (2) and (3) leads to

$$
g=B v \left/ \left(\frac{d B}{d P}-1\right). \right. \quad(4)
$$

Equation (4) has the advantage of containing quantities that are directly measurable. The quantity $g$, as mentioned, is directly known—without any fitting—from the experiment of Losee and Simmons, whereas the elastic data are now well documented by the recent experimental work of Anderson and Swenson,⁷ Birch,⁸ and Macrander.⁹ The quantity $v$ which has recently been reported by Macrander³ was the complementary quantity which was needed for a direct check of Eq. (4). It was highly desirable to be able to confine to this equation in view of the fact that microscopic theory does not provide³ reliable estimates of $g$.

In the present paper the reliability of Eq. (4) is checked for Kr. In Table I we have inserted all the necessary data. The calculated values of $g$ agree within experimental error with the experimental observations. In the same table we have inserted the corresponding values for Ar at 60 K. For this element, only upper limits of the vacancy concentration have been given by Schwalbe¹⁰ so that the agreement between the calculated value and the experimental value of $g$ for Ar cannot be considered as having equal importance as in the case of krypton.

We will now handle another result of the model based on Eq. (2): the defect entropy $s$ and enthalpy $h$ (in the region where experiments are carried out) are connected by⁴

$$
\frac{s}{h}=-\left(\beta B+\frac{d B}{d T}\right) b \left/ B_{0}^{\mathrm{SL}}, \right. \quad(5)
$$
