# INTERACTION BETWEEN POINT DEFECTS IN NONSTOICHIOMETRIC COMPOUNDS
J. B. LIGHTSTONE* and G. G. LIBOWITZ

Ledgemont Laboratory, Kennecott Copper Corporation Lexington, Mass. 02173, U.S.A.

(Received 5 June 1968; in revised form 29 November 1968)

Abstract - The variation in thermodynamic activity as a function of nonstoichiometry was derived for binary compounds exhibiting large deviations from stoichiometry. The assumptions were made that only one type of point defect is present in high concentrations, and that there are pair-wise interactions among the randomly distributed defects. It is shown that comparisons of experimentally determined activities with the theoretically derived relations may be used to deduce the type of point defect which is predominantly responsible for the nonstoichiometry, and to determine the free energy of interaction between the defects. Experimental hydrogen activities in yttrium and cerium hydrides, and thorium activities in thorium monocarbide are analyzed in this manner.

## INTRODUCTION

THE PRESENCE of point defects in a compound will in general imply a deviation from stoichio- metry, $\delta$, so that a compound whose stoichiometric formula is $MX_{s}$ may have a real composition which corresponds to the apparent formula $MX_{s \pm \delta}$. The positive sign is used when the compound has a higher $X/M$ ratio than the stoichiometric composition, and the negative sign is used when the $X/M$ ratio is less than the stoichiometric ratio. It has been shown in previous papers[1,2] that, neglecting electronic defects, for noninter- acting, randomly distributed atomic point defects, the functional dependence of the thermodynamic activity of one of the components (e.g. of $X$) on $\delta$ is characteristic of the types of point defects which are present. This characteristic dependence was used to identify the defects in $\mathrm{UH}_{3-\delta}, \mathrm{LaH}_{2+\delta}$ and $\mathrm{CeCd}_{6+\delta}$, which have moderately large deviations from stoichiometry in the 0.01-0.26 range.

For systems exhibiting relatively large deviations from stoichiometry, interactions between defects become important. In the present paper, the previous treatment[1] is extended to include the possibility of interaction between like defects. A comparison of the theoretically derived variation of activity with nonstoichiometry with that determined experimentally is then used to characterize the defect structure of various compounds, and also to provide information on the free energy of interaction between like defects.

## THEORETICAL TREATMENT

There are six possible types of simple point defects which may occur in a compound of stoichiometric formula $MX_{s}$. They are $M$ vacancies, $X$ vacancies, $M$ interstitials, $X$ interstitials, $M$ substitutionals, by which is meant $M$ atoms on normal $X$ sites, and $X$ substitutionals. The total free energy, $G$, of a crystal having an arbitrary defect structure may be written:
$$
G=G\left(N, N_{M(M)}, N_{X(X)}, N_{I M}, N_{I X}, N_{M(X)}, \begin{array}{c}
N_{X(M)}
\end{array}\right) \quad(1)
$$
where $N$ is the number of $M$ sites, $N_{M(M)}$ is the number of $M$ atoms on $M$ sites, $N_{I M}$ is the number of $M$ atoms in interstitial sites, and $N_{M(X)}$ is the number of $M$ atoms in substitutional positions. $N_{X(X)}, N_{I X}$ and $N_{X(M)}$ are the corresponding $X$ quantities.

An expression for the free energy, $G$, will be made up of four sets of terms: (1) the free energy of the ideal perfect crystal, which is

The dis- tribution of electrons and holes will be neglected. Therefore, the relation will apply only to systems where the defects are un- ionized or to metallic compounds where the distribution of electronic charge is un- important.

The present treatment, which is a modifica- tion and generalization of those used by Anderson[3] and Libowitz[4], is equivalent to a zeroth or Bragg-Williams approximation [5], where it is assumed that despite the interaction between the defects, the defects are nevertheless randomly distributed, so that the number of defect pairs is the random number of defect pairs that would obtain in the absence of any interaction, and the configura- tional entropy will be that for ideal mixing. We shall further only consider interaction between like defects.

& +\frac{z_{X}}{2} \frac{N_{M(X)}^{2}}{s N} \xi_{M(X)}+\frac{z_{M}}{2} \frac{N_{X(M)}^{2}}{N} \xi_{X(M)}

There are $s N X$ sites, and $\alpha N$ interstitial sites, so that $\alpha$ is characteristic of the crystal structure. $g_{\square M}$ is the change in free energy other than the contribution from configura- tional entropy when an $M$ atom is removed from the crystal to some standard state, such as vapor at 1 atm pressure, to form a vacancy. There are $(N-N_{M(M)}-N_{X(M)}) M$ vacancies and $(s N-N_{X(X)}-N_{M(X)}) X$ vacancies. $g_{I M}$ is the free energy of formation of an $M$ interstitial by addition of an $M$ atom to the crystal from the same standard state of $M$. $g_{M(X)}$ is the free energy of formation of an $M$ substitutional by removal of an $X$ atom from a normal $X$-site to $X$ in its standard state, and the addition of an $M$ atom to that site from $M$ in its standard state. $g_{\square X} g_{I X}$ and $g_{X(M)}$ are the corresponding $X$ quantities. The symbol $\xi$ is used to denote a pairwise free energy of interaction between like defects, so that $\xi_{\square M}$, for example, describes the pairwise free energy of interaction between $M$ vacancies. A positive value of $\xi$ implies a repulsion between defects, and a negative $\xi$ an attraction. $z_{M}$ is the number of nearest neighbor normal $M$ sites surrounding a given $M$ site. $z_{X}$ is the coordination number

of $X$ sites by $X$ sites, and $z_I$ is the coordination number of interstitial sites by interstitial sites.

The deviation from stoichiometry in the compound $M X_{s \pm \delta}$ is given in terms of the numbers of defects by the relation:

$$
s \pm \delta=\frac{N_{X(X)}+N_{X(M)}+N_{I X}}{N_{M(M)}+N_{M(X)}+N_{I M}} \tag{13}
$$

where the negative sign is used for negative deviations from stoichiometry.

It is therefore possible to compute the relationships between activity and $\delta$, using equations (3-13), and to compare the derived relationships with experimental data to obtain information about the defect structure. In order to characterize the system, it is sufficient to express the relations in terms of the activity of only one of the components. We will assume that the $X$ component is the more volatile, or the one whose activity is more easily measured. Therefore, the following discussion will be presented in terms of $a_{X}$. However, a similar treatment may be used for the $M$ component and $a_{M}$. We will consider only larger deviations from stoichiometry where only one type of defect is predominant and the concentrations of all other defects may be considered negligible. Therefore, by algebraic manipulation of equations (3-13), six relationships between $a_{X}$ and $\delta$ were derived, on the assumption that one kind of point defect alone contributes to the observed nonstoichiometry. It is convenient to discuss these relationships in terms of the general equation:

$$
y_{i}\left(a_{X}, \delta\right)=p_{i}\left(g_{i}\right)+q_{i}\left(\xi_{i}\right) x_{i}(\delta) \tag{14}
$$

where $i$ represents the type of defect assumed to be present in predominant concentration. $y_{i}$ is some function of $a_{X}$ and $\delta$, which is different for each type of assumed defect, $i$. $p_{i}$ is a constant, containing the formation energy, $g_{i}$, of the defect $i$. $q_{i}$ is another constant, containing the pairwise interaction energy, $\xi_{i}$, between $i$ defects, and $x_{i}$ is some function of $\delta$, also characteristic of the nature of the defect $i$. A plot of $y_{i}$ vs. $x_{i}$ should therefore yield a straight line if, and only if, we do in fact have $i$ type defects present. In this way the nature of the point defects present may be identified, and the free energy of interaction, $\xi_{i}$, may be determined from the slope of the line, $q_{i}$. ## COMPARISON WITH EXPERIMENTAL DATA
### Yttrium dihydride
The rare earth dihydrides exhibit very large deviations from stoichiometry, and in cases such as yttrium hydride, where the non-stoichiometry may be as high as 50 per cent $(\delta=1 \cdot 0)$, the simple relations[2] derived under the assumption of no interaction are not applicable. In this paper, equations (18-20) are applied to the activity data of Yannopoulos *et al.*[6] for negative deviations from stoichiometry in yttrium dihydride. Figure 1 shows graphs of $y_{i}$ as a function of $x_{i}$ (according to equation (14)) at $651^{\circ} C$ for various assumptions of the nature of the defect.

It can be seen that only for the case of hydrogen vacancies is a straight line relationship obtained. For yttrium interstitials, the plot of $y_{i}$ vs. $x_{i}$ shows a deviation from the straight line (indicated by the dashed curve) at lower values of $x_{i}$. In the case of $Y$ substitutionals the curvature is more pronounced. The inference, therefore, is that the non-stoichiometry in $Y H_{2-\delta}$ is, not unexpectedly, due to hydrogen vacancies.

In the plot of $y_{\square H}$ vs. $x_{\square H}$, the point at lowest $\delta$ does not fall on the straight line. Such deviations of the theoretical curve from the experimental points are to be expected at

The stoichiometric composition does not imply a perfect crystal, but rather that there are two opposite types of defects present in equivalent concentrations. (These are usually referred to as intrinsic defects.) Therefore, as the stoichiometric composition is approached, the concentration of the second type of defect becomes significant and the assumption that only one type of defect is present is no longer valid.

Here we recognize $N$ as a variable, obtaining equation (9) when $G$ is minimized with respect to it. The essential role of equa- tion (9) is that of providing a relationship between the activities of the two crystal components. The net result is that a different functional relationship between thermo- dynamic activity and deviation from stoichio- metry is obtained for each type of defect.

At very low deviations from stoichiometry, the interaction between defects can usually be neglected and the expressions involving activity and $\delta$ given in [1] are applicable. However, if the interaction energy is large, the interactions must be taken into consideration, particularly at larger values of $\delta$ as indicated by the last term in equations (15-20). This is illustrated in Fig. 7.

As mentioned on page 1030, the Bragg Williams random distribution assumption does not hold at very high deviations from stoichio- metry in the vicinity of a phase boundary. One would also expect the relationships derived in this study to be valid only for low values of $\xi / k T$. This condition is met by $Y H_{2}(\xi / k T \simeq$ $0 \cdot 3)$ and $\mathrm{CeH}_{2}(\xi / k T \simeq 0 \cdot 1)$. However, the fact that the equations are applicable to thorium monocarbide, where the calculated value of $\xi / k T$ appears to be 5 at $1000^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, is surprising.
