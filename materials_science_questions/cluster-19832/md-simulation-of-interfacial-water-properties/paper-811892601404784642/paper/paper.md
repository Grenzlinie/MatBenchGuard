## ABSTRACT

The nature of the surface forces, acting in the wetting films of pentane, hexane and heptane on water and on aqueous NaCl solutions is discussed. It is shown that an important contribution to the surface forces originates from the low solubility of water in short-chain alkanes. The isotherms of water adsorption, the disjoining pressure and the excess free energy were calculated for wide range of temperatures and salinities of underlying aqueous solution. To account for the screening action of ions in the aqueous solution on the image-charge interactions in alkane films we have solved the set of Poisson equations with the appropriate boundary conditions and within the Debye-Hückel approximation applied for the electrolyte phase. The analysis of the influence of temperature and salinity on different components of the disjoining pressure allows the conclusion that both factors shift the total isotherm onto the region of positive values resulting in the wetting transition. Such transitions for the range of temperatures and salinities studied were detected for pentane and hexane films only. Achievement of the wetting transition in heptane requires higher salinities and temperatures. The nature of wetting transitions from partial to complete wetting and from thin $\alpha$- to thick $\beta$-films was analyzed on the basis of Derjaguin -Frumkin theory taking into account the behaviour of the disjoining pressure and the excess free energy isotherms. It was shown that the surface forces, considered here, provide in the range of vapour undersaturation two sequential (partial-to-complete followed by thin-to-thick films) wetting transitions which combine at saturation into a single transition. For the oversaturated vapours only partial-to-complete wetting transition may be described.

## 1. Introduction

Three phase coexistence is ubiquitous in nature and many phenomena of scientific and technological importance are related to it. The equilibrium between a substrate and a liquid drop or between two semi-infinite media, separated by a thin liquid interlayer are examples of such coexistence. The peculiarities of equilibrium in the above cases can be used to characterize wetting phenomena in the system. Thus in an unsaturated vapour the thickness of liquid interlayer on the top of the substrate is indicative of the type of wetting, whereas for the case of supersaturated vapour the contact angle value should be used. For the systems with partial wetting the nonzero contact angle is characteristic, whereas zero contact angles are typical for complete wetting. The wetting of an air/water or air/aqueous solution interface by alkanes and some other organic liquids attracted the particular attention of researchers during the last decade and has been the subject of numerous studies [1-13]. Such interest was initiated, on one hand, by the fundamental importance of wetting phenomena in mixed organic/inorganic systems for various nanotechnological applications. On the other hand, wetting films of alkanes represent a convenient model system to assess the role of different types of surface forces in wetting phenomena.

While the nature of long-range forces is well understood and is associated with the van der Waals interactions, the physical insight into the origin of short-range forces in alkane wetting films still remains disputable [2,5,11,14,15]. Recently we have shown that the solubility of water in alkanes, although small, contributes essentially to the stability of nanosized wetting films of alkanes on a water surface [16,17]. It was demonstrated that the peculiarities of wetting behaviour and the temperature evolution of pentane, hexane or heptane on top of a water substrate can be considered to be the result of a joint action of two mechanisms of surface forces—van der Waals and image-charge interactions. The calculations of van der Waals forces were based on the exact Dzyaloshinsky, Lifshitz and Pitaevsky (DLP) equations [18] and using the detailed presentation of dielectric permittivity spectra of contacting phases. The detailed analysis allowed to show that van der Waals forces alone cannot account either for the experimentally observed equilibrium between alkane wetting film and alkane drop, nor for the equilibrium of thin alkane films with unsaturated vapour. At the same time invoking an additional—image-charge—mechanism, which takes into account the solubility of water in alkane and is related to the interaction of dipoles of water molecules in the adsorbed layer with their images in confining phases, provides a detailed description of all types of equilibrium. In particular, the temperature evolution of the contact angle as well as the wetting transition observed experimentally in a pentane/water system [19] can easily be understood.

Further experiments [20,21] showed that the wetting transition can be achieved not only for pentane on a pure water surface, but also for hexane on the surface of brine. The extension of our approach for the quantitative description of wetting behaviour of alkanes on the surface of electrolyte solution required, on one hand, solving the electrostatic problem of image-charge interactions accounting for screening action of ions in the electrolyte solution. On the other hand, for the calculations of van der Waals forces based on the exact DLP equations it was necessary to obtain information on the behaviour of dielectric permittivity spectra for electrolyte solutions in the ultra- violet region. These two issues will be analyzed in this paper. Another very important point to be addressed here is the nature of the wetting transition and of the thin-to-thick film transition taking place in both systems, that is pentane on water and hexane on sodium chloride solutions.

## 2. Surface forces in wetting films of alkanes on aqueous solutions

In general, the surface forces acting in a thin liquid interlayer are determined by its composition and structure [22,23]. The simplest case corresponds to one-component films of short-chain alkanes, confined by semi-infinite uncharged dielectric phases. Weak interac- tions, both between the liquid and confining phases and between alkane molecules inside the film, allow neglecting the deviation of structure and density of liquid in the film from the corresponding bulk characteristics. In this case the stability of alkane films will be determined by universal surface forces, which act in any system—that is, the van der Waals (molecular) forces.

For the systems considered in this paper—wetting films of short- chain alkanes on the surface of aqueous solutions—it is necessary to take into account the two-component composition of the film due to the solubility of water in alkanes. Although this solubility (of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ molar fraction) has only a minor influence on the van der Waals interactions, it causes the formation of adsorption layers, resulting in the appearance of additional surface forces, associated with the compositional non-uniformity of the liquid film.

The calculations of van der Waals forces might be rigorously performed on the basis of macroscopic DLP theory [18]. They have shown [18], that the molecular (van der Waals) component of the disjoining pressure, $\Pi_{m}(h)$, equal to the specific force of interaction between two macroscopic bodies through a uniform plane-parallel liquid interlayer having a thickness $h$, might be computed as:

$$

& \left.+\left[\frac{\left(s_{2}+a p\right)\left(s_{3}+b p\right)}{\left(s_{2}-a p\right)\left(s_{3}-b p\right)} \exp \left(\frac{2 p \xi_{N} h \varepsilon_{1}^{1 / 2}}{c}\right)-1\right]^{-1}\right\} d p,
\end{aligned}

$$

In the systems, analyzed in this paper, index 2 will correspond to the water or aqueous solutions, and index 3 to the air phase. (1) it is necessary to know the dependences $\varepsilon_{j}(i \xi_{N})$, for the representation of which two different approaches exist. The first one is based on the application of the Kramers-Kronig integral relations [24] and their modifications, defining the $\varepsilon_{j}(i \xi_{N})$ through the absorption spectrum for the whole range of frequencies. The second approach, proposed by Ninham and Parsegian [25] and referred to in the literature as the "multi- oscillator model" represents the whole absorption spectrum by a set of the most essential bands, associated with vibrations of some "effective oscillators". Usually for nonpolar media two bands, describing the absorption in the infrared and the ultraviolet regions are enough to represent the spectrum adequately [22,26]. Reliable results for polar media are obtained on the basis of a three oscillator representation, by adding the band corresponding to the microwave region. The analysis of computed data shows the following. At room temperature for the films on water surfaces for all three alkanes studied, weak van der Waals interactions changing from attraction at small film thicknesses to repulsion at higher thicknesses are characteristic. The increase of either temperature or the salt concentration shifts the thickness, corresponding to the change of the sign of van der Waals interactions, towards lower film thicknesses. It is worth noting that for all systems analyzed here the asymptotic behaviour $\Pi_{m}(h)=$ $A_{123}/6\pi h^{3}$, widely used in the literature for description of the van der Waals interactions, is not applicable due to a significant change of the effective $A_{123}$ value (and even of its sign) with variation of film thicknesses already at the nanoscale.

Let us now address the additional surfaces forces, related to the presence of dissolved water molecules in alkane films. The specific feature of the system under consideration is the polarity of the water molecule. Bearing the constant dipole moment, the water molecules polarize the phases, confining the alkane film giving rise to the additional surface forces. As we have shown earlier [30], in general, three different mechanisms of additional forces should be taken into account: 1) the interaction between diffuse adsorption layers; 2) correlation interaction between dense adsorption monolayers of dipole molecules formed at the opposite interfaces of wetting film, and 3) electrostatic interaction between adsorbed dipole molecules and their images in confining phases. The first mechanism might cause detectable forces at solute concentrations essentially greater [22,31] than the ultimate solubility of water in short-chain alkanes and thus is not relevant for systems considered in this paper.

Finally, the third mechanism, associated to the image- charge interactions between the adsorbed monolayer of polar molecules and its images in the confining phases, induces a notable contribution to the stability of wetting films even in the case of very dilute solutions [30].

To evaluate the role of the two latter mechanisms for the wetting behaviour of alkane wetting films on aqueous surfaces it was necessary to calculate the magnitude of the adsorption of water molecules at the opposite interfaces of the film at different temperatures and salt concentrations. Following the approach devel- oped in [30], for the surface coverage at 1-2 and 1-3 interfaces one can write:

$$
\frac{\Gamma^{(12)}}{\Gamma_{0}}=\frac{b^{(12)} x}{1+b^{(12)} x} ; \quad \frac{\Gamma^{(13)}}{\Gamma_{0}}=\frac{b^{(13)} x}{1+b^{(13)} x} ;
\tag{4}
$$

where $x$ is a molar fraction of water solution in alkane, $\Gamma^{(i j)}$ is the amount of water adsorbed at the $i-j$ interface, $\Gamma_{0}$ is the number of adsorption sites per unit of interfacial area,

$$

\tag{5}
$$

$$
b^{(13)}=\exp \left(\frac{-G_{a}^{(13)}}{\boldsymbol{k}_{B} T}\right)=\exp \left(-\frac{U_{m}+U_{i m}^{p}}{\boldsymbol{k}_{B} T}\right),
\tag{6}
$$

In Eqs. (5) and (6) it was taken into account that the hydrogen bonds are created only by the water molecules adsorbed at an alkane-aqueous substrate interface, while the energetically preferential orientation of dipoles at alkane-air interface corresponds to dipole moments parallel to the interface [32].

One of the most important questions arising here is: what is the orientation of the dipole moment of water molecules adsorbed at an alkane-aqueous solution interface? The answer to this question, on one hand, will determine the number of hydrogen bonds, which the adsorbed molecule can establish with water molecules in the underlying aqueous medium, that is, the contribution of the hydrogen bonding to the adsorption potential. On the other hand, this orientation also influences the magnitude of $U_{i m}^{n}$ and $U_{i m}^{p}$. There have been many experimental and theoretical studies on water at interfaces with low dielectric constant media, either vapour or oil. While there is no general agreement on the orientation of the water molecules at the interface, most recent calculations indicate that the orientations of the dipole moments of the interfacial water are not isotropic [33-36]. Our calculations were based on the results of the molecular dynamic simulations of a water-hydrocarbon system [36]. It was established there that closer to the hydrocarbon surface, water molecules inside the water phase preferentially align their O-H bonds normal to the interface. Having this in mind it was physically reasonable to assume that the water molecules adsorbed at the interface from the alkane phase have an orientation favouring hydrogen bonding with one O-H bond normal to the interface. In that case the total dipole moment of a water molecule forms an angle of about $52.23^{\circ}$ (that is, half of the internal H-O-H angle) with the normal to the interface. For the convenience of further calculations this dipole moment was decom- posed on two constituents, normal and parallel to the phase boundary. The corresponding contribution to the disjoining pressure might be computed as [30]:

$$
\Pi_{i m}=-\frac{d \Gamma^{(12)}}{d h}\left(U_{i m}^{n}+U_{i m}^{p}+U_{l a t}\right)-\frac{d\left(U_{i m}^{n}+U_{i m}^{p}+U_{l a t}\right)}{d h} \Gamma^{(12)}. \tag{12}
$$

The analysis of Eq. (12) jointly with the relations (4), (5), (7)-(11), shows that the contribution of the image forces to the total disjoining pressure decays faster than $1/h^{4}$, indicating essentially a shorter range of action of the image forces compared to that of van der Waals forces (see also Fig. 3). The quantitative comparison of contributions from the image-charge and the van der Waals forces indicates that at lower temperatures for alkane films with thickness of order of 1 nm namely the former provides the fulfillment of the condition of film stability

$$
\partial \Pi / \partial h<0, \tag{13}
$$

making it possible to establish an equilibrium of the wetting films with both the undersaturated vapours at positive disjoining pressures and the liquid droplets at negative ones. For thicker films, due to the short range character of image-charge interactions, the shape of the total isotherm of the disjoining pressure is determined mainly by the van der Waals forces (cf. the inset in Fig. 3). The influence of the salt concentration in the aqueous phase on the magnitude of the image- charge forces is realized mainly through the parameter $\tilde{\beta}_{12}$, which in the system under consideration varies from -0.95 for water to -1 for aqueous solutions with $N\geq 025$ mol l $^{-1}$, thus making the image-charge forces almost insensitive to the salinity. At the same time, the influence of the temperature on the image-charge forces is more pronounced, causing a moderate decrease of these forces with increasing temperature. According to Eq. (12) such behaviour is explained first of all by the fact that the adsorption itself, as well as its thickness derivative, varies with temperature due to both the increase of water solubility in alkanes and the alteration of the energy of dispersion interaction between dissolved water molecules and the phases confining the film.

Since for wetting films of all three alkanes studied in this paper, the van der Waals forces are much more sensitive to the variation of the temperature and the salinity, than image-charge forces, increase of the temperature and of the salt concentration leads to a reasonable shift of the isotherms of the total disjoining pressure to the region of positive pressures (Fig. 4), resulting in a gradual decrease of contact angle and finally in a transition from partial to complete wetting (Table 2).

### 3. The analysis of contact angles and wetting transitions

The physics of wetting and wetting transitions has been con- sidered in the literature from different points of view [see, for example, Refs. 2,5,11-17,22,38,39]. One might divide the multiplicity of approaches into two groups. In one group of methods the peculiarities of wetting are related to the correlation between the excess free energy of the system under consideration and the properties of interfaces separating the semi-infinite bulk phases or bulk phases and adsorbate. The interface is represented in terms of density profiles, intermolecular correlations at interfaces, orientation of molecules and so on [40]. The density profile can be either

![](./images/811892601404784642_4.jpg)

Fig. 4. The calculated isotherms of the total disjoining pressure for pentane wetting films on the surface of aqueous solutions with different salinities at 20 °C (a) and 40 °C (b). NaCI concentration: (from lower right to upper left lines): 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 and 3.0 mol/l.

continuous as is assumed in mean field approaches or a step function, characteristic to capillary wave models. In the first case the width of the transition region between two phases is proportional to the bulk correlation length and thus approaching the critical point of the system leads to a diverging correlation length and broadening of the transition zone. In the second case the increase of temperature leads to the error function representation of the density profile with a width that is system size dependent. This first group of methods can be considered as a microscopic approach since it operates with the details of intrinsic profiles between phases. The second group of methods, based on consideration of forces of interaction between two semi-infinite phases separated by thin liquid interlayer (surface forces), will be referred to as a macroscopic approach, because all the peculiarities of structure and composition of the constituents of the system are accounted through the magnitude and the character of surface forces. This powerful approach was developed in the Derjaguin-Frumkin theory of wetting phenomena (for a detailed description see Ref. [22]) and has been convincingly confirmed by modern density functional theory [14].

Derjaguin and Frumkin have considered the peculiarities of three phase equilibrium in the system containing a solid (liquid) substrate, abulk liquid meniscus (either a meniscus near the wall or a sessile drop) and a vapour phase. Traditionally the three phase equilibrium in such systems is characterized by a macroscopic contact angle $\theta_{0}$. Derjaguin has derived the relation between the equilibrium value of the contact angle $\theta_{0}$ and the isotherm of disjoining pressure $\Pi(h)$:

$$
\sigma_{L V} \cdot \cos \theta_{0}=\sigma_{L V}+\Pi\left(h_{0}\right) \cdot h_{0}+\int_{h_{0}}^{\infty} \Pi(h) d h, \tag{14}
$$

where $h_{0}$ is the thickness of the wetting film being in equilibrium with the meniscus of the bulk liquid or with the sessile drop, $\sigma_{L V}$ the surface tension of the liquid, and $\Pi(h_{0})$ the disjoining pressure in the wetting film, characterizing the combined action of different mechanisms [23] of surface forces. Recall that the disjoining pressure may be expressed through the relative vapour pressure, $p / p_{s}$, in the gas phase being in equilibrium with the wetting film as $\Pi=-\frac{k_{B} T}{v} \ln p / p_{s}$, where $v$ is the molecular volume, and $p_{s}$ is the equilibrium saturated vapour pressure above a flat surface of the bulk liquid. Since at equilibrium the disjoining pressure in the film should be equal to the capillary pressure in the meniscus or in the drop [22], undersaturated vapours (corresponding to positive disjoining pressure in the wetting film) can be in equilibrium with a concave meniscus only (for example, liquid in the capillary). The drop or convex meniscus are character- ized by negative capillary pressure (capillary pressure is determined as $P_{capil }=P_{air }-P_{liquid }$ [41]). Thus, the drops can be in the equilibrium with oversaturated vapour only. Certainly, the stable wetting film, which coexists with the drop and the oversaturated vapour, has a negative disjoining pressure.

The disjoining pressure $\Pi(h_{0})$ appearing in the middle term on the right hand side of Eq. (14) corresponds to the equilibrium value, maintained by external conditions, for example, by definite over- saturation of vapour above the sessile drop. Having in mind that typically experimentators deal with macroscopic drops, whose effective curvature and capillary pressure are close to zero (for spherical drops with effective radius of curvature $R$ the contribution of the term $\Pi(h_{0}) \cdot h_{0}$ into $\cos \theta_{0}$ is equal to $h_{0} / R$ , that is for nanometer thick films and millimeter drops amount to $10^{-6}$ ), usually in calculations this term is neglected and $h_{0}$ is assumed to be equal to the thickness, corresponding to zero disjoining pressure (as shown in Fig. 5), although, strictly speaking, the drop cannot be in equilibrium with saturated vapour at $p / p_{s}=1$ . It is necessary to underline one more advantage of the Derjaguin-Frumkin theory and Eq. (14). This equation predicts the dependence of contact angle on the drop size through its capillary pressure influencing both the second and third terms of Eq. (14). As is seen from the above analysis of the term $\Pi(h_{0}) \cdot h_{0}$ such dependence will be pronounced in the case of small drop radii. To elucidate the above points let us consider the evolution of a drop deposited on the substrate into the system with $p/p_s$=1. Various scenarios are possible. If the system is closed (vapour volume is fixed) then the equilibration of the drop will be accompanied by an increase of relative vapour pressure due to the drop evaporation and by a decrease of the capillary pressure in the drop. The equilibrium will be achieved when $P_{capil}=-\frac{k_B T}{v} \ln p/p_s$, that is the equilibrium drop shape and oversaturation are determined by such external conditions as volumes of the vapour phase and the drop. For an open system, or an infinite volume of the vapour phase, the drop will evaporate, leaving either a "dry" substrate or a thin film with thickness, corresponding to zero disjoining pressure, if such film is thermodynamically stable (see Eq. (13)). Finally, for "infinite" volume of the drop, the equilibration will lead to an infinitely thick film, again with zero disjoining pressure.

In a general case the total isotherm of the disjoining pressure contains short range and long range components. The contributions of long range components like molecular (van der Waals) or ion-electrostatic components demonstrate a relatively slow decay with increasing film thickness, whereas the short range components like steric, some kind of structural, image-charge forces play an important role for the stability of films with thicknesses of a few molecular diameters.

It was shown [22] that depending on the type of isotherm of disjoining pressure (see Fig. 5 for the simplest and most widespread forms), three types of equilibrium, meeting the condition of film stability (13), may be established for a drop (with finite volume) deposited onto a substrate: between the sessile drop and the dry substrate (characteristic for the isotherms of types 2 and 4), between the drop and the substrate covered by wetting or adsorption film (isotherm of type 3), and the coexistence of thin and thick films (isotherm of type 3 when the value of integral in Eq. (14) is positive). The first two types of equilibrium can be realized only for the case when the vapour pressure of the liquid is higher than the saturation pressure. The regime of wetting is determined by the sign and the magnitude of the last two terms in Eq. (14): a negative sign corresponds to partial wetting or nonwetting, and a positive one to complete wetting. And while for the isotherm 2 the sign definitely will be negative, for isotherms 3 and 4 the sign (for the macroscopic drops, when $\Pi_0 h_0 \cong 0$) is determined by the ratio of areas under the positive and negative branches of the isotherm of the disjoining pressure.

Another way to characterize the wetting behaviour in the system is to consider the equilibrium between an undersaturated vapour and a substrate. Depending on the type of isotherm and relative vapour pressure $p/p_s$ we may have the equilibrium coexistence of dry substrate with undersaturated vapour for the systems characterized by the isotherm 4 if $p/p_s < (p/p_s)_d$ and isotherm 2 if $p/p_s$<1. For $p/p_s > (p/p_s)_d$ and isotherm 4 there will be an equilibrium between undersaturated vapour and substrate covered by thick liquid film ($h \in [d',e]$). If the three phase system is associated with an isotherm of type 3, then for $p/p_s < (p/p_s)_d$ the coexistence of vapour and substrate, covered by thin $\alpha$-film ($h \in [a,b]$) will take place, whereas at $p/p_s > (p/p_s)_d$ two situations are possible-coexistence of the vapour with the $\alpha$-film or with the thick $\beta$-film ($h \in [d,e]$). Depending on the magnitudes of excess free energy of $\alpha$- and $\beta$-films one of the above situations will be related to the thermodynamic equilibrium state and another to the metastable state. Experimentally $\alpha$- and $\beta$-films have been detected for aqueous KCl solution films on mica and quartz [42] and it was found that metastable $\beta$-films ruptured to form $\alpha$-films. However if the energies of both states coincide or differ within thermal fluctuations one may observe films of both thicknesses simultaneously. Such type of coexistence of films with different thicknesses was demonstrated in numerical experiments [43] and reported for oleic acid on the interface between mercury and electrolyte solution [44].

Finally for the systems described by an isotherm of type 1, the wetting film being in equilibrium with undersaturated vapour grows with an increase of saturation and its thickness tends to infinity as $p/p_s \to 1$.

The variation of temperature in the system or of the physico-chemical parameters, characterizing one of the contacting phases (for example changing the salt concentration in the underlying aqueous substrate) leads to a change of the magnitudes and, in some cases, even of the signs of components of the disjoining pressure. As a result the alteration in the shape of the total isotherm of the disjoining pressure may cause not only contact angle variation but also the transition from thin to thick films or vice versa, if the energetic preference of the two states of films is changed. It is necessary to note that such a jump in the film thicknesses might be realized for the definite types of the isotherms of disjoining pressure. In the range of oversaturation of vapour pressure to provide such transition from thin to thick wetting films being in equilibrium with the drop one needs to have a quite complex shape of isotherm of disjoining pressure with at least two negative branches (see also the discussion of Fig. 8 below), which is not the case for the forces considered in our paper. In contrast for undersaturated vapour pressures the thin-to-thick film transition can be achieved in the systems characterized for example by the isotherm of type 3 with complete wetting and we will demonstrate that below with an example of hexane films on brine.

To apply the Derjaguin and Frumkin approach for the analysis of wetting behaviour of alkanes on water and aqueous solutions we have calculated the isotherms of the disjoining pressure for wetting films of pentane, hexane and heptane in the temperature range from 20 to 50 °C and NaCl concentrations between 0 and 3 mol/l. As it follows from the isotherms and from Table 2, for $p/p_s$>1 the partial wetting regime is characterized by equilibrium of the drop and the substrate, covered by thin wetting film with thickness of order of 2 nm which gradually increases with the contact angle diminishing. The wetting transition from nonzero to zero angles can be easily achieved for pentane wetting films on aqueous substrate by increasing either temperature or salt concentration. To obtain the same result for hexane films one needs to use joint action of increasing the temperature and the salinity. The complete wetting was detected only for 3 mol/l solution at $T \approx 45.5$ °C. Further growth of the temperature and the salinity is necessary to provide complete wetting for heptane films.

## 4. Discussion and concluding remarks

In the preceding sections we have analyzed the nature of surface forces, determining the stability of wetting films of pentane, hexane and heptane on water and on aqueous NaCl solutions. The isotherms of the disjoining pressure constituted by the van der Waals and the image-charge forces have been calculated on the basis of exact equations with the detailed representation of spectra of dielectric permittivities of contacting media. The screening action of ions in the underlying water substrate on the image-charge interaction of alkane thin film boundaries was considered by the solution of the set of Poisson equations with the appropriate boundary conditions and within the Debye-Hückel approximation applied for the electrolyte phase. It was found that many peculiarities of wetting [19,45] and possible types of equilibrium, established for the wetting films of all three alkanes studied are well reproduced by calculated isotherms. The analysis of the influence of the temperature and the salinity on different components of the disjoining pressure allows one to conclude that both factors shift the total isotherm into the region of positive values resulting in the wetting transition from partial to complete wetting. Such transitions for the range of temperatures and salinities studied were detected for pentane and hexane films only. Achievement of the wetting transition in heptane requires higher salinities and/or temperatures. The nature of wetting transitions from partial to complete wetting and from thin $\alpha$- to thick $\beta$-films was analyzed on the basis of Derjaguin -Frumkin theory taking into account the behaviour of the disjoining pressure and the excess free energy isotherms. For the description of the isotherms of disjoining pressure we have taken into account two types of surface forces, having a clear physical nature and relevant to the systems under consideration. It was shown that for the systems studied, the $\alpha$- to $\beta$-film transitions are first order transitions. The temperatures of the above transitions are not lower than the temperature of wetting transitions from partial to complete wetting and are affected by the value of disjoining pressure (relative vapour pressure).

It would be of great interest to quantitatively and qualitatively compare the predictions from our analysis with experimental data on wetting transitions in similar systems [19-21]. Unfortunately the quantitative comparison meets serious difficulties due to lack of important experimental details in the published papers. Thus, as was shown above the drops of liquid cannot be in equilibrium with saturated vapours, characterized by $p/p_s$=1. It means that in the closed experimental cell at the equilibrium some oversaturation should have been established. The description of experiments in [19-21] does not provide information either on the magnitude of this oversaturation nor on the way to control the chemical potential in the course of temperature variation. As for the qualitative explanation of the possibility to detect two sequential (thin-to-thick films followed by partial-to-complete) wetting transitions reported in the experiment, the surface forces considered in our analysis do not reproduce such a sequence. From the basis of the Derjaguin-Frumkin theory it follows that to provide a thin-to-thick film transition in the region of oversaturated vapours (equilibrium with the drop) the isotherm of disjoining pressure should have at least two negative branches corres- ponding to stable wetting films, for example to be like the isotherm A in Fig. 8. Let us consider the temperature evolution of the contact angle and film thickness at definite disjoining pressure in the range of small oversaturations. Then for the equilibrium between the drop and thermodynamically stable thin $\alpha$-film at some temperature $T_1$ one needs the simultaneous fulfillment of inequalities:

$$-S_{1}+S_{2}-S_{3}<0 \quad S_{2}<S_{3},\tag{15}$$

where $S_i$ are the areas under the different branches of the isotherm of disjoining pressure. The thick $\beta$-film will be metastable since $U_{\alpha}(h)=\int_{a}^{\infty} \Pi(h) d h<U_{\beta}(h)=\int_{b}^{\infty} \Pi(h) d h$. The increase of temperature will be accompanied by the shift of the isotherm towards the area of positive disjoining pressures due to, say, temperature behaviour of van der Waals forces. At temperatures higher than $\alpha$- to $\beta$-film transition temperature the inequalities (15) transform to:

$$-S_{1}<0 \quad S_{2}>S_{3},\tag{16}$$

providing the stability of thick $\beta$-film and metastability of thin $\alpha$- film. As was shown in the previous section the $\alpha$- to $\beta$-film transition is a first order transition. A further increase in the temperature may cause the transition of the total isotherm of disjoining pressure from type A in Fig. 8 to the type B, characterized by $\Pi_{h \rightarrow \infty}>0$ simultaneously with $\int_{c}^{\infty} \Pi(h) d h \geq 0$. The temperature of such a transition corresponds to the transition from partial to complete wetting. However such a change in the contact angle is accompanied by a continuous growth of $\beta$-film thickness, which due to the smooth behaviour of the stable negative branch of the isotherm of disjoining pressure may look like diverging of the film thickness.

Our calculations on the basis of exact DLP equations accounting for the total van der Waals contribution and with the experimentally determined set of parameters characterizing the systems under consideration indicate that the resulting isotherm of van der Waals forces corresponds not to the type A in Fig. 8, but to the type 4 in Fig. 5. The difference between these two types is related to the presence of a second negative and stable branch of the isotherm of disjoining pressure for thick films, which is necessary to provide the thin-to-thick film transition in the range of oversaturated vapour. So, to quantitatively reproduce the isotherm of type A in Fig. 8, it is necessary to supplement two mechanisms of surface forces considered here, namely short-range image-charge interactions due to water solubility and long range van der Waals interactions, with an additional long-range mechanism of surface forces. The analysis of surface forces of different types [23] indicates that electrostatic interactions, which are weakly temperature sensitive and very long ranged might be relevant. However, taking into account the absence of charges at oil/air interface the origin of such electrostatic interactions remains unclear. Moreover, among numerous experimental data on the stability of wetting alkane films we did not find any indication on such type of forces. We hope that new experimental data will shed light on the points considered here will appear soon.

### Appendix A. Electrostatic field induced in the dielectric film by single point charge and by a point dipole, located near the interface with an electrolyte solution

Our model system contains a film of a dielectric with thickness $h$ and dielectric permittivity $\varepsilon_{1}$, confined between two semi-infinite media with dielectric permittivities $\varepsilon_{2}$ and $\varepsilon_{3}$, as shown in Fig. 9. The medium 2 represents an electrolyte solution, while two others are apolar. The point charge $q$ is located in the film at distance $d$ above the 1-2 interface, and we chose the Cartesian coordinate system with the origin coinciding to the charge location and $z$ axis normal to the interface. The electrostatic field induced by point charge is screened in the electrolyte solution due to a cloud of soluted salt ions, and is affected by the polarization of phase boundaries. Within the Debye-Huckel approximation (DHA), valid for moderate charges, the counterion density is proportional to the screened electrostatic potential inside the solution. Thus the electrostatic potential in all contacting media is described by the following set of Poisson-Boltzmann equations:

$$
\begin{cases}
\Delta \varphi^{(1)}=-\frac{4 \pi}{\varepsilon_{1}} q \delta(x, y, z) & -d<z<-d+h \\
\Delta \varphi^{(2)}-\kappa^{2} \varphi^{(2)}=0 & z<-d \\
\Delta \varphi^{(3)}=0 & z>-d+h
\end{cases} \tag{A1}
$$

where $\delta(x, y, z)$ is the Dirac's delta-function, $\kappa=(n_{0} 8 \pi e^{2} / \varepsilon_{2} k T)^{1 / 2}$ is the inverse Debye length, $n_{0}$ is the mean salt ion density in bulk electrolyte solution, $e$ is the elementary charge, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the temperature.

By virtue of the cylindrical symmetry of the problem, it is expedient to use a 2D Fourier transform for $x$ and $y$ axes, which leads to the system of ordinary differential equations

$$
\begin{cases}
\frac{d^{2} f^{(1)}(z)}{d z^{2}}-\lambda^{2} f^{(1)}(z)=-\frac{2 q}{\varepsilon_{1}} \delta(z) & -d<z<-d+h \\
\frac{d^{2} f^{(2)}(z)}{d z^{2}}-\left(\kappa^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right) f^{(2)}(z)=0 & z<-d \\
\frac{d^{2} f^{(3)}(z)}{d z^{2}}-\lambda^{2} f^{(3)}(z)=0 & z>-d+h
\end{cases} \tag{A2}
$$

with boundary conditions

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
z \rightarrow \infty & f^{(3)} \rightarrow 0 & a \\
z \rightarrow-\infty & f^{(2)} \rightarrow 0 & b \\
z=h-d & f^{(1)}=f^{(3)} & c \\
z=h-d & \frac{\varepsilon_{1} \partial f^{(1)}}{\partial z}=\frac{\varepsilon_{3} \partial f^{(3)}}{\partial z} & d \\
z=-d & f^{(1)}=f^{(2)} & e \\
z=-d & \frac{\varepsilon_{1} \partial f^{(1)}}{\partial z}=\frac{\varepsilon_{2} \partial f^{(2)}}{\partial z} & f.
\end{array} \tag{A3}
$$

Solving the above boundary problem we find the Fourier transform of the potential in the film

$$
f^{(1)}(z)=\frac{q}{\lambda \varepsilon_{1}} \frac{\exp (-\lambda|z|)+\beta_{13} \exp [\lambda(z-2(h-d))]+\beta_{12}^{\prime} \exp [-\lambda(z+2 d)]+\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13} \exp [-\lambda(2 h-|z|)]}{1-\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13} \exp (-2 \lambda h)} \tag{A4}
$$

where $\beta_{13}=(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{3}) /(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{3})$;

$$
\beta_{12}^{\prime}=\left(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2} \sqrt{1+\kappa^{2} / \lambda^{2}}\right)\left(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2} \sqrt{1+\kappa^{2} / \lambda^{2}}\right). \tag{A5}
$$

By using the identity

$$
\frac{1}{1-\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13} \exp (-2 \lambda h)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp (-2 k \lambda h), \tag{A6}
$$

we can represent (A4) as a sum of contributions from the charge itself and its sequential images in confining phases:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{(1)}(z)= & \frac{q}{\lambda \varepsilon_{1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\{\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp [-\lambda(|z|+2 k h)\right. \\
& +\beta_{12}^{\prime}\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp [-\lambda(z+2 d+2 k h)] \\
& +\beta_{13}\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp [-\lambda(-z-2 d+2(k+1) h)] \\
& \left.+\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k+1} \exp [-\lambda(2(k+1) h-|z|)]\right\}
\end{aligned} \tag{A7}
$$

To compute the potential distribution across the film, one needs to perform the inverse 2D Fourier transform of Eq. (A7).

![](./images/811892601404784642_10.jpg)

Fig. 10. Parameter $\beta_{12}$ as a function of $\lambda$ for different values of inverse Debye length $\kappa$: $10^{4}(1)$, $10^{5}(2)$, $10^{6}(3)$, $10^{7}(4)$ and $10^{8}\ \text{cm}^{-1}(5)$. Calculations were performed for $\varepsilon_{1}$=2;and $\varepsilon_{2}$=80.

Due to cylindrical symmetry it reduces to the Hankel transform:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi^{(1)}(\rho, z) & =\int_{0}^{\infty} f^{(1)}(\lambda, z) J_{0}(\rho \lambda) \lambda d \lambda & & \text { (A8) } \\
& =\frac{q}{\varepsilon_{1}} \int_{0}^{\infty} J_{0}(\rho \lambda) d \lambda \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\{\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp [-\lambda(|z|+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)]\right. & & \\
& +\beta_{12}^{\prime}\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp [-\lambda(z+2 d+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)] & & \\
& +\beta_{13}\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \exp [-\lambda(-z-2 d+2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h)] & & \\
& \left.+\left(\beta_{12}^{\prime} \beta_{13}\right)^{k+1} \exp [-\lambda(2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h-|z|)]\right] & &
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\rho=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{1 / 2}$ and $J_{0}(\rho \lambda)$ is the zero-order Bessel function of the first kind.

In the general case due to dependence of $\beta_{12}^{\prime}$ on $\lambda$ (cf. Eq. (A5)), the integral in Eq. (A8) cannot be expressed in analytical functions and has to be evaluated numerically. However, taking into account the fact that this integral is determined mainly by those values of wave vector $\lambda$, for which the exponential terms in the integrand are close to unity, the further simplification of (A8) might be based on the peculiarities of function $\beta_{12}^{\prime}(\lambda)$ (Fig. 10).

Note that we are dealing with the case of dielectric film on the electrolyte substrate, so that $\varepsilon_{2}>\varepsilon_{1}$. For this case $\beta_{12}^{\prime}(\lambda)$ monotonically varies between -1 for $\lambda \ll \kappa$ and $\beta_{12}=(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}) /(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})$ for $\lambda \gg \kappa$, with a rather marked switch between the two asymptotic regimes at $\kappa \approx \lambda$.

The simplest for the analysis is the case when $\varepsilon_{2} \gg \varepsilon_{1}$. Then it is possible to put $\beta_{12}^{\prime}=-1$, and the integration of Eq. (A8) gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi^{(1)}(\rho, z) & =\frac{q}{\varepsilon_{1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(-\beta_{13}\right)^{k}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(|z|+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(z+2 d+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)^{2}}}\right. \quad \text { (A9a) } \\
& \left.+\frac{\beta_{13}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+[-z-2 d+2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h]^{2}}}-\frac{\beta_{13}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+[2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h-|z|]^{2}}}\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same result is valid when the inequality
$$2(h-d) \kappa \geq 1 \quad \text { (A10) }$$
holds. The condition (A10) is the consequence of the simultaneous requirements for smallness of arguments of exponential functions in (A8) and for holding of the inequality $\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2} / \lambda^{2}} \gg 1$ in (A5). Evidently, the condition (A10) for highly concentrated solutions is satisfied already at small film thicknesses and thus might be applied for the analysis of electrostatic potentials and interaction forces in the case of polymolecular wetting films. At the same time, the lowering of electrolyte concentration reduces the range of film thicknesses for which the relation (A9a) might be considered as a sufficiently close approximation.

Another asymptotic limit represents the case of thin films on the surface of diluted electrolytes with $2 \kappa h \ll 1$, when simultaneously for the entire range of $\lambda$ values, essential for the integral (8), the relation $\lambda \gg \kappa$ holds. One may then put
$$\beta_{12}^{\prime} \approx\left(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\right) /\left(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\right)=\beta_{12} \quad \text { (A11) }$$
and the integration of Eq. (A8) gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi^{(1)}(\rho, z) & =\frac{q}{\varepsilon_{1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\beta_{12} \beta_{13}\right)^{k}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(|z|+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)^{2}}}+\frac{\beta_{12}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(z+2 h+2 \boldsymbol{k} d)^{2}}}\right. \quad \text { (A9b) } \\
& \left.+\frac{\beta_{13}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+[-z-2 d+2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h]^{2}}}+\frac{\beta_{12} \beta_{13}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+[2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h-|z|]^{2}}}\right\}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note by the way that tending film thickness in (A9b) to infinity leads to the well-known equation for the electrostatic field created by a point charge located near the interface between two semi-infinite dielectrics:
$$\varphi^{(1)}(\rho, z)=\frac{q}{\varepsilon_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+|z|^{2}}}+\frac{\beta_{12}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(z+2 d)^{2}}}\right) \quad \text { (A12) }$$

Thus for the potential of the electrostatic field induced in the dielectric film by single point charge, located near the interface with electrolyte solution one might write
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi^{(1)}(\rho, z) & =\frac{q}{\varepsilon_{1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\tilde{\beta}_{12} \beta_{13}\right)^{k}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(|z|+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)^{2}}}+\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{12}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(z+2 d+2 \boldsymbol{k} h)^{2}}}\right. \quad \text { (A13) } \\
& \left.+\frac{\beta_{13}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+[-z-2 d+2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h]^{2}}}+\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{12} \beta_{13}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+[2(\boldsymbol{k}+1) h-|z|]^{2}}}\right\}
\end{aligned}
$$
with
$$\begin{array}{ll}
\tilde{\beta}_{12}=\beta_{12} & \text { for } \quad 2 \kappa(h-d) \ll 1 \\
\tilde{\beta}_{12}=-1 & \text { for } \quad 2 \kappa(h-d) \gg 1.
\end{array} \quad \text { (A14) }$$

Writing Eq. (A13) for negative and positive point charges and using the principle of superposition of electric fields, after some algebra we obtain the equation for the intensity, $\mathbf{E}$, of electric field induced in the film by a point dipole with dipole moment $\mathbf{p}$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{E}^{(1)}(X, Y, Z) & =\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{l=1}^{4}\left(\frac{3 \mathrm{~b}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}\left(\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}\right) \mathbf{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}}{\mathrm{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}^{5}}-\frac{b_{l \boldsymbol{k}} \mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}^{3}}\right)- \\
& -\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{l=2}^{3}\left(\frac{3 \mathrm{~b}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}\left(\mathbf{p} \cdot \boldsymbol{k}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}\right)}{\mathrm{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}^{5}}-\frac{b_{l \boldsymbol{k}}(p \cdot \boldsymbol{k}) \boldsymbol{k}}{\mathrm{r}_{l \boldsymbol{k}}^{3}}\right)
\end{aligned} \quad \text { (A15) }
$$
where $\boldsymbol{k}$ is the unit vector along the $z$ axis, and the following notations are used
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbf{r}_{1 \boldsymbol{k}}(X, Y, Z)=\left[X, Y, Z_{1 \boldsymbol{k}}=(2 \boldsymbol{k} h+|Z|)\right] \\
& \mathbf{r}_{2 \boldsymbol{k}}(X, Y, Z)=\left[X, Y, Z_{2 \boldsymbol{k}}=(2 \boldsymbol{k} h+2 d+Z)\right] \\
& \mathbf{r}_{3 \boldsymbol{k}}(X, Y, Z)=\left[X, Y, Z_{3 \boldsymbol{k}}=(2 \boldsymbol{k} h+2 h-2 d-Z)\right] \\
& \mathbf{r}_{4 \boldsymbol{k}}(X, Y, Z)=\left[X, Y, Z_{4 \boldsymbol{k}}=(2 \boldsymbol{k} h+2 h-|Z|)\right]
\end{aligned} \quad \text { (A16) }
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
& b_{1 \boldsymbol{k}}=\left(\tilde{\beta}_{12} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \\
& b_{2 \boldsymbol{k}}=\tilde{\beta}_{12}\left(\tilde{\beta}_{12} \beta_{13}\right)^{k} \\
& b_{3 \boldsymbol{k}}=\beta_{13}\left(\tilde{\beta}_{12} \beta^{13}\right)^{k} \\
& b_{4 \boldsymbol{k}}=\left(\tilde{\beta}_{12} \beta_{13}\right)^{k+1}.
\end{aligned} \quad \text { (A17) }
$$

It can be easily seen that the expression (A15) for $\mathbf{E}^{(1)}$ coincides with that derived earlier [27] for the intensity of electric field induced by a point dipole in the dielectric film, confined by two insulators, with $\beta_{12}$ replaced by $\tilde{\beta}_{12}$. Thus, within the approximations concerning the peculiarities of behaviour of function $\beta_{12}'(\lambda)$, all the analysis of image-charge forces, performed in [30] holds in the case of films on the surface of electrolyte solutions considered in this paper.
