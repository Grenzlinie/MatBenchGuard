## ABSTRACT:
The dielectric properties of the polar solvent on the protein-solvent interface at small intercharge distances are still poorly explored. To deconvolute this problem and to evaluate the pair-wise electrostatic interaction (PEI) energies of the point charges located at the protein-solvent interface we used a nonlocal (NL) electrostatic approach along with a static NL dielectric response function of water. The influence of the aqueous solvent microstructure (determined by a strong nonelectrostatic correlation effect between water dipoles within the orientational Debye polarization mode) on electrostatic interactions at the interface was studied in our work. It was shown that the PEI energies can be significantly higher than the energies evaluated by the classical (local) consideration, treating water molecules as belonging to the bulk solvent with a high dielectric constant. Our analysis points to the existence of a rather extended, effective low-dielectric interfacial water shell on the protein surface. The main dielectric properties of this shell (effective thickness together with distance- and orientation-dependent dielectric permittivity function) were evaluated. The dramatic role of this shell was demonstrated when estimating the protein association rate constants. © 2007 Wiley Periodicals, Inc.

In the present work, we analyzed dielectric properties of the aqueous solvent at the protein-solvent interface in the framework of the macroscopic continuum electrostatic model, which is a convenient and widely used methodol- ogy. $^{17-20,23,27,28}$ This model considers the dipole polarization of a volume element of a medium rather than the polariza- tion of each atom. In the framework of this model, a protein medium can be presented as a uniform, low-dielectric region(with the dielectric constant within an interval of 2 and 4) surrounded by a solvent of high dielectric constant $(\sim 80)$ . In the present work, such a model is referred to as a classical(CL) model.

More sophisticated microscopic and semi-microscopic models consider the solvent as a system of rotated Langeven dipoles that interact electrostatically and present a polariza- tion $P(r)$ at point $r$ as a static quantity. $^{15,21,22,27}$ In these models, the macroscopic electric field $E(r)$ in each point $r$  determines the polarization at the same point (linear local relationship: $P(r)=\chi(r) E(r)$ , where $\chi(r)$ is electric suscepti bility). These models can be viewed as local space models presenting a particular case of the dielectric response for thehomogeneous dielectric medium. $^{29}$

In a more general case, the linear dielectric response for infinite homogeneous dielectric media can be presented by an integral (or nonlocal (NL)) relationship between the elec- tric field and polarization. $^{29}$ This relationship is required for studying the electric fields that are changing on the characteristic molecular length-scale of a condensed medium. $^{29-34}$  In contrast to the local relationship, the NL relationship assumes that the polarization $P$ at point $r$ is determined not only by the value of the field $E$ at the point $r$ , but also by the values of this field at the points $r^{\prime}$ located in a small region surrounding the point $r$ . This constitutive relationship is considered in the so called, NL electrostatic approach, which can be used along with a phenomenological model of the po- lar solvent (PMPS) for studies of interfacial and noninterfa- cial condensed matters (see cited Ref. 31). This paradigm(electrostatics of media with spatial dispersion) is an effective tool for the evaluation of the short-range EI in the condensed polar media and allows one to introduce a microstructure of the condensed polar media through dielectric response func- tions determined at the length-scales of the bound chargedensity fluctuations. $^{30,31,35-38}$

In PMPS, a position- and time-dependent fluctuating polarization $P(r, t)$ is considered as the dynamic variable. In contrast to the local models, PMPS allows one to take into account the set of specific polarization modes that are typical for water with inherent distinguished frequencies of motions and specific space correlations. $^{30,35}$ As a result, polarization solvent effects are associated not only with the rotation of the water dipoles in the electric field (as it is in the local models), but also with their frequency-dependent, strong correlation effects. This is because each type of polarization is correlated in space (with some effective correlation radius) due to strong nonelectrostatic interactions. $^{30-32,35-37,39-41}$ In PMPS, the effective dielectric function of the bulk phase of the sol- vent is a result of the averaging of all possible (long- and short-range) interactions that take place in a medium under- going an external electric field, while in the microscopic models these interactions are very difficult to fully take into account. When the effective dielectric function of an infinite dielectric medium is known, solving an electrostatic problem in the framework of electrodynamics of media with spatialdispersion is undemanding. $^{31}$

In the case of the system with an interface, in particular, the system that includes a macromolecule surrounded by a solvent with spatial dispersion of dielectric permittivity, cal- culation of the electrostatic field near the interface is far from straightforward (even if the corresponding dielectric function of the bulk solvent is known). $^{31}$ Solving an electrostatic prob lem for this system require the consideration of the integral- differential equations at the interface, while the classical(local) models require the solution of the Poisson or Poisson-Boltzmann equations. $^{15,17,20,31}$

Different theoretical approaches reveal the heterogeneous nature of dielectric properties of the protein at the interface. However, the results obtained by these approaches are not simply reconciled with each other and some of them are dif- ficult to interpret. $^{3,31}$ On the other hand, so far, the heteroge neous nature of the dielectric properties of the aqueous sol- vent at the protein-solvent interface is not considered with due care. This is because the screening effects of the interfa- cial water, especially at short inter-ion distances, are not gen- erally known and, therefore, are not reproduced accurately incontinuum electrostatics. $^{6,13,18,27,42,43}$

Recently, we implemented the NL electrostatic approach to evaluate the influence of the solvent structure on the pair- wise electrostatic interaction (PEI) energy in protein at the model protein-solvent interface. $^{31}$ In that work, the solvent structure was introduced by the characteristic spatial scale(~ 5 Å) at which the bound charge density fluctuations are correlated in solvent by the nonelectrostatic interactions within the orientational Debye polarization mode. $^{35,40} We$

This effect was associated with a low-dielectric solvent layer arising on the interface. These results suggest that the strong nonelectrostatic correlation effect between water dipoles is a crucial factor that determines the spatial heterogeneity of the dielectric properties and peculiarities of the EI screening in the protein at the protein-solvent interface.³¹

In the present work, the NL approach was used to estimate the influence of the solvent structure (determined in the same way as it was done in our previous work³¹) on the EI in solvent in the vicinity of the protein-solvent interface and to characterize the dielectric properties of the interfacial water on the protein surface. We evaluated the PEI energy of the point charges located in solvent in close proximity to the protein interface as well as a cross-media pair-wise electrostatic interaction (CPEI) energy between two point charges, when one charge is located in solvent and the other one in protein. The effects of the electrolyte solution associated with Debye–Hückel screening by the mobile counter-ions were not considered in the present work. In accordance with the classical theory of dielectrics, a protein was considered as the uniform low-dielectric medium. The corresponding solution of the electrostatic problem at the interface was obtained by integral-differential equations with the usual boundary conditions. General integral expressions for both PEI and CPEI energies were obtained in the form of Coulomb's laws with effective distance-dependent dielectric functions.

The NL approach allowed us: (i) to find the dielectric permittivity function of the solvent in the vicinity of the protein-solvent interface, (ii) to reveal a significant difference in the behavior of this function from its behavior in the bulk solvent phase, and (iii) to estimate how far from the surface this difference persists. Overall, the performed asymptotic and numerical analyses of the PEI and CPEI energies allowed us to reveal the existence of a rather extended, effective low-dielectric interfacial water shell on the protein-solvent interface, which cannot be found by the continuum Poisson–Boltzmann model. We evaluated the main dielectric properties of this shell (its effective thickness, as well as distance-and orientation-dependent dielectric permittivity). We have shown that, in the vicinity of the protein interface, the PEI and CPEI are much stronger than the interactions estimated by the CL solvent model based on the Poisson–Boltzmann equations.

As an application of the proposed approach, we estimated the contribution of the favorable EI to the association rate constant for an idealized model system of two protein subunits. The obtained estimation is consistent (by orders-of-magnitude) with experimental data published in the literature and is approximately two orders of magnitude higher than the estimations performed by CL solvent consideration. The presented analysis of this model system allows one to better understand how the interfacial water on the protein surface can govern the long distance preorientation of interacting molecules, accompanying their collision to form macromolecular complexes.

## METHODS

### Nonlocal Electrostatic Approach

To calculate the PEI and CPEI energies at the model protein-solvent interface, the NL electrostatic approach was used (see cited Ref. 31). The linear dielectric response for each of the media in contact was presented by the NL integral relationship between the electric induction $\mathbf{D}$ and the electric field $\mathbf{E}^{29}$:

$$
\mathbf{D}_{m,\alpha}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{\beta} \int_{V_m} \varepsilon_{m,\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}') \mathbf{E}_{m,\beta}(\mathbf{r}') \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}', \quad \alpha, \beta = x, y, z \tag{1}
$$

where $m = 1, 2$ is the number of the medium; the function $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m},\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}')$ is the dielectric permittivity tensor that is determined by the spatial correlation of the polarization of the medium in the space (instead of a single dielectric constant utilized in the classical models); and the integration is taken over the volume $V_m$ of the medium.

A simple case of the planar dielectric boundary, which models the local regions of the interface between protein and solvent, was considered in the cylindrical coordinate system $(R, Z)$. In this system, $Z$ is the axis perpendicular to the plane passing through the boundary, and $R$ is the two-dimensional (2D) radius vector in this plane. The semi-infinite region $Z < 0$ was assigned for a protein-like medium, while the region $Z > 0$ was assigned for a solvent with an immersed charge, $Q_1 = \xi_1 e$ ($\xi_1$—part of the electron charge $e$) located at the $(0, Z_0)$ point.

The so-called "specular reflection approximation" model for the system of two semiinfinite media was used.³⁰⁻³²,³⁶ In this approximation, properties of the media along the plane of the boundary are considered as homogeneous isotropic with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m},\alpha\beta} (\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{m},\alpha\beta} (\mathbf{Z}, \mathbf{Z}', \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{R}')$ that can be expressed through the bulk dielectric function.

To solve the electrostatic problem, the first Maxwell's equation, together with Eq. (1) and the usual boundary conditions, was considered. The electrostatic potentials in any $(R, Z)$ point, $\varphi_1$ ($R, Z, Z_0$) in the region $Z < 0$ and $\varphi_2$ ($R, Z, Z_0$) in the region $Z > 0$ (both created by the charge $Q_1$ $(0, Z_0)$), were presented in terms of the longitudinal Fourier-transform $\varepsilon_1(k)$ and $\varepsilon_2$ $(k)$ of the dielectric functions $\varepsilon_1 (\mathbf{r-r}')$ and $\varepsilon_2 (\mathbf{r-r}')$, which present the bulk dielectric properties of the protein-like medium and solvent, respectively:³⁰,³¹,³⁸,⁴⁴

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_m(R,Z,Z_0) &= (2\pi)^{-1} \int_{0}^{+\infty} \mathrm{d}KK \, J_0(KR) \, \varphi_m(K,Z,Z_0), \\
& \quad \quad \quad \quad Z_0 > 0, \quad m=1,2 \tag{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_{1}\left(K, Z, Z_{0}\right)= & 4 Q_{1} G(K) \\
& \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \mathrm{d} K_{Z} \exp \left(\mathrm{i} K_{Z} Z\right) /\left[k^{2} \varepsilon_{1}(k)\right], \quad Z<0, \quad(3)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_{2}\left(K, Z, Z_{0}\right)=4 Q_{1} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} & \mathrm{d} K_{Z} \exp \left(\mathrm{i} K_{Z} Z\right)\left\{\cos \left(K_{Z} Z_{0}\right)\right. \\
& -G(K)\} /\left[k^{2} \varepsilon_{2}(k)\right], \quad Z>0, \quad(4)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
G(K)=A(K) /[B(K)+C(K)],
$$

$$
A(K)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \mathrm{d} K_{Z} \cos \left(K_{Z} Z_{0}\right) /\left[k^{2} \varepsilon_{2}(k)\right],
$$

$$
B(K)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \mathrm{d} K_{Z} /\left[k^{2} \varepsilon_{1}(k)\right], C(K)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} d K_{Z} /\left[k^{2} \varepsilon_{2}(k)\right],
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}

& \int_{V m} \mathrm{~d}\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right) \exp \left[-\mathrm{i} \mathbf{k}\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right)\right] \varepsilon_{m, \alpha \beta}\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

and $J_{0}(\mathrm{KR})$ is the Bessel function of the first kind.

# Protein-Like Medium and Solvent Dielectric Functions, $\varepsilon_{1}(k)$ and $\varepsilon_{2}(k)$

The protein-like medium was considered as a uniform dielectric with a low dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{1}(k)=\varepsilon_{1}=4$ to account for the effect of small dipolar fluctuations in proteins.

In the bulk of the aqueous solvent, the dielectric function $\varepsilon_{2}(k)$, was introduced in the framework of the PMPS theory developed for the polar solvents $^{35,40,45}$ :

$$
\varepsilon_{2}(k)=\varepsilon_{*}+\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}}-\varepsilon_{*}\right) /\left[1+(L k)^{2} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}} / \varepsilon_{*}\right]
$$

where $\varepsilon_{*}=6$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}}=78.3$ are short- and long-wavelength dielectric constants of the solvent at room temperature; $L$ is the correlation length of the water dipoles and is proportional to the characteristic length $(\sim 3-5 \AA)$ of the hydrogen-bonding network of water molecules. The dielectric function $\varepsilon_{2}(k)$ is the "one pole approximation" that considers the orientational Debye polarization and neglects the higher frequency (infra-red, electronic, or optical) modes. ${ }^{31,35,40,46}$ This simple model suggests that in liquid water the whole spectrum of the polarization fluctuations can be divided into two zones: (i) the low-frequency zone associated with the orientational Debye mode; and (ii) the high-frequency zone associated with infra-red and optical modes. Because of the strong nonelectrostatic interactions, the polarizations within the orientational mode are correlated (with some effective correlation radius $L$ ) in space, while the polarizations within the high-frequency modes are assumed to be noncorrelated. ${ }^{35,39,45}$ The dielectric constant determined within the transparency zone of the electromagnetic absorption (which separates frequencies typical for low- and high-frequency zones) corresponds to the short-wavelength dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{*}{ }^{35,45}$ Thus, the "one pole approximation" dielectric model for the liquid water assumes that the function $\varepsilon_{2}(k)$ is changed at the length-scale $\sim L$ from the values characteristic of the macroscopic, long-wavelength dielectric constant, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}}$, to the value of the short-wavelength dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{*} \cong 6 .^{35,39,40,45}$ It should be mentioned that the "one pole approximation" is appropriate for the description of polar molecules without internal degrees of freedom, such as rigid, strong correlated dipoles. ${ }^{35}$

It is very important to note that the quantities $\varepsilon_{*}$ and $L$ in the bulk dielectric permittivity of the liquid water were confirmed by analysis of the known experimental Schwarzenbach's data, interpreted as the free energy of interaction between protonated amino groups in dibasic amines. ${ }^{47}$ Thus, function $\varepsilon_{2}(k)$ has some resemblance with other sigmoidal dielectric functions that are used for evaluation of the EI in biomolecular systems, ${ }^{31}$ but it differs from them by physically-grounded quantities $\varepsilon_{*}$ and $L$. It should be noted that $\varepsilon_{2}(k)$ function, Eq. (5), was an insightful solvent dielectric model utilized in the framework of the NL electrostatic approach developed in several theoretical works to explain some experimental data in the electrolyte theory, interfacial electrochemistry and computational biophysics (see cited Ref. 31).

## Limitation of Accurate Evaluations

The model of the planar interface between solvent and protein-like media can provide an accurate evaluation of EI at the interface when the remoteness of the charges from the interface $\left(Z_{0}\right)$ and inter-charge distances typical for this problem are smaller than a radius of curvature of the local region of the interface. These conditions can be satisfied for globular proteins and their complexes, membranes as well as biological supra-molecular structures. However, when these conditions are not satisfied (i.e. a curvature of the interface cannot be neglected), the corresponding evaluations will be less accurate.

## Physicochemical Standpoint on the Obtained Results
Our analyses have shown that the dielectric properties of the interfacial solvent obtained by the NL electrostatic approach on the protein surface can differ remarkably from those suggested by the CL (local) approach. These differences became very significant when distances between the interacting charges and their remoteness from the interface are comparable with the correlation length $L$ in the solvent. We revealed that these distinctions are associated with low values of the dielectric solvent permittivity arising at the interface. These values are comparable with short-wavelength dielectric constant, $\varepsilon_{*}$, and the effective low-dielectric constant $\sim (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_{*})/2$. The presented results are consistent with a concept of the presence of a low-dielectric solvent shell on the protein surface, which was utilized in our previous work$^{31}$ to explain the peculiarities of the dielectric heterogeneity of the protein-like medium at the protein-solvent interface.

The peculiarities of the solvent dielectric permittivity obtained by the NL electrostatic approach can be explained by considering two effects: (i) interactions (particularly, due to the hydrogen-bonding, $\mathrm{H-O\ldots H}$) between water molecules; and (ii) interactions of the water molecules with the protein surface.

In the NL electrostatic approach, the first effect, (i), is simulated by the spatial dispersion of the solvent bulk dielectric function, Eq. (5). This function is a result of averaging all possible interactions (short- and long-range) between water molecules in polar aqueous medium undergoing an external electric field.$^{31}$ The second effect, (ii), is also simulated (effectively, but nonevidently) in the considered model system by the procedure of presenting the NL dielectric function of the solvent at the interface in the framework of the "specular reflection approximation" model.$^{30,31}$ Indeed, the presence of the second effect is supported by a significant difference between the NL solvent permittivity on the interface and in the bulk phase (Figure 3B, curves $1^{\parallel}$, $1^{\perp}$ vs. curve 4). As can be seen from this figure, the values of the effective NL solvent permittivity, $\varepsilon_{\text{eff\_NL}}\ (r = r_{12},\ Z_0)$, are significantly smaller than the corresponding values determined by its "bulk limit," the effective NL dielectric function $\varepsilon_{\text{eff\_NL\_bulk}}\ (r = r_{12})$. This difference can be explained in the following way. Since the dominant contribution to the high water permittivity under room temperature is due to the reorientation of water dipoles, the stronger restrictions in rotations of the water dipoles within some spatial regions of the interfacial system, the lower dielectric permittivity in this region (and vice versa). It is clear that the additional restrictions in rota-

tions of the water molecules on the protein surface (in addition to restrictions due to the effect (i)) should be associ- ated with the effect (ii). It is also clear that at small distances from the interface $(Z_{0}<L)$ and rather small intercharge distances $(r_{12}<Z_{0}<L$ and $Z_{0}<r_{12}<L)$ , both effects deter mine the low values of the dielectric permittivity $\varepsilon_{eff_NL }(r_{12}$ , $Z_{0})$ , which are comparable with the values $\sim \varepsilon_{*}$ and $\sim(\varepsilon_{*}$ + 81)/2.

Both of the aforementioned effects are responsible for the fact that the dielectric permittivity $\varepsilon_{eff_NL }^{\perp}(r_{12}, Z_{0})$ may appreciably exceed the values of the $\varepsilon_{eff_NL }^{\|}(r_{12}, Z_{0})$ (Figure3, curves $1^{\perp}$ and $1^{\|}$ , respectively). This peculiarity has a sim ple explanation. Indeed, the interactions between water mol- ecules and protein surface (the second effect (ii)) may pro- vide a strong structurization and immobilization of the bound water molecules at the protein surface. For two inter- acting charges, located in the vicinity of the protein surface and oriented in $\|$ or $\perp$ to the interface, the total quantities(on average) of the water molecules involved in the electro- static field screening can be considered as comparable. Never-theless, it is reasonable to suggest that in the $\|$ case (vs. the $\perp$  case) there are a significantly larger number of water dipoles(located along the electric lines between interacted charges) that are immobilized on the surface and restricted stronger in their rotational motions. As a result, the larger quantity of water molecules that are more restricted in their motions will determine the lower dielectric permittivity values of the $\varepsilon_{eff_NL }^{\|}(r_{12}, Z_{0})$ , compared to the values of $\varepsilon_{eff_NL }^{\perp}(r_{12}, Z_{0})$ .

Thus, by taking into consideration the major effects asso- ciated with water interactions on the surface of bio-molecu- lar structures, the NL electrostatic approach allows one to capture the main dielectric properties of the interfacial water on the protein surface.

The peculiarities of the dielectric heterogeneity of the NL solvent surrounding a protein, captured in the present work, are consistent with a modern concept of the existence of the ordered boundary water on the protein surface $^{50}$ (see also cited Ref. 31). This concept is based, in particular, on the direct experimental studies of the water dynamics at the pro- tein surface, which indicate that interactions of the water molecules with the protein surface tend to slow down their rotational dynamics. $^{51-53}$ The authors of these works formu lated the model concept of a partially structured interfacial water layer ("dynamically ordered water") with a thickness of~ 7 A. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in explicit water also revealed the coherent ordering of water molecules within the effective distance of $\sim 5 \AA$ from the protein sur face, with the following slow decaying and vanishing of the ordering at the distances $\sim 10 \AA.^{42}$ Moreover, studies of the "site-dipole field" (the average orientation of water molecules that pass through each spatial position) between biomole- cules performed by MD simulations have also suggested the existence of a boundary layer of the ordered water molecules(with a thickness of $\sim 7 \AA$ ). $^{54}$ This is in agreement with the results obtained by MD simulations $^{55}$ that have shown thatin close proximity to the protein surface (within distance $\sim 7$  A), the average time required for rotational reorientation of the water molecules is significantly longer than the average time in bulk water.

Below, the dielectric properties of the interfacial water layer on the macromolecular (protein) surface are discussed. Our asymptotic and numerical analyses suggest that (for two interacting charges, when one of them is localized in close proximity to the interface) the spatial variation of the dielec- tric solvent permittivity along the normal to the protein sur- face (Figure 3, curve $1^{\perp}$ ) can be associated with dynamical properties of the water molecules within a combined solva- tion shell containing three successive shells. $^{50,53}$ These shells can be assigned as: (1) a diffusion restricted water shell that includes the bound water molecules on the surface; (2) a dif- fusion intermediate water shell; and (3) a bulk water shell. The bound water molecules in the first shell are partially im- mobilized. Rotations of all other water molecules in this shell are significantly modified due to their interactions with the macromolecular surface and stronger immobilized water molecules. The second (intermediate) shell is the region where the interactions of water molecules with the interface are significantly reduced in comparison with the interactions that take place in the first shell. The third shell is the region of the bulk or free water.

The first (diffusionally restricted) shell can be considered within the effective distance of $\sim 7 \AA$ from the protein solvent interface, as was estimated in the aforementioned ex- perimental works and works performed in silico. According to our evaluations (see Figure 3), the values of the dielectric permittivity that are characteristic for this shell grow from the values of $\sim 6-7$ to the values of $\sim 12-13$ . It should be noted that evaluations of the dielectric permittivity values just on the protein surface are consistent with the data which is well established in electrochemistry. According to that data, the dielectric permittivity of the first water layer at a surface is to be one order-of-magnitude smaller than thatcharacteristic for the bulk water. $^{38,56}$

The second (diffusion intermediate) shell can be esti- mated to be within an interval of $\sim 7-27 \AA$ from the inter face. Within this shell, the dielectric permittivity is changing from the values of $\sim 13$ to the values of $\sim 40$ .

Finally, the third (bulk) shell can be considered to be at the distances more than $27 \AA$ from the interface. At these dis tances, the values of the effective dielectric permittivity are

approximating to the values $\sim (\varepsilon_{1} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{S}})/2$ that are characteristic for the CL solvent model.

It should be noted again that our estimations of dielectric properties of the interfacial water can be well justified for large protein complexes, membranes, and other biological supramolecular structures. In the case of "typical" proteins, these estimations of the dielectric properties of the first interfacial water shell can also be reasonably accurate. This is because the thickness of this shell (i.e. $\sim 7$ Å) is smaller than the effective radii of "typical" proteins (17-27 Å). However, in the cases when a curvature of the protein interface cannot be disregarded, our estimation of the dielectric properties of the second shell of interfacial water molecules can be less accurate.

### Influence of Interfacial Solvent Shell on Molecular Recognition
Above we have shown that the electrostatic field of a charge located at the protein surface should be weakly screened along the normal to the interface at rather extended distances from it. In particular, we have found that the effective crossmedia dielectric function $\varepsilon_{\text{eff_NL_cross}}$ ($R = 0$, $Z$, $Z_{0}$) derived from Eq. (16) is changing from values $\sim 6$–7 (at distances less than $L$) to the values of $\sim 20$ (at the distances $\sim 2L$ from the interface) (see Figure 3, curve 3). It is evident that the revealed effect of the low-dielectric solvent permittivity at the interface should be taken into account for a better understanding of how interfacial water can mediate macromolecular assembling and functioning.

The importance of our findings can be illustrated by an example of the protein–protein complex formation. It is known that protein associations may have a much faster rate than a free diffusion would allow. $^{9,57-61}$ In particular, the experimentally obtained rate constants of the complex formation for several protein associations can achieve values in the range of $\sim 10^{9}$ to $10^{10}\ \mathrm{M}^{-1}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$, which are about four to five orders of magnitude faster than a basal (without the EI between subunits) association rate ($\sim 10^{4}$ to $10^{6}\ \mathrm{M}^{-1}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$).

The analysis of many protein associations has shown that the opposite-charged and polar residues located in the area of the binding sites exhibit the tendency to form interprotein electrostatic contacts. $^{3,9-12,62-64}$ Many experimental and theoretical works have shown that the process of the protein binding is significantly dependent on favorable EI between the two associating molecules. $^{3,7,9-12,16,57-60,62,64-72}$ Several of the aforementioned theoretical works $^{3,11,64,68,69}$ have reported the existence of favorable electrostatic binding and demonstrated a good agreement with experimental data on rate constants for protein association. These works were performed by Brownian dynamic simulations in water in the framework of the classical solvent model. It should be noted, however, that within quite a number of theoretical works dedicated to the analyses of protein-protein interactions there is no consistence on whether the EI are favorable or unfavorable for the process of binding. There are theoretical works that have suggested the unfavorable computed electrostatic binding. $^{6,43,73,74}$ Calculations of the Coulomb interactions performed in these works (in the framework of the continuum classical electrostatics$^{48}$) suggest that these interactions are generally not strong enough to compensate the unfavorable desolvation effects.

Thus, currently, there is an uncertainty in the opinion about the role, which the electrostatic attractions play in the protein complex formations. In this context, apparently, it is reasonable to admit that in both groups of the theoretical works (that have reported favorable or unfavorable electrostatic binding), all interfacial water molecules have been treated as if they belong to the bulk solvent, and the corresponding value of dielectric constant has been assumed to be equal to $\sim 80$. This approximation, however, may be incorrect even in a general case. In fact, many experimental$^{51-53}$ and theoretical works utilizing molecular dynamic simulations$^{42,50,54,55,75}$ (see also cited Ref. 31) suggest the existence of the effective layer of partially structured interfacial water on the surface of the folded and unfolded proteins. As we have shown in the present work, this structured water determines the effective, low dielectric permittivity of the solvent on the protein surface that results in the significant reduction of the electric field screening in this region.

To address the problem of the adequate evaluation of the dielectric properties of the solvent in close proximity to the protein-solvent interface and highlight the role of their peculiarities in the process of protein association, we considered the following. Experimental data suggest that for two associating proteins (with the rigid structures) there is a "transition state," which is an early, weakly specific complex, where nonspecific EI dominate. $^{59,72}$ This "transition state" can be defined by spatial orientations in which these proteins are shifted apart on $\sim 6$–7 Å (in comparison to the case when the stereo-specifically bound complex is to be formed) and their binding surfaces are rotated away from one another on $0^\circ$–$3^\circ$. $^{3,59,66}$ From this state, the complex is formed instantaneously$^{59}$ by multiple noncovalent interactions across the contact surface. $^{76}$ Since a complex can form as a result of the rotational and translational Brownian motions of proteins in the aqueous solution, a process of their association is diffusion-limited, and the corresponding constant of the association rate can be estimated by the EI energy as$^{67}$:

The results of many works $^{79-86}$ suggest that interfacial water molecules can actively partake in biochemical binding and recognition events due to their liability to form hydrogen bonds between each other, as well as with the backbone polar groups and charged side chains. Our results, taken together with the results of the aforementioned works, allow us tosuggest the dual role, which interfacial water plays in forming of macro-molecular complexes:

i In solvent, water dipoles are strongly correlated by a net- work of hydrogen bonds. They are oriented along the electric lines between interacted charges (in particular, at the protein-solvent interface) and determine peculiar-ities of the dielectric properties of the solvent;

ii The hydrogen-bonded chains of water molecules in sol- vent can form bridges between functional polar or oppositely charged groups located at the surface of the interacting protein subunits. $^{79-81}$ These bridges can determine effective water-mediated interactions between these groups.

Two sides of the same effect (an ability of the water mole- cules to form hydrogen-bonds) can be considered as a major physicochemical mechanism in biological association and aggregation. This mechanism suggests that if protein subu- nits should establish configuration in the transition state by the favorable EI, the first step of their recognition can be approached by the long-range electrostatics. $^{78}$ As a result, the rotational-translational entropy of the protein subunits in the solvent is significantly reduced by the strong electrostatic attraction, which is dramatically increased when the protein subunits approach each other. Additionally, at the distances when the protein separation is about a chain containing~ 2-4 hydrogen-bonded water molecules, the effective water-mediated interactions are arising between those func- tional polar groups, which are close in space. $^{79-81}$ In such a way, at these distances the water-mediated interactions between the functional groups, together with very strong EI,

can facilitate the appearance of the transition state and enhance its stability in the protein association process.

## CONCLUSIONS
It has become commonly accepted that water is an integral and active component of biomolecular systems, where it plays dynamic and structural roles. $^{53,81-83,85,86}$

In the present work, the peculiarities of the dielectric properties of the interfacial water on the macromolecule (protein)-solvent interface were studied. For this purpose, the NL electrostatic approach, together with a PMPS has been utilized. This approach allowed us to introduce the internal dynamic solvent structure into the continuum solvent model developed for the interfacial biomolecular system.

Using the NL approach we have calculated the PEI energies at the interface: the PEI determined in solvent and the CPEI determined for cross-interface media. The asymptotic expressions obtained for these energies at the large spatial scales coincide with the corresponding CL expressions for two uniform dielectrics at the interface. Overall, the common expressions obtained for these energies generalize the corresponding CL expressions for the case of a small spatial scale (comparable with the correlation length $L$ of the water dipoles that is inherent in the orientational Debye polarization mode in the aqueous solvent). Thus, we determined limitations of the applicability of the CL solvent models at the considered model interface. Our analysis suggests that for accurate and adequate evaluations of EI at the interface of any macromolecular system at rather small distances ($r_{12} \sim L$ and $Z_0 \sim L$), one needs to take into account the NL effects of the solvent microsticture [Eqs. (6) and (15)], while at the large distances ($r_{12} \gg L$ and $Z_0 \gg L$), the corresponding evaluation of the EI at the surface of a biological supra-molecular structure (including, for example, protein-protein and DNA-protein complexes) can be provided by the CL models [Eqs. (13) and (18)]. In this context, it should be noted that some numerical approaches that take into account the revealed NL solvent effects for the macromolecules with the native-like boundary geometry have been developed in our lab. $^{87}$

We have shown that, when considering the realistic spatial dispersion of the solvent dielectric function, the PEI and CPEI energies in the vicinity of the protein interface are much stronger than those estimated by the CL model with the use of the Poisson-Boltzmann equations. This distinction arises on the characteristic scales of changing the electrostatic field, when distances between interacting charges and their remoteness from the interface became comparable with the correlation length $L$ of the water dipoles.

The use of the NL electrostatic approach allowed us to reveal distance- and orientation-dependent dielectric function of the solvent at the protein-solvent interface and capture the major effects associated with specific and nonspecific interactions between water molecules in solvent as well as between water molecules and the interface.

We have analyzed how and to what extent the dielectric properties of the interfacial water on the protein surface differ from the dielectric properties of the bulk water. The presented results suggest that the corresponding dielectric function of the solvent along the normal to the interface is changing from the values of $\sim$ 6-7 (at distances less than $L$) to the values of $\sim$ 20 (at the distances $\sim 2L$ from the interface). These results directly point at the existence of an extended low-dielectric solvation shell of partially-structured water molecules on the protein surface. This conclusion is well correlated with data obtained in literature, experimentally $^{50-53}$ and in silico $^{42,54,55,75}$ The effective dropping of the solvent dielectric permittivity at rather extended distances from the protein surface is consistent with a concept of the presence of a low-dielectric solvent layer on the protein surface, which was introduced in our previous work $^{31}$ to explain the peculiarities of the dielectric heterogeneity of the protein-like medium at the protein-solvent interface.

The importance of the revealed peculiarities of the solvent dielectric permittivity at the protein-solvent interface was demonstrated by estimating the Boltzmann factor for the association rate constant for two model protein subunits that approached a transition state, in which they are separated by the solvent. Estimations performed on the basis of the NL electrostatic approach exceeded (by about two orders of magnitude) the corresponding estimation made with CL solvent consideration and are in a good agreement (by orders of magnitude) with the maximum association rate constant obtained experimentally. We showed that such a strong effect can be explained by an existence of the extended low-dielectric interfacial water shells through which the EI between two protein subunits are accomplished. As a result, these EI are much stronger than the EI estimated when implementing the Poisson-Boltzmann models, which do not consider the presence of these shells.

The results obtained in the present work suggest that the interfacial water molecules possessing the ability to form hydrogen-bonds in aqueous solvent play the dual role. On one hand, the water molecules have the possibility of providing an effective (correlated in space) screening of the EI. On the other hand, the water molecules can organize (by the water bridges) a water-mediated interaction between functional polar groups of the macromolecular subunits. It is reasonable to suggest that both of these effects can significantly

eliminate the number of possible binding modes in the protein-protein complex formation.

Overall, the results obtained in this work and in our previous work $^{31}$ suggest that the low-dielectric interfacial water layer or "dynamically ordered water"$^{52}$ on the protein surface is a crucial factor that is responsible for the effective screening of the EI inside and outside of proteins. This factor should be taken into account in studies of the processes of folding, assembling and functioning of proteins and other biological macromolecules in solvent.
