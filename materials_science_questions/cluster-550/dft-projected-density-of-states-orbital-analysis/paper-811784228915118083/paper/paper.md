# Hardness calculations of 5d transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure

Jun Yang and Faming Gao*

Hebei Key Laboratory of Applied Chemistry, Yanshan University, Qinhuangdao 066004, P.R. China

Received 19 March 2010, revised 15 April 2010, accepted 16 April 2010
Published online 2 June 2010

Keywords 5d transition metal monocarbides, elastic properties, tungsten carbide

*Corresponding author: e-mail fmgao@ysu.edu.cn, Phone: +86 335 806 156 9, Fax: +86 335 806 156 9

The electronic and elastic properties of 5d transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure have been studied by first principles calculations. The calculated lattice parameters, elastic properties, and hardness are in good agreement with available experimental and other theoretical values. The hardness calculations indicate that ReC and WC are the harder materials among the corresponding 5d transition metal monocarbides. We find that a valence electron number of 10/cell may have a causal relationship with the highest hardness of WC.

## 1 Introduction
5d transition metals (TM) have relatively high bulk modulus [1] but low shear strength, and therefore they have low hardness [2]. Through the introduction of nonmetallic B, C, or N element, the non-directional metallic bonding in the 5d transition metals might be partly changed into highly directional covalent bonding, leading to excellent mechanical properties of the corresponding monoborides, monocarbides, or mononitrides. Presently, these compounds are very attractive in synthesizing and designing new superhard materials.

For 5d transition metal monocarbides, WC has been known to crystallize in hexagonal tungsten carbide structure [3] and has diverse applications such as machine tools in industry. Theoretical calculations on ReC indicated that tungsten carbide type structure is energetically the most stable among tungsten carbide, NiAs, wurtzite, NaCl, CsCl, and zinc blende structures [4]. In addition, the calculations of Gou et al. [5] signified that the incompressibility of ReC with tungsten carbide structure exceeds that of diamond under higher pressure and it is a potential hard material due to the high bulk and shear modulus [5, 6]. OsC was synthesized in tungsten carbide structure at zero pressure by Kempter and Nadler [7], and the theoretical studies on OsC [8] with tungsten carbide structure suggested that the bulk modulus of OsC are smaller than that of diamond but larger than cubic BN.

However, the theoretical hardness of 5d transition metal monocarbides has not been studied systematically yet. The tungsten carbide structure was considered because many 5d transition metal monocarbides were synthesized in this structure, as mentioned above. Since it is difficult to measure the hardness for most 5d transition metal monocarbides at ambient condition, the theoretical prediction of hardness is valuable for further experimental studies. The hardness was calculated based on the semiempirical hardness model. The relationship between theoretical hardness and metallicity was analyzed. The effects of elastic properties, such as bulk modulus, shear modulus, Young's modulus, and Poisson's ratio on hardness were also discussed. The elastic and electronic properties of 5d transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure were also studied by the first principles calculations.

## 2 Method of calculation
5d transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure (space group P-6m2) were investigated using CASTEP code [9]. The electron-ion interactions were described by using Vanderbilt ultrasoft pseudopotential. The exchange and correlation functional were treated by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) as proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof [10], and the local density approximation (LDA) used the Ceperley-Alder expression [11] as parameterized by Perdew and Zunger [12]. The k points of $12 \times 12 \times 12$ were generated using the Monkhorst-Pack scheme. Lattice parameters and atomic positions were optimized simultaneously. ## 3 Results and discussion
### 3.1 Lattice parameters and elastic properties of
5d transition metal monocarbides The elastic properties for 5d transition metal monocarbides have been calculated using the equilibrium lattice parameters. All hexagonal crystals have five independent elastic constants of $C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, $C_{13}$, $C_{33}$, and $C_{44}$. For hexagonal phase [14], the Voigt bulk modulus $B_V$ and Reuss bulk modulus $B_R$ are calculated in terms of the elastic constants using the following relations:

$$
B_{V}=\frac{2\left(C_{11}+C_{12}\right)+4 C_{13}+C_{33}}{9}, \tag{1}
$$

$$
B_{R}=\frac{C^{2}}{M}. \tag{2}
$$

The Voigt shear modulus $G_V$ and the Reuss shear modulus $G_R$ are

$$
G_{V}=\frac{M+12 C_{44}+12 C_{66}}{30}, \tag{3}
$$

$$
G_{R}=\frac{5 C^{2} C_{44} C_{66}}{2\left[3 B_{V} C_{44} C_{66}+C^{2}\left(C_{44}+C_{66}\right)\right]}, \tag{4}
$$

where

$$
M=C_{11}+C_{12}+2 C_{33}-4 C_{13}, \tag{5}
$$

$$
C^{2}=\left(C_{11}+C_{12}\right) C_{33}-2 C_{13}^{2}, \tag{6}
$$

$$
C_{66}=\frac{C_{11}-C_{12}}{2}. \tag{7}
$$

The mechanical stability criteria are given by $C_{44}>0$, $C_{11} > |C_{12}|$, $(C_{11}+2C_{12})C_{33} > 2C_{13}^{2}$ [14].

Hill [14] proved that the Voigt and Reuss equations represent upper and lower limits of the true polycrystalline constants. Hence, the bulk modulus and shear modulus of hexagonal crystals can be approximated by

$$
B=\frac{B_{V}+B_{R}}{2}, \tag{8}
$$

$$
G=\frac{G_{R}+G_{V}}{2}. \tag{9}
$$

Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $v$ are obtained by the following formulas:

$$
E=\frac{9 B G}{3 B+G}, \tag{10}
$$

$$
v=\frac{3 B-2 G}{2(3 B+G)}. \tag{11}
$$

Since GGA usually overestimates the lattice parameters and underestimates the elastic constants, while LDA underestimates the lattice parameters and overestimates the elastic constants, the arithmetic average values of lattice parameters and elastic constants of GGA and LDA values were used as the better theoretical values [15]. It can be seen from Table 1 that the calculated equilibrium lattice parameters and elastic properties for WC, ReC, OsC, IrC, and PtC compare favorably with the previous theoretical values. Compared with the experimental values, the calculated lattice parameters $a_0$ ($c_0$) for WC and OsC show 0.5% (0.7%) and 0.1% (4%) deviations, respectively. It also can be seen from Table 1 that LDA gives larger elastic constants than GGA. This might be due to the smaller lattice parameters predicted in LDA than in GGA. ### 3.2 Electronic structure analysis of 5d transition metal monocarbides
The calculated density of states (DOS) for $5d$ transition metal monocarbides, as shown in Fig. 3, indicate the metallic behavior of 5d transition metal monocarbides due to the finite $N(E_{\text{F}})$ at the Fermi level. It is seen that both TM-5d and C-2p orbitals contribute to the metallic behavior.

It is also shown in Fig. 3 that there exists a strong hybridization between TM-5d and C-2p orbitals, leading to the covalent bonding. But a very high DOS at Fermi level could lead to the instability of the electronic structure and resulting in structural distortions [22, 23]. The more stable crystals usually accommodate more valence electrons into the bonding states so as to form a valley near the Fermi level and lead to a lower $N(E_{\text{F}})$ at the Fermi level. The deep valley near the Fermi level is often termed as pseudogap. It can be seen from Fig. 3 that the pseudogap energy for 5d transition metal monocarbides shifts from right to left with increasing atomic number except LaC.

The atomic population analysis of 5d transition metal monocarbides indicates a charge transfer from 5d transition metal cations to carbon anions. The net charges on TM and carbon are +0.43 to +0.87 and $-$0.43 to $-$0.87, respectively, which reveals an ionic contribution to the bonding. Therefore, our results demonstrate that there exist the unusual mixtures of metallic, covalent, and ionic bonding for 5d transition metal monocarbides.

### 3.3 Hardness calculations of 5d transition metal monocarbides
The theoretical hardness of compound crystals with metallic bonding can be calculated as follows [5, 24]:
$$
H_{v}(\mathrm{GPa})=A\left(P-P^{\prime}\right) v_{b}^{-5 / 3},\tag{12}
$$

$$
f_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{P^{\prime}}{P},\tag{13}
$$

$$
P^{\prime}=\frac{n_{\text{free}}}{V},\tag{14}
$$

$$
n_{\text{free}}=\int_{E_{\text{p}}}^{E_{\text{F}}} N(E) d E.\tag{15}
$$
where $H_{v}$ is the Vickers hardness, $P$ is Mulliken population, $P'$ is metallic population, $f_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the metallicity of crystals, $n_{\text{free}}$ is the number of effective free electron in a cell, $V$ is the volume of cell, $N(E)$ is DOS, $E_{\text{F}}$ is Fermi energy, $E_{\text{p}}$ is pseudogap energy, and $v_{b}$ is the bond volume. For $5d$ transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure, each TM atom has six equidistant C atoms as nearest neighbours, and vice versa. So there are six TM-C bonds for each cell, and the bond volume $v_{b}$ can be calculated by
$$
v_{b}=\frac{V}{6}.\tag{16}
$$

It is to be noted that owing to the high sensitivity of Mulliken population to basis set [25], the constant $A$ in

In some cases, it is difficult to determine the position of pseudogap for metallic compounds due to two or three valleys near the Fermi level. We found that the bonding orbitals and nonbonding orbitals of C-2p orbitals in $5d$ transition metal monocarbides are distinguished easily in the PDOS of C-2p orbitals. Usually, the pseudogap corresponds to the top of bonding orbitals in the PDOS of C-2p orbitals. So when the PDOS of C-2p orbitals are gradually reduced to 0 or negligible, the corresponding energy may be considered as the pseudogap energy $E_{\text{p}}$, as shown in Fig. 3 (ReC). It is well known that the energygap separates the valence band from conduction band for semiconductors and insulators, thus we assume that the pseudogap is the separation of pseudo-valence band and pseudo-conduction band. The electrons below the pseudogap are relatively localized due to the strong hybridization between TM-$5d$ and C-2p orbitals, and the electrons occupying the levels above pseudogap become relatively delocalized. It was suggested that strong and directional covalent bonding and high valence electron density (VED) are the necessary conditions for hard materials to resist both elastic and plastic deformation [31]. For 5d transition metal monocarbides, the valence electron number increases in steps of unity from 7 for LaC to 15 for AuC. For 5d transition metal monocarbides, WC possesses a valence electron number of 10/cell. It may be speculated that 10/cell is a stable valence shell configuration for 5d transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure, which may have a causal relationship with the peak of hardness values. Similar phenomena are also found in 5d transition metal monoborides and mononitrides with tungsten carbide structure. Jhi et al. concluded that the family of transition metal carbo-nitrides had a peak shear modulus at a valence electron number of 8.4/cell [32]. Our findings are qualitatively similar to those obtained with $ab$ initio computations by Jhi et al.

4 Conclusions The hardness, electronic, and elastic properties of $5d$ transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure are investigated by using the density functional theory. The results show that all the monocarbides are mechanically stable except LaC, HfC, and AuC. The bulk modulus increases first and reaches a maximum value, then decreases with increasing atomic number. The calculated DOS show that all the 5d transition metal monocarbides are metallic. The position of pseudogap for metallic compounds may be determined by DOS analysis. Moreover, results reveal that the pseudogap energy for 5d transition metal monocarbides shifts from right to left with increasing atomic number. The calculations indicate that ReC and WC possess negligible metallicity and strong covalency, leading to their high hardness. Results also reveal that WC and ReC have larger bulk modulus, shear modulus, Young's modulus and smaller Poisson' ratio than other 5d transition metal monocarbides. In addition, a valence electron number of 10/cell is a stable valence shell configuration for 5d transition metal monocarbides with tungsten carbide structure, which may have a causal relationship with the high hardness of WC.
