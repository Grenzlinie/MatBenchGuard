# Phase stabilities, electronic and electrochemical properties of compounds in the Li-Al-Si system

Monique Tillard *, Claude Belin, Laurent Spina, Yong Zhong Jia

Laboratoire des Agrégats Moléculaires et Matériaux Inorganiques, UMR CNRS 5072, CC 15, Université de Montpellier II, Sciences et Techniques du Languedoc, 2 place E. Bataillon, 34095 Montpellier Cedex 5, France

Received 5 April 2004; accepted 25 April 2005
Available online 28 June 2005

## Abstract
We have previously concluded that at least four compounds exist in the ternary system lithium-aluminum-silicon, their complex crystal structures have all been determined without ambiguity from single crystal X-ray analysis. Compounds LiAlSi, $Li_{5} AlSi_{2}$ and $Li_{9} AlSi_{3}$ have been tested as potential anodic materials for lithium batteries. The best electrochemical behavior was found for $Li_{9} AlSi_{3}$ that affords a capacity of about $1040 mA h g^{-1}$ , almost three times that of the carbon negative electrode used in commercial lithium-ion batteries. It is assumed that the significant disorder (atomic mixing and vacancies) that occurs in the tetragonal structure of $Li_{9} AlSi_{3}$ is responsible for this interesting property. First principle DFT calculations have been carried out to determine the energetic stabilities and electronic structures of these compounds.

## 1. Introduction
Lithium has been found to combine easily with moder- ately electronegative elements of groups 13 and 14. Within large ranges of stoichiometry, these combinations give many phases that exhibit various crystal structures. These phases are regarded as Zintl phases in which the electropositive el- ement (Li) transfers electrons to the main group elements allowing the formation of anionic frameworks. These struc- tures take the form of simple stacking of single ions or of one-, two- or three-dimensional arrangements in which some clustering may occur as the consequence of local electron deficiencies. With respect to relative electronegativities of group 13 or 14 elements, the electrochemical potentials of binary lithium compounds are within 0.3-1.3 Volts above pure lithium. In the early seventies, many studies predicted these materials to be able to replace metallic lithium an- odes in primary, secondary and thermal batteries [1,2]. In most cases, as exemplified by Si, Sn, lithiation of the main group element yields series of intermetallic compounds with successive voltage steps and slopes in the charge/discharge diagrams, possibly coupled with large volumetric changes. These drawbacks can be palliated by the use of ternary lithium phases particularly those containing some transition elements. In these phases, the insertion/removal of lithium is sometimes described as a single-phase process in which the transition element acts as an electronic buffer during the redox exchanges.
This paper reports results obtained from galvanostatic cy- cling of $Li / Li_{x} Al_{y} Si_{z}$ electrochemical cells using LiAlSi, $Li_{5} AlSi_{2}$ and $Li_{9} AlSi_{3}$ , the crystal structures of which have been published previously [3,4]. We also describe results of DFT CASTEP [5] calculations relative to geometry op- timizations, electronic properties and energetic stabilities of these compounds.

## 2. Experimental

As already described [3], the alloys were prepared from mixtures of the elements at high temperatures inside tantalum reactors, weld sealed under argon atmosphere. To avoid oxidation, reagents and products were manipulated in a glove box filled with purified argon. Products of the reactions were ground into calibrated powders (20–30 µm) for testing in electrochemical ${\rm Li/LiPF_6,(EC,DEC)/Li}_x{\rm Al}_y{\rm Si}_z$ cells. The electrodes comprised a thin pellet of the active material mixed with 10% carbon black and a foil of pure metallic lithium. The electrolyte was supplied by Merck laboratories. Swagelok-type two-electrode cells were mounted in a glove box under argon atmosphere. Cell charge–discharge experiments were carried out using a MacPile multichannel galvanostat–potentiostat. The choice of current densities was driven by preliminary experiments intended to determine the optimal working conditions of the batteries.

## 3. Computational details

Density functional electronic calculations were performed using the CASTEP package [6] based on the total energy plane-wave pseudopotential technique. The ion-electron interaction is modeled by an ultrasoft nonlocal pseudopotential [7] and, to reduce the number of plane waves, generated pseudopotentials were used for all atoms. The electron exchange correlation effects were taken into account using the GGA/PBE gradient corrected approximation [8]. The energy cutoff of plane-wave basis set was chosen to be 330 eV. A Monkhorst–Pack uniform grid [9] of automatically generated $k$-points was used for numerical integration in the Brillouin zone. For the primitive cubic cells of LiAlSi and Li₂AlSi, the mesh of $k$-points was taken as $7 \times 7 \times 7$. To incorporate the effects of disorder and mixed occupancies in compounds Li₇Al₃Si₄, Li₅AlSi₂ and Li₉AlSi₃, we have simulated their structures in periodic supercells. This was done by distributing defects in the most symmetrical way. The $k$-point mesh was taken as $4 \times 4 \times 4$ for the primitive of the cubic supercell (28 atoms) of Li₇Al₃Si₄, $2 \times 4 \times 4$ for the monoclinic supercell (48 atoms) of Li₅AlSi₂ and $3 \times 3 \times 2$ for the primitive of the orthorhombic supercell (52 atoms) of Li₉AlSi₃. Convergence with respect to the $k$-point sampling was checked in order to compare relative stabilities of different configurations. The geometry optimizations were performed within symmetry-constrained unit cells using the BFGS algorithm and ultrafine level of accuracy (energy convergence tolerance threshold of $5 \times 10^{-6}$ eV/atom). SCF tolerance threshold was taken as $5 \times 10^{-7}$ eV/atom (computational ultrafine level of accuracy).

## 4. Results and discussion

### 4.1. Electrochemical tests

In this first stage, we will describe preliminary electrochemical testing of three phases: cubic LiAlSi ($a = 5.928$ Å, ${\rm \bar{F}43m}$ space group), hexagonal Li₅AlSi₂ (${\rm P6_3/m}$, $a = 7.549$, $c = 8.097$ Å) and tetragonal Li₉AlSi₃ (${\rm I4_1/amd}$, $a = 6.179$, $c = 12.199$ Å).

In cubic LiAlSi, the atoms Al, Si and Li lie at $4c$ ($1/4,1/4,1/4$), $4b$ ($1/2,1/2,1/2$) and $4d$ ($3/4,3/4,3/4$) sites respectively, while site $4a$ $(0,0,0)$ remains empty (Fig. 1). Filling this structure with one more lithium would lead to the stoichiometry Li₂AlSi, a phase which never could be obtained by high temperature melting of the elements.

LiAlSi was submitted to lithium insertion in galvanostatic mode at a current density of 0.10 mA cm⁻² (C/50 regime, discharge completed in 50 hours). In the early stage of the first discharge (Fig. 2), the voltage rapidly drops from 1.15 to 0.06 V, and this is followed by a voltage plateau (two-phase insertion process?) up to the global composition Li₂.₃AlSi. From this point, the first charge-discharge cycle involving the exchange of one lithium corresponds to a specific capacity of 400 mA h g⁻¹. $Li_5AlSi_2$ displays a pseudo-graphitic hexagonal structure $[LiAlSi_2]_n$ in which the interlayer space is entirely filled with lithium (Fig. 3). Extraction of all this lithium would lead to a theoretical capacity of $900$ mA h g$^{-1}$ but only one third (capacity of $300$ mA h g$^{-1}$) was removed in the course of the first oxidation step (Fig. 4). Also in this case, rapid fading of the capacity is observed even when operating at lower current densities (C/100 regime), from the second cycle onwards that is associated with strong polarization.

$Li_9AlSi_3$ crystallizes in a tetragonal structure built on a lacunal and disordered tetrahedral network comprised of silicon and aluminum atoms (Fig. 3). Lithium atoms are of two types: mixed with aluminum at site $16f$ and half filling the $8e$ position (mm symmetry). Interestingly, $Li_9AlSi_3$ displays a different electrochemical behavior from that of the preceding compounds (Fig. 5).

Starting from the composition $Li_{18}Al_2Si_6$ (two $Li_9AlSi_3$ formula units) by a charge (oxidation), the electrochemical cell was cycled between stoichiometry limits $Li_{8.5}Al_2Si_6$ and $Li_{22}Al_2Si_6$ giving a specific capacity of $1040$ mA h g$^{-1}$ for the first cycle. Interestingly, polarization was much lower than for LiAlSi or $Li_5AlSi_2$ and the voltage versus composition curve displays a slope characteristic of a single-phase process.

The densities of states (DOS) and the band structure are good tools to judge whether a material is promising for electrochemical applications. Bonding and electronic properties of LiAlSi, $Li_5AlSi_2$, $Li_9AlSi_3$, $Li_7Al_3Si_4$ have been analyzed using CASTEP DFT calculations. The electronic structure and energy stability of the hypothetical compound $Li_2AlSi$ have also been determined.

### 4.3. LiAlSi and the uncertain existence of $Li_2AlSi$

#### 4.3.1. Structural conformations for LiAlSi

For LiAlSi ($a = 5.928$ Å, F$\overline{4}$3m, cF12) the unit cell of which contains a vacancy (site $\overline{4}$3m), energies were calculated for the three possible atomic arrangements (Table 2) retaining the cubic symmetry. The results given in Table 3 are in good agreement with the previous LMTO-LDA/DFT calculations of Christensen [10].

Arrangement I (experimental crystal structure) is the most favorable with regard to the total energy and Mulliken charges which are most consistent with the relative electronegativities of the elements [11]. Note that in CASTEP, the Mulliken charges are calculated following the formalism described by Segall et al. [12,13], and the population analysis is performed by using a projection of the plane-wave states onto a localized basis set generated from the used pseudo-potentials. In consequence the absolute magnitudes of atomic charges have little physical meaning because of their sensitivity to the chosen basis set. However, consideration of their relative values can yield useful information on bonding and properties, particularly when comparing different structural arrangements for a same compound. We have also checked that the results are not altered when calculations are carried out in symmetry unconstrained triclinic cells.

The band structures of LiAlSi in arrangement I (Fig. 1) and arrangement II (Fig. 6) are closely related, their optimized cell parameters deviating by less than 0.5% from the experimental values and they are both characteristic of narrow gap semiconductors. Deviation from the experimental cell parameter is larger (2.8%) for arrangement III which has longer Al–Si distances ($a/2$) compared to arrangements I and II ($a\sqrt{3}/4$). As reported in Table 3, for arrangement III the bonding in the Al–Si framework is less covalent, and the band structure and density of states indicate clearly that this polymorph would be metallic.

Although the band structures, represented in Fig. 7, appear significantly different for the two configurations, their respective total energies (Table 4) are almost identical.

The band structure obtained for the non-centrosymmetric F$\overline{4}$3m Li₂AlSi compound closely relates to that of LiAlSi (and also elemental silicon). This is not surprising since the vacancy in the LiAlSi cubic cell has been filled with a lithium atom that, within a rigid band model, acts as a one-electron donor, just lifting the Fermi level into the conduction band (open shell).

The marked covalent character (oriented bonding) can be visualized from the representation (slice $1\overline{1}0$, Fig. 8) of the total electron density. Owing to the difference in electronegativities between Al and Si, bonding is highly polarized towards silicon. There is no overlap between Al, Si and lithium cations (note that density around lithium is the non-valence semi-core 1s).

Density of states is relatively important at Fermi level and, in the band structure, the two crystal orbitals that are cut by the Fermi level have nearly the same energies at point $\Gamma$. Distribution of the electron density over these two partially filled crystal orbitals is represented in Fig. 9 where it is clearly seen that these levels are Al–Si antibonding, in agreement with previous EHT-COOP calculations [3].

Owing to a larger separation between Al and Si atoms in the Li₂AlSi centrosymmetric Fm$\overline{3}$m structure, bonding is less polarized towards silicon than in the non-centrosymmetric structure. This is shown by a 0.12 increase in the atomic charge for silicon and by a 0.08 decrease in the Al–Si overlap population. With a better mixing of bands (higher dispersion) on both sides of the Fermi level (Fig. 7(a)), the centrosymmetric structural arrangement appears more metallic than the non-centrosymmetric one for which valence and conduction bands are separated by a substantial gap (Fig. 7(b)). The partially filled crystal orbitals are now found slightly bonding, making this centrosymmetric arrangement a little bit more stable.

In view of these results, we better understand why electrochemical insertion/desinsertion of lithium into the cubic LiAlSi host structure is difficult. Reduction of the host matrix keeping the original F$\overline{4}$3m symmetry would be highly destabilizing (open shell with antibonding populations) while changing to Fm$\overline{3}$m with an octahedral Al–Si network would require profound structural modifications (exchange of Al and Li positions). This explains that any attempt to prepare the Li₂AlSi phase by alloying the elements at high temperature was unsuccessful, leaving unreacted silicon and the compound Li₇Al₃Si₄.

### 4.4. Li₇Al₃Si₄: an alternative to the unstable Li₂AlSi?

Li₇Al₃Si₄ crystallizes in the cubic F$\overline{4}$3m space group ($a=6.115$ Å) and, owing to its disordered structure [3], can be formulated as Li₇Al₃Si₄□₂. Site $4b$ is half-filled with lithium, and three Al and one Li are mixed at site $4a$. Site $4c$ is fully occupied by silicon and site $4d$ by lithium. In order to estimate the electronic properties of the Li₇Al₃Si₄ compound, it was necessary to build a realistic ordered model and we tested two very similar arrangements.

The first, built with the tetragonal $1\times1\times2$ supercell ($a=6.115,c=12.230$ Å) displays P$\overline{4}$2m symmetry and contains 14 Li atoms at sites $1c,1d,2e,2g,4n$ ($z=3/8$ and $7/8$), 6 Al atoms at $1a,1b,4m$ ($z=1/4$) and 8 Si atoms at $4n$ ($z=1/8$ and $5/8$).

The second, built with a cubic $2\times2\times2$ supercell, retains the F$\overline{4}$3m symmetry ($a=12.230$ Å) and contains 56 Li atoms at sites $4a,4b,4c,4d,16e$ ($x=3/8$) and $24f$

### 4.5. Hexagonal $Li_5AlSi_2$: a pseudo-graphitic structure

The compound $Li_5AlSi_2$ crystallizes in the hexagonal space group P6$_3$/m, and its structure comprises $[LiAlSi_2]_n^{4-}$ anionic layers entangled with a Li (site 12i) cationic diamond-like sublattice. Site 2h is filled with aluminum and site 6d with silicon while aluminum is found mixed with lithium at sites 2a (54.5% Al) and 2c (14.8% Al). An ordered model was first built with a $2 \times 2 \times 1$ hexagonal supercell ($a = 15.098$, $c = 8.097$ Å) thereafter reduced to a monoclinic cell (P2$_1$/m, $a = 13.075$, $b = 8.097$, $c = 7.549$ Å and $\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90^\circ$) by the "Find Symmetry" module of the MS Modeling program [14].

As indicated by the electron density distribution within the layer $[LiAlSi_2]_n^{4-}$ (Fig. 11), there is no buildup of charge between the Li and Si atoms, and the anionic layer might be better viewed as a polymer $[Al_3Si_6]_n^{15-}$ containing $AlSi_3$ molecular fragments. This molecular description is relevant to the covalent nature of the Al-Si bonds (2.467 Å) that are meaningfully shorter than those around 2.600 Å found in the cubic and tetragonal phases.

The electronic structure (Fig. 12) displays a very low density of states at the Fermi level with band cutting mainly in the $\Gamma-Y$ segment of the Brillouin zone that corresponds to the c-axis direction of the original hexagonal cell. With regard to the structural approximations made to build this periodic model, the compound $Li_5AlSi_2$ appears as a very poorly conducting material. Electrical conduction does not occur within the anionic layers but between layers through small overlap involving interlayer Li empty states.

#### 4.5.1. The tetragonal $Li_9AlSi_3$, a good anodic material?

At first sight, the tetragonal structure of $Li_9AlSi_3$ (I4$_1$/ amd, $a = 6.179(1)$, $c = 12.199(4)$ Å) derives from that of cubic $Li_7Al_3Si_4$ by doubling the cell in one direction. It is characterized by atomic substitutions and vacancies: site 4a is filled with Si, sites 4b and 8e are half-filled with Si and Li respectively, while Al/Li mixing (16% Al) occurs at site 16f. Owing to the occupational disorder, each silicon atom is statistically surrounded by one Al and seven Li atoms (2.614–2.717 Å), and because of the complexity of this disorder, we have searched and tested several ordered periodic models that satisfy the atomic coordinations and for which the simulated X-ray diffraction patterns are as close as possible to that of the original tetragonal structure. As a consequence, all models built on polymerized $AlSi_3$ units in which Al is tetracoordinated were discarded. The best model was obtained with a $2 \times 2 \times 1$ supercell ($a = 12.357$, $c = 12.199$ Å, symmetry C222$_1$) containing isolated $AlSi_3$ units (tricoordinated Al) within a lithium matrix (six $Li_9AlSi_3$ formula units per cell). The band structure and density of states obtained for this model are represented in Fig. 13, the valence band is partially filled and density of states is high at Fermi level. This allows us to expect good metallic properties for $Li_9AlSi_3$.
