# Local Electronic Properties Induced at the Molecule−Metal Interface

S. Masuda,* Y. Koide, M. Aoki, and Y. Morikawa

Department of Basic Science, Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, The University of Tokyo, Komaba, Meguro, Tokyo 153-8902, Japan, and The Institute of Scientific and Industrial Research, Osaka University, 8-1 Mihogaoka, Ibaraki, Osaka 567-0047, Japan

Received: May 28, 2007

Electron emission spectra resulting from thermal collisions of He*(2³S) metastable atoms with alkanethiols ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{SH}$, $n=1-3$) on a Pt(111) surface were measured to clarify the electronic properties induced at the molecule−metal interface. Upon chemisorption, the S 3p-derived states are broadened significantly by admixture with the Pt bands, yielding a sharp Fermi edge, whereas such a metallic state is missing in weakly bound species. The spectral feature is in agreement with first-principles calculations using density functional theory. Furthermore, our data show that the local density of states at the Fermi level decreases rapidly on going from the S atom to the outer C atoms, indicating that the chemisorbed species are metallic in character at the S end and insulating at the alkyl end. A new classification of adsorbates into insulating, metallic, and amphibious molecules would be useful for understanding ordinary charge transport at the molecule−metal interface.

## 1. Introduction

Much attention has been paid to clarifying transport properties in molecular junctions such as a thiol molecule bridged by a pair of metal electrodes.¹⁻⁷ It is now well recognized that the current−voltage ($I-V$) characteristics are strongly affected by the bonding nature between the molecule and metal substrate, and the electronic states induced at the molecule−metal interface. For alkanethiol, Cui et al. showed that the conductance of the molecular junctions depends significantly on whether the molecule is chemisorbed or physisorbed to the contacts.² Furthermore, Engelkes et al. indicated that the conductance at zero bias varies by 2 orders of magnitude or more depending on the type of metal (Ag, Au, or Pt) electrodes.³ The critical importance of the interface states (or chemisorption-induced states) has also been pointed out in theoretical work,⁵⁻⁷ but the experimental information remains rather limited.

Metastable atom electron spectroscopy (MAES) is based on energy analysis of electrons emitted by thermal collision of metastable atoms such as He*(1s2s, 2³S) with a solid surface.⁸,⁹ When the metastable atom impinges on a metal surface such as Pt(111), it deexcites to the ground state predominantly via a two-step process, in which the 2s electron of He* resonantly tunnels into a conduction band of the surface (resonance ionization, RI) and then the resultant ion is neutralized by an Auger process (Auger neutralization, AN). In the latter process, the energy difference between $\text{He}^+(1\text{s}, \, 1^2\text{S})$ and $\text{He}(1\text{s}^2, \, 1^1\text{S})$ is shared by two electrons in the valence bands to yield a broad spectrum reflecting the self-convolution of density of states. On an insulator surface such as the alkanethiol multilayer, the RI process is suppressed owing to a band gap, and the metastable atom decays via Penning ionization, where a valence electron transfers to the 1s of He* and the 2s electron is emitted simultaneously. In this case, the excitation energy of He*(2³S, 19.82 eV) is used simply to eject an electron from the valence band, yielding the one-hole states similar to photoemission. Unlike photons used in ultraviolet photoemission spectroscopy (UPS), the metastable atoms do not penetrate into the bulk; therefore, the local electronic states at the outermost layer can be probed selectively. This has been applied successfully to detection of the chemisorption-induced states near the Fermi level ($E_\text{F}$), for example, $2\pi^*$-derived states of CO on Ni(111)¹⁰,¹¹ and $\pi$-derived antibonding states of benzene on Pd(110).¹²

In this letter, we report the MAES and UPS spectra of alkanethiols ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{SH}$, $n=1-3$) on a Pt(111) substrate, together with the first-principles calculations. The purpose for the present study is threefold. The first is to observe directly the adsorption-induced states, especially those near the Fermi level. The second is to clarify their spatial distribution by changing the alkyl chain length. The third is to examine the relationship between the adsorption-induced states and charge transport in molecular junctions.

## 2. Experimental Section

The experimental apparatus and related procedure are reported elsewhere.¹³,¹⁴ The Pt(111) substrate was cleaned by repeated $\text{Ar}^+$ ion sputtering and heating cycles. The clean substrate showed a well-ordered $1\times1$ pattern and no impurities within the limit of Auger electron spectroscopy. The condensed films of alkanethiols ($\text{CH}_3\text{SH}$, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{SH}$, and $\text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{SH}$) were prepared by exposing the gaseous molecules to a clean Pt(111) substrate held at 50 K. The chemisorbed monolayers were obtained by heating the condensed multilayer up to 200 K. According to thermal desorption and X-ray photoemission studies,¹⁵ the physisorbed species in the multilayer desorb at 140 K and the remaining chemisorbed molecules dissociate to form thiolate at 200 K.

### 3. Computational Details
All calculations based on a generalized gradient approximation in density functional theory were carried out using a program package "STATE" (simulation tool for atom technol- ogy). $^{16}$ The pseudopotentials of H 1s, C 2p, and Pt 5d states are constructed by Vanderbilt's scheme, $^{17}$ whereas those of other components are constructed by the normconserving scheme. $^{18,19}$ The cutoff energies for the wave function and the augmented charge are 25 and 225 Ry, respectively. A repeated slab model is used, with each slab composed of six atomic layers, separated by a vacuum region of $14.4 \AA$. Thiolates are introduced on one side of the slab. For the structural optimization, thiolates and the top three layers of the substrate atoms are allowed to relax.

### 4. Results and Discussion
To elucidate the valence states of alkanethiols without direct contact to the metal substrate, we measured the UPS and MAES spectra of the condensed films on $Pt(111)$ using the He I resonance line $(h \nu=21.2 eV)$ and $He^*(2^3S, 19.8 eV)$, respectively. Figure 1 shows typical data for a $CH_3SH$ film with the thickness of three monolayers (MLs), together with the gas- phase MAES spectrum. The latter spectrum reveals five bands, which are assigned to electron emissions from the valence orbitals; the first band is due to the nonbonding orbital $(n_S)$ composed of $S 3p$, the second band to bonding orbital $(\sigma_{CS})$ along the CS bond, and the higher-lying bands mainly to methyl- derived orbitals $(\sigma_{SH}$ and pseudo $\pi_{CH 3}).^{20}$ The corresponding bands appear in the condensed-phase spectra. The Pt $5d$ bands are clearly seen just below $E_F$ in the UPS spectrum but are missing in the MAES spectrum because the $He^*(2^3S)$ atoms interact effectively with the $CH_3SH$ molecules at the topmost layer. The absence of a band near $E_F$ in the MAES spectrum indicates that the electronic structure of the topmost molecule(without a chemical bond to the Pt substrate) is insulating, with a wide HOMO-LUMO gap. A similar feature was observedin the $C_2H_5SH$ and $C_3H_7SH$ condensed multilayers. $^{14}$

Figure $2 ~A$ and $B$ show the He I UPS and $He^*(2^3S)$ MAES spectra of a chemisorbed monolayer of alkanethiols on $Pt(111)$, where molecules are bound to the substrate by forming thiolate $(C_nH_{2 n+1} ~S-Pt).^{15,21-23}$ In the UPS spectra, the band at $4.1 eV$  below $E_F$ is due to the $S 3 p$-derived states and the higher-lying bands are due to an admixture of the $S 3 p$ - and alkyl-derived states (Figure 3). The MAES spectra reveal three characteristics. At first, for the $C_2H_5S$ and $C_3H_7S$ monolayers, the $S 3p$-derived band at $4.1 eV$ hardly appears as a prominent peak despite the clear appearance of alkyl-derived bands, which is in contrast to the cases of the gas- and condensed-phase spectra. As illustrated in Figure $2 C$, the thiolate molecules are anchored to the substrate via an $S-Pt$ bond. In this molecular arrangement, Penning ionization takes place more easily at the outer-located alkyl group than at the inner-located $S$ atom. Second, all thiolate species show a weak tail structure extending up to $E_F$, which is assigned to the interface states induced by the $S-Pt$ interactions. The tail structure exhibits a clear Fermi edge, and the intensity relative to the higher-lying Penning bands decreases as the molecular length increases. These features indicate that the interface states are metallic and localized near the $S-Pt$ bond. Finally, resonance ionization followed by Auger neutralization operates on the thiolate monolayers, in competition with Penning ionization. This trend is remarkable for methanethiolate, where the $He^*(2^3S)$ atoms can closely approach the $S$ and $Pt$ atoms, and bands via Penning ionization (except the weak tail) are almost missing. The dominance of resonance ionization means that a large part of methanethiolate is embedded in the metallic wave functions originating from the interface states and substrate bands.

To confirm these findings, we performed the first-principles calculations for $CH_3S$ and $C_3H_7S$ on $Pt(111)$, using the density functional theory. A $(\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{3}) R 30^{\circ}$ overlayer was assumed, based on the structural analysis. $^{22}$ The total energy calculations indicate that the bridge site is more stable than the terminal and threefold hollow site, and the SC bond in thiolate is tilted to about $50^{\circ}$ relative to the surface normal. Figure $3 ~A$ and $B$  show the layer-resolved density of states (LDOS) in the Pt, S, and $C$ layers, where the layer boundary is taken on a bisected plane between the adjacent atoms. For $CH_3S$ on $Pt(111)$, the outer-valence levels are broadened, owing to the orbital hybridizations with the substrate bands. Particularly in the second and third layers including the $S$ atom, energy levels are widely distributed below and above $E_F$ to form the metallic states. The empty states just above $E_F$ are responsible for resonance ionization of $He^*(2^3S)$. The LDOS at $E_F$ decreases rapidly on going from the $S$ layer to the outer layers, indicating that the metallic wave functions attenuate near the methyl group. For $C_3H_7S$ on $Pt(111)$, the electronic states are metallic in the inner layers including the $S$ atom, but insulating in the outer layers at the molecular end, leading to the predominance of Penning ionization. Thus, the $C_3H_7S$ species could be regarded as an amphibious molecule in the sense that the metallic and insulating parts coexist in the molecule.

In the present case, the Pt 5d orbitals extend to the S and adjacent C atoms in alkanethiolate, owing to the formation of the interface states, and attenuate to zero in the outer region. Engelkes et al. measured the $I-V$ traces of $C_{n}H_{2n+1}S$ ($n = 6, 8, 10$) sandwiched by the Pt electrodes and found that the resistance of the junction increases exponentially with chain length, which is characteristic for nonresonant tunneling.³ This is quite reasonable because a great part of long alkyl chain is a good insulator with no resonance state near $E_{F}$. To our knowledge, the $I-V$ trace of small species (such as methanethiolate and ethanedithiolate) has not been reported. However, it is expected that the molecule–metal contacts in such a short junction is barrierless and the ballistic transport operates dominantly. The barrierless contacts have been theoretically predicted for benzenedithiol linked by two Pt electrodes.⁷ Engelkes et al. found that the resistance of alkanethiolates between Pt electrodes is 2 orders of magnitude lower than that between the Au electrodes.³ This may be attributed to the nature of the interface states (we intend to perform the MAES experiments).

### 5. Conclusions
We have identified the interface states induced upon chemisorption of alkanethiols onto Pt(111) using metastable atom

electron spectroscopy. The interface states are derived from the S 3p-Pt 5d mixing, yielding a sharp Fermi edge. The local density of states at the Fermi level decreases rapidly on going from the S atom to the outer C atoms, meaning that the chemisorbed alkanethiols are metallic in character at the S end and insulating at the alkyl end. Our findings enable us to classify ordinary adsorbates on a metal surface into insulating, metallic, and amphibious molecules. This classification would be useful for understanding ordinary charge transport at the molecule−metal interface, as in the case of a well-known classification into hydrophilic, hydrophobic, and amphiphilic molecules used in molecule−water interactions.
