# The Structure and Bonding of $\mathbf{Au_{25}(SR)_{18}}$ Nanoclusters from EXAFS: The Interplay of Metallic and Molecular Behavior

Mark A. MacDonald, Daniel M. Chevrier, and Peng Zhang*

Department of Chemistry and Institute for Research in Materials, Dalhousie University, Halifax, NS, B3H 4J3, Canada

Huifeng Qian and Rongchao Jin

Department of Chemistry, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15213, United States

![](./images/811647300400054273_1.jpg)

**ABSTRACT:** While recent advances in the synthesis and crystallography of gold−thiolate nanoclusters have led to enormous progress in understanding the structure and bonding of gold thiolate nanomaterials, the changes in structure experienced by these nanomaterials in different conditions, such as low temperature and solvation, is still largely lacking. Herein, we report a temperature- and solvation-dependent extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) study of thiolate-protected $Au_{25}$ nanoclusters. The structural changes experienced by $Au_{25}$ in response to low temperature and different solvation environments are illustrated in great detail using a site-specific EXAFS fitting approach consisting of one $Au−S$ and three $Au−Au$ shells. EXAFS simulations as well as ab initio calculations of the local density of states are used to support the EXAFS analysis and correlate the structural characteristics of $Au_{25}$ with its electronic properties. These experimental and theoretical studies point out the existence of the interplay between the “metallic” behavior of the $Au_{13}$ core and the “molecular” behavior of the six $RS−Au−S(R)−Au−SR$ “staple” units within $Au_{25}$, which may shed light on its catalytic mechanism and aid in the design and synthesis of other gold−thiolate nanoclusters by balancing the metallic and molecular interactions.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Understanding the atomic structure of gold−thiolate nanoparticles represents an important area of research for both fundamental studies and technological applications of nanomaterials.¹² Recent advances in the synthesis and crystallization of ultrasmall (<2 nm) gold−thiolate nanoclusters³⁻⁶ has led to a deeper understanding of the atomic structure of these materials and their structure−property relationships.⁶⁻⁸ However, many questions still exist about the way in which these nanoclusters will behave in complex systems with many external variables, such as temperature,⁹ additive solute(s)¹⁰¹¹ and various dispersing solvents.¹²¹³ A thorough structural and electronic characterization of the $[TOA]^{+}[Au_{25}(SCH_{2}CH_{2}Ph)_{18}]^{-1}$ (TOA = tetraoctylammonium) nanocluster has emerged from the literature over recent years.¹⁴ The well-documented and highly symmetric structure of this nanocluster offers a unique opportunity to study the structure of gold−thiolate nanomaterials in different environments to observe how the structure and electronic properties of these gold−thoilate nanomaterials are affected by external variables.

To gain further insight into the structure and bonding of these materials, we present a Au $L_{3}$-edge extended X-ray absorption spectroscopy fine structure (EXAFS) study of the Au-thiolate cluster $Au_{25}$ in solution using both toluene and acetonitrile as solvents, and the solid phase at low (10 K) and room temperatures (300 K). The structural changes experienced by $Au_{25}$ in response to low temperature and different solvation environments are investigated in great detail and compared with EXAFS simulations as well as ab initio calculations of the local density of states (I-DOS). The interplay between the “metallic” behavior of the $Au_{13}$ core and the “molecular” behavior of the six $RS−Au−S(R)−Au−SR$ “staple” units within $Au_{25}$ is highlighted in this work.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

### 2.1. $\mathbf{Au_{25}}$ Synthesis.
The synthesis of ultrapure $[TOA]^{+}[Au_{25}(SCH_{2}CH_{2}Ph)_{18}]^{-}$ nanoclusters was described in our previous work.⁶ Briefly, in a typical synthesis, $HAuCl_{4}·3H_{2}O$ (0.1576 g, 0.4 mmol) was dissolved in 5 mL Nanopure water, and tetraoctylammonium bromide (TOAB, 0.2558 g, 0.47 mmol) was dissolved in 10 mL toluene. The toluene solution of Au(III) was cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath over a period of ~30 min under constant magnetic stirring. After overnight, the aqueous layer at the bottom of the flask was removed, and the toluene solution was dried by rotaevaporation. Ethanol was added to separate Au₂₅ nanoclusters from TOAB and sideproducts (e.g., disulfide). Pure Au₂₅ nanoclusters were collected by extracting with acetonitrile. Crystallization of Au₂₅ nanoclusters was performed in a mixed toluene/ethanol (1/4 v/v) solution.

2.2. X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy Measurement, Fitting, Simulation and I-DOS Calculation. The Au L₃-edge EXAFS measurements were conducted in transmission mode at the PNC/XSD beamline of the Advance Photon Source at Argonne National Laboratories in Argonne, IL. A Si(111) monochromator crystal was used in conjunction with a platinum harmonic rejection mirror. For solid-state measurements, a toluene solution of Au₂₅ was drop-cast onto polyimide (Kapton) tape and folded until ample signal was observed ($\Delta\mu_0 \approx$ 0.5−1.1). Solution-phase measurements were recorded by dispersing Au₂₅ in toluene or acetonitrile in a Teflon sample cell with polyimide windows. Low-temperature measurements were conducted using a liquid He cryostat.

Data reduction was performed using the WinXAS program and following the standard procedures.⁷,¹³ Simulation of EXAFS phase and amplitude along with angular momentum density of states (I-DOS) was performed using the FEFF8 program code¹⁵,¹⁶ and the crystalline structure of Au₂₅ previously reported.⁶

In the EXAFS refinement $S_0^2$ was fixed at a value of 0.9 (deduced from the fitting of Au foil) while the coordination number (CN) values of different Au−S and Au−Au coordination shells were fixed based upon the crystal structure of Au₂₅ (Scheme 1). First, a preliminary refinement of the Fourier transform of $\chi(k)\cdot k^3$ ($\text{FT}[\chi(k)\cdot k^3]$) was performed using one Au−S and one Au−Au shell for each sample following our previously reported site-specific procedure.¹⁶ Next, all parameters associated with the Au−S bond obtained from the preliminary refinement were fixed, and the refinement with the site-specific approach using three Au−Au shells (scheme 1) was performed with correlated $E_0$ values. This yielded values of $R$ and $\sigma^2$ for each shell. All refinements of the FT-EXAFS of Au₂₅ were conducted with a $k$-range of 3−14.5 Å⁻¹ for solution phase samples and 3−12 Å⁻¹ for solid state samples. An $R$-range of 1.64−3.06 Å was used in the refinement of the 300 K EXAFS, while a range of 1.6−3.3 Å was used for all other spectra. The number of independent variables used in all the refinement is 7, whereas the maximum numbers of independent variables calculated by the WinXAS program are 10−14. A general error estimate of 0.01 Å was provided for inner shell bond lengths and 0.02 Å for outer shell bond lengths (i.e., (Au−Au)₃) by systematically comparing the fitting results from different scans of each sample.

## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1. Site-Specific Bonding of Au₂₅. A detailed discussion of the structure of Au₂₅ has been previously reported in dedicated research articles.⁵,⁶ In general, the structure consists of a 13-atom icosahedral Au core (12 surface and 1 central Au) surrounded by six dimeric RS−Au−S(R)−Au−SR “staple” units. These staples are directly anchored to the surface via Au−S bonding, while the staple Au atoms have been observed to interact with the icosahedral surface via long-range gold−gold bonds. An interesting attribute of Au₂₅ for EXAFS analysis is how the structure breaks into several distinct bonding domains (Scheme 1). The first Au−Au group consists of short bonds (~2.8 Å) between the central Au atom of the icosahedral core and the 12 surface Au atoms, with several short bonds also existing between adjacent surface Au atoms (Scheme 1, (Au−Au)₁). These six short surface bonds are located directly beneath the apical sulfur of each staple unit. The second Au−Au shell is composed of midlength Au−Au bonds (~2.95 Å) located entirely on the surface of the icosahedral core between adjacent surface Au atoms (scheme 1, (Au−Au)₂), while the third Au−Au shell, composed of long-range (~3.15 Å) surface−staple interactions, exists between staple Au atoms and the icosahedral surface (Scheme 1, (Au−Au)₃). The breakdown of Au₂₅ into these different bonding domains is clearly visible in the simulated $\text{FT}[\chi(k)\cdot k^3]$


spectra (Figure 1). By following the bonding of $Au_{25}$ via these different groups of bond lengths, we can probe the solvation and temperature dependence of the bonding in this cluster from a site-specific perspective and gain deeper insight as to the physical properties of this cluster.

In general, the EXAFS refinement reveals average bond lengths that are in good agreement with the previously reported crystal structure of $Au_{25}^{5,6}$ The large mean squared displacement ($\sigma^{2}$) for each group of bonds can be attributed both to the distribution of bond lengths within the cluster (static disorder, $\sigma^{2}_{static}$), as well as the low-coordinate Au bonding environment (dynamic disorder, $\sigma^{2}_{dynamic}$), which has been shown previously to cause an increase in the mean square displacement of coordination environments. $^{17}$ From the EXAFS refinement, a close comparison of the structural changes that occur upon temperature reduction can be made.

In general, a decrease in bond length is experienced within the 13-atom icosahedral core of the $Au_{25}$ nanocluster upon temperature reduction. This is accompanied by an apparent increase in the average surface-staple (i.e. as the core contracts the surface staple distance becomes longer) $(Au-Au)_{3}$ bond length. It is interesting to note the large effect of temperature on the mean squared displacement for the $(Au-Au)_{3}$ bonds. This could possibly be attributed to the highly variable nature of the $(Au-Au)_{3}$ bond length ($\sigma^{2}_{static}$), or the local chemical environment of the $Au_{25}$ staple gold ($\sigma^{2}_{dynamic}$) being more highly affected by the temperature reduction.

Previous studies on the temperature-dependent bonding of both bulk gold and gold−thiolate nanoparticles have shown that a contraction of the lattice should be expected with temperature decrease. $^{18,19}$ Indeed this is not uncommon behavior for metallic nanoparticle systems, with only a few examples of negative or zero thermal expansion having been reported among supported metal nanoparticles. $^{9,18,20,21}$ What is interesting to note, however, is the differing thermal behavior of the metallic icosahedral

Interestingly, the more “molecular” S−Au−S−Au−S staple system demonstrates significantly less response to the temperature change than the “metallic” 13-atom core. It has been proposed recently that bonding interactions exist between the surface and staple Au sites and are essential to the robustness of these materials. $^{22−24}$ The lack of thermal contraction of the staple Au could then be due to several reasons. One possibility could be associated with the local atomic environment of the staple Au. Whereas Au-complexes typically maintain S−Au−S bond angles of $\sim 180^{\circ}$, the cost of distorting this approximately linear S−Au−S geometry versus the gain in Au−Au bonding with the $Au_{13}$ surface may not be worthwhile. $^{25}$ However, another possible reason for the differing thermal behavior could be associated with the R-group of the thiol ligand. Crystallographic data has shown that in the solid state, the aromatic units of phenylethanethiol group together to accommodate $\pi−\pi$ interactions in the surrounding ligand network of the nanocluster (Figure 4). $^{5,6}$ As a result, it is plausible that it is the hardness of these aromatic ligand tail interactions that prevents the staple systems from contracting with the relatively soft $Au_{13}$ core, rather than the local electronic environment of the staple Au (i.e., Au−S−Au and/or S−Au−S bond angle).

It is noted that the site-specific nature of this contraction could potentially be exploited in the design of other gold−thiolate nanostructures with controlled physical and/or chemical properties. Previous studies have demonstrated that both chemical and optical behavior can be attributed to local atomic environments in $Au_{25}$ and other nanoclusters. $^{6,22,26}$ It is envisioned that new stable mono- or multimetallic nanocluster systems could be developed by balancing different metal−metal (core−surface and surface−staple), metal−ligand (ligand−surface and ligand−staple), and ligand−ligand (tailgroup−tailgroup) interactions. $^{23,24,27,28}$

### 3.3. Solvation Dependent Bonding.
A critical question in nanoscience pertains to the structure of nanomaterials in their applied environment. While solid-state structures of nanomaterials are invaluable in the information they provide, ultimately the synthesis and applications of these materials occur in a solvated environment. To gain further insight into the interplay of the metallic and molecular portions of $Au_{25}$, we next compare the structure of $Au_{25}$ solvated in toluene and acetonitrile to the solid state structure at 300 K. Table 1 compares the structure of $Au_{25}$ nanoclusters when solvated in toluene and acetonitrile versus the solid state at 300 K. The absolute and imaginary components and their refinements of the Fourier transform of $\chi(k)\cdot k^{3}$ are shown in Figure 2c,d.

By comparing the EXAFS results of the solid state (300 K) and solution-phase samples, a general decrease in the bond length of the $Au_{13}$ core $((Au-Au)_{1}, (Au-Au)_{2})$ is observed with an accompanying increase in the $(Au-Au)_{3}$ bond length of the cluster when solvated in toluene (Figure 3). This trend is the same as what was observed in the temperature dependent study (see discussions above); however, while in the temperature dependent study, the observed structural changes of the nanomaterial can be rationalized by the metallic nature of the cluster core; in this scenario the observed structural change is purely a result of solvation.

This idea is supported by the general decrease in the mean squared displacement (Figure 5) of the $(Au-Au)_3$ bond length observed upon cluster solvation in toluene (Table 1). Whereas these comparisons were conducted at constant temperature, this suggests less static disorder in this group of surface-staple Au bonds. This idea is also supported by the solid state structure of the neutral form of $Au_{25}$ previously reported. $^{30}$ In the crystal structure of the neutral $Au_{25}$ cluster, the bulky TOA⁺ cation is absent; as a result, the ligand tailgroups surrounding $Au_{25}$ are found to adopt a slightly different conformation than in the anionic form of $Au_{25}$.

It is interesting to compare the behavior of $Au_{25}$ with that of the closely related $Au_{38}$ structure upon solvation in toluene. It has been previously observed that upon solvation, $Au_{38}$ experiences a general increase in bond length throughout the cluster. $^{13}$ This differing behavior can possibly be attributed to several factors. First, whereas $Au_{38}$ exists in the neutral charge state, it does not need to accommodate a counterion into the solid-state packing structure. As a result, the bonding in the solid-state of $Au_{38}$ may have less ligand-induced strain than $Au_{25}$ and thus lead to a different solvation-induced structural change. This is somewhat supported by considering the differing structural disorder within the structure of the neutral $Au_{25}$ cluster when compared to the anionic cluster. In addition, the neutral charge state of $Au_{38}$ will make the cluster inherently more hydrophilic and thus may further influence the interaction between the cluster and the solvent. Whereas $Au_{25}$ exists as an anion, the ligand behavior may be influenced by this formal electrostatic charge. A detailed comparison of the solvation behavior of $Au_{25}$ and $Au_{38}$ is difficult to make, however, due to the larger degree of structural disorder in $Au_{38}$. This makes analyzing the solvation behavior from a sitespecific perspective much more difficult than with $Au_{25}$.

Comparing the solvation effect of acetonitrile on $Au_{25}$ a more pronounced decrease in bond length is observed within the $Au_{13}$ core $((Au-Au)_1$ and $(Au-Au)_2$ shells) relative to the toluene system (Figure 3). However, following solvation, the $(Au-Au)_3$ bond lengths stay approximately the same as the solid state, 300 K sample (Table 1). Whereas polar solvents such as acetonitrile lack any kind of aromatic organic structural unit, one possible explanation for the differing solvated structure could arise from an inability of acetonitrile to disrupt the $\pi-\pi$ interactions of the ligand shell, $^{29}$ as a result, the ligand-induced strain on the nanocluster is not reduced as effectively as with toluene. This could potentially explain the smaller reduction in the $(Au-Au)_3$ mean square displacement observed when dispersed in acetonitrile (Figure 5). As a result of the different interactions of the ligand tailgroups of $Au_{25}$ and the nonaromatic dispersing solvent, slightly different structural properties are observed within the cluster.

Whereas acetonitrile represents a Lewis basic solvent, it seems reasonable that coordination to the $Au_{25}$ surface may also be possible upon solvation. This idea develops relatively intuitively, whereas Lewis basic nitrogen moieties (amine, cyanide, etc.) have long been known to have a high affinity for coordinating to gold. It appears, as a result of interaction with the solvent, that an electronic interaction could take place which causes the $Au_{13}$ core to contract while leaving the ligand shell, and thus the $(Au-Au)_3$ bond length, relatively unaffected. What is interesting, however, is the observation that acetonitrile is interacting with the relatively "metallic" $Au_{13}$ cluster core directly, rather than the "molecular" staple Au. A reason for this may follow from the consideration of the electronic properties of these two Au environments.

It has been shown previously that the Au atoms in the thoroughly studied $Au_{102}$ nanocluster exhibit different electronic distributions about each coordination environment, that is, the Au atoms of the staple sites in $Au_{102}$ exhibit a higher occupation of their sp-band versus their core-Au counterparts. $^{22}$ This change in electronic character is rationalized as a direct result of the local bonding topology of the system. It has also been shown that amine molecules interact with gold through a donation of their lone-pair electrons into the Au 6s orbital in single molecular junctions. $^{31}$ Extending this rationale to $Au_{25}$, it seems reasonable to observe preferential interaction of acetonitrile with the available unoccupied s-states of the $Au_{13}$ core rather than the Au staple sites due to the higher density of unoccupied s-states of the

core (see Figure 6). Indeed, the calculated s,p,d-electron counts presented in Figure 6 clearly illustrate the anticorrelated s−d electron density of $Au_{25}$ from a site-specific perspective, that is, the metallic $Au_{13}$ unit (core and surface sites) has higher d-electron and lower s-electron density than the staple Au site, which is consistent with previous density functional theory (DFT) calculations that predict a largely sp-character lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) composed primarily of Au core atomic orbital character.⁶

## 4. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the structure and bonding of $Au_{25}$ nanoclusters have been examined under varied external environmental conditions such as low temperature and different solvents from a site-specific perspective via EXAFS. EXAFS simulations as well as ab initio calculations of the l-DOS are used to support the EXAFS analysis and correlate the structural characteristics of $Au_{25}$ with its site-specific electronic properties. The structural changes observed for $Au_{25}$ illustrate the interplay of the "metallic" and "molecular" bonding environments and its role upon the overall physical and chemical properties of this nanocluster. These findings will be useful in developing a deeper understanding of the structure−property relationships of $Au_{25}$ such as its catalytic mechanism in various solvents and aid in the rational design and synthesis of other gold−thiolate nanoclusters by balancing the metallic and molecular interactions.
