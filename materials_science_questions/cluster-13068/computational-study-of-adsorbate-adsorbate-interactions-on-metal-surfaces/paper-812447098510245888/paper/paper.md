Received 10 October 1979; accepted for publication 18 December 1979

The discharge of hydrogen ions at the metal-electrolyte interface is investigated with attention to both chemisorptive bonding interactions and hydration effects. Due to the strongness of the interactions, tunneling approximations are inadequate. In this work we describe the metal-electrolyte system in the framework of the Anderson-Newns model and obtain the Hartree-Fock solution for the adiabatic energy along the reaction path. The charging behavior and activation energy are studied as functions of the parameters describing the metal and electrolyte. The results show gradual bond formation, smooth reaction barriers, and smaller activation energies than obtained by tunneling estimates.

## 1. Introduction

The elementary process of hydrogen ion discharge at electrodes is of interest both fundamentally and technologically [1]. Much attention has been devoted to understanding the factors determining the rate of the process

$$\mathrm{Me}^{-}+\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+} \rightarrow \mathrm{MH}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O} \text {, }\tag{1}$$

in which a hydrated proton is neutralized by a metal electron and becomes chemisorbed. The rate is known to be sensitive to material parameters and most importantly to the strength with which the metal chemisorbs H [2,3].

The current theories of change transfer reactions [4] are based on tunneling and, although appropriate for cases of weak coupling to the surface, the tunneling picture is at best a crude representation of the formation of chemisorption bonds. An approach to the study of reaction (1) which allows full consideration of the resonant electron hopping charac- terizing the MH bond may be based on the Anderson hamiltonian. In addition to providing a more accurate reaction barrier it may be used to derive explicitly various trends in the reaction rate as functions of the parameters defining the electrode and electrolyte.

## 2. Reaction path and adiabatic energy

Data on the hydrogen evolution reaction show that (1) is a thermally activated process. The temperature dependence of the current (or rate) in an electrochemical cell at moderate (<50 mV) overpotential $\eta$ is

$$\ln i=-\left(E_{\mathrm{act}}+\alpha \eta\right) / k_{\mathrm{B}} T+\text { constant },$$

where the transfer coefficient $\alpha \approx 0.5$ and $E_{\text {act }}$ is the activation energy.

The activation barrier has its origin in the large proton hydration energy which places $\epsilon_{\mathrm{a}}$, the electron energy level on the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$, *above* the Fermi level of most metal electrodes [5]. While $\epsilon_{a}=-I=-13.6 \mathrm{eV}$ for a bare proton, the value in aqueous solution is

$$\epsilon_{a}(y)=-I+L(y)+R(y)-e V_{\mathrm{H}},\tag{2}$$

where $V_{\mathrm{H}}$ is the electrostatic potential at the $\mathrm{H}, L$ is the hydration energy and $R$ is the repulsive potential energy of $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$. Both these functions depend on an internal coordinate, $y$, describing the degree of excitation of the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$. Usually, this reaction coordinate is described as the length of the $\mathrm{H}^{+}-\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ bond [4]. In the equilibrium configuration $y=y_{0}$ the hydration energy is large: $L\left(y_{0}\right)=11.6 \mathrm{eV}$ and $R\left(y_{0}\right) \approx 1 \mathrm{eV}$. As shown in fig. 1, the placement of $\varepsilon_{a}\left(y_{0}\right)$ makes electron transfer energetically unfavored.

In the usual description, the charge transfer process occurs only if $\epsilon_{a}(y)=\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}$, which is the condition for radiationless tunneling of an electron from the metal to the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$. This situation requires a preliminary thermal excitation of the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$, which lowers $\epsilon_{a}(y)$, and the energy necessary is the activation energy. Although this tunneling picture may give a qualitative understanding of the effect of chemisorption on the activation energy, it is an unphysical description of the bond formation and charging behavior. Typical chemisorption energies of $2 \mathrm{eV}$ correspond to electron hopping rates $\sim 10^{15} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ which is fast on the time scale of molecular vibrations with frequencies $\sim 10^{13} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$. It becomes necessary to account for the return tunneling of the electron back into the electrode.

## 3. Model Hamiltonian and Hartree-Fock energy

To investigate the bonding of the hydrogen ion to the metal substrate it is natural to apply the Anderson-Newns model [6] of chemisorption theory. Here it must be modified to include hydration effects and thermal excitation of the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$during the charge transfer reaction. The metal/electrolyte system is represented by the Hamiltonian

$$
\mathscr{H}=\sum_{k, \sigma} \epsilon_{k} c_{k \sigma}^{\dagger} c_{k \sigma}+\sum_{\sigma} \epsilon_{\sigma}(y) c_{a \sigma}^{\dagger} c_{a \sigma}+\beta^{\prime} \sum_{k, \sigma}\left(c_{k \sigma}^{\dagger} c_{a \sigma}+c_{a \sigma}^{\dagger} c_{k \sigma}\right), \tag{4}
$$

where $c_{k \sigma}^{\dagger}$ and $c_{a \sigma}^{\dagger}$ are creation operators for electrons of spin $\sigma$ in the band state $k$ and on the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$, respectively. The first term describes the metal electrode, which in the calculations below is specialized to a single, elliptical tight-binding d-band of width $4 \beta$ and Fermi level $\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}$. The second term describes the absorbate and includes electron-electron correlation on the proton within the Hartree-Fock approximation:

$$
\epsilon_{\sigma}(y)=\epsilon_{a}(y)+U n_{-\sigma}.
$$

Here $\epsilon_{a}(y)$ is the electron energy level on the ion, $U$ is the electron correlation strength and $n_{\sigma}=\left\langle c_{a \sigma}^{\dagger} c_{a \sigma}\right\rangle$, the expectation value of charge of spin $\sigma$ on the adsorbate. The last term of (5) is the coupling, with strength $\beta^{\prime}$ taken to be a constant. Note that in the present context the adsorbate level $\epsilon_{a}(y)$ contains hydration terms and is a function of $y$, as given in (2).

The model may be solved [6] to determine the chemisorption energy, $\Delta E$, defined as the change in energy resulting from coupling the (neutral) $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ to the metal. It is a function of $\epsilon_{a}(y)$, hence $y$, and may be expressed

$$
\Delta E(y)=\sum_{\sigma} \Delta E^{1 \sigma}-U n_{\sigma} n_{-\sigma}-\epsilon_{a}(y).
$$

Measuring energy in units of $2 \beta$ and letting $B=2 \beta^{\prime 2}, \Delta E^{1 \sigma}$ is given by the

following integrals:

for $\epsilon_{\sigma}+1-B>0$,

$$
\Delta E^{1 \sigma}=\pi^{-1} \int_{1}^{\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}} \tan _{\pi}^{-1} C \mathrm{~d} \epsilon ; \tag{5a}
$$

for $\epsilon_{\sigma}+1-B<0$

$$
\Delta E^{1 \sigma}=\pi^{-1} \int_{-1}^{\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}} \tan _{\pi}^{-1} C \mathrm{~d} \epsilon+\epsilon_{l \sigma} ; \tag{5b}
$$

where

$$
C=\frac{-B\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)^{1 / 2}}{(B-1) \epsilon+\epsilon_{\sigma}}.
$$

$$
\epsilon_{l \sigma}=\frac{(1-B) \epsilon_{\sigma}+B\left(2 B+\epsilon_{\sigma}^{2}-1\right)^{1 / 2}}{1-2 B}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}},
$$

and $0<\tan _{\pi}^{-1}<\pi,-\pi<\tan _{-\pi}^{-1}<0$. The charge $n_{\sigma}$ must be determined by the self-consistency condition

$$
n_{\sigma}=\partial E^{1 \sigma} / \partial \epsilon_{\sigma}. \tag{6}
$$

Eqs. (5) and (6) are solved by numerical iteration. The total system energy, measured relative to the energy of separated reactants, is

$$
E(y)=E_{P}^{0}(y)+\Delta E(y).
$$

A typical example of the energy curve calculated by this procedure is shown as the solid line in fig. 2. The charge on the adsorbate gradually increases from $n=0$ to the full chemisorption value as $y$ increases, and the arrows in fig. 2 show where the $10 \%$ and $90 \%$ values are attained. The rounding of the reaction barrier due to the coupling is a pronounced feature of these calculations and is discussed below.

The height of the reaction barrier depends on the various parameters entering the model. For given $\phi, L, R, V_{\mathrm{M}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{H}}$, the point $\mathrm{G}$ of fig. 2 is fixed and defines the Gurney value of the activation energy $E_{\text {act. }}^{0}$. The coupling causes the actual barrier to be lower, and the degree of lowering,

$$
\delta E_{\text {act }}=E_{\text {act }}^{0}-E_{\text {act }},
$$

depends on $\beta, \beta^{\prime}, \epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}$ and $U$. The dependence of $\delta E_{\text {act }}$ on these parameters will now be discussed.

Near the point $\mathrm{G}$ the curves $L$ and $R$ may be linearly approximated, and following Bockris [1,4] we shall assume the slopes to be equal. ## 4. Discussion

The model Hamiltonian approach provides a simple phenomenological description of the coupling of the $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$to the electrode during neutralization. Parameters relevant to a particular metal/electrolyte system can at present only be obtained from the corresponding vacuum chemisorption data. The influence of solvent molecules on the process enter this study solely through the hydration energy contribution to $\epsilon_{a}(y)$. Other dielectric effects which could alter the vacuum chemisorption values of $\beta^{\prime}$ and $U$ are expected to be less important, and are presently being investigated.

In general, $\beta'$ and $U$ are themselves dependent on both $y$ and $x$, the M-H separation. To calculate reaction *entropy*, the dependence on $x$ would be necessary, but for our present concern with activation *energy* it is irrelevant. Elementary considerations of molecular orbital overlap suggest that $\beta'$ may be lowered by at most a factor $\sqrt{3}$ compared to the vacuum case, however the effect on bonding energy is partially counteracted by a similar lowering of $U$. The coupling in any case remains strong and the contention holds that a non-perturbative approach is necessary.

The striking feature of the above numerical results is that $E_{\text{act}}$ is reduced by typically $2\,\text{eV}$ below the predictions of the tunneling theories. The mathematical reason for this is clear. The coupling term

$$
\mathscr{H}^{\rho'}=\beta'\sum_{k\sigma}\left(c_{k\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{a\sigma}+c_{a\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{k\sigma}\right)
$$

applied to the non-interacting ground state with $\epsilon_{a}<\epsilon_{\text{F}}$ gives a leading perturbation correction $W$:

$$
W\sim\beta'^{2}/(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{\text{F}}).
$$

This value is larger for $\epsilon_{a}\approx\epsilon_{\text{F}}$ than for the chemisorption case where, $\epsilon_{a}$ is several volts below $\epsilon_{\text{F}}$. Physically, the resonant exchange of the electron is more effective in lowering the system energy when $\epsilon_{a}\approx\epsilon_{\text{F}}$.

This method allows one to quantify the barrier rounding that occurs in the chemisorptive neutralization process, and can form a basis for more quantitative studies using more refined estimates of the coupling parameters and hydration functions in the metal/electrolyte environment.
