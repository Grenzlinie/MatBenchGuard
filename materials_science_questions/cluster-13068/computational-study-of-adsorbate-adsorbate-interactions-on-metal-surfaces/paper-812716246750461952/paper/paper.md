The two-dimensional superfluid transition in H $\downarrow$

E V L Mello and G M Carneiro

Departamento de Física, Pontifícia Universidade Católica, CxP 38071, Rio de Janeiro, RJ,
Brazil

Received 15 April 1985, in final form 19 September 1985

Abstract. The surface density as a function of temperature for H $\downarrow$ adsorbed on superfluid
helium film is studied for several gas densities. The theory used is exact in the low-density
limit and predicts Kosterlitz-Thouless transition temperatures lower than those predicted
by previous calculations, and also predicts lower saturation density than predicted
previously. The results confirm the possibility of a 2D superfluid transition under conditions
close to those achieved experimentally.

### 1. Introduction

It is now well established that atomic hydrogen can be stabilised against molecular
recombination by polarising the electron spin (Silvera and Walraven 1980, Hardy *et al*
1980, Cline *et al* 1980). Densities as high as $5 \times 10^{17}$ atoms cm$^{-3}$ have already been
reached. The largeness of the H $\downarrow$ quantum parameter ($\eta = \hbar^2/M\varepsilon\sigma^2$, $\varepsilon$ and $\sigma$ are
Lennard-Jones (L-J) interatomic constants) implies that it does not liquefy down to $T =$
0 K. Effort has been devoted to achieving sufficiently high densities or low temperatures
to observe Bose-Einstein (BE) condensation. To avoid the highly reactive hydrogen
atoms recombining at the container walls and therefore decreasing the gas density,
current experiments are performed with a thick superfluid helium film as a neutral
coating for the vessel containing H $\downarrow$. Nevertheless, some atoms are adsorbed on the
walls since the H $\downarrow$-helium adsorption energy is known to be $\sim 1$ K (Goldman and
Silvera 1981, Morrow *et al* 1981). Therefore the properties of the H $\downarrow$ film can be
observed at the same time that the properties of H $\downarrow$ gas are being studied.

A two-dimensional Bose fluid is known (Bishop and Reppy (1978); for a review,
see Reppy (1980)) to undergo a Kosterlitz-Thouless (KT) transition (Kosterlitz and
Thouless 1972) into a superfluid phase. Edwards and Mantz (1980) (EM) suggested that
the adsorbed H $\downarrow$ film becomes superfluid at higher temperatures and lower bulk
densities than those necessary for BE condensation in the H $\downarrow$ gas. They reached this
conclusion by calculating the film density assuming that at low temperatures the shift in
the film chemical potential due to adatom-adatom interactions could be approximated
by the $T = 0$ K value, which they estimated to lowest order in the density. A similar
method was used by Silvera and Goldman (1980) to obtain the film density. In a
subsequent calculation Mello *et al* (1983) (MRV) obtained the H $\downarrow$ film density using
the quantum virial expansion which provided temperature-dependent corrections to the
chemical potential and reached similar conclusions about the KT transition.

In this article we attempt to improve upon these calculations by using results for the correction to the film chemical potential that are exact to lowest order in density at $T=0$ K (Schick 1971) and for finite temperature (Vetrovec and Carneiro 1980). The Vetrovec and Carneiro (VC) theory does not take into consideration the effects of vortex excitations or fluctuation in the condensate phase, which are known to be important below the KT transition temperature, $T_{\mathrm{KT}}$. Thus the VC results are valid only above $T_{\mathrm{KT}}$. They reduce to the virial expansion truncated after the second term for $n_{\mathrm{s}} \lambda^{2} \ll 1$ ($n_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the surface density and $\lambda$ the de Broglie thermal wavelength). Since at the KT transition $n_{\mathrm{s}} \lambda^{2}=4$ (assuming all the fluid becomes superfluid) the VC result yields more accurate values of the film density in this region. In the narrow region between $T_{\mathrm{KT}}$ and $T=0$ the chemical potential is interpolated as described below.

Current experiments with $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$ are performed in magnetic fields of typically $B=$ $10 \mathrm{~T}$ and at temperatures of about $1 \mathrm{~K}$. In such conditions $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$ has two states; $|a\rangle=$ $|\downarrow \uparrow\rangle-\varepsilon|\uparrow \downarrow\rangle$ and $|b\rangle=|\downarrow \downarrow\rangle$ (the first (second) arrow represents the electron (proton) spin). The fact that state $|a\rangle$ has a component with the 'wrong' electron spin was exploited to create a gas of pure state $|b\rangle$ (Cline et al 1981, Sprick et al 1982). Thus, we perform calculations both for the single-type hydrogen and for a two-component mixture of states $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ with equal concentration.

## 2. Calculation

Equilibrium between the bulk $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$ gas and the adsorbed $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$ film occurs when the chemical potentials of the two systems are equal. At the densities and temperatures of experimental interest the $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$ gas can be considered a classical ideal gas (Mello 1983). Thus we can write
$$
\mu_{\mathrm{G}}(n, T)=k_{\mathrm{B}} T \ln \left(n \lambda^{3}\right)
$$
where $n$ is the gas density. The film chemical potential can be written as
$$
\mu_{\mathrm{F}}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)=E_{\mathrm{a}}+\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)
$$
with the adsorption energy $E_{\mathrm{a}}=0.9 k$ (Goldman and Silvera 1981) and where $n_{\mathrm{s}}$ is the surface $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$ density.

At low densities the binary interactions between the particles are the most important correction to the ideal case. VC derived thermodynamic expressions that are exact to leading order in the density in terms of two-body scattering amplitudes and phase shifts. They obtained the following expression for $\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}$ :
$$
\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)=\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}^{0}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)+\Delta \mu\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)
$$
where $\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}^{0}\left(\mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)=k_{\mathrm{B}} T \ln \left[1-\exp \left(-n_{\mathrm{s}} \lambda^{2}\right)\right]$ is the ideal 2D Bose gas chemical potential and
$$
\Delta \mu\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)=\sum_{p p^{\prime}} f_{p p^{\prime}}\left(\partial n_{p}^{0} / \partial \varepsilon_{p}^{0}\right) n_{p^{\prime}}^{0} / \sum_{p} \partial n_{p}^{0} / \partial \varepsilon_{p}^{0}
$$
with $\varepsilon_{p}^{0}=p^{2} / 2 M$ and $n_{p}^{0}=\left\{\exp \left[\left(\varepsilon_{p}^{0}-\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}^{0}\right) / k_{\mathrm{B}} T\right]-1\right\}^{-1}$. The function $f_{p p^{\prime}}$ is the energy of interaction of a particle of momentum $p$ with a particle of momentum $p^{\prime}$ and in $2 \mathrm{D}$ is given by
$$
f_{p p^{\prime}}=-\frac{4}{M A} \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta_{m}(k)
$$

where $\delta_{m}(k)$ is the scattering phase shift for partial waves of relative angular momentum $m$, $k = |\boldsymbol{p} - \boldsymbol{p}'|/2$, $M$ is the particle mass and $A$ is the area of the system. For identical spinless particles the above sum is restricted to either even or odd values of $m$ and has an additional factor of two due to the particle indistinguishability.

The phase shifts are calculated by solving the Schrödinger equation numerically by the Numerov method (Chow 1972). The function $f_{pp'}$ is constructed by a polynomial interpolation for several values of $k$ and the double sum in (4) is converted to a triple integral that is evaluated by an extension of the Romberg method (Stiefel 1963). The H-H potentials are well known (Kolos and Wolneiwicz 1965) and the best analytical form for the triplet potential is the Silvera fitting (Mello 1983) which, in atomic units, is
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(r)= & \exp (0.09678-1.10173 r-0.039 r^{2}) \\
& +C(r)\left(6.5 / r^{6}+124 / r^{8}+3285 / r^{10}\right)
\end{aligned}\tag{6}
$$
with
$$
C(r)= \begin{cases}1 & r>10.0378 \\ \exp -(10.0378 / r-1)^{2} & r \leqslant 10.0378.\end{cases}
$$

To calculate the $T=0$ K chemical potential we used the results of Schick (1971). Since the above potential is very weakly attractive we used a hard disc potential of radius equal to the 3D scattering length. In this case $\mu_{2D}$ is given by
$$
\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T=0\right)=-\left(4 \pi \hbar^{2} / M\right) n_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\ln n_{\mathrm{s}} a^{2}\right)^{-1}.\tag{7}
$$

To lowest order in $n_{\mathrm{s}}$. The scattering length $a=0.727 \AA$ was calculated using effective-range theory by MRV for the potential of (5).

In figure 1 we plot the VC chemical potential as a function of temperature for $n_{\mathrm{s}}=$ $10^{13} \mathrm{~cm}^{-2}$. We see that the VC result does not reduce Schick's value at $T=0 \mathrm{~K}$. This is expected since the VC calculations are not valid for $T<T_{\mathrm{KT}}$. In the region $0<T<T_{\mathrm{KT}}$ we use a linear interpolation between $\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}$ calculated within these limits. Other interpolation procedures were studied and they do not change the results of this article in any appreciable manner. We expect this to be a reasonable procedure since the KT region is a narrow temperature interval for the densities of interest and $\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}$ is a continuous function of the temperature.

The above results apply to single-component $H \downarrow$ . For equal concentration mixtures of states $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ the chemical potential is given by
$$
\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}^{\mathrm{MIX}}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}}, T\right)=\mu_{2 \mathrm{D}}^{0}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}} / 2, T\right)+\Delta \mu^{\mathrm{MIX}}\left(n_{\mathrm{s}} / 2, T\right)\tag{8}
$$
where $\Delta \mu^{\mathrm{MIX}}$ is given by (4) with $f_{pp'}$ substituted for by $f_{pp'}^{\text {aa }}+f_{pp'}^{\text {ab }}$, which are given by (5).

### 3. Results and conclusion
We determine the film density $n_{\mathrm{s}}$ as a function of $T$ and $n$ from the equilibrium condition $\mu_{\mathrm{G}}=\mu_{\mathrm{F}}$ (cf (1) and (2)). Our results are shown in figures 2 and 3 as $n_{\mathrm{s}}-T$ curves for fixed $n$.

The difference between the present calculation and previous ones lies in the expression used for the chemical potential shift $\Delta \mu$. We use for $\Delta \mu$ equation (4), which

The expression used by MRV, based on the second virial expansion, can be written in a form similar to (4) but instead of the Bose statistics expression for $n_k^0$, $n_k^0 = \{\exp[\beta(\varepsilon_k^0 - \mu_{2\text{D}}^0)] - 1\}^{-1}$, it uses the Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics expression $n_k = \exp[-\beta(\varepsilon_k^0 - \mu_{2\text{D}}^0)]$. In the calculation of EM, $\Delta\mu$ is assumed to be independent of $T$ and proportional to $n_s$.

At high temperatures, or more precisely when $n_s\lambda^2 \ll 1$, equation (4) reduces to the second virial expression. In this limit our results for $\Delta\mu$ are identical to those of MRV since we use the same $\text{H}\downarrow-\text{H}\downarrow$ interaction potential. At low temperatures, when $n\lambda^2 \gg 1$ the second virial expansion fails and the VC theory gives a more reliable approximation for $\Delta\mu$. In this regime, our results for $\Delta\mu$ are systematically larger than those of MRV; this is a consequence of the 2D Bose statistics which gives higher occupation of the low-energy states than the Boltzmann statistics and of our $f_{pp'}$ (equation (4)) which is positive for small $p$ and $p'$. This leads to smaller values of $n_s$ for given $T$ and $n$. Thus our $n_s$-$T$ curves lie about 25% lower than the corresponding ones obtained by MRV. The EM results for $\Delta\mu$ would be identical to ours if $f_{pp'}$ were independent of $p'$ and $p$, which is not the case for the $\text{H}\downarrow-\text{H}\downarrow$ potential. Thus, our corrections for $\Delta\mu$ are more precise than those of EM and our $n_s$-$T$ curves are also lower than theirs when compared for the same adsorption energy.

The $\text{H}\downarrow$ mixture saturation density is around $8 \times 10^{13}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$ as can be seen in figure 2. This result is 30% lower than the results of EM, MRV and the more recent work of Guyer and Saslow (1983). However, as Goldman and Silvera (1981) pointed out, the EM results for the surface density become lower when effects of exchange are taken into account in the interaction energy. In figures 2 and 3 we also show the KT transition line (broken line) obtained from
the KT formula for neutral films (Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973):

$$
n_{\mathrm{sf}}=2 k_{\mathrm{B}} T_{\mathrm{C}} M^{*} / \pi \hbar^{2} M. \tag{9}
$$

As in EM and MRV, we assume that $M=M^{*}$ and that the superfluid density $n_{\mathrm{sf}}$ is
equal to its maximum value, that is, the film density $n_{\mathrm{s}}$.

Thus, the KT transition line is calculated as in the previous work of EM and MRV
but since the surface densities are essentially different, we predicted different KT
transition temperatures and densities than EM and MRV. We see from figures 2 and 3
that for a gas density, close to experimental realisation, of $10^{18} \mathrm{~cm}^{-3}$ we find KT transition
temperatures of $T_{\mathrm{KT}}=180$ and $T=160 \mathrm{mK}$ for the mixture and single-component $\mathrm{H} \downarrow$
respectively. The mixture prediction of EM (with adsorption energy $E_{\mathrm{a}}=-0.9 \mathrm{~K}$) and
MRV at the same gas density is $T_{\mathrm{KT}}=200 \mathrm{mK}$. MRV also predicted $T_{\mathrm{KT}}=200 \mathrm{mK}$ for
the single component.

We conclude by pointing out that our results for the film KT transition are about
$20\%$ lower than those of EM and MRV. Close to the KT line $n_{\mathrm{s}} \lambda^{2} \simeq 4$, thus, our
calculations are more appropriate than the quantum virial expansion of MRV. Our
method is also more reliable than the temperature-independent corrections used by EM.
We agree with these authors that the film-superfluid transition may be easier to reach
than the BE condensation in the gas and we expect that the improvement derived here
may be important for future experimental work.
