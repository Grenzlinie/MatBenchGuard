# A THEORETICAL STUDY OF ADSORPTION OF $CO_{2}$ ON THE (100) FACE OF SODIUM CHLORIDE

B. DEPRICK and A. JULG

Laboratoire de Chimie Théorique, Université de Provence, Place Victor Hugo, 13331 Marseille 3, France

Received 28 June 1984

The adsorption energy of a $CO_{2}$ molecule on various sites of the (100) face of NaCl is determined (i) by considering the molecule in the electrostatic field created by the substrate, and (ii) by treating the cluster $(CO_{2}Cl_{2})^{2-}$ in the field created by the rest of the substrate. The electrostatic and polarization contributions are obtained by means of the SCF method. The dispersion-repulsion term is successively estimated by means of the hard-sphere model and an adapted potential model. Whatever the model used, the calculated adsorption energy is in the range 6-10 kcal/mole; this is in acceptable agreement with experiment. However, only the cluster model gives the correct splitting of the $\nu_{2}$ frequency of $CO_{2}$.

## 1. Introduction

The adsorption of organic compounds on the surface of alkali halides has been the subject of numerous previous studies, both experimental and theoretical. The interaction between a molecule and a solid is directly connected with the heat of adsorption and with the vibrational frequencies of the adsorbate, and is therefore of great interest in catalysis. Since the direct treatment of the supersystem (molecule + crystal) is of course impossible, approximations are necessary. Hückel tackled this problem in 1928 by means of Lennard-Jones pair potentials [1]. Up to the past few years, all the research has been continued in this way (see, for example, ref. [2]). At the present time, due to the capabilities of electronic calculations, theorists more and more are turning to much more elaborate treatments which explicitly introduce all the electrons of the system. A first approach consists of treating the molecule within the electric field created by a finite number of point charges, designed to simulate the infinite crystal [3]. A more sophisticated method consists of treating the supermolecule built from the adsorbate itself and a certain number of neighboring ions of the substrate, all the other ions being replaced by point charges [4] (cluster method). This latter procedure is certainly more satisfying. However, when the sizes of the adsorbed molecule and the crystal ions increase, it leads to calculations which are too lengthy, so that the pair method still remains in favor. In fact, instead of being satisfied with semi-empirical Lennard-Jones pair potentials, some authors [5] use more sophisticated potentials, determined from self-consistent-field (SCF) calculations, in order to simulate the theoretical scheme of the cluster method. Generally, the methods which use pair potentials lead to satisfactory adsorption energies and to correct sites of adsorption. In contrast, these methods seem to encounter difficulties in predicting the crystal effect on the vibrational spectrum of the adsorbed molecule. The case of $CO_{2}$ adsorption on NaCl is typical in this connection. A pair potential method, fitted to SCF calculations, predicts the splitting of the $\nu_{2}$ vibration of the free molecule [6] with an increase in both resulting frequencies [7], whereas experiment shows a decrease in both these frequencies [8].

The aim of this note is to compare the various possible approaches in the particular case of the adsorption of $CO_{2}$ on the (100) face of NaCl in order to determine the method best adapted to this kind of problem.


## 2. The method

It is well known that adsorption phenomena are governed by the Gibbs energy. Nevertheless, in order to determine the site of adsorption and the main characteristics of the adsorbed molecule at low temperature (a few K), as a first approach, we can consider the adsorption energy only, owing to the fact that, at these temperatures, the entropy terms do not play a determining role. Moreover, we assume that the $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ concentration is sufficiently small to allow us to neglect the intermolecular interactions.

First, by means of the ab initio SCF method [9], we determine the difference $\Delta_{\mathrm{SCF}}$ between the energy of the molecule (or the cluster) in the isolated state and one within the electric field created by the point charges designed to simulate the substrate. This SCF term includes both the electrostatic interaction between the adsorbed molecule (or the cluster) and the substrate, and the polarization of the adsorbed system by the point charges. The energy of polarization of the ions by the adsorbate is negligible (about $\Delta_{\mathrm{SCF}} / 1000$).

In order to improve the evaluation of the adsorption energy, we introduce a dispersion-repulsion term, $\Delta_{\mathrm{DR}}$. We use a semi-empirical pair potential that we can write as

$$
V_{\mathrm{DR}}=-\sum_{\mathrm{MN}}\left(A^{\mathrm{MN}} / R^{6}\right)\left(1-B / R^{6}\right). \tag{1}
$$

M and N are respectively an ion of the substrate and an atom of the adsorbed molecule (or cluster). $A^{\mathrm{MN}}$ is the asymptotic dispersion coefficient given by London [10,11]:

$$
A^{\mathrm{MN}}=\frac{3}{2}\left[I_{\mathrm{M}} I_{\mathrm{N}} /\left(I_{\mathrm{M}}+I_{\mathrm{N}}\right)\right] \alpha_{\mathrm{M}} \alpha_{\mathrm{N}}, \tag{2}
$$

where $I$ and $\alpha$ are respectively the ionization energy and the polarizability of the corresponding atom or ion. Table 1 gives the values we have used [12-15].

Within the hard-sphere model,

$$
B=\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{0}^{\mathrm{MN}}\right)^{6}, \tag{3}
$$

$R_{0}^{\mathrm{MN}}$ being the sum of the van der Waals radii of M and N. In this model, expression (1) is valid only if $R \geqslant R_{0}^{\mathrm{MN}}$. For $R<R_{0}^{\mathrm{MN}}, B$ is equal to infinity.

In fact, the atoms or ions are not rigorously impenetrable. In order to take this effect into account, as a first approximation we introduce a coefficient

$\lambda$, which is the same for all MN pairs. In other words, we write $B$ as

$$
B=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda R_{0}^{\mathrm{MN}}\right)^{6}. \tag{4}
$$

Given that $\Delta_{\mathrm{DR}}$ is weak with respect to $\Delta_{\mathrm{SCF}}$, this approximation is sufficient.

In the hard-sphere model, the minimal approach distance is equal to the sum of the corresponding van der Waals radii of the atoms in contact. On the other hand, the introduction of $\lambda$ requires the determination of the position of the molecule by minimization of the total energy. For the cluster model, we assume that the ions of the substrate are not relaxed, only the position of the molecule itself being able to vary with respect to these ions. A minimization calculation is necessary.

Whatever the model used, the adsorption energy appears as

$$
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathrm{SCF}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{DR}}. \tag{5}
$$

Positive values of $\Delta$ correspond to an adsorption on the crystal surface.

We have used a version of the GAUSSIAN 70 program, modified to take exterior point charges into account [16], with the STO-6G basis set. The substrate has been simulated by $7 \times 7 \times 4=196$ point charges equal to $\pm 1$, located at the sites of the ions in the $\mathrm{NaCl}$ crystal. No relaxation has been introduced $(d_{+-}$ $=2.814 \AA)$.

### 3.2. Cluster method (B)

In this model, we consider the cluster $\left(\mathrm{CO}_{2} \mathrm{Cl}_{2}\right)^{2-}$ built from the $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ molecule and the two nearestneighboring $\mathrm{Cl}^{-}$ions. The distance $z$ between $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ and the surface is determined by minimization of the energy. The calculated value of $z$ is equal to $1.89 \AA$, and the corresponding adsorption energy to $\approx 10.2$ $\mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mole}$.

The net charges of the atoms in the cluster are given in table 5. We see, in particular, that no signifi- cant transfer from $\mathrm{Cl}^{-}$ions towards the $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ molecule occurs. This point is important, because it justifies the point-charge approximation used for the substrate. In passing, we note that, if in an attempt to improve the description of the system we considered the clus- ter $\left(\mathrm{CO}_{2} \mathrm{Cl}_{2} \mathrm{Na}_{2}\right)^{0}$ built up from $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ and the four nearest-neighboring ions $\left(2 \mathrm{Cl}^{-}\right.$and $\left.2 \mathrm{Na}^{+}\right)$, we should certainly obtain a bad result owing to the fact that the $\mathrm{Cl}_{2} \mathrm{Na}_{2}$ molecule, in the isolated state, has a strongly covalent character, in complete disagreement with the ionic structure of $\mathrm{NaCl}$.

Finally, we have studied the effect of a vertical oscillation of the $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ molecule itself around its equi librium position. The zero-point vibrational energy is equal to $0.2 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mole}$. Consequently, the effect on the adsorption energy is negligible.

## 4. Vibrational frequencies

As stated above, the crystal field removes the de- generacy between the two components of the $\nu_{2}$ vi bration of the free $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ molecule. We observe two frequencies $\left(\nu_{2 a}\right.$ and $\left.\nu_{2 b}\right)$ corresponding respectively to vibrations parallel to $\left(\nu_{\|}\right)$and perpendicular to $\left(\nu_{\perp}\right)$ the surface, whose frequencies are lower than that of $\nu_{2}$.

In both models $\mathrm{A}$, we obtain a splitting, but with a general shift towards high frequencies. On the con- trary, model $\mathrm{B}$ (cluster $\mathrm{CO}_{2} \mathrm{Cl}_{2}^{2-}$ ) gives a correct shift for both frequencies (table 6). In all the calculations, we have taken the anharmonicity into account [17].

Nevertheless, the frequencies themselves are sys- tematically overestimated. For instance, for the initial $\nu_{2}$ frequency, our calculation gives $601.3 \mathrm{~cm}^{-1}$ [6]. This is also the case for the frequencies on splitting. This result is not surprising. Indeed, it is well known that only a very extended configuration interaction per- mits one to obtain good values for the force constants [18]. However, our result can be considered as being correct, owing to the fact that quantum mechanics is better able to reproduce variations of a given quan- tity than to predict the actual value of this quantity.

These calculations have allowed us to verify that $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ remains practically linear in its equilibrium posi tion (the angular deformation is less than $0.1^{\circ}$ ).

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Experiment shows that $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ adsorbs in a parallel position with respect to the (100) surface of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ and that the adsorption energy is $\approx 6 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mole}$ [19].

Our calculations indicate the same adsorption type. However, the values obtained for the adsorption ener- gy are systematically larger. The hard-sphere model and the cluster model give practically the same value $(\approx 10 \mathrm{kcal} / \mathrm{mole})$. This agreement is certainly acciden tal. The very small approach distance $z(1.89 \AA)$ in

the cluster model arises from the fact that the interac- tion between the two $Cl^{-}$ ions and the $CO_{2}$ group is much stronger in the cluster under consideration than in reality, the $Cl^{-}$ ions also being linked with the other ions of the crystal. Certainly, the approach distance $z$ is greater than $1.89 \AA$ and, consequently, the adsorp tion energy is less than $10 kcal / mole$ .

Moreover, one can object to our calculation in that it does not include correlation effects. In fact, given that the adsorption energy is obtained as a difference between isoelectronic systems, without electron trans- fer between one another, even in the cluster model the effects of correlation on the value of the adsorp- tion energy must practically be cancelled out. In con- trast, introduction of polarization d orbitals on $C$ and $O$ should modify the value of $\Delta$ in an appreciable manner, but without changing the general conclusions.

Let us recall that Heidberg, using pair potentials fitted to SCF calculations, obtained the same prefer- ential site and practically the same adsorption energy(6.0-11.5 kcal/mole) [5] as us. In contrast, he ob- tained a shift towards higher frequencies for both components of $\nu_{2}$ [17].

In conclusion, insofar as the results obtained in the particular case of $CO_{2}$ adsorption on $NaCl$ are general, the simple model which considers the adsorbed mol- ecule within the electric field created by the ions of the substrate, with a dispersion-repulsion pair poten- tial like eq. (4) is sufficient to determine the adsorp- tion site and the order of magnitude of the adsorp- tion energy. This conclusion is of great interest for treating adsorption of large molecules (butadiene, benzene) in view of catalytic applications.

Nevertheless, our results (methods $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$ ), and that of Heidberg [7] using a pair-potential method, clearly show that, if we want to obtain information concerning the vibrations, it is necessary to use the cluster model, even when the cluster under considera- tion is too small to obtain a good value for the adsorp- tion energy.
