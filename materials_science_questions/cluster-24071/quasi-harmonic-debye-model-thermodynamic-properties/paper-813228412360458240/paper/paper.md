## 1 Introduction
The perovskite structure with the general composition ABX3 is adopted by many oxide compounds, where A and B are cations and X is an oxide anion. The structure, which emphasizes the twelvefold coordination of the A atom, can be described by a cubic unit cell which contains an A atom at the center of a cube, B atoms at the corners, and X atoms at the center of the cell edges. Another common way to describe the structure is to shift the origin so that a B atom is at the center of the cube, A atoms at the comers, and X atoms on the face centers. The latter emphasizes the octahedral coordination of the B atom by six X atoms. Antiperovskite structures, in which X and B interchange their roles, are also known [1], i.e., the X anion is at the cationic B site instead and becomes the center of an octahedron formed by six metal atoms. Among nitride antiperovskites, two classes can be further distinguished. In the first class, nitrogen can be thought of as an interstitial atom which goes into the octahedral hole of an already existing metal framework with a slightly modified cell constant. Therefore, extensive metal-metal bonding continues to exist in the nitride. In the second class, the A and B sites are occupied by anions, and the X sites are occupied by cations; thus, the cation and anion positions are completely reversed from a normal oxide perovskite.

For the perovskite-like nitrides, the most symmetric structures of this kind have a cubic structure with close packing of atoms to the $A_{3}B$ composition. The general composition of these compounds is conventionally recorded as $A_{3}BN$, where A and B are metals, and N is nitrogen [2]. Recently, Gäbler et al. [3] have studied the magnetic properties of $InNCe_{3}$ and they have determined the magnetic spin structure in the antiferromagnetic state. The ternary compounds $(R_{3}N)In$, $R$=rare-earth metal, have also been synthesized by Kirchner et al. [4], by arc melting and sintering reactions from the binaries $RN$ and $R_{2}In$. The field of ternary nitrides had considerable interest over the last years [5-7].

To the best of our knowledge, there are no reported theoretical or experimental data in the literature on the first-order elastic constants and their pressure dependences for the compound of interest. Moreover, the thermodynamic properties of this compound have not been calculated or measured yet. The reasons mentioned above motivate us to perform these calculations, using for the framework the full-potential linear muffin-tin orbital (FP-LMTO) method in order to provide reference data for the experimentalist and to complete the existing theoretical studies on this material.

This study is organized as follows: in Sect. 2, we briefly describe the computational techniques used in this study. Results and discussion of the structural, elastic, ther- modynamic, electronic, and magnetic properties will be presented and compared with the available experimental data and theoretical results in Sect. 3. Finally, conclusions and remarks are given in Sect. 4.

## 2 Calculation Methodology

The ground state of the configuration of the constituting atoms of antiperovskite $\text{InNCe}_3$ is: In: [Kr] $4\text{d}^{10}5\text{s}^25\text{p}^1$, N: [He] $2\text{s}^22\text{p}^3$, and Ce: [Xe] $4\text{f}^26\text{s}^2$. In order to simulate the phase of $\text{InNCe}_3$, the following positions for atom unit cells are used. The ideal cubic antiperovskite structure for the $\text{InNCe}_3$ compound (space group Pm3m; #221) includes one formula with the Wyckoff positions of the atoms as follows: **In** 1a (0, 0, 0), **N** 1b (0.5, 0.5, 0.5), and **Ce** 3c (0, 0.5, 0.5).

In this study, the FP-LMTO method, as employed in the lmtart code [8,9], has been applied to perform first-principles total energy calculations. This method is based on density functional theory (DFT) which is a universal quantum mechanical approach for many-body problems. In this approach, a quantum many-body problem of an interacting electron gas is mapped exactly onto a set of single particle one-electron equations moving in an effective local potential with the same density as is real, and the obtained one-electron equations are called Kohn–Sham equations [10,11]. In this method, the space is partitioned into non-overlapping muffin-tin spheres $S_{\text{R}}$ surrounding every atom and the remaining interstitial region $\Omega_{\text{int}}$. Within the spheres, the basis functions are represented in terms of numerical solutions of the radial Schrodinger equation for the spherical part of the potential multiplied by spherical harmonics. In the interstitial region, they include Fourier transforms of the linear muffin-tin orbitals (LMTOs). To take into account exchange and correlation effects, the local spin density approximation (LSDA) as parameterized by Perdew and Wang [12], has been applied. In addition, for the electronic band structure and magnetic properties the Coulomb interaction $U_{\text{eff}}$ on the localized Ce-4f electrons was applied. In both cases, spin–orbit coupling is not included. In order to achieve energy eigenvalue convergence, the wave functions in the interstitial region were expanded in plane waves with a 82.71 Ryd energy cut-off and the number of plane waves is equal to 9170. The muffin-tin sphere radius is taken to be (3.70, 1.84, and 2.64) a.u. for In, N, and Ce, respectively. The charge density and the potential are represented inside the muffin-tin spheres (MTS) by spherical harmonics up to $l_{\text{max}} = 6$. The $\boldsymbol{k}$-integration over the Brillouin zone is performed up to the (6, 6, 6) grid in the irreducible Brillouin zone (IBZ), using the tetrahedron method [13].

The study of thermal effects was done within the quasi-harmonic Debye model implemented in the Gibbs program [14]. The quasi-harmonic Debye model allows us to obtain all thermodynamics quantities from the calculated energy–volume points. The common thermodynamic parameters that depend on temperature and pressure are used to derive other macroscopic properties. We give here a brief description of the quasi-harmonic Debye model; detailed descriptions of this procedure can be found in [15–17]. In this model, the non-equilibrium Gibbs function $G^{*}(V; P, T)$ takes the form

$$
G^{*}(V; P, T) = E(V) + PV + A_{\text{vib}}(\theta(V), T), \tag{1}
$$

where $E(V)$ is the total energy per unit cell, $PV$ corresponds to the constant hydrostatic pressure condition, $\theta(V)$ is the Debye temperature, and $A_{\text{vib}}$ is the vibrational term.

![](./images/813228412360458240_3.jpg)

which can be written using the Debye model of the phonon density of states as

$$
A_{\mathrm{vib}}(\theta(V), T)=n K_{\mathrm{B}} T\left[\frac{9}{8} \frac{\theta}{T}+3 \ln \left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\theta}{T}}\right)-D(\theta / T)\right],
\tag{2}
$$

where $n$ is the number of atoms per formula unit and $D(\theta/T)$ represents the Debye integral. For isotropic solids, $\theta$ is expressed as

$$
\theta=\frac{\hbar}{K}\left[6 \pi^{2} V^{1 / 2} n\right]^{1 / 3} f(\sigma) \sqrt{\frac{B_{s}}{M}},
\tag{3}
$$

where $M$ is the molecular mass and $B_s$ is the adiabatic bulk modulus which can be approximated by the static compressibility as

$$
B_{s} \cong B(V)=V \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2} E(V)}{\mathrm{d} V^{2}}
\tag{4}
$$

$f(\sigma)$ is given by

$$
f(\sigma)=\left\{3\left[2\left(\frac{21+\sigma}{31-2 \sigma}\right)^{3 / 2}+\left(\frac{11+\sigma}{31-\sigma}\right)^{3 / 2}\right]^{-1}\right\}^{1 / 3},
\tag{5}
$$

where $\sigma$ is Poisson's ratio. Therefore, the non-equilibrium Gibbs function $G^{*}(V ; P, T)$ as a function of $(V ; P, T)$ can be minimized with respect to volume $V$,

$$
\left(\frac{\partial G^{*}(V, P, T)}{\partial V}\right)_{P, T}=0
\tag{6}
$$

By solving Eq.6, one can obtain the thermal equation of state (EOS) $V(P, T)$. The isothermal bulk modulus $B_{T}$, the heat capacity at constant volume $C_{V}$, the heat capacity at constant pressure $C_{P}$, and the thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha$ are given by

$$
B_{T}(P, T)=V\left(\frac{\partial^{2} G^{*}(V, P, T)}{\partial V^{2}}\right)
\tag{7}
$$

$$
C_{V}=3 n K_{\mathrm{B}}\left[4 D(\theta / T)-\frac{3 \theta / T}{\mathrm{e}^{\theta / T}-1}\right]
\tag{8}
$$

$$
C_{P}=C_{V}(1+\alpha \gamma T)
\tag{9}
$$

$$
\alpha=\frac{\gamma C_{V}}{B_{T} V},
\tag{10}
$$

![](./images/813228412360458240_4.jpg)

where $\gamma$ is the Grüneisen parameter, which is defined as

$$
\gamma=-\frac{\mathrm{d} \ln \theta(V)}{\mathrm{d} \ln V}. \tag{11}
$$

## 3 Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Structural Properties

For calculating the ground state properties of this compound, a series of different values of the lattice constants $(a)$ are set to calculate the total energy $(E)$, and the corresponding primitive cell volumes $(V)$. The $(E-V)$ curve is fitted to the Birch- Murnaghan EOS [18] to determine the ground state properties such as the equilibrium lattice constant $a_{0}$, the bulk modulus $B$, and its pressure derivative $B^{\prime}$. The calculated equilibrium lattice constant $a_{0}$ turns out to be $4.85 \AA$, as compared to the experimental values of $5.041 \AA$ [3] and $5.0472 \AA$ [4]. The underestimation of our calculated lattice constant is attributed to the neglect of the corrections in the exchange and correlation energy due to the inhomogeneities in the electronic charge density. The values of the bulk modulus and its pressure derivative $(B_{0}$ and $B^{\prime})$ evaluated at zero pressure, are predicted to be $87.62 \mathrm{GPa}$ and 4.0, respectively. There is no experimental or theoretical data for the bulk modulus and its pressure derivative to be compared with our theoretical result.

### 3.2 Elastic Constants

The elastic constants define the properties of a material that undergoes stress and deformation, and then recovers and returns to its original shape after the stress ceases. These properties play an important part in providing valuable information about the binding characteristics between adjacent atomic planes, the anisotropic character of binding, and the structural stability. Hence, to study the stability of this compound with its antiperovskite structure, we have calculated the elastic constants at the equilibrium lattice parameter. The elastic moduli require knowledge of the derivative of the energy as a function of the lattice strain. It is possible to choose this strain in such a way that the volume of the unit cell is preserved. In the case of a cubic system, there are three independent elastic constants, namely, $C_{11}, C_{12}$, and $C_{44}$. Thus, for the calculation of them, we have used the Mehl method [19].

Another important parameter is the internal strain parameter introduced by Klein- man [20], which describes the relative positions of the cation and anion sublattices under volume, while conserve strain distortions for which positions are not fixed by symmetry. We use the following relation:

![](./images/813228412360458240_5.jpg)

$$
\zeta=\frac{C_{11}+8 C_{12}}{7 C_{11}+2 C_{12}} \tag{12}
$$

The macroscopic elastic parameters are typically obtained using Voigt and Reuss schemes [21] in which the bulk compression modulus is defined as

$$
B_{V, \mathrm{R}}=1 / 3\left(C_{11}+2 C_{12}\right) \tag{13}
$$

and the shear moduli as

$$
G_{V}=\left(C_{11}-C_{12}+3 C_{44}\right) / 5 \tag{14}
$$

$$
G_{\mathrm{R}}=\left[5 C_{44}\left(C_{11}-C_{12}\right)\right] /\left[4 C_{44}+3\left(C_{11}-C_{12}\right)\right] \tag{15}
$$

The shear modulus $G$ is given by

$$
G=\left(G_{V}+G_{\mathrm{R}}\right) / 2 \tag{16}
$$

The Zener anisotropic factor $A$, Young's modulus $E$, Poisson's ratio $v$, and Lamé's constants ($\lambda$ and $\mu$), which are frequently measured for polycrystalline materials when investigating their hardness, are calculated by using the following relations:

$$
A=2 C_{44} /\left(C_{11}-C_{12}\right) \tag{17}
$$

$$
E=9 B G /(3 B+G) \tag{18}
$$

$$
v=(3 B-E) / 6 B \tag{19}
$$

$$
\lambda=E v /[(1-2 v)(1+v)] \tag{20}
$$

$$
\mu=E / 2(1+v) \tag{21}
$$

The calculated elastic constants $C_{i j}$, the shear modulus $G$, the Young's modulus $E$, the Poisson's ratio $v$, and Lamé's constants are summarized in Table 1. The requirement of mechanical stability in cubic crystal leads to the following restrictions on the elastic constants, $C_{44}>0, C_{11}>\left|C_{12}\right|$, and $C_{11}+2 C_{12}>0$ [22]. The calculated elastic constants listed in Table 1 satisfied the above stability condition, indicating that the cubic $\mathrm{InNCe}_{3}$ is elastically stable.

The critical value which separates ductile and brittle materials is around 1.75; if $B/G\ >\ 1.75$, the material behaves in a ductile manner; otherwise, the material behaves in a brittle manner. Frantsevich et al. [25], in a similar fashion, have suggested $B/G\ \approx\ 2.67$ as the critical value separating brittle and ductile behaviors. In our case, the $B/G$ ratio is found to be 1.67. Referring to the Pugh criteria, the ratio $B/G$ is slightly below the critical value, revealing the slight brittleness of this compound. We may also refer to Frantsevich et al. For brittle materials, the Poisson ratio is less than 0.33; otherwise, the material behaves in a ductile manner. Here, the calculated $\sigma$ values are smaller than $1/3$ for this compound, hence categorizing it as a brittle material. Another index of the brittle–ductile characteristic of the materials is Cauchy's pressure, defined as the difference between the elastic constants $C_{12}\ -\ C_{44}$. If the Cauchy's pressure is negative (positive), the material is expected to be brittle (ductile). Here, the Cauchy's pressure is negative which also reflects the brittle nature of this compound.

As an extension, the Debye temperature ($\theta_{\text{D}}$) which determines the thermal characteristic of materials, is also calculated. This temperature is used to distinguish between the high and low temperature regions in solids. If $T > \theta_{\text{D}}$ we expect all modes to have an energy $k_{\text{B}}T$, and if $T < \theta_{\text{D}}$, one expects high-frequency modes to be frozen [26]. One popular method for calculating the Debye temperature ($\theta_{\text{D}}$) is from elastic constant data, since $\theta_{\text{D}}$ can be estimated from the average sound velocity, $v_{\text{m}}$ by the following equation [27]:

$$
\theta_{\text{D}} = \frac{h}{k} \left[ \frac{3n}{4\pi} \left( \frac{N_{\text{a}} \rho}{M} \right) \right]^{1/3} v_{\text{m}}, \tag{22}
$$

where $v_{\text{m}}$ is the average wave velocity, $h$ is Planck's constant, $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $N_{\text{a}}$ is Avogadro's number, $n$ is the number of atoms per formula unit, $M$ is the molecular mass per formula unit, $\rho = M/V$ is the density, and $v_{\text{m}}$ is obtained from [28]

$$
v_{\text{m}} = \left[ \frac{1}{3} \left( \frac{2}{v_{\text{t}}^3} + \frac{1}{v_{\text{l}}^3} \right) \right]^{-1/3}, \tag{23}
$$

where $v_{\text{l}}$ and $v_{\text{t}}$ are the longitudinal and transverse elastic wave velocities, respectively, which are obtained from Navier's equations [29]:

$$
v_{\text{l}} = \left( \frac{3B + 4G}{3\rho} \right)^{1/2} \tag{24}
$$

and

$$
v_{\text{t}} = \left( \frac{G}{\rho} \right)^{1/2}. \tag{25}
$$

$$

At $U_{\text{eff}} = 2.5$ eV, the peaks of the 4f states begin to pull away and move to the highest energies. for $U_{\text{eff}} = 5$ eV, the detachment is clearer for majority and minority spin electrons with a peak appearing for the Ce-4f majority spin electrons at about $-4$ eV. The influence of the parameter $U_{\text{eff}}$ on the contribution of different atoms in the total spin magnetic moment is shown in Table 5. The inclusion of electron correlations is also to localize the f electrons and thus increase the absolute value of the spin magnetic moment contained in the atomic spheres for the atoms of Ce. The magnetic moments of atoms of nitrogen decrease almost linearly with $U_{\text{eff}}$. This decrease is almost $30\%$: the spin magnetic moment of the nitrogen atoms values from $0.0635\,\mu_{\text{B}}$ to $0.0442\,\mu_{\text{B}}$ between $U_{\text{eff}}=0\mathrm{eV}$ and $U_{\text{eff}}=5\mathrm{eV}$. All other absolute values of magnetic moments increase, this increase is continuous and strong. The contribution of the interstitial region to the magnetic moment stabilizes between $0\mathrm{eV}$ and $2.5\mathrm{eV}$, and then increases when $U_{\text{eff}}$ ranges from $2.5\mathrm{eV}$ to $5\mathrm{eV}$. [3]. This lower of the magnetic moments is explained by the presence of a commensurate–incommensurate transformation between these temperatures. This remark explains the difference between our results and those of Gäbler et al.; high-field magnetic susceptibility measurements reveal a multi-step metamagnetic behavior [3]. Although the magnetic properties are dominated by the magnetic moment of Ce, the deficiency of nitrogen was experimentally observed [3,4]. ## 4 Conclusions

Employing the FP-LMTO approach based on DFT, within the local density approximation, we studied the structural and elastic properties of the $\text{InNCe}_{3}$ compound. A summary of our results follows.

(a) The calculated structural parameters at zero pressure are in agreement with the available data.

![](./images/813228412360458240_19.jpg)

(b) A numerical first-principles calculation of the elastic constants was used to calcu-
late $C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, and $C_{44}$ and their pressure dependence for the $InNCe_3$ compound. A
linear pressure dependence of the elastic constants and bulk modulus was found.

(c) We calculated the shear modulus, Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, and Lamé's
constants for $InNCe_3$.

(d) The value of the $B/G$ ratio for the $InNCe_3$ compound is lower than the critical
value separating brittle and ductile behavior; hence, this compound is brittle in
nature.

(e) We calculated the sound velocity and Debye temperature for the $InNCe_3$ com-
pound.

(f) The temperature effect on the bulk modulus, thermal expansion coefficient, spe-
cific heats, and Debye temperature in the pressure range from 0 to 30 GPa and
temperature range from 0 to 1000 K have been determined.

(g) The results showed that the methods take into account the electron correlations as
the LSDA+$U$ approximation gave good results in describing the electronic and
magnetic properties. The calculated densities of states predict the metallic nature
of the compound, independent of the approximation used.
