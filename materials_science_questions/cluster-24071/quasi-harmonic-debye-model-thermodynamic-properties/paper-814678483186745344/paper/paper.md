## ABSTRACT

Phase transition of rhenium mononitride (ReN) in NaCl, CsCl, zincblende (ZB), NbO, wurtzite (WZ), NiAs, WC, PtS, Pmn2₁ and Cmc2₁ structures have been studied by using the projector augmented wave method. It is found that NbO-type structure is the most stable. This conclusion is consistent with the report of Wang et al., while contrary to the results of Zhao et al., Chen et al., Asvini et al., and Hlynsson et al. The phase transition from NbO-type to NiAs-type occurs at ca. 52.8 GPa, which is also in good agreement with that of Wang et al. The elastic constants of NbO- and NiAs-type ReN under high pressure are calculated and found to be increased with the increasing pressures. At the same time, the ductile-brittle behavior is evaluated by Pugh's criteria. Also, we have predicted the density of states and Vickers hardness for NbO and NiAs types of ReN. Finally, the Debye temperature $\Theta_D$, thermal expansion $\alpha$ and heat capacity $C_V$ for NbO-type structure at high pressures are also derived through the quasi-harmonic Debye model.

## 1. Introduction

As one of the 5d transition metal nitrides, rhenium mono-nitride (ReN) exists excellent properties, such as high hardness, good electrical-thermal conductivity and superconductivity, so it could be widely used as cutting tools, diffusion barriers layer, thin films in solar cells and so on [1–6]. In addition, the higher valence electron density and the stronger hybridization between metal d orbitals and nonmetal p orbitals in ReN can make it strongly resist shearing strain or shape change [7]. Therefore, the study on ReN is of great interest for physical scientists.

In 2008, Zhao and Wu [1] used ultra-soft pseudopotential plane-wave based on density functional theory (DFT) method to study the phase transformation for NaCl-, CsCl-, ZB-, WZ-, NiAs- and WC-type ReN and found the structural phase transition WZ → NiAs to be 42 GPa. Chen and Jiang [2] in 2010 reported phase transition from ZB-type to NaCl-type to be 39 GPa for ReN using the same method. In 2011, Asvini et al. [3] studied the phase transformation for NaCl-, CsCl-, ZB-, NiAs- and WC-type ReN via the tight-binding linear muffin tin orbital (LMTO) method. Qualitative analysis showed the phase transform from NiAs-type to ZB-type occurs at a certain high pressure. Though Vanderbilt ultrasoft pseudopotential plane wave method, Wang et al. [4] predicted the phase transition from NbO-type to NiAs-type structure to be 51.9 GPa in 2012. Recently, Hlynsson et al. [5] investigated the structural properties of ReN in NaCl and ZB structures by the projector augmented wave (PAW) method, and they reported the transition pressure ZB→NaCl to be 27 GPa. To conclude, the most stable structure is highly controversial.

On the other hand, the elastic constant is not only correlated with the mechanical properties, such as load deflection, thermo-elastic stress, internal strain, sound velocities, and fracture toughness, but also associated with thermodynamic properties, such as specific heat, thermal expansion, and Debye temperature. It also relates to various fundamental solid-state phenomena, as interatomic bonding, equations of state, and phonon spectra [8,9]. Up to date, several theoretical methods have been used to investigate the elastic constants of ReN at 0 GPa, such as ultra-soft pseudo-potentials (US-PP) method [1,4,10], tight-binding linear muffin tin orbital (LMTO) method [3,11] and pseudopotential plane wave (PP-PW) method [12]. However, elastic properties at high pressures have not been reported for ReN.

Therefore, in this paper, we study the structure phase transition and elastic properties of ReN under high pressure by projector augmented wave method. The other sections are organized as follows. In Section 2, we make a brief review of the calculation

## 2. Theoretical method

### 2.1. Total energy electronic structure calculations

The electronic structure total energy calculations are based on the density functional theory as implemented in the Vienna ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) [13]. We have employed the projector augmented wave (PAW) method [14,15] within the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) form of generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [16,17] for the exchange-correlation functional. In the PAW method, the pseudopotentials used for calculations are constructed by the electron configurations as Re $5d^56s^2$ and N $2s^22p^3$. The plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff is set to 650 eV for NaCl-, CsCl-, ZB-, NbO-, WZ-, NiAs-, WC-, PtS-, Pmn2₁- and Cmc2₁-type structures and 700 eV for NiAs-type structure, respectively. The Monkhorst-Pack [18] k-points for Brillouin-zone sampling are $21 × 21 × 21$, $21 × 21 × 21$, $19 × 19 × 19$, $21 × 21 × 21$, $21 × 21 × 17$, $21 × 21 × 17$, $19 × 19 × 17$, $21 × 21 × 17$, $15 × 12 × 11$ and $16 × 16 × 8$ for NaCl-, CsCl-, ZB-, NbO-, WZ-, NiAs-, WC-, PtS-, Pmn2₁- and Cmc2₁-type structures, respectively. All the parameters are tested to be fully converged with respect to total energy and are sufficient to give good results. The accurate total energy is calculated by the linear tetrahedron method with Blöchl's correction [19] and the total energy self-consistent convergence precision is set to $1 × 10^{-6}$ eV/cell.

### 2.2. Elastic constants

As is well-known, the elastic constants can be calculated according to the second derivatives of the internal energy with respect to the strain tensor. Based on the theoretical method proposed by Sin'ko and Smirnov [20], we have successfully investigated the elastic constants of some materials (i.e. c-BN [21], Zr [22], Ti [23], Hf [24], HfN [25]) under high pressure. This method is described simply as follows:

Considering a crystal compressed by the isotropic pressure $P$ to the density $\rho_1=1/V_1$ (where $V_1$ is the distorted volume from the lattice distortion $\varepsilon_{ij}$), there leads to a small homogeneous deformation in the strained lattice. Then the Bravais lattice based vector $\vec{R}$ turns into the strained lattice $\vec{R}'$:

$$
\vec{R}_{\mathrm{i}}{ }^{\prime}=\sum\left(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\right) \vec{R}_{\mathrm{j}}
\tag{1}
$$

For a homogeneous strain, the parameter $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecher delta and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ are simple constants, where the subscripts $i$ and $j$ indicate the Cartesian components. Since cubic and hexagonal crystal possess three and five independent elastic constants, respectively, we thus use three and five independent strains as shown in Table 1. The strains are non-volume-conserving, and atomic positions are optimized at all strains. For each strain, series of small values of $\gamma$ are taken to calculate the total energies $E$ for each crystal structure. The obtained $E$–$\gamma$ points are then fitted to fourth-order polynomial $E\left(\rho_{1}, \gamma\right)$, and the second-order derivatives of $E\left(\rho_{1}, \gamma\right)$ with respect to $\gamma$ are easily gained.

### 2.3. Thermodynamic properties

The quasi-harmonic Debye model [26] in which the phonon effects are considered, has been successfully investigated the thermodynamic properties of c-BN [27], TcC [28] and TiC [29]. In quasi-harmonic Debye model, the non-equilibrium Gibbs function $G$ take the following form:

$$
G=E+P V-A_{\mathrm{vib}}(\Theta ; T)
\tag{2}
$$

where $E$, $P$, $V$ and $\Theta$ are the total energy, pressure, volume and Debye temperature, respectively, and the vibrational Helmholtz free energy $A_{\text{vib}}$ can be given as [30]

$$
A_{\mathrm{vib}}(\Theta ; T)=n k T\left[\frac{9}{8} \frac{\Theta}{T}+3 \ln \left(1-e^{-\Theta / T}\right)-D(\Theta / T)\right]
\tag{3}
$$

where $D(\Theta/T)$ is the Debye integral, $n$ is the number of atoms per formula unit. By solving the thermal equations of states

$$
\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial V}\right)_{P, T}=0
\tag{4}
$$

the isochoric heat capacity $C_{\mathrm{V}}$ and thermal coefficient $\alpha$ can be obtained. The related formulas are

$$
C_{\mathrm{V}}=3 n k\left[4 D\left(\frac{\Theta}{T}\right)-\frac{3 \Theta / T}{e^{\Theta / T}-1}\right]
\tag{5}
$$

$$
\alpha=\frac{\gamma C_{\mathrm{V}}}{B_{\mathrm{T}} V}
\tag{6}
$$

$\gamma$ and $B_{\mathrm{T}}$ are the Grüneisen parameter and isothermal bulk modulus, respectively, which are defined as

$$
\gamma=-\frac{\gamma d(\ln \Theta(V))}{d(\ln (V))}
\tag{7}
$$

$$
B_{\mathrm{T}}=V\left(\frac{\partial^{2} G}{\partial^{2} V}\right)_{P, T}
\tag{8}
$$

For cubic structures:

As is known to all, the arithmetic average of the Voigt $(B_{V},G_{V})$ and the Reuss $(B_{R},G_{R})$ can be commonly used to estimate the bulk modulus $B$ and shear modulus $G$, which can be evaluated by the Voigt-Reuss-Hill scheme [36]
$$B=\frac{1}{2}(B_{\mathrm{R}}+B_{\mathrm{V}}) \tag{11}$$

$$G=\frac{1}{2}(G_{\mathrm{R}}+G_{\mathrm{V}}) \tag{12}$$

For cubic structure:
$$B_{\mathrm{R}}=B_{\mathrm{V}}=\frac{1}{3}(C_{11}+2C_{12}) \tag{13}$$

$$G_{\mathrm{R}}=5(C_{11}-C_{12})C_{44}/[4C_{44}+3(C_{11}-C_{12})] \tag{14}$$

$$G_{\mathrm{V}}=\frac{1}{5}(C_{11}-C_{12}+3C_{44}) \tag{15}$$

For hexagonal structure:

$$B_{\mathrm{V}}=\frac{1}{9}[2(C_{11}+C_{12})+4C_{13}+C_{33}] \tag{17}$$

$$G_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{5}{2}C^{2}C_{44}C_{66}/\left[3B_{\mathrm{V}}C_{44}C_{66}+C^{2}(C_{44}+C_{66})\right] \tag{18}$$

$$G_{\mathrm{V}}=\frac{1}{30}(M+12C_{44}+12C_{66}) \tag{19}$$

where
$$C^{2}=(C_{11}+C_{12})C_{33}-2C_{13}^{2} \tag{20}$$

$$M=C_{11}+C_{12}+2C_{13}-4C_{13} \tag{21}$$

$$C_{66}=\frac{1}{2}(C_{11}-C_{12}) \tag{22}$$

The compressional wave velocity $v_{\mathrm{p}}$ and shear wave velocity $v_{\mathrm{s}}$ can be calculated by Navier's equations [37]:

$$
v_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{G / \rho} \tag{23}
$$

$$
v_{\mathrm{p}}=\sqrt{\left(B+\frac{4}{3} G\right) / \rho} \tag{24}
$$

The averaged wave velocity $v_{\mathrm{m}}$ can be expressed by

$$
v_{\mathrm{m}}=\left[\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{2}{v_{\mathrm{s}}^{3}}+\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{p}}^{3}}\right)\right]^{-1 / 3} \tag{25}
$$

The polycrystalline Young's modulus $E$ and the Poisson's ratio $\sigma$ can also be obtained by

$$
E=\frac{9 B G}{3 B+G} \tag{26}
$$

$$
\sigma=\frac{3 B-2 G}{6 B+2 G} \tag{27}
$$

Our theoretical bulk modulus $B$ (GPa), Young's modulus $E$ (GPa), shear modulus $G$ (GPa), and Poisson's ratio $\sigma$ for NbO- and NiAs-type ReN at $P=0$ GPa and $T=0$ K are summarized in Table 3. Also, we have plotted the variations of bulk modulus, Young's modulus and shear modulus for NbO-ReN and NiAs-ReN as a function of pressure in Fig. 5. It is clearly that $B$, $E$, and $G$ (GPa) show a linear increase. The compressional, shear wave velocities and the averaged wave velocity also ascend linearly with increasing pressure (Fig. 6).

The ratio of bulk modulus to shear modulus ($B/G$) introduced by Pugh [38] is a kind of standard judgment for ductile or brittle behavior. Pugh [38] also gave a certain value for ductile-brittle transition, i.e., if $B/G<1.75$, the material behaves in a brittle manner and vice versa. At 0 K and 0 GPa, our calculated values of $B/G$ for NbO-ReN and NiAs-ReN are 2.026 and 1.778, respectively, which suggests the ductility for the two structures.

Now, we turn to discuss the anisotropic behavior, which is necessary for understanding the evolution of bonding in the system. For cubic crystal, the elastic anisotropy is assessed by [39]

$$
A=\frac{2 C_{44}}{C_{11}-C_{12}} \tag{28}
$$

For hexagonal crystal, the elastic anisotropy relates to three types of elastic waves, the anisotropy for the compressional wave $\Delta P$ can be calculated by [40]

$$

$$

### 3.3. Density of states

To elucidate the origin of mechanical properties, we have presented the pressure-induced variations of total and partial density of states for the NbO-ReN and NiAs-ReN, respectively. As seen in Fig. 8, both the structures have finite DOS at the Fermi energy level which indicates the metallic behavior, and the Fermi energy level is mainly originated from the strong hybridization between Re 5d and N 2p orbitals. With the increasing pressure, the peaks of the density of states decrease and notably move away from the Fermi level, which indicates the obvious increase of non-locality. At the same time, the gradually wider pseudo-gap means the gradually stronger covalent bonding.

### 3.4. Hardness

As a measure to resist penetration, deformation, abrasion, and wear, hardness plays an important role in industrial applications [11]. Given to the above investigation, ReN quite possibly possesses excellent hardness. Here we predict the Vickers hardness by Eq. (32) introduced by Tian et al. [41].

$$H_{\mathrm{V}}=0.92(G / B)^{1.137} G^{0.708} \tag{32}$$

The calculated results are 16.3 GPa and 21.8 GPa (Table 3) for NbO- and NiAs-type, respectively, which is close to but smaller than the previous theoretical values (18.6 GPa and 24.6 GPa [4]). The difference may be caused by the different theoretical method for Vickers hardness.

### 3.5. Thermodynamic properties

As a fundamental parameter of a material, the Debye temperature $\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}$ correlates with an enormous range of mechanical-physical lattice properties ranging from atomic-vibration amplitudes to fracture strength [42]. Thus, we have estimated $\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}$ by the following formula [43]:

$$\Theta=\frac{h}{k}\left(\frac{3 n}{4 \pi}\left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{A}} \rho}{M}\right)\right)^{1 / 3} v_{\mathrm{m}} \tag{33}$$

where $h$, $k$, $N_{\mathrm{A}}$, $n$, $\rho$, and $M$ take their usual meanings.

The calculated Debye temperature $\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}$ for NbO-ReN is 525 K, which is in good agreement with the results of Wang et al. (525 K) [4], and Liu et al. (549 K) [10]. We can also find the $\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}$ increase as the pressure increasing (Fig. 5). Fig. 9 shows the thermal expansion coefficient and the relationship between temperature and pressure for the NbO-ReN. We note that the thermal expansion decreases monotonically with the increase of pressure at given temperature (400, 800, 1200 K), but the increase of $\alpha$ with temperature becomes smaller, especially at higher temperature. It is demonstrated clearly that $\alpha$ increases rapidly with $T^{3}$ at low temperature and approaches gradually to a linear increase at high temperature.

It is observed that when $T < 1000$ K, the heat capacity $C_V$ depends on both temperature $T$ and pressure $P$, which is due to the anharmonic approximations of the Debye model. However, as the temperature and pressure continue to rise, the $C_V$ is close to the Dulong-Petit limit ($\approx 49.90\,\text{J}\,\text{mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$) which is due to the suppressed anharmonic effect on heat capacity.

## 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have conducted a theoretical analysis about the electronic structure, phase transition, elastic, and thermodynamic properties of ReN using first-principles technique. Our investigation predicts that the NbO-type structure is thermodynamically the most stable at 0 K and 0 GPa, and can translate into NiAs-type when the pressure is high than 52.8 GPa, which is in accordance with the find of Wang et al., while opposite to the results reported by Zhao et al., Chen et al., Asvini et al., and Hlynsson et al. From the calculated elastic constants, we have predicted the aggregate modulus ($B, E, G$), compressional and shear wave velocities for NbO- and NiAs-type ReN, and these quantities obtained present monotonicity with the pressure. By analyzing $B/G$, the ductile-brittle behavior under high pressure is assessed. The anisotropy of ReN at high pressures is also analyzed for a complete description. Moreover, we have calculated the density of states and Vickers hardness for NbO-ReN and NiAs-ReN, respectively. Furthermore, we have obtained the Debye temperature under high pressure for NbO-ReN and NiAs-ReN. And through the quasi-harmonic Debye model, thermal expansion $\alpha$ and heat capacity $C_V$ dependence on pressure and temperature for NbO-ReN are also calculated.
