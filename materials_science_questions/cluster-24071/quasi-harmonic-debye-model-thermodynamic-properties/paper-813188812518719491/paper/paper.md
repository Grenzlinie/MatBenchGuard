## ABSTRACT

Thermal expansion coefficient of AlB₂-type WB₂ was investigated using in-situ x-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements and quasi-harmonic Debye approximation combined with density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The calculated average volumetric thermal expansion coefficient $24.86 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ agrees with the experimental value $21.52 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ in the temperature range of 298 K to 473 K. Different linear thermal expansion coefficients along the $a$ ($6.04 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) and $c$ ($9.39 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) directions have been found experimentally. This anisotropic behavior is attributed to the different bonding characteristics along the two directions. A Lindemann-like melting rule has been found for AlB₂-type MB₂ ($M$=Ti, Zr, V, Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo and Mn), and the melting point of AlB₂-type WB₂ has been estimated about $2833.80 \pm 18.4$ K according to this rule. Furthermore, the temperature and pressure dependence of thermal expansion and heat capacity is also studied. To our knowledge, it is the first time to investigate the thermal properties of AlB₂-type WB₂.

## 1. Introduction

Transition metal borides display many excellent properties, such as high melting point, high hardness, chemical inertness and good thermal and electrical conductivity, which make them the most promising engineering materials with a wide range of industrial applications [1–4]; and the interest in transition metal borides has increased owning to the search for new ultra-incompressible, superhard materials [5–12]. Very recently, we have successfully prepared the AlB₂-type WB₂ coatings by dc magnetron sputtering [13]. Knowledge of the thermal properties (such as thermal expansion and heat capacity) of this compound has great scientific and practical significance. Particularly, the thermal expansion is an important parameter for designing hard coating system, since the difference of thermal expansion coefficients between the coating and substrate is crucial to the adhesion strength of the coating/substrate system. However, the thermal properties of this compound are not available due to the difficulties in its preparation as a high-pressure phase which is only stable above 65 GPa predicted theoretically [14,15]. The successful synthesis of its coating material makes it possible to determine its thermal expansion coefficient by in-situ x-ray diffraction measurements. However, coatings may have different properties with their bulk materials attributed to the size effect caused by dimensional constrains [16]. This is because that the dimension in one direction of coatings is much smaller than in the other ones. To eliminate the size effect, the coating samples have to be treated into powders. In addition to the determination of thermal expansion coefficient using experimental approaches, theoretical calculations also provide powerful tools to evaluate thermal properties of solid materials. Recently, calculations based on first-principles methods have demonstrated that the quasi-harmonic approximation provides a reliable way to determine the thermal properties of many bulk materials below the melting point [17]. GIBBS2 [18] is a program based on the quasi-harmonic approximation [19]. The calculation of temperature and pressure dependence of the thermodynamic properties of solid phases can be easily carried out by this program. When conducting the calculation the energy versus volume data is needed, which can be yielded form the electronic structure and crystal calculations. This code's predecessor is GIBBS [20]. Since its publication, the GIBBS code has became a popular and inexpensive way to determine the thermal properties. Therefore, experimental measurements combined with the theoretical calculations can be used to investigate the thermal expansion coefficient of AlB₂-type WB₂. Besides, the melting point is another important thermal property, but it is still not available for AlB₂-type WB₂. Moreover it is difficult to determine its melting point in the coating form. For the group IV–VII transition metal diborides crystallized in AlB₂-type, a fairly linear relationship between the mean thermal expansion coefficient and the reciprocal of the melting point has been observed (AlB₂-type WB₂ was not included for the absence of its thermal expansion coefficient) [21]. This relationship makes it possible to evaluate the melting point of AlB₂-type WB₂ with knowledge of its thermal expansion coefficient. Moreover, heat capacity can also be investigated as a by-product result from the theoretical calculations.

In this work, we apply the in-situ x-ray diffraction together with quasi-harmonic Debye model, implemented in GIBBS2 code to investigate the thermal properties of AlB₂-type WB₂ such as thermal expansion, heat capacity and melting point. And to our knowledge, this is the first time to study its thermal properties.

## 2. Experimental and computational methods

### 2.1. Preparation and characterization

The AlB₂-type WB₂ coating used to investigate the thermal expansion coefficient was deposited by dc magnetron sputter- ing. This system has been described in detail in our previous work [13]. Briefly, the deposition system consists of a cylindrical stainless steel vacuum chamber equipped with a water-cooled rect- angular planar target (WB₂-type WB₂ bulk with 99.5% purity and 270 mm × 70 mm × 7 mm in size). The substrates are mounted onto a heater opposing the target with a distance of 5 cm. Before the deposition, the aluminium substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone and ethanol and then dried. The vacuum during the deposition is fixed at 0.3 Pa with Ar as working gas. The substrate was negatively biased at −150 V for one and a half hours and then the bias voltage was reduced to −60 V for half an hour. The substrate temperature and target voltage and current were main- tained at 580 °C, 365 V and 0.55 A, respectively. To eliminate the size effect and influences of the substrate on the thermal properties and make the obtained results close to its intrinsic values, the sample coatings were treated in the following steps. Firstly, the substrate was removed by corroding it using hydrochloric acid solution (36%), and the coatings were remained due to its high chemical inertness. And then, the remained coatings were smashed into powders in hydrochloric acid solution (36%) ultrasonically.

The treated sample powders were investigated using a Bruker AXS, D8 advance diffractometer with a Mo strip heater. The diffractometer worked in the $\theta/2\theta$ mode using Cu $K_\alpha$ radia- tion. The temperature varied from room temperature to 473 K at $2.2 × 10^{-2}$ Pa to $1.5 × 10^{-2}$ Pa pressure. During the heating process, the sample was hold for 2 min at each temperature to make sure the sample in the thermal equilibrium state. The Mo (1 1 0) diffraction line was used as a reference line to calibrate the diffraction position. Three samples have been tested to obtain reliable results. Besides, Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; S-3400N, Hitachi, Japan) was carried out to characterize the morphology of the sample powders; the composition was analyzed by Electron probe x-ray microanal- yser (EPMA; EPMA-1600, Shimadzu, Japan) with WB₂-type WB₂ as the calibration standard sample.

### 2.2. Computational details

Calculations of thermal properties using GIBBS2 program are based on the energy versus volume data. To this end, we firstly cal- culate the energy versus volume curve by performing plane-wave pseudopotential method based on the density functional theory (DFT) [22]. Within DFT calculations, the exchange and correla- tion functional was treated by generalized gradient approximation (GGA-PBE) [23] and the Vanderbilt ultra-soft pseudopotential [24] was used with cutoff energy of 400 eV. The Brillouin zone was sampled with 9 × 9 × 8 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh [25]. At the beginning, geometry optimization has been performed from the calculation of the total energy and the equilibrium lattice constants ($a$ and $c$) can be obtained. Then the $E(V)$ data can be obtained by cal- culating the minimum total energy using a set of $a$ and $c$ with a fixed $c/a$ ratio.

Poisson ratio $\nu$ is also an important input parameter within the GIBBS2 code. To obtain the Poisson ratio $\nu$, the elastic properties of AlB₂-type WB₂ need to be calculated. There are five independent components of the elastic tensor for AlB₂-type WB₂, i.e. $C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, $C_{13}$, $C_{33}$, $C_{44}$, which were obtained by computing the stress gen- erated by applying a small strain to the optimized unit cell. The knowledge of elastic constants allows us to calculate the bulk and shear modulus. To our best of knowledge, single crystal AlB₂-type WB₂ has not been prepared experimentally, and polycrystalline samples are synthesized and investigated in many cases. Thus, it will be more useful to calculate the bulk and shear modulus for the polycrystalline AlB₂-type WB₂. And an available method is to employ the Voigt–Reuss–Hill (VRH) approximation [26–28]. The Reuss bulk modulus ($B_R$), Reuss shear modulus ($G_R$), Voigt bulk modulus ($B_V$), Voigt shear modulus ($G_V$), as well as bulk modulus ($B_H=(B_R+B_V)/2$) and shear modulus ($G_H=(G_R+G_V)/2$) can be calcu- lated. And then the Poisson ratio $\nu=(3B_H-2G_H)/[2(3B_H+G_H)]$ can be obtained. Besides, the thermal properties are strongly depend- ent on the crystal structure; therefore, to understand the thermal properties fundamentally, the electron density was also calculated at zero pressure.

In quasi-harmonic Debye model, the non-equilibrium Gibbs energy $G^*(V;p,T)$ can be written as
$$G^{*}(V ; p, T)=E(V)+p V+A_{v i b}(\Theta(V) ; T),\tag{1}$$
where $E(V)$ is the total energy per unit cell, $A_{vib}(\Theta(V);T)$ is the vibra- tional Helmholtz free energy, an $\Theta(V)$ is the Debye temperature. Vibrational contribution $A_{vib}$ can be written as in the form
$$A_{v i b}(\Theta ; T)=n k T\left\{\left(\frac{9}{8}\right)\left(\frac{\Theta}{T}\right)+3 \ln \left[1-\exp \left(\frac{-\Theta}{T}\right)\right]-D\left(\frac{\Theta}{T}\right)\right\},\quad(2)$$
where $D(y)$ is the Debye integral, $\Theta$ is the Debye temperature, and $n$ is the number of atoms per formula unit.

In Debye theory the vibration of solid is considered as elastic waves, and in this case, the Debye temperature is related to an aver- age sound velocity. With the isotropic approximation and Poisson ratio $\nu$, the $\Theta$ can be given by
$$\Theta=\frac{\hbar}{k}\left[6 \pi^{2} V^{1 / 2} n\right] f(\nu)\left(\frac{B_{S}}{M}\right)^{1 / 2},\tag{3}$$
where $M$ is the molecular mass per formula unit, $B_S$ is the adiabatic bulk modulus and $f(\nu)$ is given by [29,30]. $B_S$ can be approximated by the static compressibility and can be further simplified as
$$B_{S} \approx B_{\text {static }}(V)=V\left[\frac{d^{2} E(V)}{d V^{2}}\right],\tag{4}$$

Then, the $(p,T)$ equilibrium can be obtained by minimizing the Gibbs function $G^*(V;p,T)$ with respect to volume $V$:
$$\left[\frac{\partial G^{*}(V ; p, T)}{\partial V}\right]_{p, T}=0,\tag{5}$$

In this way, the thermal equation of state (EOS) $V(p,T)$ can be obtained by solving Eq. (5), and the isothermal bulk modulus $B_T$, heat capacity $C_V$ and thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha$ can be calcu- lated as
$$B_{T}(p, T)=V\left[\frac{\partial^{2} G^{*}(V ; p, T)}{\partial V^{2}}\right] p, \quad T,\tag{6}$$
$$C_{V}=3 n k\left\{\frac{4 D(\Theta / T)-(3 \Theta / T)}{\exp (\Theta / T)-1}\right\},\tag{7}$$
$$\alpha=\frac{\gamma C_{V}}{B_{T} V},\tag{8}$$
where $\gamma$ is the Grüneisen parameter, defined as
$$\gamma=-\left[\frac{d \ln \Theta(V)}{d \ln V}\right].\tag{9}$$

Besides, the average linear thermal expansion coefficients along the $a$ and $c$ direction and the average volumetric thermal expansion coefficient are determined experimentally from the following relations

$$
\alpha_{a}=\frac{1}{a_{0}}\left[\frac{a-a_{0}}{T-T_{0}}\right], \tag{10}
$$

$$
\alpha_{c}=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\left[\frac{c-c_{0}}{T-T_{0}}\right], \tag{11}
$$

$$
\alpha_{V}=\frac{1}{V_{0}}\left[\frac{V-V_{0}}{T-T_{0}}\right] \tag{12}
$$

$V$ and $V_0$ refer to the primitive cell volumes at $T$ (K) and $T_0$ (K), respectively. And the same notation was also used for the lattice parameter $a$ and $c$. In this work, the thermal properties calculated with the temperature range from 0 K to 2000 K and pressure range from 0 GPa to 100 GPa.

## 3. Results and discussion

The coating exhibits dense columnar structure as shown in Fig. 1(a). Average thickness is about $3.7\pm0.5$ μm. The ultrasonically smashed sample powders are in the form of irregular debris as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). Besides, the EPMA gives the W/B atomic ratio about 1:1.96, which is close to the stoichiometric ratio 1:2. The compact structure with stoichiometric ratio makes it possible to investigate its thermal properties eliminating the influence of structural defects.

The calculated lattice constants and $c/a$ ratio are listed in Table 1 together with other available theoretical and experimental data. The computed lattice parameter $a$ is in a good agreement with the theoretical data [14,31,32] and the deviation is less than 1.8%. Compared with the experimental results [13,33], the lattice constant $a$ is only underestimated by 0.43%, the lattice constant $c$, however, is overestimated by 9.8%. This is not a surprise since other computed data (like 3.3197 [31], 3.334 [32] and 3.323 [14]) are also overestimated by about 8% compared with the experimental value [13,33]. This high disagreement in the lattice parameter $c$ might be due to the GGA method which usually overestimates the lattice constants when compared with experimental values.

The calculated zero-pressure elastic properties together with other available calculated results [14,31,32] are listed in Table 2. Note that the agreement of our calculated data to available published results indicates the accuracy and reliability of the computational program applied. Since $C_{11}>0$, $(C_{11}-C_{12})>0$, $C_{44}>0$ and $(C_{11}+C_{12})C_{33}-2C_{12}{}^2>0$, which satisfy the well known Born criteria [34], indicating the mechanical stability of AlB₂-type WB₂. Besides, knowledge of elastic coefficients provides a way to calculate the linear compressibility. Terms of $k_a$ and $k_c$ refer to the linear compressibility coefficient along $a$ and $c$ direction, respectively. The ratio between linear compressibility coefficients $k_c/k_a$ for hexagonal crystal can be expressed as [35]

$$
\frac{k_{c}}{k_{a}}=\frac{C_{11}+C_{12}-2 C_{13}}{C_{33}-C_{13}}. \tag{13}
$$

This ratio is a measure of the degree of anisotropy for the linear compressibility along the $c$ and $a$ axis. The ratio $k_c/k_a=1$ means the isotropic compressibility. Any deviation from the unity value implies the anisotropic compressibility. The $k_c/k_a$ value of AlB₂-type WB₂ is about 1.42 (see Table 2), suggesting the incompressibility along the $c$ axis is much smaller than that along the $a$ axis. In addition, the elastic constant along the $c$ axis ($C_{33}$=475.9 GPa) is smaller than that along the $a$ axis ($C_{11}$=634.3 GPa), which can also indicate the anisotropy.

The calculated Poisson ratio $v$ is 0.2704 which is slightly smaller than the calculated values reported by other authors [14,31,32]. The $E(V)$ curve can be plotted by fitting the $E(V)$ data using Birch-Murnaghan equation [36] (see Fig. 2).

The effect of temperature on the lattice parameters $a$ and $c$ has been characterized experimentally as illustrated in Fig. 3(a). Note that the error bars are invisible for both $a$ and $c$ on fig. 3(a). With the increasing temperature, both the lattice parameters $a$ and $c$ grow linearly, and the linear thermal expansion coefficients $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_c$ were calculated from the formula (10) and (11) as listed in Table 3. Note that $\alpha_c$ is larger than $\alpha_a$ indicating the anisotropy of the AlB₂-type WB₂ with higher incompressibility along the $a$ axis than that along the $c$ axis, and this is in accordance with the discussion about the $k_c/k_a$. And this can be understood in terms of the atomic arrangement in the crystal structure. AlB₂-type WB₂ adopts a simple layered hexagonal crystal structure (space group $P6/mmm$) [32]. As shown in Fig. 4, closed packed tungsten layers alternate with graphite-like boron layers in sequence ...AHAHAH... perpendicularly to the $c$ direction. The boron atoms are arranged at the corners of a hexagon with three nearest neighbor boron atoms in each plane. The tungsten atoms are located at the center of the boron hexagons, midway between adjacent boron layers. The primitive cell contains one tungsten and two boron atoms ($Z$=1). And the W and B atoms are located on the 1a (0, 0, 0) and 2d (1/3, 2/3, 1/2) Wyckoff sites, respectively. Fig. 5 shows the charge density distribution in (110), (001) and (002) planes. From Fig. 5(a) we can see weak bonds are formed between boron and their nearest tungsten atoms along the $c$ axis, since little electrons are found between boron and tungsten atoms. While, strong covalent bonds are found between neighbor boron atoms, which can be also confirmed by Fig. 5(c) plotted in the (002) plane. In this plane, strong covalent bonds are formed among boron atom and its three other neighbor boron atoms forming a strong covalent 2D graphite-like network. However, metallic bonds between tungsten atoms in the (001) plane is weak as presented in Fig. 5(b), indicating the strong boron layers play a dominated role in the directions parallel to the (001) plane. Therefore, within boron layers, atoms are aligned directly with strong covalent bonds. However, along the $c$ axis, boron and tungsten layers are packed alternately, and only weak W-B bonds are formed. And these bonding characters explain the anisotropy discussed above.

The thermal expansion coefficients and melting points of 8 other AlB₂-type MB₂ ($M$=Ti, Zr, V, Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo and Mn) [21,37–44] are also listed in Table 3. As reported by Lonnberg [21], there is a linear relationship between the mean thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha_m=(2\alpha_a+\alpha_c)/3$ and the inverse melting temperature $T_m^{-1}$ (K$^{-1}$). Here, we calculate the average slope $C$ by linear fitting the available experimental data using least-squares method. As shown in Fig. 6, the linear fitting gives the average slope $C$=$0.0203\pm0.0064$. In this case, the relationship between $\alpha_m$ and $T_m$ satisfies a formula $\alpha_mT_m=C$=$0.0203\pm0.0064$. This formula is similar with the "Lindemann melting rule" [45,46] which is considered to be one of the basic melting criteria for metals and alloys. Here, this equation was applied to AlB₂-type WB₂, and with the mean linear thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha_m(e)$=$(7.16\pm0.001)\times10^{-6}$ K$^{-1}$ (obtained from experimental data), the melting point of AlB₂-type WB₂ can be estimated about $2833.80\pm18.4$ K. The high melting point explains the difficulties in preparing it by means of powder metallurgy (PM) method with sintering temperature usually below $1800\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ [47–51], such a low temperature is below the typical sintering temperature about 2/3 to 3/4 around the melting point of AlB₂-type WB₂. Therefore, there are rare reports about its preparation using PM. Besides, theoretical calculations have confirmed that AlB₂-type WB₂ is a high-pressure phase which is only stable above 65 GPa [14,15]. However, high pressure condition is still a challenge for current experimental setups. In contrast to PM method, high temperature and high pressure conditions can be easily achieved by magnetron sputtering due to the "thermal spike" [52] caused by ion bombardment. And this could be confirmed by our previous work depositing AlB₂-type WB₂ successfully using dc magnetron sputtering [13].

## 4. Conclusions

In conclusion, AlB₂-type WB₂ coating has been deposited by dc magnetron sputtering. The ultrasonically smashed sample pow- ders were in the form of irregular debris with the thickness about $3.7\pm0.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. The sample coatings were dense with the W/B atomic ratio (1:1.96) close to the stoichiometric ratio. The thermal expansion coefficient was investigated by in-situ x-ray diffraction method. And the thermal properties were also studied by per- forming the quasi-harmonic Debye model combined with density functional calculations. The calculated average volumetric thermal expansion coefficient $24.86\times10^{-6}\mathrm{K}^{-1}$ is in accordance with the experimental value $21.52\times10^{-6}\mathrm{K}^{-1}$ at temperatures from 298 K to 473 K. The measured linear thermal expansion coefficients $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_c$ is about $6.04\times10^{-6}\mathrm{K}^{-1}$ and $9.39\times10^{-6}\mathrm{K}^{-1}$, respectively. And the anisotropic behavior of the thermal expansion coefficients could be explained by its crystal structure, since strong B-B cova- lent bonds exist in the ab-plane, while only weak W-B bonds are formed along the $c$ direction; and a Lindemann-like melting rule has been found for AlB₂-type MB₂ ($M$=Ti, Zr, V, Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo and Mn), and the melting point of AlB₂-type WB₂ has been estimated about $2833.80\pm18.4\mathrm{K}$ according to this rule. Besides, the tempera- ture and pressure dependence of the thermal expansion $\alpha$ and heat capacity $C_V$ have been also investigated in this work.
