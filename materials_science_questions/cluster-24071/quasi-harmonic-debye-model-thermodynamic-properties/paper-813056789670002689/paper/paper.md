## ABSTRACT

The structural, electronic, optical and thermal properties of ternary transition metal boride (CrAlB) are studied using full potential linearized augmented plane wave method in the framework of the density functional theory. The exchange-correlation potential is solved using the modified Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation (PBEsol-GGA). The structural parameters are optimized to obtain equilibrium lattice constant, bulk modulus and its pressure derivative. The calculated band structure, density of states and electronic charge density show that the material possesses metallic nature and have covalent band character. Optical spectra calculations such as the real and imaginary part of dielectric constant, refractive index, extinction coefficient, reflectivity, absorption coefficient, conductivity, and loss function are performed within the photon energy range of 0-40 eV. In this case the obtained plasma frequency corresponds to 24.12eV. For radiation above this energy the property of CrAlB changes from metallic to dielectric. The calculations of the thermal properties using Gibb's program have been performed in the range 0-5000 K at 0, 20 and 50GPa of pressure. The studies of optical and thermal properties show that it is an eligible candidate for optoelectronic devices, and the devices working at high temperature. The results for CrAlB are reported for the first time as neither CrAlB has been synthesized nor any theoretical calculation has been performed till so far.

Keywords: Layered structure, First principles, Structural properties, Electronic structure, Optical properties, Thermal properties

## Introduction
Transition metals such as nitrides, borides and carbides are the hardest materials and have extremely high melting points, thus they are regarded as refractory materials [1]. These binary transition metals are also regarded as Ultra High Temperature Ceramics (UHTCs) as they have applications in hypersonic vehicles [2], plasma arc electrodes, cutting tools, furnace elements and high temperature shielding [3]. However, the obstacle that hamper the applications of binary transition metal borides are intrinsic

brittleness, which shows that they have low fracture toughness, low damage tolerance, poor resistance to chemical degradation at high temperature [4], poor thermal fatigue resistance and difficulty in machining into sharp or complex shapes [2]. Aluminum layer inserted into binary transition metals to form ternary layered structure [5] improves their brittleness and makes them corrosion resistant. Ultra-high temperature ceramics possesses dual characteristics, i.e., of metal as well as of ceramics. As metal they have good thermal conductivity, thermal shock resistance and machinability, as ceramics they are good oxidation resistant. Because of above two properties CrAlB is an eligible candidate for large industrial applications [6].

According to literature survey, no experimental and theoretical work has been reported on ternary Chromium Aluminum Boride (CrAlB), therefore, it was thought that it is worthwhile to report the ab-initio calculations within density functional theory to predict the structural, electronic, optical and thermal properties of CrAlB keeping in mind usability of ternary layered compound containing Al layer.

### Technical details
The calculations in the present work have been carried within the frame work of density functional theory (DFT). The full potential linearized augmented plane wave (FP- LAPW) method has been used to solve Kohn-Sham equations as implemented in Wien2k code [7]. Modeling the XC interactions is the main difficulty of DFT. The most commonly used GGA functional is the one proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzherhof (PBE). It almost always overestimates the lattice constants of solids, while LDA consistently underestimates them. In both cases, the typical errors amount to 1-2% of the lattice parameters. A series of alternative GGA functional have been proposed to overcome this problem. One of them is the PBEsol [8] functional. It is seen as revised versions of PBE specifically adapted for solids [9].

In this manuscript the exchange and correlation energy has been obtained using the PBEsol-GGA functional. The atomic muffin tin radii were chosen such that the optimized values neither overlap with each other nor the core charge leaks out of the sphere. The plane wave cutoff was decided by $R_{MT}* K_{max}=7.0$, where $R_{MT}$ is the smallest muffin tin radius and $K_{max}$ is the maximum value of the lattice vector of the basis set. The expansion of the wave functions was set to $l=10$ inside the muffin tin spheres. The number of k-points in the irreducible wedge of Brillouin zone was fixed to 7×7×6 k-point on the basis of converged value of the total energy as a function of k- points for the same set of the unit cell inputs. The calculations were stopped only when the total energy and the charge of the system reached the convergent limit of the order of $10^{-4}$ Ry and $10^{-3}e^{-}$ respectively. After this converged values of the total energy with respect to different unit cell volume were used to obtain equilibrium cell parameters. Now corresponding to equilibrium lattice constants self consistent cycle (SCF) was completed to obtain band structure and density of states plots and also to complete optical properties calculations.

Further obtained sets of total energy and cell volume were used to determine the temperature effects on CrAlB by using quasiharmonic Debye model as implemented in

GIBBS code [10]. This model generates Debye temperature $\Theta_D$ (V) and obtains the non-equilibrium Gibbs function G*(V; P, T) and minimize G* to derive the thermal equation of state V (P, T). In the quasi-harmonic Debye model the non-equilibrium Gibbs functions G*(V; P, T) is written as:

$$
G^{*}(V ; P, T)=E(V)+P V+A_{v i b}(\theta(V) ; T)
$$

Here E(V) is the total energy per unit cell, $PV$ is the product of hydrostatic pressure and unit cell volume, and $A_{vib}$ is the Helmholtz free energy [10-12].

For an isotopic solid the Debye temperature, $\theta_D$ is expressed as

$$
\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}=\frac{\mathrm{h}}{k}\left[6 \pi^{2} V^{1 / 2} n\right]^{1 / 3} f(\sigma) \sqrt{\frac{B_{S}}{M}},
$$

where M is the molecular mass per formula unit and $B_S$ is the adiabatic bulk modulus. Details on $f(\sigma)$ can be found in Refs. [10-12]. Further, $B_S$ is approximated by the static compressibility as

$$
B_{\mathrm{S}} \cong B(\mathrm{~V})=\mathrm{V} \frac{d^{2} E(V)}{d v^{2}}
$$

New the thermal EOS V(P, T) can be obtained minimizing the non equilibrium Gibbs function G* (V; P, T) with respect to volume V as:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial G^{*}(V ; P, T)}{\partial V}\right)_{\mathrm{P}, \mathrm{T}}=0
$$

Thus through the Quasi harmonic Debye model, one can calculate the thermal quantities at different temperature and pressure from the calculated E-V data at T=0 and P=0. The standard thermodynamic relations are then used to derive other macroscopic properties.

## Results and Discussion

### Geometry Optimization and Structural Properties
The space group of ternary chromium aluminium boride is $Cmcm$ (space group no.63) [5]. In this structure, atomic positions are taken as: Cr (0, 0.4149, 0.25), Al (0, 0.1943, 0.25) and B (0, 0.03360, 0.25). The optimization of geometry was obtained by calculating the total energy as a function of three variables $u$, $b/a$ and $V$, where $u$ represents the internal coordinates of the atoms, $a$ and $b$ represent the lattice parameters and $V$ is related to the volume of the unit cell. It was done in two steps. In first step, the internal geometry of the cell was optimized by the procedure of geometry minimization where the forces acting between the atoms are minimized. The process was repeated until the change in the average force falls below 1mRyd/Bohr. In second step the b/a ratio optimization of the crystal energy was done for the unit cell volume $V$ and $c/a$ ratio. Different values of $b/a$ were chosen for fixed volume and then a polynomial was fitted to determine the optimized $b/a$ ratio. Once the value of $b/a$ is decided, corresponding to different volumes, the total energy has been calculated. The conversed

values of the muffin-tin radius used in the calculations are 2.30, 1.90 and 1.54 Bohr, respectively, for Cr, Al and B. Using the obtained sets we have determined the equilibrium volume $V_{o}$, the bulk modulus $B_{o}$ and the pressure derivative of the bulk modulus $B_{0}'$ by fitting the total energy as a function of volume $V$, to the Murnaghan equation of state [13].

Corresponding to the equilibrium lattice parameters the obtained crystal structure of CrAlB has been shown in fig. 1(a). It is the trigonal prism like structure and possesses 4 formulas per unit cell. The optimized equilibrium lattice parameters are given in table 1. The length of various bonds in CrAlB shown in fig. 1(a) are listed in table 2.

## Electronic Properties
The band structure, total and partial density of states of CrAlB, are shown in fig. 2(a) and fig. 2(b)-2(e). In fig. 2(a) the valance band overlaps the conduction band at Fermi level which indicates metallic nature of CrAlB. From fig. 2(c) it is evident that near Fermi level, main contribution in PDOS plot is due to $3-d$ states of chromium atom and less contribution of 3p and 2p states of Al and B atoms.

The charge density shown in fig. 1(b) and 1(c) gives the information about the probabilistic measure of presence of an electron at a specific point. It reveals the density associated with the chemical bond and the areas, where charge densities are positive and negative and also the accumulation and depletion of electronic charges. The covalent bond predicts about the accumulation of change between the sharing atoms whereas the ionic bond predicts about the nature of the charge density at the atomic positions i.e. positive and negative [14]. The unit of electronic charge density is $e/\mathring{A}^3$ within the crystallographic plane (110). Chromium has high electron density compared to Aluminum and Boron. There exists strong covalent bond between B-B atoms and Al-Al atoms. This bonding between Al-Al atom results into Al-layer in the compound,

which is less strong than B-B bond [15]. The figure also predicts that bonds between B-
Cr, Al-Cr, and Al-B are covalent in nature.

### Optical Properties

The complex dielectric function $\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_{1}(\omega)+i \varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ has been used for knowing
the optical properties of CrAlB. The imaginary part of the dielectric function, $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$,
was calculated from the momentum matrix elements between the occupied and
unoccupied wave functions, taking into account the appropriate selection rules. The real
part of the dielectric function can be calculated from $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ using Kramers-Kronig
relation [16]. The refractive index, the extinction coefficient, reflectivity, absorption
coefficient and the energy loss are calculated from the values of dielectric function. The
response of the material toward the radiation is important for the optoelectronic
applications. The optical constants are calculated upto photon energy of 40eV for both
parallel and perpendicular directions of propagation.

The real and imaginary parts of the dielectric function are shown in fig. 3(a) and
3(b). Since the CrAlB possess orthorhombic structure, therefore, it contains three
dielectric tensor components such as $\varepsilon^{xx}(\omega), \varepsilon^{yy}(\omega)$ and $\varepsilon^{zz}(\omega)$. The obtained static
dielectric constant of CrAlB are $\varepsilon_{1}^{xx}(0)=82.1985, \varepsilon_{1}^{yy}(0)=58.5518$ and $\varepsilon_{1}^{zz}(0)=$
45.1714. These large values of the dielectric constants make it an eligible candidate for
optoelectronic devices.

Figure 3(a) also shows that the real part of the dielectric function goes on
decreasing with increase in energy. Its value becomes negative from 3.55 eV to
24.69eV. Beyond 24.69 eV the real part of the dielectric function becomes positive and
more than the value of the imaginary part of the dielectric constant. The imaginary part
gives information about the nature of absorption of the material. In fig. 3(b) all the
observable peaks of $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ are contained within the energy range 0 -5eV. The calculated

highest peak values of $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ along three directions are: $\varepsilon_{2}^{xx}(\omega)=44.9929$, $\varepsilon_{2}^{yy}(\omega)=$ 29.6428 and $\varepsilon_{2}^{zz}(\omega)=24.2499$, respectively, lying at 0.29eV, 1.67eV and 1.67eV. The value of $\varepsilon_{2}(\omega)$ approaches to zero beyond 24.69 eV.

The refractive index, n(ω), is calculated from the real part of the dielectric function [17] and it gives the information about the phase velocity, while the imaginary part k(ω) known as the extinction coefficient, indicates about the amount of loss in the absorption when electromagnetic wave propagates through material. Figure 3(c) shows the variation in refractive index of CrAlB as a function of energy of the incident radiation. The values obtained for the refractive index are $n^{xx}(0)=9.17$, $n^{yy}(0)=6.9$ and $n^{zz}(0)=7.53$.

The extinction coefficient, shown in fig. 3(d), shows many peaks between 0.28eV and 13.18eV. The maximum value of the extinction coefficient, 3.63 is obtained at 3.54eV for $k^{xx}(\omega)$ . From this figure it is observed that within the range 3.54eV to 30eV, the k(ω) is larger than n(ω), which concludes that the radiation of this region cannot propagate in the medium [18].

The amount of energy absorbed by the material is given by the term known as absorption coefficient. Figure 3(e) shows the anisotropic spectrum of absorption coefficient. It starts from zero photon energy, and reaches the highest peak of $23.42x10^{5}cm^{-1}$ at 12.93eV (95.88 nm) for $\alpha^{zz}(\omega)$. The graph shows the strongest absorption between 2.24eV (553.5 nm) to 22.16eV (55.94 nm).

As the material CrAlB possess no band gap, its optical conductivity starts increasing from zero photon energy. The optical conductivity as a function of energy is shown in fig. 3(f). In this figure the maximum values of the optical conductivity ($\sigma^{xx}=$ $11.94x10^{3}\Omega^{-1}cm^{-1}$) is obtained at ~ 3.46eV of the photon energy. Beyond this energy conductivity decreases with increase in energy with a second small peak at 12.93eV. Thus one can concluded that the CrAlB is more conductive when the photon energy is about 3.46eV.

Reflectivity is defined as the ratio of the energy of reflected wave from the surface to the energy of the wave incident on the surface. Figure 3(g) presents the frequency dependent anisotropic reflectivity spectra $R^{xx}(\omega)$, $R^{yy}(\omega)$ and $R^{zz}(\omega)$ as a function of energy. The obtained value of reflectivity at 0 eV are, respectively, 0.64, 0.55 and 0.59 for x-, y- and z- components. For all three components, reflectivity varies ~ 0.5 upto ~20 eV, beyond which it starts decreasing and attains asymptotic nature near 30 eV.

When a fast moving electron traverses through material, the energy lost due to this is known as energy loss function. It describes the optical spectra and the excitation produced by fast moving electrons in a solid. The loss function is obtained from the imaginary part of the reciprocal of the complex dielectric function. Figure 3(h) shows the loss function as a function of photon energy. In this figure the pronounced maxima at 24.12eV corresponds to plasma frequency of the material [18]. After this frequency there is rapid decrease in reflectivity, which predicts that the material changes from metallic to dielectric response if the incident light has the frequency larger than 24.12eV. Because of large value of the imaginary part of the dielectric function, the loss function does not show any distinct maxima upto 18.16eV [19].

## Thermal Properties

The study of thermal properties gives information about the phase stability, melting point, and strength and bonding nature. The thermodynamic properties of CrAlB are investigated under high pressure and high temperature. Figure 4(a) represents the

variation of the unit cell volume as a function of temperature at different pressures. From figure, it is observed that the volume increases gradually with increasing temperature at a given pressure while with increase in pressure, volume decreases at a given temperature. The rate of increase in volume with temperature decreases with increase in pressure. From the fig. 4(b), it is observed that the bulk modulus decreases with the increase in temperature at a constant pressure. Also at a constant temperature the bulk modulus increases with the increase in pressure. Figure 4(c) shows the variation of Debye temperature with temperature and pressure. This figure shows that the Debye temperature increases with increase in pressure, at a constant temperature and decreases with increase in temperature at a constant pressure. Figure 4(d) represents the variation of the thermal expansion coefficient, $\alpha$, with temperature at several pressures. It is observed that the expansion coefficient increases rapidly upto 500K and after this temperature it increases linearly. This figure also shows that its value decreases with increase in pressure.

The heat capacity of a substance gives the information about the vibrational properties. Heat capacity at constant volume at various temperature and pressure is shown in fig. 4(e). It is observed that the heat capacity increases rapidly, approximately upto 500K, and after this it increases linearly and attains a constant value of 74.78 J/mol*K, which is also called Dulong-Petit limit [20], which is almost common for all solids at high temperature. Similar is the behaviour of specific heat at constant pressure at low temperature, as shown in fig. 4(f). At 5000 K and zero pressure, $C_P$ is =88.94 J/mol*K. Entropy is a measure of disorder of system. With the rise in temperature, the vibrational contribution increases which increases the entropy of the system. Figure 4(g) describes the variation of entropy with temperature and pressure. With the increase in pressure, entropy increases. The internal energy at zero pressure is shown in fig. 4(h) as a function of temperature. This figure shows that the internal energy, U, is not zero at zero temperature. The obtained value of internal energy for CrAlB at zero temperature and zero pressure is 12.47kJ/mol. Our plot shows that the value of internal energy increases linearly with the increase in temperature.

## Conclusions

In present work the structural, electronic, optical and thermal properties of ternary transition metal boride CrAlB has been studied. The above mentioned properties of this material are reported for the first time. To the best of our knowledge neither experimental nor theoretical work has been done on CrAlB. The electronic band structure and the DOS plot show that the behavior of CrAlB compound is metallic. Charge density shows that the compound possesses covalent bond nature between the atoms. The study of optical and thermal properties shows that it is an eligible candidate for optoelectronic devices as well as in devices working at high temperature.
