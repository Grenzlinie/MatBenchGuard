# LOW TEMPERATURE THERMAL PROPERTIES OF MIXED CRYSTAL KBr-KI*

B.D. Nathan, L.F. Lou,† and R.H. Tait‡

Laboratory of Atomic and Solid State Physics
and
Materials Science Center, Cornell University, Ithaca, NY 14853, U.S.A.

(Received 12 March 1976 by J. Tauc)

The low temperature specific heat and thermal conductivity of $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$ mixed crystal are measured. This material is known to have compositional fluctuations on a scale of order $1000\,\mathring{A}$. The thermal properties, however, are well described by the Debye model and show no indication of glass-like thermal behavior.

THE LOW TEMPERATURE thermal properties of glasses are very different from those of crystalline solids.$^{1,2}$ Glasses exhibit a specific heat in excess of that predicted by the Debye model using measured sound velocities. Below $\sim1$ K, the specific heat is dominated by a contribution which varies approximately linearly with temperature.$^{3}$ The thermal conductivities below $\sim1$ K vary as $T^{1.9}$, and their magnitudes are the same within a factor of ten for all glasses measured. Many theories have been proposed to explain these phenomena, including several$^{4-6}$ using mass density and elastic constant fluctuations or long-range correlations (on a scale of $5\,\mathring{A},^{4}70\,\mathring{A}^{6}$ and $1000\,\mathring{A}^{5}$).

The mixed alkali halide KBr-KI thus appeared to be a material in which it was reasonable to look for possible glass-like thermal behavior. Nair$^{7}$ found for a crystal of average composition $KBr_{0.5}I_{0.5}$, three intermixed crystalline phases. The composition, average particle size, and approximate percentage of the three phases were, respectively, $KBr_{0.26}I_{0.74},630\,\mathring{A},43\%$; $KBr_{0.5}I_{0.5},1450\,\mathring{A},24\%$; and $KBr_{0.87}I_{0.13},550\,\mathring{A},33\%$, according to X-ray analysis by Nair. We calculated densities for each phase using Vegard's law (straight-line interpolation of the lattice constant with composition between those of pure KBr and KI crystals). The resulting densities were: $3.06$ g/cc for $KBr_{0.26}I_{0.74}$, $2.96$ g/cc for $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$ and $2.81$ g/cc for $KBr_{0.87}I_{0.13}$. Use of Nair's approximate percentages yielded an average density $2.95$ g/cc for the intermixed crystal, slightly lower than the measured value $3.02$ g/cc.

The present samples were cut from a boule prepared at the Cornell Crystal Growing Facility. Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis performed at the Cornell Analytical Facility on pieces from both ends of the boule yielded an approximate overall concentration $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$. Powder diffraction X-ray analysis on a pressed pellet of powder ground from the boule confirmed the existence of three phases with lattice spacings consistent with those found by Nair. Laue back-scattering from a large crystal from the boule showed that the crystallites were nearly aligned.

Specific heat and thermal conductivity were measured by the standard transient heat pulse technique and four-probe method respectively.$^{1,2,8}$ An adiabatic demagnetization cryostat, a dilution refrigerator and a $^{4}$He cryostat were employed to cover the desired range of temperatures.

From Figs. 1 and 2 it is evident that both the specific heat and thermal conductivity exhibit a low temperature $T^{3}$ temperature dependence, characteristic of Debye crystalline behavior. The specific heat yields a Debye temperature $\theta_{D}=148$ K. Karlsson,$^{9}$ studying the homogeneous solid solution $KBr_{x}Cl_{1-x}$, found the relation $1/\theta_{D}^{2}=x/\theta_{D,\text{KBr}}^{2}+(1-x)/\theta_{D,\text{KCl}}^{2}$ to describe that system well. We have applied the analogous relation to each of the three phases present in $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$ using$^{10}$ $\theta_{D,\text{KBr}}=172$ K and $\theta_{D,\text{KI}}=132$ K. Using densities for each phase as calculated above, a specific heat was obtained for each phase.

Heat capacities were added according to fractional volumes determined by Nair and an average Debye temperature for the polycrystalline sample was calculated. The result, $\theta_D = 150$ K is in good agreement with experiment.

Boundary limited thermal conductivities were calculated from specific heat data using the Debye model and the relation $K = C_V v_D l \rho /3$ where $\rho$ is the measured density, $l$ is the phonon mean free path $(= 1.12a^{1/2}$ for a rectangular sample of cross-sectional area $a)$ and $v_D$ is the (calculated) Debye velocity. The calculation yields a value roughly twice that of measured. It is plausible that the lower value of the measured conductivity is due to a phonon mean free path limited by internal cracks or faults. This is not unexpected, since the material has been found to shatter very easily.

Scattering by the crystallite boundaries in the case of $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$ is evidently much weaker than in the case of fully dense sintered ceramics where there is no alignment of crystallites. In that case, Tait$^8$ found a phonon mean free path limited to about ten times the particle diameter, far smaller than the dimension of the sample.

Fig. 2. Thermal conductivities of KBr, KI, and $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$. Curves for pure KBr and KI are from reference 12. Also included are Casimir boundary limited $T^3$ conductivities for each sample calculated from specific heat data. Cross-sectional areas were 0.5 cm $\times$ 0.5 cm for the pure crystals and 0.298 cm $\times$ 0.336 cm for $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$. Calculated Debye velocities (in units of $10^5$ cm/s) were: KBr: 1.93; KI: 1.57; and $KBr_{0.53}I_{0.47}$: 1.73.

The high temperature thermal conductivity is more than an order of magnitude lower than that of pure KBr or KI. This effect has been observed previously for homogeneous mixed alkali halides where it was discussed in terms of large numbers of point defects$^{11,12}$ (in our case, one can view this as a 47% substitution of $I^-$ for $Br^-$ ions).

To summarize, we have found no indication of glass-like thermal behavior in this disordered solid. It is hoped that these findings might stimulate further analysis of the effects of large scale fluctuations of the crystalline environment on the thermal properties of dielectric solids.
