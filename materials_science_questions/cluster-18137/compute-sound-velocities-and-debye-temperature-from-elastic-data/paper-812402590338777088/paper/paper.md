# DETERMINATION OF THE ELASTIC CONSTANTS OF POLYCRYSTALLINE Al₃Sc

R. W. Hyland, Jr.* and R. C. Stiffler*

*Alcoa Laboratories
Alcoa Center, PA 15069

(Received October 8, 1990)
(Revised December 4, 1990)

## Introduction
The fundamental importance of reliable elastic constant measurements of crystalline solids can be easily understood from the quantitative description of such theories as the thermodynamics of stressed solids [1], fracture mechanics [2], calculations of interfacial structure and energetics [3], and diffusional solid state phase transformations [4]. Efforts to explore the possible usage of high strength to weight ratio intermetallic and intermediate phases [5] for high temperature structural applications have relied upon the values of the elastic properties as a test for suitability [6]. Recently, advances in the study of the solid state binding and interatomic potentials of metallic systems based on electronic density functional theories like the Embedded Atom method [7-9] have shown that the equations which describe the embedding functions can be deduced empirically by fitting them to experimentally determined physical properties of the bulk material. A common test of the correctness of these fitted functions is to see how well they reproduce the elastic constants and several other bulk properties. Since these functions are used to gain information concerning the structure and energy of metals from atomic scale calculations it is important that reliable experimental values of the elastic constants be available. Here we report results for the L1₂ phase Al₃Sc.

## Procedure
### 1. Materials
Starting materials of 99.999% Al produced by the Aluminum Company of America and 99.5% Sc metal obtained from Rare Earth Products Company were first arc melted under reduced argon pressure to produce an ingot button of approximately 10 g weight. The nominal starting composition was Al-25 atomic% Sc. Determination of the major impurities was accomplished via electron probe X-ray microanalysis using a JEOL 8600 Superprobe® electron microprobe operating at 20 keV. Figure 1 shows X-ray density maps taken at 750X for a randomly chosen field. A search from atomic number 5 to 92 indicated the presence of Fe, Cu, C, O and W. It is seen that the major impurity appears to be tungsten. The bulk composition was determined by choosing random fields for microprobe spot analysis. The average composition was determined to be 73.4 at % Al balance Sc with the major impurity being W. This indicated that the Sc content of the ingot was slightly greater than the charge composition. Quantitative metallographic analysis of random planar sections of the ingot using backscattered electron imaging indicated a tungsten level of 0.93 ± 0.1 wt% which was found to be in the form of discrete particles. The origin of this impurity is uncertain but may be related to the electrode material used during arc melting or to the initial processing of the Sc metal (99.5% pure). A small amount of fine scale internal porosity was also found, but was very irregularly distributed. Subsequent to chemical analysis, the ingot was machined for ultrasonic measurement of the sound velocities, and the density was measured in high purity water maintained at 298 K.

### 2. Measurement of Elastic Properties
For a statistically isotropic material, as is expected in the case of a random polycrystal, there are two independent wave velocities. A distortional or shear wave propagating in a material has a phase velocity given by
$$V_{s}=\sqrt{\mu / \rho} \tag{1}$$

Where $\mu$ is the Shear modulus and $\rho$ is the density of the material. A corresponding equation for the dilatational or longitudinal wave phase velocity is [10]
$$V_{p}=\sqrt{(\lambda+2 \mu) / \rho} \tag{2}$$


By measuring the wave speeds $V_p$ and $V_s$ and the density of the polycrystal, one can obtain Lamé's constant, $\lambda$, by
$$\mu=\rho \mathrm{V}_{s}^{2}\tag{3}$$
$$\lambda=\rho\left(\mathrm{V}_{p}^{2}-2 \mathrm{~V}_{s}^{2}\right)\tag{4}$$

From these two equations the polycrystalline elastic constants can be found from
$$\mathrm{c}_{11}=\lambda+2 \mu=\rho \mathrm{V}_{p}^{2}\tag{5}$$
$$\mathrm{c}_{44}=\mu=\rho \mathrm{V}_{s}^{2}\tag{6}$$
and
$$\mathrm{c}_{12}=\mathrm{c}_{11}-2 \mathrm{c}_{44}=\lambda\tag{7}$$

The other engineering elastic constants dependent on the Lamé constants are calculated from standard linear elasticity relationships [11].

The pulse-echo overlap technique was used to measure the shear and longitudinal wave velocities in $Al_3Sc$. In this technique an ultrasonic wave (either shear or longitudinal) is generated with a piezoelectric transducer coupled to the specimen. The ultrasonic wave propagates through the material and is reflected at the free surface of the sample. The ultrasonic pulse then propagates back toward the transducer and is reflected at the transducer:specimen interface. The reflection between the two interfaces continues until the pulse is completely attenuated. The transducer detects the waves at the transducer:specimen interface, and its output is amplified resulting in a train of reflected pulses when viewed on an oscilloscope. The technique measures the time between two successive echoes by measuring the time delay required to overlap adjacent pulses on an oscilloscope screen. This gives the sound velocity through the equation
$$\mathrm{V}=2 d / t\tag{8}$$
where t is the delay time and d is the specimen thickness. The factor of 2 accounts for the round trip distance of the pulse. Four to six useable echoes were observed in these measurements. A Matec $^{\circledR}$ MBS 8000 system was used to generate a 5 MHz, gated sine wave of 10 cycles for producing the ultrasonic waves. Transducers having a center of frequency of 5 MHz were used to generate both longitudinal (Panametrics V109) and shear (Panametrics V155) waves. These transducers are 1.25 cm in diameter and are PZT5A piezoelectric elements. Table I contains the specimen dimensions, density, and time of flight for the longitudinal and shear waves. The largest relative error (c.a. 1%) in the reported values of the velocity was due to the uncertainty in the measured thickness of the specimen (Table I).

### Discussion
It is interesting to note that $Al_3Sc$ is the only known example of an equilibrium $L1_2$ superlattice in binary Al alloys, although a large number of transition phases possess this structure. Drits et al. [12] have reported measurements of the temperature dependence of Young's modulus in $Al_3Sc$ but reported no values of the elastic constants with which to compare the present results. However, recent first principle calculations of the elastic, electronic, and fracture properties of the $Al_3Sc$ have been reported by Fu [13] who investigated these properties using the full potential linearized augmented plane wave method (FLAPW). This author calculated the orientation dependence of the anti phase boundary (APB) and superlattice intrinsic stacking fault (SISF) energies in two $Al_3X$ structures (X = Sc, Ti). The single crystal elastic constant values calculated by Fu appear in Table II. The calculated polycrystalline values were obtained by using the Voight average of the calculated single crystal values to provide a comparison between the measured and calculated polycrystalline values. The equations used to make these calculations are given in Reference [10]. It can be seen in Table III that quite acceptable agreement has been obtained between the measured and first principles calculations of the polycrystalline elastic properties of this phase. It is difficult to assess the effect that the tungsten particles will exert on the measured values since the solubility of tungsten in this phase is not known. However, the scattering of the acoustic waves due to the presence of these particles may not present a large error since the wavelength is large in comparison to the particle size (5-10 $\mu \mathrm{m}$) in this case. From the results of the microprobe analysis it may be inferred that the tungsten level in the $Al_3Sc$ matrix is not contributing strongly to the measured values of the bulk intermetallic phase. The off stoichiometric composition of the alloy indicates a wider free energy vs composition curve than predicted by modelling the phase as a line compound, and investigation of dilute Al-Sc alloys by Sano et al. [14] using atom probe field ion microscopy provides initial experimental support for this assertion.

Anderson [15] has given a simple means of calculating the Debye temperature from the room temperature elastic constants under the assumption that the average sound velocity is not strongly temperature dependent. For several metals such as Ag, Cu, and Mg, the mean velocity changes by less than 5% on going from room temperature to 0 K, and it is also a good approximation for materials that are nearly isotropic. Following Anderson (loc cit.) and Gilvarry [14] the mean sound velocity may be computed from the measured shear and longitudinal sound velocities of an isotropic polycrystal via

$$
V_{m}=\frac{1}{3}\left[2 / V_{s}^{3}+1 / V_{p}^{3}\right]^{-1 / 3} \tag{9}
$$

where $V_s$ is the shear sound velocity and $V_p$ is the longitudinal sound velocity. The Debye temperature may then be calculated from the result quoted by Anderson,

$$
\theta=(\mathrm{h} / \mathrm{k})[(3 \mathrm{q} / 4 \pi)(\mathrm{N} \rho / \mathrm{M})]^{1 / 3} \mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{m}} \tag{10}
$$

In this expression, h is Planck's constant, k is Boltzmann's constant, N is Avogadro's number q is the number of atoms in the basis, M is the formula weight, and $\rho$ is the density. As pointed out previously by Anderson, the simple averaging of the mean velocities from a knowledge of the elastic constants obviates having to solve for the components of velocity from the stress tensor. Using $V_s = 4750$ m/s and $V_p = 7763$ m/s gives a mean sound velocity of $V_m = 5244$ m/s. Substitution into equation (14) gives a Debye temperature of $\theta = 604$ K. This value is considerably higher than either Al (428 K) or Sc (360 K) and indicates that the phase will be stiff in comparison to either Al or Sc. The calculation of the anti phase boundary energy by Fu (loc cit.) indicates that plastic deformation of the Al₃Sc phase may be difficult even though it possesses the requisite number of independent slip systems to accommodate deformation.

A strong anisotropy of Al-Sc interatomic bonding was identified as a reason for the high values of the APB and SISF energies evaluated for several orientations. By comparison of average cleavage energy with the work of formation of a super dislocation pair in Al₃Sc, Fu asserted that the cleavage stress may exceed the stress for dislocation generation which would lead to brittle failure. Indeed, brittle cleavage failure of Al₃Sc has been observed and reported by Angers [17] in studies of high temperature deformation of Al intermetallic phases.

### Conclusions
The elastic constants of polycrystalline Al₃Sc have been investigated at room temperature and have been found to be in satisfactory agreement with recent calculations. The Debye temperature was estimated to be nearly 604 K and the bulk elastic properties have been presented. It is suggested that such measurements are important in the construction of physically realistic interatomic potentials of metallic solids. Furthermore, they are of importance in consideration of the structural applications of Al based intermetallic phases where fabrication and mechanical behavior modelling are significant issues.
