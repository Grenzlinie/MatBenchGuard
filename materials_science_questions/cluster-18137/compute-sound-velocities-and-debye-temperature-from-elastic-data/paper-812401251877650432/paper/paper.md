# ELASTIC CONSTANTS OF $\mathbf{Se_{1-x}Te_x}$ SOLID AMORPHOUS ALLOYS - THE ROLE OF TELLURIUM

G. CARINI, M. CUTRONI, M. FEDERICO and G. GALLI

Istituto di Fisica and GNSM del CNR, Università di Messina, Messina, Italy

Received 9 March 1983
Revised manuscript received 26 October 1983

From the 15 MHz ultrasound velocity (shear and longitudinal) the elastic parameters ($E$, $G$, $K$) have been calculated, other than the Debye temperature $\theta_{\mathrm{m}}$ in solid $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ amorphous alloys in the temperature region containing the $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ range. Assisted by results of optical spectroscopy and by shear viscosity tests an analysis has been carried out to explain the role of tellurium in these alloys. We find a double action played through the covalent bonds in the inside of the copolymeric chains and between them by means of the Van der Waals forces. Other results, from the literature, have been taken into account to support our hypothesis.

## 1. Introduction

Due to the increasing interest in the amorphous state and particularly in the chalcogenide amorphous semiconductors, attention has been devoted to $\mathrm{Se}-\mathrm{Te}$ alloys by various authors during the past fifteen years. While partial success has been achieved in describing some of the properties in the liquid phase, where continuous miscibility may be obtained over the whole $\mathrm{Se}-\mathrm{Te}$ concentration range, the solid phase has not given the same results. The $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ alloys do not crystallize or amorphize in the same way at the two extremes of the miscibility range. Solutions having a high selenium content are mainly amorphous and the contrary is true for tellurium rich alloys. People interested in the amorphous or crystalline state limit themselves to a narrow concentration range at the extremes of the miscibility range. As a consequence, until now it has not been clearly known how the structural species changes through the miscibility range from that characteristic of Selenium in the crystalline or amorphous state, to the corresponding ones of tellurium.

An analysis [1] of previous calorimetric, dilatometric and Raman experiments seems to give reasonable information concerning the structure of a-Se and amorphous $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ alloys. The results can be summarized as follows: a-Se is formed by a mixture of ring (Se) structural units and distorted long polymeric chains $\mathrm{Se}_{n}$; the ratio ($\mathrm{Se}_{n}/\mathrm{Se}_{8}$) then increasing with temperature above $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ [2]. The alloying with tellurium seems to increase the population of Se

Recently [3] the existence of rings has been doubted and the opinion has arisen that such a structure could be simulated by chain distortion.

To improve our knowledge on this subject, the mechanical and rheological properties of a-Se have also been studied much more than the $Se_{1-x}Te_x$ amorphous alloys.

We now report an analysis of the transverse and longitudinal ultrasound velocity measurements carried out, as far as we know, for the first time on these alloys in the temperature region containing the so-called transition temperature range. From the experimental results a picture is obtained of the role played by tellurium in $Se_{1-x}Te_x$ alloys.

## 2. Experimental technique and results

All the samples were obtained from high purity selenium and tellurium components, following a procedure already described [4] together with the procedure to check the amorphization and shaping for ultrasonic measurements.

Ultrasound generation and detection were obtained using a commercial Matec apparatus integrated with a home-made devices [5] for direct velocity measurements. Both shear and longitudinal velocity data were taken at equilibrium as shown by the condition $v(t)=$ cost at each temperature. Thermal stabilization $(10^{-2}{^\circ}C)$ was guaranteed over the whole temperature range by a conventional thermostatic bath. In order to evaluate the effect on the ultrasound velocity and the elastic constant, the thermal expansion, and then the density, were measured over the same range of equilibrium. ## 3. Discussion

As is well known, many physical properties, as measured in polymers and in inorganic amorphous solids, show an abrupt change in their temperature coefficient corresponding to a characteristic value called the "glass transition temperature". Such a peculiarity has been extensively studied in the field of polymeric materials and a review can be found in a paper by Saito et al. [9] as well as in Ferry's book [10]. But not all abrupt changes in the temperature coefficient of different properties fall at the same temperature in the same material, producing as a consequence some confusion on the definition of "glass transition temperature". The point is that it is not generally possible to refer to the "glass transition temperature" of a material, but rather to it as sketched by a material with respect to a specific physical property measured in

it. As an example, as well as from thermal expansion, calorimetric, NMR, dielectric constant and other data, from the ultrasound velocity and then from the connected elastic moduli, Wada et al. [11] have isolated in the plot of the shear modulus $G$ versus temperature $T$ (fig. 1), three different points available for a "glass transition temperature" definition:

(i) $T_{\mathrm{g}}$, in the strict sense of the word (only by historical tradition) as obtained from the bending of the $G^{\prime}(T)$ curve, at low temperatures;

(ii) $T_{\mathrm{m}}$, where the ratio $G^{\prime \prime} / G^{\prime}$ between the imaginary and the real part of $G(T)$ has its maximum [and $G^{\prime}(T)$ has a flex point], due to relaxation effects: then frequency dependence occurs as a result of the probe used [12];

(iii) $T_{\mathrm{n}}$, falling at higher temperatures, where $G^{\prime}(T)$ has a minimum.

Following this scheme the bending points in our $G(T)$ curves, as sketched in fig. 2, have to be referred to as $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ and, as shown in fig. 3 (curve b), they decrease linearly as the tellurium concentration in the alloys increases.

An analogous decreasing behavior of $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ versus $x$ has been found in $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ amorphous alloys from thermal expansion measurements [8]; the data, roughly extracted from the original paper give the curve a in fig. 3 of this work. Irrespective of the absolute values and mainly referring to the decreasing behaviour, this happens as expected. In fact, as has already been proposed [13], the bending in the $G(T)$ at $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ must be attributed to the strong dependence of the shear modulus from the specific volume and it is, then, an effect of the similar bending in the volume temperature curve at $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ : with the consequence that the $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ measured by ultrasound velocity or by a thermal expansion coefficient are almost the same. In such a framework, considering small strains, no thermal hysteresis and a low attenuation of sounds, it is possible to connect the rate of change of the shear modulus with the rate of change of the ultrasound velocity, via the thermal expansion coefficient. From $G=\rho v_t^2$ in fact, in the limit $(\partial v/\partial T)_v=0$ it follows that

$$
\frac{1}{2G}\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_p=\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_p-\frac{\alpha}{2}
$$

from which one may calculate $\alpha$, the thermal expansion coefficient, from the $G(T)$ and $v_t(T)$ curves. In the case of our $Se_{1-x}Te_x$ samples $\alpha$ behaves almost linearly, versus $x$, with: $\partial \alpha_{g x}/\partial x=-2\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}\ x^{-1}$ below $T_g$ and $\partial \alpha_{L x}/\partial x=-13\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-}\ x^{-1}$ above. Unfortunately in the literature the data about the expansion coefficient values for $Se_{1-x}Te_x$ solid amorphous alloys, with which compare our results are not available. But taking [8] $\alpha_{g0}=1.640\times10^{-4}$ $\text{K}^{-1}$ and $\alpha_{L0}=5.425\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ the thermal expansion coefficient of pure amorphous selenium below and above $T_g$, $\alpha_{g x}$ and $\alpha_{L x}$ can be calculated. Then if one indicates as $V_0$ the specific volume at $T=0$ K, the specific volume at each temperature above $T_g$ (where $\alpha_{\text{L}}>\alpha_{\text{g}}$) will be:

$$
V_{\text{T}}=V_0\left[\left(1+\alpha_{\text{g}}T\right)+\left(1+\alpha_{\text{g}}T_{\text{g}}\right)\left(\alpha_{\text{L}}-\alpha_{\text{g}}\right)\left(T-T_{\text{g}}\right)\right]=V_{iT}+V_{\text{L}T}
$$

in which $V_{iT}$ is the specific volume at each temperature due to the "as crystalline" thermal expansion with coefficient $\alpha_{\text{g}}$ and $V_{\text{L}T}$ is the "overvolume" at each temperature above $T_g$, where the overall thermal expansion coefficient is $\alpha_{\text{L}}$. All of this results in a lesser "overvolume", $V_{\text{L}T}$, availability in the $Se_{1-x}Te_x$ alloys, as $x$ increases.

The decreasing slope of $G(T)$ versus $x$ above $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ brings with it the other consequence that the flex point $(T_{\mathrm{m}})$ must shift toward higher temperatures for increased $x$. That is, probably due to the lesser "overvolume" (free volume), transitions between different vibrational states other than the transition be- tween different configurations of local equilibrium will be allowed at higher temperature. This was noted in a paper by Das et al. [7] where they discussed this point as a "glass transition temperature" that they found, clearly, increas- ing with $x$. It is to be noted that, surely, the "glass transition temperature" they measured is not to be referred to as $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ as considered in this work, but probably rather, as a $T_{\mathrm{m}}$ in the sense of Wada as the thermograms they sketched clearly indicate [10]. Their results, roughly extracted from the original paper, are also plotted in fig. 3, curve c, and are used in fig. 4 (curve c). The two elements, the decreasing of $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ versus $x$ and the contemporary decrease of "overvolume" above it, seem to suggest some hypothesis about the role played by the intrachain and interchain forces, as modified by the increased tellurium percentage.

The intrachain action of tellurium, because it weakens the covalent bonds, reduces the chain stiffness [15] in correspondence with Se-Te chemical bonds. In addition it increases the probability of the chain breaking in correspondence with these points, shifting the gaussian distribution of molecular weight to the left. Both of these effects result in a shift of $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ toward lower temperatures [14,15] as it is observed in $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$.

If the stiffness and molecular weight lowering (that is the intrachain action) were the only effect of the tellurium it would result in the decrease of the shear viscosity as well, as observed in organic polymers [14]. On the contrary in bulk amorphous $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ the shear viscosity rises with $x$ as checked by us and indirectly observed by other authors [7] by microhardness measurements.

The structure of this equation, because of its strong dependence on $E_{04}$, gives $\eta(=1 / \phi)$ values strongly decreasing with $E_{04}$ and then with $x$; whatever $T_{\mathrm{g} 0}$ we use from plot $\mathrm{c}$ or plot $\mathrm{b}$ in fig. 4. That is, with increasing tellurium content, the strength of the covalent bonds decreases as shown by the lowering of $E_{04}$; and consequently the viscosity would decrease as foreseen from the modified Fulcher equation. This last circumstance does not occur. On the contrary, the viscosity values strongly increase with $x$ as we checked in $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ at various temperatures above $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ using an already described technique [18].

It has not been possible, because of the too high viscosity values, to perform a regular series of measurements for various temperatures above $T_{\mathrm{g}}$; but the indicative values extracted agree, qualitatively, with microhardness measurements [7] also indicating an increase of hardness in $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ amorphous alloys with $x$.

The disagreement between the theoretical and experimental data in the viscosity of $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ alloys versus $x$, while it inadequately defines the $T_{\mathrm{g}}-E_{04}$ relationship in such alloys it does furnish an indicative test on the role of structure softening and probably breaking, played intrachain by tellurium atoms.

The question now arises as to why all this happens in $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x} \mathrm{Te}_{x}$ alloys, or equivalently how the presence of increasing quantities of tellurium in the alloys reduces the availability of free volume in their status as supercooled liquids. It seems unlikely that the increasing tellurium may produce the formation of new structures in the range of miscibility we have explored. In such a case, in fact, tracks would be formed in the Raman spectra and this seems not to be the case [1].

In our hypothesis tellurium atoms play a double role in this field, besides determining the values of the mechanical, electrical and optical properties.

First, inside the copolymeric chains, that is like-selenium chains containing substitutional tellurium atoms, there is a local weakening of the covalent bond strength.

Such an action, by itself, may play a role in determining a better packing of structures in the material, because of the increased possibility of chain-folding;

but the other reasonable hypothesis exists that the increasing number of weak points (Se-Te bonds) in the chains also increases the probability of "chain breaking"; more effectively on the long chains whose average length may be reduced as a result, together with the average molecular weight.

Surely this is not the only effect of the presence of tellurium, because in such a case the most evident effect should be a corresponding drop in the viscosity.

The second effect, from this point of view, is the action of Van der Waals forces between chains, results enhanced with $x$, both due to the stronger Se-Te interchain Van der Waals strength and to the increased flexibility and the reduced single chain cluttering, producing a better packing and compactness and thus less free volume. Such a circumstance is particularly revealed by the large difference in $\mathrm{d}G/\mathrm{d}T$ (curve a) in the insert of fig. 2. Contemporaneously the enhanced action of the Van der Waals intermolecular forces may give an account of the experimentally observed increased viscosity and microhardness with $x$ in the $\mathrm{Se}_{1-x}\mathrm{Te}_{x}$ alloys at each temperature.
