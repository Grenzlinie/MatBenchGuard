## ABSTRACT

A systematic investigation on tuning the charge carrier concentration by substitution of the Ru position in RuIn₃ is performed. Samples with nominal composition Ru₀.₉₅T₀.₀₅In₃ (T=Re, Rh, Ir) were synthesized via liquid–solid-reaction and subsequent spark plasma sintering treatment. The chemical composition was characterized by X-ray, metallographic and microstructure analysis revealing solid solutions in the samples with Rh and Ir, whereas Re cannot be incorporated in RuIn₃. Minor oxide impurities in the commercially available starting elements, the homogeneity range of RuIn₃ and the redox potentials of the participating elements are the key for interpreting the observed lattice parameters and the corresponding composition. Both, substitutions with Rh or Ir and Re inclusions in RuIn₃ lead independently to a significant decrease of the total thermal conductivity down to approximately one half of the value observed for binary RuIn₃, prepared with commercially available starting materials. The electrical resistivity was reduced by substitution and the temperature dependence changes from semiconductor-like, for RuIn₃, to metal-like in the substitution derivatives. At the same time the sign change in the thermopower at high temperatures, characteristic for binary RuIn₃, is suppressed, attaining only electrons as majority carriers.

## 1. Introduction

The intermetallic compounds $TM_3$ with $T$ being a transition metal of the iron or cobalt group and $M$ being gallium or indium exhibit interesting electrical properties. Depending on the valence electron concentration 18-electron compounds ($T$=Co, Rh, Ir) show metallic behavior, whereas 17-electron phases ($T$=Fe, Ru, Os) are semiconductors [1]. One main characteristic of the materials is a strong $d(T)$-$p(M)$-hybridization with a steep gradient in the density of states near the Fermi level [2], which is a prerequisite for high Seebeck coefficient [3].

First, the $TM_3$ phases were reported to crystallize in the noncentrosymmetric space group $P$-$4n2$ (CoGa₃ structure type) [4,5,6]. Later, the analysis of single crystal data of RuIn₃ verified the higher symmetric space group $P4_2/mnm$ (FeGa₃ structure type) [7]. RuIn₃ was found to be a semiconductor with a band gap of 0.3 eV by density functional theory based calculations and 0.4–0.5 eV by measuring the electrical resistivity of single crystals [2,8].

Recently, we investigated the intermetallic semiconductor RuIn₃ and its substitution derivatives RuIn₃₋ₓ$A_x$ ($A$=Sn, Zn) and reported their thermoelectric (TE) properties [9,10]. Binary RuIn₃ exhibits a change from $n$-type to $p$-type behavior with high absolute Seebeck values depending on the temperature and a relatively low thermal conductivity with a shallow minimum around 4 W K⁻¹ m⁻¹ between 500 K and 600 K. However, the TE performance of the unsubstituted compound is rather low with a maximal figure of merit $ZT_{\text{max}}$=0.07(2) at 680 K.

According to band structure calculations of RuIn₃, the charge carrier concentration can be tuned with the chemical substitution of indium with tin or zinc. For this reason the type of transport can be adjusted to only $n$-type (Sn) or $p$-type (Zn) maintaining the high absolute Seebeck values. This is accompanied with a transition of semiconductor- to metal-like and a simultaneous reduction of the thermal conductivity down to one half of the unsubstituted material. The systematic investigation led to a significant increase of the figure of merit with $ZT$=0.76 for RuIn₂.₉₇₅Zn₀.₀₂₅. This experimental data are very well in agreement to the maximal calculated $ZT$ value [11].

Nevertheless, an evaluation of suitable elements to substitute ruthenium in RuIn₃ in order to further increase the TE performance is missing. A substitution with cobalt shows no improvement in $ZT$ [11], and Ru₀.₉₉Ir₀.₀₁In₃ exhibits a poor TE performance [12]. In order to ascertain the possibility of tuning the charge carrier concentration in RuIn₃ by substitution of Ru, we investigated Re, Rh and Ir as possible

Preparation was performed using a liquid–solid reaction with subsequent spark plasma sintering (SPS) treatment of $Ru_{0.95}T_{0.05}In_3$ ($T$=Re, Rh, Ir) materials for simultaneous densification and homogenization according to our previous studies [9,10]. X-ray powder diffraction and metallographic analysis were applied in order to characterize the chemical composition and microstructure of the materials.

Additionally to distinct composition of a TE material, secondary phases are important with respect to its physical properties. In this sense, small oxide impurities and their effect on the TE performance were investigated in different systems. Experimental findings in $Bi_2Te_3$ [13], $Zn_4Sb_3$ [14] and $Si_{80}Ge_{20}$ [15] exhibit no clear trend in improving or diminishing the thermoelectric properties. Combining experimentally obtained lattice parameters and chemical compositions, we report here on the effect of small oxide impurities, coming from the commercially available starting elements, on the products of chemical reaction.

## 2. Experimental section

### 2.1. Synthesis

For preparation of $Ru_{0.95}T_{0.05}In_3$ ($T$=Re, Rh, Ir) and $Ru_{0.95}Ir_{0.05}In_{2.95}Zn_{0.05}$, commercially available elemental Ru (99.9%, powder, ChemPur), Re (99.99%, powder, Alfa Aesar), Rh (99.9%, powder, ChemPur), Ir (99.999%, powder, ChemPur), In (99.9%, shots, ChemPur) and Zn (99.9999%, shots, Alfa Aesar) were used as starting materials. Prior to use, all metals were analyzed for oxygen impurity content using the carrier-gas hot-extraction technique with helium in a TCH600 N/O/H analyzer of LECO. Within the limit of detection of 0.025 mass% no oxygen was detectable in In and Zn, whereas the transition metals contain various amounts of oxygen ranging from 0.040(2) mass% for Ir until 0.84(1) mass% for Re.

Starting materials were weighed in alumina crucibles in an argon-filled glove box and kept at 973 K for 48 h in evacuated quartz ampoules. The crucibles were primarily decontaminated at 1273 K in a muffle furnace. The fine-powdered samples were densified and homogenized using the SPS treatment at 973 K for 2 h applying an uniaxial pressure in graphite dies. For chemical and physical characterizations bar-shaped specimens with more than 6 mm in length were cut perpendicular to the pressure axis from the SPS-compacted samples. Previous investigations of $RuIn_{3-x}Zn_x$ materials have revealed no dependence on the microstructure and the pressure direction during SPS experiment [10]. Furthermore, the TE properties of the isostructural compound $FeGa_3$ show nearly no anisotropy depending on the crystallographic orientation above 300 K [16].

### 2.2. Characterization

After SPS treatment, the samples were investigated via X-ray powder diffraction. Data were collected on an Image Plate Guinier Camera Huber G670 (Cu $K\alpha_1$ radiation, $\lambda$=1.54056 Å, $3^\circ\leq2\theta\leq100^\circ$, $LaB_6$ as internal standard, $a$=4.1569(1) Å). The WinCSD program package was used for lattice parameter refinement [17].

For metallographic investigations, sample pieces were embedded in conductive resin (PolyFast, Struers). After grinding and polishing with micron-sized diamond powders, the chemical composition was determined with a Cameca SX 100 wavelength dispersive X-ray spectrometer (WDXS) using the pure elements as standards.

### 2.3. Physical measurement and experimental error

The thermal diffusivity $\alpha$ of the sintered pellets was measured within the temperature range from 300 to 773 K in a LFA 427 (Netzsch) to obtain the thermal conductivity as $\kappa=\alpha C_pD$. The heat capacity $C_p$ for binary $RuIn_3$ does not change by substitution with Sn or Zn. Thus, data of binary $RuIn_3$ are taken from Ref. [9] to calculate the thermal conductivity. Additionally, the density $D$ was measured with the Archimedean method reaching 98% of the theoretical value (absolute density of $RuIn_3$: $8.33\ \text{g}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ [7]). The electrical resistivity $\rho$ and Seebeck coefficient $S$ were determined simultaneously in commercial ZEM-3 equipment (ULVAC-Riko) between 300 and 800 K.

The inaccuracy for the resistivity measurement is mainly determined by the inaccuracies in the evaluation of the size dimensions for the rectangular sample rod and especially the distance between the voltage probes. Typically, an inaccuracy of ± 0.03 mm can be assumed for width and height of the bar and ± 0.10 mm for the probe distance. This results in an uncertainty of ± 10% for the resistivity. Since the Seebeck coefficient is only determined from electrical measurements (thermopower of the sample and of the differential thermocouple for $\Delta T$) the inaccuracy for the Seebeck coefficient can be estimated to be ± 5%. Additionally, the thermal conductivity depends on three independent measurements ($\alpha$, $C_p$, $D$). Both, for specific heat and density determination the experimental errors are assumed to be 5%, while being larger for the laser flash technique with around 10%. Finally, thermal conductivity measurements have a typical accuracy of ± 20%. A total error of 25% is estimated for the calculated figure of merit $ZT=S^2T/(\rho\kappa)$. The experimental errors are calculated for all quantities and are given in the figures with error bars. Additionally, the numerical errors are given in the text with brackets influencing the last digit of the value.

### 2.4. Thermodynamic calculation

Due to the presence of oxide impurities in the commercial starting elements the relationships between the metals and the corresponding oxides are calculated using the general Eq. (1), where $R$ is the oxidizing agent and $M$ is the reducing agent. A thermodynamic description, which ignores kinetic reaction, results from the consideration of the free reaction enthalpy and the stability of the corresponding oxides. It is assumed that mainly the most stable oxides $R_xO_y$ occur in the starting elements, namely $IrO_2$, $Rh_2O_3$, $RuO_2$, $ReO_2$, $In_2O_3$ and ZnO [18]. Considering the melting points of In (430 K) and Zn (693 K), the different thermodynamic data for solid and liquid elements were used. The calculations were made using the program package *Thermodynamik* [19] using thermodynamic data from Ref. [20]. After standardizing to one mole of $R$, the results are visualized in an Ellingham diagram, where the free reaction enthalpy is plotted against the temperature [21].

## 3. Results and discussion

All X-ray diffraction patterns of $Ru_{0.95}T_{0.05}In_3$ ($T$=Re, Rh, Ir) materials were indexed with the tetragonal lattice of $FeGa_3$ structure type in space group $P4_2/mnm$ (Fig. 1). The $RuIn_3$ derivatives are identified as main phases with traces of cubic $In_2O_3$ and ZnO in sample $Ru_{0.95}Ir_{0.05}In_{2.95}Zn_{0.05}$ (here and hereafter the nominal composition is used for identification of the samples). In sample $Ru_{0.95}Re_{0.05}In_3$ remaining Re is observed. The refined lattice parameters are collected in Table 1. No significant change in the $a$ parameter is observed with substitution. In contrast, the $c$ parameters show slight deviations depending on the substitution species. These deviations can be explained in terms of the homogeneity range of binary $RuIn_3$ using the redox potentials of

the participating elements and compounds. The $RuO_2$ oxide as impurity plays an important role, while being present in the main component ruthenium. $RuO_2$ from commercially available Ru powder is primarily reduced by liquid indium during the preparation of $Ru_{0.95}T_{0.05}In_3$ ($T=$Re, Rh, Ir) samples. This is due to the facts that indium oxide is much more stable than the transition metal oxides. Additionally, the melting point of indium is considerably below the applied reaction temperature. In liquid indium, the solubility of $RuO_2$ seems to be enhanced favoring the reaction between these two components.

The calculated Ellingham diagram (Fig. 2) shows the highest negative free reaction enthalpies for the reduction of $RuO_2$ with indium and zinc. Therefore, these reactions are more favorable in comparison to the reductions with rhodium, iridium or rhenium. Thus, the consumption of indium in $Ru_{0.95}Rh_{0.05}In_3$, $Ru_{0.95}Ir_{0.05}In_3$ and $Ru_{0.95}Re_{0.05}In_3$, leading to the formation of $In_2O_3$, is predicted by the thermodynamic calculations. The same results are qualitatively derived from the calculations for $Rh_2O_3$, $IrO_2$ and $ReO_2$. The presence of weak $In_2O_3$ reflections in the X-ray powder pattern confirms the utilization of indium as reducing agent and leading to a deficit of indium in the target product. Thus the lattice parameter $c$ of $Ru_{0.95}Rh_{0.05}In_3$ and $Ru_{0.95}Ir_{0.05}In_3$ is identical with that of $Ru_{0.35}In_{0.65}$, referring to the indium-poor part of the homogeneity range of $RuIn_3$ (Table 1).

Contrariwise, in $Ru_{0.95}Re_{0.05}In_3$, where no rhenium is incorporated in $RuIn_3$, an excess of indium occurs ending up in a lattice parameter $c$, which is consistent with the indium-rich part of the homogeneity range of $RuIn_3$. There is no further change of the lattice parameters of $Ru_{0.95}Rh_{0.05}In_3$ and $Ru_{0.95}Ir_{0.05}In_3$ because of the negligible difference of the atomic radii of Ru (134.0 pm), Rh (134.5 pm) and Ir (134.0 pm) [22]. This model is confirmed with the sample $Ru_{0.95}Ir_{0.05}In_{2.95}Zn_{0.05}$. Here, instead of $In_2O_3$ only ZnO is found as a secondary phase by X-ray investigation. WDXS analysis confirms that less zinc is incorporated at the indium site in comparison to iridium at the ruthenium site, because of the utilization of zinc for the reduction of $RuO_2$ (Table 1).

For Ru₀.₉₅Ir₀.₀₅In₃ the resistivity values at room temperature are very well comparable with previously published data at low temperature [12].

The adjustment of the charge carrier concentration to only n-type behavior in Ru₀.₉₅Rh₀.₀₅In₃ and Ru₀.₉₅Ir₀.₀₅In₃ is verified with the thermopower measurements (Fig. 4b). The multiple-band behavior of binary RuIn₃ is suppressed exhibiting mainly electrons as majority charge carriers up to 700 K. Thus, chemical substitution leads to relatively high negative Seebeck values for the Rh ($S_{\text{max}}(425\ \text{K})=-166(8)\ \mu\text{V}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) and Ir ($S_{\text{max}}(400\ \text{K})=-132$ (7) $\mu\text{V}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) derivatives. In comparison, RuIn₂.₉₅Sn₀.₀₅ exhibits $S_{\text{max}}(580\ \text{K})=-156(8)\ \mu\text{V}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ [9], which is very well in agreement to Ru₀.₉₅Rh₀.₀₅In₃ and Ru₀.₉₅Ir₀.₀₅In₃, having all the same substituent concentration. Consequently, adjusting the charge carrier concentration to n-type is possible either by chemical substitution at both, the ruthenium position or the indium positions. The competing situation between iridium (n-type) and zinc (p-type) in nominal Ru₀.₉₅Ir₀.₀₅In₂.₉₅Zn₀.₀₅ leads to nearly the same behavior like in Ru₀.₉₅Ir₀.₀₅In₃. Due to the reduction of RuO₂ with Zn forming ZnO, the experimental content of Zn is smaller in comparison to Ir in Ru₀.₉₅Ir₀.₀₅In₂.₉₅Zn₀.₀₅ (Table 1). The holes introduced by zinc are compensated by the electrons introduced by iridium. Thus, the resulting thermopower values between experimental Ru₀.₉₉(4)Ir₀.₀₃(3)In₂.₉₈(1) and Ru₀.₉₆(7)Ir₀.₀₅(6)In₂.₉₇(2) Zn₀.₀₂(1) are comparable. However, the discussed material properties depend not only on the intrinsic behavior of the compound, but also on the contributions of the microstructure after SPS treatment (e.g. grain boundaries). In order to exclude such extrinsic effects a TE study on single crystals, like we have done on isostructural RuGa₃ [25] and FeGa₃ [26], is under consideration.

The thermal conductivity of the RuIn₃ substitution derivatives, depicted in Fig. 5a, shows a shallow minimum in a wide temperature range from 450 to 650 K. In general, the reduction of the thermal conductivity by approximately 50% of that of binary RuIn₃, prepared with conventional starting materials, is independent from the substitution elements. This is mainly due to the high lattice contribution of the thermal conductivity. The relative contribution of the lattice part to the total thermal conductivity is depicted in the inset of Fig. 5a, showing values always higher than 80%. Thus, chemical substitution in RuIn₃ either on the ruthenium or on the indium position effectively introduces additional distortions in the lattice, which can act as scatterers for phonons. This confirms the results of our previous report on RuIn₃₋ₓSnₓ and RuIn₃₋ₓZnₓ [9]. On the other hand, also the rhenium inclusions in Ru₀.₉₅Re₀.₀₅In₃ also decrease the thermal conductivity acting as additional scatterers for phonons. The small upturn of the thermal conductivity above 500 K probably originates from bipolar interactions between the different charge carriers [27]. This is supported by the shallow inflection points in the same temperature range in, both, the electrical resistivity above 500 K and the Seebeck coefficient above 450 K [28]. The similar behavior is observed in CoSb₂.₉₅Te₀.₀₅ [29]. Nevertheless, the behavior of the thermal conductivity of Ru₁₋ᵧIn₃ samples ($0\leq y\leq0.025$) is not yet completely understood.

## 4. Conclusions

We synthesized substitution derivatives of RuIn₃ with rhodium, iridium and zinc aiming for tuning the TE properties by electron and phonon engineering. A thermodynamic evaluation of the system was performed taking into account, that minor oxide impurities are present in the commercially available starting elements. The scenario with the formation of small amounts of indium oxide and zinc oxide was experimentally confirmed. Small deviations in lattice parameters can be explained using the redox potentials of all participating elements and compounds combined with the homogeneity range of binary Ru₁₋ᵧIn₃. Metallographic and microstructure analysis support the model showing lower experimental substitution concentrations for zinc in comparison to iridium and rhodium. This can be explained by the preferred utilization of zinc for reducing the present impurity RuO₂. Rhe- nium was not incorporated in RuIn₃. The presence of rhenium inclusions in Ru₀.₉₅Re₀.₀₅In₃ lead to the same electrical resistivity and Seebeck coefficient as in the binary RuIn₃. The reduction of the thermal conductivity leads to an enhancement in ZT to around 0.15(4). The rigid-band model of the electronic structure of binary RuIn₃ allows to explain the strong change of the TE properties of Ru₀.₉₇(3)Rh₀.₀₄(2)In₂.₉₉(1) and Ru₀.₉₉(4)Ir₀.₀₃(3)In₂.₉₈(1). The electrical resistivity switches from semiconductor- to metal-like and the Seebeck coefficient adjusts to only n-type behavior over a wide temperature range. Additionally, the thermal conductivity was reduced toward approximately one half compared to binary RuIn₃. Nevertheless, the electrical resistivity is still on a high level yielding a rather low ZT value around 0.05 for the rhodium- and iridium-substituted materials.
