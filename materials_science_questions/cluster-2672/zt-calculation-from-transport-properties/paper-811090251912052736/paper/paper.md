# Effects of high pressure sintering on the microstructure and thermoelectric properties of BiCuSeO

Hongyu Zhu$^{a,b}$, Yan Li$^{a}$, Hongtao Li$^{c}$, Taichao Su$^{b}$, Chunying Pu$^{d}$, Yongsheng Zhao$^{a}$, Yanming Ma$^{a}$, Pinwen Zhu$^{a}$ and Xin Wang$^{a}$

$^{a}$State Key Lab of Superhard Materials, Jilin University, Changchun, People's Republic of China; $^{b}$School of Physics and Electronic Information Engineering, Henan Polytechnic University, Jiaozuo, People's Republic of China; $^{c}$Technical Center for Industrial Product and Raw Material Inspection and Testing of SHCIQ, Shanghai, People's Republic of China; $^{d}$College of Physics and Electronic Engineering, Nanyang Nomal University, Nanyang, People's Republic of China

## ABSTRACT
Polycrystalline BiCuSeO oxides are prepared by solid-state reaction followed by pressureless sintering (PLS) and high pressure sintering (HPS) methods. Both the experimental results and the density functional theory calculations indicate that the crystal defect concentrations of BiCuSeO can be reduced under the effect of high pressure. By comparing with the PLS sample, a larger power factor and a lower thermal conductivity can be obtained for the HPS sample. The maximum figure of merit ZT~0.4 @ 800 K was obtained for the HPS sample, which is about 3 times higher than the PLS sample. These results indicate that the effect of high pressure is beneficial to modifying the microstructure and improving the thermoelectric performance of BiCuSeO oxyselenide.

## 1. Introduction
Thermoelectric materials and devices can realize direct heat electricity conversion and have attracted extensive interest owing to their widespread applications in the areas of solid-state cooling [1], power generation [2,3], and waste heat recovery [4]. A good ther- moelectric material should possess high electrical conductivity, $\sigma$, large Seebeck coeffi cient, $S$, and low thermal conductivity, $\kappa$, to retain the heat at the junction and to reduce the heat transfer losses, giving a large dimensionless figure of merit ZT (ZT = $S^{2}\sigma/\kappa$, where $T$ is absolute temperature).

The extensive work on thermoelectric materials until now has mainly focused on alloy compounds such as $Bi_2Te_3$ [5], PbTe [6], and Skutterudites [7]. Although they have high ZT value, most of them contain toxic elements and they would be easily oxidized at higher temperature when exposed to air. Compared with alloy compounds, the oxide thermo- electric materials are regarded as potential competitor for high-temperature thermoelec- tric application due to their low cost and high thermal stability [8].

Recently, BiCuSeO-based oxychalcogenides have attracted considerable attentions due to its low phonon thermal conductivity (<1 Wm⁻¹ K⁻¹ at room temperature) [9–13]. Many methods including solid-state reaction (SSR) [9], mechanical alloying [10], and hydrothermal synthesis [11] have been used to prepare BiCuSeO. An extraordinary high ZT~1.4 for BiCuSeO has been obtained by combining with doping and hot-forging process [12]. The high ZT value can even be comparable to those of the best TE alloys [5–7]. Besides, distinct electrical behaviors were also reported by different groups. The synthesis technique (process techniques) is the key to the electrical transport properties of BiCuSeO. Li has found that the simple milling can effectively modify the electrical transport properties and then improve the thermoelectric performance of pristine BiCuSeO [13].

Except of milling, the high pressure can also have significantly influence on the microstructure (such as density, grain size, defect concentrations) and then change the electrical transport and thermoelectric performance [14,15]. As far as we know, there are few reports about the effect of high pressure on BiCuSeO, and the thermoelectric performance of BiCuSeO under high pressure was predicted only in theory [16].

In this paper, we focused on the effects of high pressure sintering (HPS) on the microstructure and the thermoelectric properties of BiCuSeO. It was found that the high pressure can significantly change the microstructure and enhance thermoelectric properties of BiCuSeO. The possible underlying mechanism for the improved thermoelectric properties was discussed.

## 2. Experimental

### 2.1. Sample synthesis and characterization

BiCuSeO samples were first synthesized by an SSR route. A stoichiometric mixture of Bi (99.999%), Cu (99.99%), Se (99.95%), and $Bi_2O_3$ (99.99%) powders were mixed and pressed into bars and then sealed in silica tube under vacuum, which was heated at 573 K for 6 h and then 973 K for 10 h. The as-prepared sample was crushed into powders, and then cold pressed into two bars with dimensions of 10.5mm × 4 mm. One of the bulk samples was sintered under a pressure of 3 GPa and 973 K for 30 min by using a cubic anvil high pressure apparatus. The detailed HPS process is similar to our previous reports [15]. For comparison, the other bulk BiCuSeO sample was sintered by pressureless method (PLS) under vacuum at 973 K for 10 h.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements with Cu–Kα radiation were measured on an X-ray diffractometer (D/MAX-RA). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (JEOL JSM-6700F) was used to probe morphology. The Seebeck coefficient and electrical resistivity at different temperatures from room temperature to 800 K were measured simultaneously by a LSR-3 apparatus (Linseis). The thermal conductivity $\kappa$ was calculated by using $\kappa = D\rho C_p$, where $\rho$ is the sample density measured by the Archimedes method, and $C_p$ is specific heat capacity estimated by the Dulong–Petit law. The thermal diffusivity $D$ was measured on a Netzsch LFA457 laser flash apparatus.

### 2.2. Computational details

The density functional theory (DFT) calculations employed the frozen-core projector augmented-wave method [17,18] encoded in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package [19]. The

generalized-gradient approximation [20] of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof was used for the exchange-correlation functional with the plane wave-cut-off energy of 550 eV. The crystal structure optimization for the BiCuSeO compound was carried out using 4×4×4 Mon- khorst $k$-points for the irreducible Brillouin zone. The convergence thresholds for energy were chosen as $10^{-5}$ eV and $10^{-4}$ eV/atom, respectively. The calculations of Bi and Cu vacancy formation energy for BiCuSeO as a function of pressure were performed in a supercell with 32 atoms according to the formation energy equation.

$$
E_{f}=E_{i}-E_{0}+\sum n_{i} \mu_{i}, \tag{1}
$$

where $E_{i}$ and $E_{0}$ are the DFT total energies of a supercell with and without the defect, respectively. The $n_{i}$ is the number of type $i$ atoms removed to the host and $\mu_{i}$ is the atomic chemical potential. The elemental chemical potentials of metallic Bi and Cu ($\mu_{Bi}$ and $\mu_{Cu}$) can be obtained by calculating the energy of pure bulk Bi and Cu. All the calculated values including $E_{i}, E_{0}, \mu_{Bi}$, and $\mu_{Cu}$ are listed in Table S1.

### 3. Results and discussion

The results indicated that both samples were single tetragonal phase (space group: P4/nmm, No. 129). The refined lattice constants, atomic parameters, and fitting statistics are given in Table 1. All the crystal structure par- ameters are similar to that of BiCuSeO obtained by the classical route in [11]. However, it was found that the cell volume for the PLS is slightly smaller than that of the HPS. As well known, the cell volume usually decreases with an increase of the pressure. The abnormal change of the cell volume for the PLS and the HPS samples can be attributed to the differ- ent concentrations of crystal defect, which will be discussed in the following parts.

The origin of this microstructure evolution into fine grains structure may be attributed to the layered structure of BiCuSeO. The crystal structure of BiCuSeO is comprised of $Cu_2Se_2$ layers alternately stacked with $Bi_2O_2$ layers along the c-axis of the tetragonal cell [9]. During compression under the high pressure, BiCuSeO has experienced severe deformation and would fracture into very fine pieces through shearing cracking along the weak bond in-between $Cu_2Se_2$–$Bi_2O_2$ slabs and bonding failure across these slabs. The fine grain size induced by high pressure was also found in other TE materials with layer structure such as $TiS_2$ [22] and $Bi_2Te_3$ [23].

Figure 3 shows the temperature dependence of resistivity for BiCuSeO prepared with different methods, the PLS and the HPS. It can be seen that the electrical resistivity of the HPS is higher than that of the PLS. The temperature dependence of resistivity for the PLS shows a metal behavior before 500 K and then changes to a semiconductor behavior. The transition temperature of metal to semiconductor (M–S) shifts to 400 K for the HPS sample. According to the optical band gap measurements [24], BiCuSeO is a typical semiconductor with $E_g$~0.8 eV. The metallic conduction behavior of BiCuSeO obtained in this study at low temperature may be attributed to the impurity phases or the crystal defects.

Figure 4 presents the microstructure of polished cross-sections for both samples. It clearly shows that the PLS sample (Figure 4(a)) consists of more impurity phases than that of the HPS (Figure 4(b)) sample. Further EDS analysis shows that the black areas are Cu-rich phases (Figure 4(c)) and the white areas are Bi-rich phases (Figure 4(d)).

Figure 5, the formation energies of Cu and Bi vacancy were calculated to be 1.08 and 0.14 eV per formula unit under ambient pressure, respectively. Both types of vacancies could contribute to the hole generation. As the pressure increases in BiCuSeO, both the vacancy formation energies in the Bi and Cu sites increase. These calculated results indicate that the high pressure can restrain the formation of crystal defects. From the DFT calculation and the BES-EDS results, it shows that the PLS sample hosts larger numbers of defects, including $V_{Bi}$ and $V_{Cu}$ type point defect and segregated Bi and Cu in BiCuSeO matrix, which decreases the electrical resistivity and Seebeck coefficient.

Table 2 is the electrical transport properties of BiCuSeO measured at room temperature by the Van der Pauw method. It can be seen that the carrier concentration of HPS is much lower than that of PLS. The Hall mobility is about 0.576 and $2.563\ \mathrm{cm^2 V^{-1} S^{-1}}$ for PLS and HPS, respectively. The low carrier concentration and the high Hall mobility for the HPS sample also indicate that the defect concentrations were depressed by the effect of HPS.

Figure 6 shows the temperature dependence of Seebeck coefficient for BiCuSeO prepared with different methods, PLS and HPS, respectively. Similar to the temperature-dependent resistivity, the Seebeck coefficient of HPS is much higher than that of PLS in the measured temperature range. The lower Seebeck coefficient of PLS can be mainly attributed to the high carrier concentration, resulting from the high crystal defect density. In addition, the large number of Bi and Cu rich impurities included in PLS can also decrease the Seebeck coefficient.

The power factor values of both samples increase monotonically with increasing temperature. Moreover, the value of power factor obtained here for the HPS is higher than that of the PLS in the whole investigated temperature range. A high figure of merit for the HPS would be expected if a low thermal conductivity was obtained expected for the large power factor.

## 4. Conclusion
The effects of HPS on the microstructure and thermoelectric properties of BiCuSeO were investigated. The results indicated that high pressure can effectively restrain the formation of crystal defects and decrease the grain sizes. Compared with PLS, HPS sample has larger Seebeck coefficient, higher resistivity, and lower thermal conductivity. The maximum figure of merit ZT~0.4 @ 800 K was obtained for HPS, which is about three times higher than PLS. These results indicate that HPS is beneficial to modifying the microstructure and improving the thermoelectric performance of BiCuSeO.
