## ABSTRACT
Zinc phosphate glasses (ZPG) have emerged as new energy materials due to their femtosecond response and chemical durability. The present investigation renders the methodology to enhance the thermoelectric properties of ZPG by adding external guest graphitic particles. Herein, transport parameters, Seebeck coefficient, and thermal and electrical conductivities of zinc phosphate glass composites were mea- sured as a function of graphite concentration, followed by the calculation of the thermoelectric figure of merit $(ZT)$ . It is observed that the thermoelectric properties of the ZPG/graphite composites are superior to that of the pristine ZPG matrix and significantly depend on the volume concentration of graphite filler. ZPG/graphite composite exhibiting with 5 vol. % graphite content imparts the best thermoelectric performance with a power factor of $PF \approx 0.98 \mu W m^{-1} ~K^{-2}$ and $ZT \approx 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$ .

## I. INTRODUCTION
In the current context of environmental concerns, green energy is one of the greatest challenges of the 21st century. $^{1}$ Continuous efforts are being made to find out the solutions for sustainable energy production through various emerging technologies; among them, thermoelectricity could be one of the possible solutions which offer a direct conversion of heat energy into electricity. $^{2-4}$ In this technique, the conversion efficiency is linked to a dimensionless parameter called figure of merit via the following relation:

$$
\eta_{\max }=\left(\frac{T_{H}-T_{C}}{T_{H}}\right) \cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+Z T}-1}{\sqrt{1+Z T}+\frac{T_{C}}{T_{H}}}\right), \quad(1)
$$

where $T_{H}$ is the hot side temperature, $T_{C}$ is the cold side tempera ture, and $ZT$ is the figure of merit that can be represented by the following equation:

$$
Z T=\frac{\sigma \mathrm{S}^{2} \mathrm{~T}}{\kappa}, \quad(2)
$$

where $\sigma, \kappa, S$, and $T$ are electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, Seebeck coefficient, and absolute temperature, respectively. Theoretically, to get a high $ZT$ value, thermoelectric (TE) materials must have a high power factor $(PF=\sigma S^{2})$ and low thermal conductivity.

Phosphate glasses have been found to be technically important due to their greater thermal expansions and lower transition

temperatures as compared to that of the silicate or borate glasses. Due to these technical merits, phosphate glasses have become a subject of extensive research. Thanks to the physicochemical properties of the ZPGs, which are used in various fields of applications such as sealing glasses, $^{5}$ bioactive applications, $^{6,7}$ laser or optical systems, $^{8-10}$ and biomedical applications. $^{11}$ Besides, zinc phosphate glass-based (45 mol. %ZnO, 55 mol. % $P_{2}O_{5}$) systems are characterized by higher Seebeck coefficient $^{12}$ that allows us to study a pristine glass matrix containing conductive fillers to enhance the electrical conductivity followed by the optimization of the figure of merit. Among conductive inclusions, carbon allotrope such as graphite (Gr), which is characterized by the high electrical conductivity of the order of $3 \times 10^{2}-10^{5}\ \text{S/m}$ at $20^{\circ}\text{C}$, has been chosen as filling element $^{13}$ for the pristine ZPG matrix.

In the present study, ZPG/Gr composites were prepared into nine different volume ratios to achieve the enhanced thermoelectric properties. The thermoelectric properties such as $\sigma$, $S, \kappa$, power factor (PF), and $ZT$ of the composites have been characterized as functions of the graphite content. The determination of thermal and electrical conductivities and Seebeck coefficient has been carried out as a function of graphite loading fraction and analyzed in the light of the Effective Medium Theory (EMT) and generalized effective medium theory (GEMT). The physical parameters in each phase of the composite have been extracted using the aforementioned theories. A remarkable increase in the figure of merit has been observed at a particular graphite concentration, i.e., 5 vol. % and explained using the percolation mechanism.

## II. EXPERIMENTAL

### A. Preparation of zinc phosphate glass composite

#### 1. Preparation of zinc phosphate glass

The zinc phosphate glass was prepared using the quenching method as discussed in the previous works. $^{12,14}$ Powders of ammonium dihydrogen phosphate $(\text{NH}_{4}) \text{H}_{2}\text{PO}_{4}$ (Panreac form, purity $>98\%$) and zinc oxide (Panreac form ZnO, purity $>99\%$) were mixed in adequate molar proportion to produce the binary system $(45\% \text{ZnO} - 55\% \text{P}_{2}\text{O}_{5})$. Furthermore, the obtained mixture was placed in the alumina crucible and heated in a furnace. The heating was realized in three steps: (1) Maintaining the temperature at $170^{\circ}\text{C}$ during 1h, (2) establishing the temperature during 12h at $300^{\circ}\text{C}$ with the purpose to take out the traces of $\text{NH}_{3}$ and $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$, and (3) increasing the temperature until $950^{\circ}\text{C}$ during 1h to avoid the volatilization of $\text{P}_{2}\text{O}_{5}$. The heating rate in the furnace was around $5^{\circ}\text{C}/\text{min}$. The formation of the zinc phosphate glass was obtained according to the following equation (3):

$$\text{ZnO} + 2[(\text{NH}_{4}) \text{H}_{2}\text{PO}_{4}] \rightarrow \text{ZnO}(\text{P}_{2}\text{O}_{5}) + 3\text{H}_{2}\text{O} + 2\text{NH}_{3}. \quad (3)$$

The binary system exhibiting molar percentages of 45% ZnO and 55% $\text{P}_{2}\text{O}_{5}$ was chosen due to its most stable glass chemical composition as stated by Maaroufi *et al.*$^{15}$

### 2. Preparation of composites

ZPG/graphite composites were prepared by mixing zinc phosphate glass powder and graphite into the adequate filler volume fractions. The graphite was purchased from Quimipur (Arganda del Rey, Spain; density $=2.25\ \text{g/cm}^{3}$). The size of the graphite flakes was less than $40\mu\text{m}$. Pellets were prepared from the obtained homogeneous mixture using a manual hydraulic press by applying the pressure of $10\ \text{T/cm}^{2}$ for 1 min. The diameter of circular pellets was maintained at 13 mm, whereas, 4-5 mm and $100-1000\mu\text{m}$ thickness was used for the Seebeck coefficient and thermal conductivity measurements, respectively. ZPG/Gr composites exhibiting various concentrations (i.e., 0.5, 1, 5, 3, 8, 10, and 12 vol. %) of graphite were prepared. One should note that the filler concentration greater than 12 vol. % leads to the formation of unconsolidated composite. All pellets were placed in a desiccator to avoid any water absorption by the samples. **Figure 1** renders the different processes involved in the preparation of the ZPG/Gr composites.

### B. Morphological analysis

The morphological characteristics of the ZPG/Gr composites were examined using a scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JOEL JSM-7100F) by placing the pellets on SEM specimen holder with the help of carbon tape. The energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was additionally performed to analyze the elemental mapping.

## E. Modeling of the thermoelectric transport parameters of composites

Effective Medium Theory (EMT) is analogous to the theoretical model that describes the macroscopic properties of a composite exhibiting different loading fractions. Generalized effective medium theory (GEMT) is a more extensive form of EMT that is used to calculate the effective properties of individual components and their fractions present in a composite. This theory includes the effect of a percolating network on the effective parameters. $^{19}$ Using this theory, an effective medium formula can be derived for every thermoelectric transport properties.

In this section, we will present EMT and GEMT theory for the Seebeck coefficient and thermal and electrical conductivities. These theories are used later to extract the local transport parameters such as Seebeck coefficient and electrical and thermal conductivities for both phases (matrix + inclusion) as a function of the graphite loading fraction. This analysis allows us to highlight the presence of a conducting percolating network and its dimensionality (2D or 3D).

### 1. Electrical conductivity

The electrical conductivity was measured using impedance spectroscopy in the frequency range of 20 Hz–1 MHz. The obtained experimental results are fitted using the GEMT (generalized effective medium theory) which is a continuum medium approach developed by McLachlan and co-workers.^{20,21} The effective electrical conductivity of a composite can be represented as

$$
\varphi_{f} \frac{\sigma_{f}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}}{\sigma_{f}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}+A_{\sigma} \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}}+\left(1-\varphi_{f}\right) \frac{\sigma_{m}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}}{\sigma_{m}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}+A_{\sigma} \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\sigma}}}=0, \quad (5)
$$

where $\sigma_{f}$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{e}}$, $\sigma_{m}$, $\varphi_{f}$, $t_{\sigma}$, and $A_{\sigma}$ represent, respectively, the filler electrical conductivity, the composite effective electrical conductivity, the matrix electrical conductivity, percentage of the filler, the critical exponent, and a coefficient depending on the electrical percolation threshold $\varphi_{\sigma}$ ($A_{\sigma} = \frac{1-\varphi_{\sigma}}{\varphi_{\sigma}}$). The coefficient $t_{\sigma}$ describes the fractal properties of a percolating conductive network. Its value depends on the dimentionality of the conductive network. If, its value is around 1, the conductive networks represents to be two-dimensional and the conductive network exhibiting the $t_{\sigma}$ value around 2 is considered to be three-dimensional.

### 2. Thermal conductivity

The EMT was also used to describe the evolution of effective thermal conductivity as a function of filler loading fraction using a generalized Landauer formula,^{22} which is as follows:

$$
\varphi_{f} \frac{\kappa_{f}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}}{\kappa_{f}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}+A_{\kappa} \cdot \kappa_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}}+\left(1-\varphi_{f}\right) \frac{\kappa_{m}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}}{\kappa_{m}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}+A_{\kappa} \cdot \kappa_{\mathrm{e}}^{1 / t_{\kappa}}}=0, \quad (6)
$$

where $\kappa_{f}$, $\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}$, $\kappa_{m}$, $t_{\kappa}$, and $A_{\kappa}$ represent, respectively, the filler thermal conductivity, the matrix thermal conductivity, the composite effective thermal conductivity, the critical exponent, and a coefficient that depends on the the percolation threshold.

### 3. Seebeck coefficient

Based on the EMT and the Boltzmann transport relation, Sonntag has derived an equation for calculating the Seebeck coefficient for metals or degenerate semiconductors.^{23,24} In 2009, Sonntag has extended his previous model for the non-degenerate semiconductors by replacing the heat flux by the entropy flux.^{25} In this model, the ratio of thermal conductivity and the Seebeck coefficient is represented by the following equation:

$$
\left(1-\varphi_{f}\right) \frac{\left(\frac{\kappa_{m}}{S_{m}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}-\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}}{S_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}}{\left(\frac{\kappa_{m}}{S_{m}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}+A_{\kappa / S} \cdot\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}}{S_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}}+\varphi_{f} \frac{\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{S_{f}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}-\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}}{S_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}}{\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{S_{f}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}+A_{\kappa / S} \cdot\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{e}}}{S_{\mathrm{e}}}\right)^{1 / t_{S}}}=0,
\tag{7}
$$

where $\kappa_{m}$, $\kappa_{f}$, and $\kappa_{e}$ are the specific thermal conductivities, $S_{m}$, $S_{f}$, and $S_{e}$ are the Seebeck coefficients of the matrix phase, the filler, and the composite, respectively, and $\varphi_{f}$ is the volume fraction of the incorporated phase. $A_{S}=(1-\varphi_{\kappa / S}) / \varphi_{\kappa / S}$ is a constant that depends on the percolation threshold $\varphi_{\kappa / S}$ and $t_{\kappa / S}$ is a critical exponent.

Equation (7) states that the global entropy current density, $J_{S}$, is equal to the average local entropy current density $\langle J_{Si}\rangle$ and the global electrical potential gradient $\phi$ is equal to an average local electrical potential $\langle\phi_{i}\rangle$. From the Onsager relation, we can write $^{24,25}$

$$
J_{S}=\frac{\kappa}{S} grad(\phi), \quad (8)
$$

$$
J_{S i}=\frac{\kappa_{i}}{S_{i}} grad(\phi_{i}). \quad (9)
$$

The coefficient, $\kappa/S$, is the amount of entropy current density for a given electrical potential gradient and can be treated as a transport parameter.

## D. Calculation of the thermoelectric figure of merit of ZPG/Gr composites
Thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT) depends on three parameters: the electrical conductivity $\sigma$, the thermal conductivity $\kappa$, and the Seebeck coefficient $S$ according to Eq. (2). The measurement of Seebeck coefficient and thermal and electrical conductivities allows the calculation of the experimental thermoelectric figure of merit. The evolution of the figure of merit $ZT$ as a function of filler content is illustrated in Fig. 7. The blue circles show the experimental data, and the solid line represents the simulated curve using the GEMT models of the Seebeck coefficient and electrical and thermal conductivities calculated via fitting parameters obtained in Sec. III.

It is observed that the $ZT$ varies non-monotonically with increasing filler volume fraction. The values of $ZT$ for the neat matrix and the composite with 5 vol. % filled graphite are $1.83\times10^{-7}$ and $2.64\times10^{-4}$, respectively, showing an increase in $ZT$ by three orders of magnitude. However, after the 5 vol. % of Gr filler, the $ZT$ follows a decreasing trend up to a value of $1.84\times10^{-7}$ for the 12 vol. %. Using the different GEMT models and the fitting transport parameters, a simulated $ZT$ could be estimated. The simulated $ZT$ behavior is in good agreement with the obtained experimental values.

The bottom insets show the formation of a graphitic network. At 3.5 vol. %, the sudden increase in electrical conductivity can be interpreted as charge carriers jumping from cluster to cluster by a tunneling effect. At 6 vol. % a geometrically percolating network is formed which leads to the drop of the Seebeck coefficient.

## E. Discussion
By assuming two different percolation thresholds for the electrical conductivity and the ratio $\kappa/S$ at 3.5 and 6 vol. %, respectively, the highest value of $ZT$ is obtained at 5 vol. % graphite contents.

A maxima in the evolution of $ZT$ could be explained by the shift in the percolation threshold concentration in the electrical conductivity and $\kappa/S$ ratio. The simulation results obtained with the GEMT models demonstrate that a similar percolation threshold for electrical conductivity and $\kappa/S$ ratio that will not render an optimal concentration of filler in the $ZT$ values. However, the simulation results provide a good approximation of it. In this study, we have reported an unusual behavior of the $ZT$ with increasing Gr loading fraction in the composites. The highest $ZT$ around $2.64 \times 10^{-4}$ is obtained for the 5 vol. % Gr composite. We found that the overall performance still needs to be improved; however, three order of magnitudes enhancement of the $ZT$ as compared to that of the neat glass matrix is an encouraging result. This observation shows a disagreement with the EMT as it states that the effective figure-of-merit, $ZT_{effective}$, of a two-component composite can

never be greater than its largest value among its constituting components. $^{32}$ However, the estimated effective transport parameters using the GEMT has shown a good agreement with the experimental behaviors. In addition to this, the fitting analysis has allowed to highlight two different values of percolation threshold for the effective electrical conductivity and the effective $\kappa/S$ ratio. Figure 8 shows a simulation of $ZT$ as a function of graphite content with fitted transport parameters values that are taken as input parameters with and without a shift in percolation threshold values. It is clear that the value of $ZT$ increases with an increase in the difference between the two percolation thresholds. Similar behavior has also been observed by other authors for the $Bi_2S_3/Bi_{2+x}S_3$ composites, where the maximum value of $ZT$ has been found to be 0.26 at 40 vol. % which is greater than that of the two phases of the composite. $^{33}$ This evolution is attributed to the percolation behavior of the electrical conductivity. It is noticed that the Seebeck coefficient has not decreased significantly up to 5 vol. % in comparison to the electrical conductivity and the thermal conductivity; therefore, it is considered to be almost same as a function of filler content and the percolation shift between the electrical conductivity and $\kappa/S$ ratio can be improved using the GEMT models. In a previous report, MWCNT/Te nanorod composite has shown a maximum value of $ZT=4.5\times10^{-3}$ at 2 vol. % of MWCNTs because of the low thermal conductivity and strong phonons scattering for that filler concentration. $^{34}$

Thus, the analysis of this Fig. 7 demonstrates that a peak in the evolution of $ZT$ is only allowed by the shift in the percolation threshold between the effective electrical conductivity and $\kappa/S$ ratio. To illustrate this finding, Fig. 9 presents the evolution of thermoelectric figure of merit of composites as a function of $\kappa_m/S_m$ and $\kappa_f/S_f$ ratios. It is observed that the $ZT$ evolution shows a peak with the variation of ratio parameters. It is worth to notice here that the peak maxima increases with the decreasing ratios of the $\kappa_m/S_m$ and $\kappa_f/S_f$; nevertheless, the shifting percolation has shown a remarkable influence on the $ZT$ values.

Classically, the percolation threshold could be considered as a purely geometric phenomena, $^{35-37}$ where $\varphi_c$ is the concentration needed to form a continuous pathway within a material. According to the percolation theory, both percolation thresholds should take place at the same concentration. To explain this paradox, we have evinced a possibility that the electronic conduction via tunneling effect could took place for the concentrations that are lower than that of the geometrical percolation threshold at 6 vol. % graphite content. Besides, this tunneling-percolation based electrical conduction takes place around 3.5 vol. % of the filler. In the ZPG composites, the conductive graphite particles might be electrically connecting prior to the geometrical connection via tunneling mechanism. Therefore, to improve the effective $ZT$, it is essential to establish an electrically connected network by tunneling percolation at concentrations lower than that of the geometric percolation threshold. At this point, the effective Seebeck coefficient drops due to the creation of a graphitic percolation network. Moreover, the phenomena of tunneling-percolation has already been used in different applications, such as pressure sensor, $^{38}$ piezoresistive tensile sensor, $^{39-41}$ and humidity/pressure sensing applications. $^{42}$ The phenomena of shifting threshold between the percolation-like tunneling and the geometric network percolation can be used as a tool to improve the thermoelectric figure of merit $ZT$ and also to overcome the limitations that are imposed due to the general effective medium theory. This study can be further improved for the composite matrices that are characterized by a high Seebeck coefficient and high electrical conductivity with an aim to create a large offset in the percolation threshold in terms of tunnel energy barrier between electrically conducting clusters, their interdistance distribution and their geometric shape. In numerous polymer matrices, the tunneling effect $^{36,40,42-45}$ has already been reported which is remarkably depends on the shape, orientation, and size of the filler particles. $^{45,46}$

## IV. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, ZPG reinforced graphite (Gr) composites have been elaborated with different filler volume fractions ranging from 0.5 to 12 vol. %. The thermoelectric properties of the ZPG/Gr composites were measured using different techniques. The thermal conductivity follows the Landauer model and has been found to increase with increasing volume fraction of graphite. The electrical conductivity also increases with the increase in filler content which is attributed to the percolation behavior. A similar behavior has also been recorded for the $\kappa/S$ ratio which is in good agreement with the Sonntag model. The percolation thresholds have also been examined and found to be different for the electrical conductivity and $\kappa/S$ ratio. We have also estimated the thermoelectric figure of merit ($ZT$) by determining the values of the Seebeck coefficient and thermal and electrical conductivities. The $ZT$ has rendered a peak maxima at 5 vol. % with a value of $2.64\times10^{-4}$ which is tremendously higher (i.e., three order of magnitudes), as compared to the neat matrix. This behavior has been explained via tunneling-percolation. The shift between tunneling and geometric percolation thresholds can be utilized to improve the thermoelectric $ZT$ and also to overcome the limits that are imposed by the general effective medium theory (GEMT). The essential condition to enlarge the shift between the aforementioned percolation thresholds is to use a matrix having large Seebeck coefficient and a filler exhibiting high electrical conductivity.
