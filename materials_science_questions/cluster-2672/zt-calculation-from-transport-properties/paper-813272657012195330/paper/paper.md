# Improving the Power Factor and the Role of Impurity Bands

H.J. GOLDSMID $^{1,2}$

1.—University of New South Wales, Sydney, NSW, Australia. 2.—e-mail: hjgoldsmid@phys.unsw.edu.au

The thermoelectric figure of merit, $Z$, is proportional to the ratio of the power factor to the thermal conductivity. In the past, efforts to improve $Z$ have largely been directed towards reduction of the thermal conductivity by lowering the lattice component, $\lambda_{\rm L}$. This approach has been so successful that $\lambda_{\rm L}$ is now sometimes no larger than it is in a typical amorphous material. Any further improvement would require the development of thermoelectric materials with larger power factors. Here, we consider some of the ways in which the power factor might be enlarged. The carrier mobilities and density-of-states effective masses for different semiconductors are reviewed briefly, and the relevance of these properties to the power factor is discussed. It is shown that a semiconductor with the mobility and effective mass of electrons in silicon and with the minimum lattice conductivity might have a $ZT$ value of about 6. Preferential scattering to improve the Seebeck coefficient is then considered. Finally, the effect on the power factor of a modification of the density of states through the introduction of impurity bands is calculated. It is found that such bands are not beneficial when they lie close to the edge of a main band. However, they significantly improve the power factor when they lie several $kT$ from the band edge, and their effect can be enhanced by counterdoping.

Key words: Thermoelectric, figure of merit, power factor, impurity bands

## INTRODUCTION

During the decade following the introduction of bismuth telluride $^{1}$ as a thermoelectric material in 1954, the dimensionless figure of merit, $ZT$, reached a value close to unity at room temperature. Then, using materials based on other compounds, comparable values of $ZT$ were obtained over a wide range of temperature. Gradually, $ZT$ values substantially greater than unity were reached. Among the highest reported values of $ZT$ is 2.4 for a superlattice based on bismuth telluride and antimony telluride, $^{2}$ though it is disappointing that this result has been difficult to reproduce.

Many of the advances that were made in the 1950s can be linked to the work of Ioffe and colleagues. $^{3}$ The compounds PbTe and ${\rm Bi_2Te_3}$ were selected because of their high mean atomic weights, since it was realized $^{4}$ that this would yield a low value for the lattice conductivity, $\lambda_{\rm L}$. After this came the realization that $\lambda_{\rm L}$ would be even smaller in solid solutions between isomorphous elements and compounds. $^{5}$ This heralded the appearance of lead-tin telluride, $^{6}$ bismuth-antimony selenotelluride, $^{7,8}$ and silicon-germanium alloys. $^{9}$

It was then suggested that further reduction of the lattice conductivity could be achieved through scattering of phonons at the interfaces in polycrystals of small grain size. $^{10}$ A completely different but equally fruitful approach was the introduction of cage-like structures in which loosely bound atoms or "rattlers" would be effective scattering centers. $^{11}$

All these improvements, as well as most of the more recent ones that have stemmed from the use of nanostructures, can be attributed to a reduction of the thermal conductivity. In fact, we have now reached the stage at which the phonon conductivity is often little different from the value that is characteristic of amorphous materials or glasses.

Suppose that a certain semiconductor were to have a phonon free path length equal to the lattice constant, a power factor that is as high as has ever been observed in any material, and an energy gap that is large enough to make the contribution of the minority carriers negligible. Then the value of $ZT$ would be about 4. This would undoubtedly be a great improvement on what is currently available, but it would not be enough to allow thermoelectric generators to compete in efficiency with conventional machines. To achieve this end it will be necessary to have still larger values of $ZT$, and this can only come about through a higher power factor.¹²
The power factor, $P$, is defined as $\alpha^{2}\sigma$, where $\alpha$ is the Seebeck coefficient and $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity.

## EFFECTIVE MASS AND CARRIER MOBILITY
According to simple semiconductor theory, the power factor is dependent on the Fermi energy and on $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$, where $\mu$ is the carrier mobility and $m^{*}$ is the density-of-states effective mass. Thus, it would seem that, to obtain the highest power factor, one should merely select the semiconductor with the largest $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$ and then adjust the carrier concentration to optimize the Fermi energy.

In practice, it is not quite so simple. Although the mobility in a nondegenerate semiconductor should be independent of the carrier concentration when lattice scattering predominates, it may be reduced by scattering on the ionized impurities that produce the charge carriers. Furthermore, the effective mass may not be independent of the Fermi energy. Nevertheless, the selection of a semiconductor with a high value of $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$ is a good first step.

In Table I we show values for the mobility and density-of-states effective mass, together with $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$, for a number of semiconductors. We note that $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$ has the values $530\ \mathrm{cm}^{2}/\mathrm{V\ s}$ and $564\ \mathrm{cm}^{2}/\mathrm{V\ s}$ for $n$-type and $p$-type $\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Te}_{3}$ at room temperature. Also, it will be seen that $n$-type Si and $n$-type Ge both have significantly larger values for this quantity. Thus, the value of $2192\ \mathrm{cm}^{2}/\mathrm{V\ s}$ for $n$-type Si is some 4 times greater. However, this does not necessarily mean that much larger power factors will be found for Si than for $\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Te}_{3}$. The high mobility for Si in the table is applicable only for lightly doped single crystals. The optimum Fermi energy for thermoelectric applications corresponds to a high concentration of ionized impurities. At the doping level that yields the optimum Seebeck coefficient, the mobility is much reduced by ionized-impurity scattering.¹³ Consequently, the power factor is then less than for bismuth telluride.

The effective mass and the mobility are not, of course, independent of one another. If acoustic-mode lattice scattering predominates, the mobility is proportional to $m_{\mathrm{i}}^{-5/2}$, where $m_{\mathrm{i}}$ is the inertial mass, which normally differs from the density-of-states mass, $m^{*}$. In noncubic multivalley semiconductors the inertial mass varies with orientation. The density-of-states mass is equal to $N_{\mathrm{V}}^{2/3}(m_{1}m_{2}m_{3})^{1/3}$, where $N_{\mathrm{V}}$ is the number of valleys and $m_{1,2,3}$ are the inertial masses in each direction. Provided that intervalley scattering is absent, it seems that a multivalley semiconductor with small inertial masses would have the largest value of $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$. This seems to be borne out by Table I, which also shows the number of valleys in the relevant band, where this has been quoted.
Anisotropy also presents another possibility for enlarging the difference between the density-of-states and inertial effective masses. It is worth noting that the most commonly used thermoelectric material, bismuth telluride, is anisotropic and also a multivalley semiconductor in both its $p$-type and $n$-type forms.

To some extent one can select semiconductors with large values of $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$ using theoretical band-structure calculations. Experimentally, one can focus attention on those groups of semiconductors in which at least one member has been observed with a high value of $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$. Within a group of semiconductors, as the energy gap becomes less, the effective mass falls and the mobility rises. Many of the materials with very large mobilities have small energy gaps. Bismuth,¹⁴ for example, has one of the highest values of $\mu(m^{*}/m)^{3/2}$ because of its large electron mobility, but its small (negative) energy gap has limited its effectiveness as a thermoelectric material except at low temperatures. The higher the temperature of operation, the larger the energy gap must be if minority carrier conduction is to be avoided. The optimum Seebeck coefficient, $\alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}$, is generally of the order of $\pm 200\ \mu\mathrm{V/K}$ when $ZT$ is unity or less, but, as $ZT$ becomes larger, so does $\alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}$. It is necessary for such a Seebeck coefficient to be reached with a negligible concentration of minority carriers.

These considerations are based on the assumption of simple parabolic energy bands, which is probably invalid¹⁵ unless the direct energy gap is much larger than $kT$. Thus, in many thermoelectric materials, prediction of the optimum Fermi energy and the maximum power factor is difficult.

On the other hand, it has been shown¹⁶,¹⁷ that the increase in Seebeck coefficient due to ionized-impurity scattering would be much more useful when both electrons and holes are present. Impurity scattering does not significantly increase the power factor for a single type of carrier, but it does increase the Seebeck coefficient for a particular Fermi energy. Thus, for any given Seebeck coefficient, minority carrier conduction becomes less noticeable. This is particularly important when the energy gap is very small. Thus, it was calculated that, for a material with zero energy gap, the optimum figure of merit might be more than doubled when ionized-impurity scattering becomes comparable to lattice scattering.

In semiconductors such as bismuth telluride or lead telluride, which are made up of heavy atoms, the dielectric constant is large and this shields the carriers from the coulomb forces of the impurity ions. Impurity scattering is much stronger in semiconductors such as silicon that have a smaller dielectric constant.¹⁸

Silicon would have many practical advantages as a thermoelectric material. It has already been shown by a number of authors¹⁹⁻²¹ that the lattice conductivity can be greatly reduced through structural modification. Silicon is, of course, much more abundant than many of the elements used in thermoelectric materials. Is it possible that the very large value of $\mu(m^*/m)^{3/2}$ for pure silicon (particularly $n$-type) can be maintained at heavy doping levels? Recent studies by Bahk et al.²² suggest that this may be achievable.

These authors proposed a structure consisting of embedded nanoparticles that donate electrons to the matrix through which conduction takes place. Scattering from these ionized nanoparticles is not nearly as strong as scattering from individual ions at the same doping level. It was found that the power factor in $\text{In}_{0.53}\text{Ga}_{0.47}\text{As}$ in the temperature range 300 K to 800 K could be increased by 15% to 30% by this means. There does not seem to be any reason why the concept should not be applied to silicon, though the improvement seems limited to a narrow range of ionized nanoparticle size.

# THE DENSITY OF STATES

Although it does not seem to be possible to increase the mobility over its value for pure lattice scattering, there remains the prospect of increasing the carrier concentration through a change in the density of states.

It is likely that the best thermoelectric material will be a solid solution rather than a simple element or compound, since this will lead to a reduction in the lattice conductivity. It is possible that the density of states for a particular material will be raised when it is alloyed with a second material, as seems to occur when PbS is added to PbTe.$^{24}$ Another possibility is the formation of an alloy between substances that have different multivalley band structures.$^{25}$ At a particular composition one might expect the band extrema to become equal for the two structures. In this case the carriers in both bands should contribute to the transport processes. This approach should be particularly useful if both components of the solid solution have large values of $\mu(m^*/m)^{3/2}$. However, the power factor will be increased only if intervalley scattering is absent or, at least, not too strong.

Theoretical work on nanostructures suggests that the band structure may be modified in a useful way when the electrons are confined within crystals of less than a few interatomic spacings in size. Since the use of nanostructures was first proposed by Hicks and Dresselhaus,$^{26,27}$ many experiments on this type of material have been carried out. However, in nearly all cases, any improvement in $ZT$ has come about through a reduction in the lattice thermal conductivity. If there is indeed an improvement in the properties of the charge carriers through the formation of a nanostructure, it will become apparent through an increase in the Seebeck coefficient for a given electrical conductivity. A small effect of this nature has been observed in a system based on lead telluride.$^{28}$ A much stronger effect is the increase in Seebeck coefficient that has been found in bismuth nanowires,$^{29}$ though this has not yet led to the development of bismuth or bismuth alloys with high figures of merit. Recent theoretical work suggests that nanostructured material will in most cases have a power factor that is less than the bulk value$^{30,31}$ and that its true worth lies in the reduction of the lattice conductivity.

# IMPURITY BANDS

Another approach that seems promising lies in the introduction of impurity levels within the main bands. It should not be forgotten that, as the temperature is lowered, the carrier concentration of bismuth telluride does not become less,$^{32}$ even close to 0 K. This must surely indicate that the impurity levels have broadened into bands that overlap the valence or conduction bands. There is some evidence that the extra states produced by doping can be useful: For example,$^{33}$ double doping of PbTe with chromium and iodine appears to increase the density of states close to the Fermi level with a consequent rise in the power factor to the high value of $56.16\ \mathrm{W/m\ K^2}$ at 500 K. There is also the reported increase in the power factor of PbTe through doping with Tl producing resonant levels within the valence band.$^{12}$

We shall show that the relationship between the Seebeck coefficient and the electrical conductivity for a semiconductor with an impurity band can be derived as a function of the depth of that band within the main band.$^{34}$ We assume that the density of states in the main band is proportional to the square root of the energy and that the width of the impurity band is small compared with $kT$ but large enough to allow conduction. We suppose that the impurity states have hybridized with the main band

states and that acoustic-mode lattice scattering is predominant so that all the carriers are equally mobile.

Let us suppose that the narrow impurity band lies within the main band at a depth $E_{\mathrm{I}}$ from its edge. The contribution of any group of carriers to the electrical conductivity will be proportional to its concentration. We can calculate the relative concentrations, $n_{\mathrm{I}}$ and $n_{\mathrm{M}}$, of the carriers, in the impurity band and the main band, respectively, in terms of the Fermi energy, $E_{\mathrm{F}}$, and $E_{\mathrm{I}}$, since the total concentration of impurity states must be equal to $(n_{\mathrm{M}}+n_{\mathrm{I}})$. It is easily shown that

$$
n_{\mathrm{I}}=n_{\mathrm{M}} \frac{f\left(E_{\mathrm{F}}-E_{\mathrm{I}}\right)}{1-f\left(E_{\mathrm{F}}-E_{\mathrm{I}}\right)}, \tag{3}
$$

where $f$ is the Fermi distribution function. The carriers in the main band will have a Seebeck coefficient given by

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{M}}= \pm \frac{k}{e}\left(\eta-\frac{K_{1}}{k T K_{0}}\right), \tag{4}
$$

where $\eta=E_{\mathrm{F}} / k T$ and $K_{0}$ and $K_{1}$ are Fermi-Dirac integrals. This is the more generalized form of Eq. (1) that does not assume nondegeneracy. Since the carriers in the impurity band all have the same energy, their Seebeck coefficient is given by

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{I}}= \pm \frac{k}{e}\left(\eta-\frac{E_{\mathrm{I}}}{k T}\right). \tag{5}
$$

The total Seebeck coefficient is found by averaging the contributions from the carriers weighted according to their concentrations

$$
\alpha=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{I}} n_{\mathrm{I}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{M}} n_{\mathrm{M}}}{n_{\mathrm{I}}+n_{\mathrm{M}}}. \tag{6}
$$

This surprisingly simple approach allows us to express the Seebeck coefficient in terms of the electrical conductivity and as a function of the position of the impurity band. Our results are shown in Fig. 4. The Seebeck coefficient is shown for a $p$-type conductor, but the conclusions would be the same for $n$-type material. We have selected $E_{\mathrm{I}}$ equal to $kT$ and $4kT$ in our calculations, since these are the values quoted for the resonance levels of $\mathrm{Sn}$ in bismuth telluride $^{34}$ and for $\mathrm{Tl}$ in lead telluride. $^{12}$

When the Seebeck coefficient exceeds about $300\ \mu\mathrm{V/K}$ the impurity band has little effect whatever its position. However, for smaller values of the Seebeck coefficient it can make a significant difference. When the impurity band lies at the edge of the main band, it significantly reduces the Seebeck coefficient for any given electrical conductivity. When $E_{\mathrm{I}}$ is equal to $kT$, the Seebeck coefficient is slightly less than it would be with no impurity band but greater than it would be for $E_{\mathrm{I}}=0$. On the other hand, when $E_{\mathrm{I}}=4kT$ the Seebeck coefficient becomes somewhat larger than it would be for no impurity band.

Figure 5 shows how the power factor varies with the Seebeck coefficient for the various locations. For a Seebeck coefficient of $200\ \mu\mathrm{V/K}$, the power factor is marginally greater than for no impurity band when there is such a band at a level of $4kT$ and marginally less when the level is $kT$. However, when the impurity band is at the edge of the main band the power factor is reduced by about $30\%$.

This result is more or less consistent with the observations of Heremans et al. $^{12}$ for Tl-doped PbTe, though our explanation is different from that proposed by these authors. An impurity band at the edge of the main band provides extra carriers, but their energy is much smaller than the average value for the carriers in the main band so they reduce the overall Seebeck coefficient by a substantial amount. On the other hand, an impurity band that is located at more than $2kT$ from the band edge provides both extra carriers and an increase in the Seebeck coefficient. If the concentration of compensating states is $n_C$, the concentration of impurity states in the region of the main band becomes
$$
n_{\mathrm{I}}=\left(n_{\mathrm{M}}+n_{\mathrm{I}}+n_{\mathrm{C}}\right) \mathrm{f}\left(E_{\mathrm{F}}-E_{\mathrm{I}}\right). \tag{7}
$$

This allows us to express the concentration of impurity states as
$$
n_{\mathrm{I}}=\left\{\frac{S}{(1-R)-R S}\right\} n_{\mathrm{M}}, \tag{8}
$$

Our calculations show that impurity bands cease to have much influence on the power factor when the Seebeck coefficient exceeds about $400\ \mu\text{V/K}$. ## CONCLUSIONS

There seem to be good prospects for the achievement of a $ZT$ value of about 4 in the next few years through a reduction in the lattice thermal conductivity in existing thermoelectric materials. However, further improvement will require an increase in the power factor.

It is possible that new semiconductors with higher values of $\mu(m^*/m)^{3/2}$ will be found, but there already exist materials in which this quantity is greater than in bismuth telluride. Silicon is such a material, but its mobility is significantly reduced by impurity scattering when the carrier concentration is large enough for optimization of the power factor. It is possible, however, that impurity scattering can be minimized through the use of ionized nanoparticles rather than atoms. This allows us to target a value of 6 for $ZT$. Further increases may be possible through modifications of the density-of-states function.

Deep-lying impurity bands can yield a small improvement in the power factor, but shallow impurity bands, which probably exist in most thermoelectric materials, can have a deleterious effect. For a Seebeck coefficient of about $200\ \mu\text{V/K}$, as applies to present-day materials, the power factor is nearly 50% larger when the impurity band is shifted into the main band from the edge by only $kT$. It is increasingly evident that the selection of dopants is an important factor in the optimization of any particular element or compound.
