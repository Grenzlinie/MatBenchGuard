# Dynamic Phase Transitions In The Spin-2 Ising System Under An Oscillating Magnetic Field Within The Effective-Field Theory

Mehmet Ertaş¹, Bayram Deviren² and Mustafa Keskin¹,#

1Department of Physics, Erciyes University, 38039 Kayseri, Turkey
2Department of Physics, Nevşehir University, 50300 Nevşehir, Turkey
#Corresponding author. E-mail: keskin@erciyes.edu.tr

**Abstract.** The dynamic phase transitions are studied in the spin-2 Ising model under a time-dependent oscillating magnetic field by using the effective-field theory with correlations. The effective-field dynamic equation is derived by employing the Glauber transition rates and the phases in the system are obtained by solving this dynamic equation. The nature (first- or second-order) of the dynamic phase transition is characterized by investigating the thermal behavior of the dynamic order parameter and the dynamic phase transition temperatures are obtained. The dynamic phase diagrams are presented in (T/zJ, h/zJ) plane.

**Keywords:** Dynamic phase transition; Oscillating magnetic field; Mean-field dynamic equations of motion; Glauber-type stochastic dynamics
**PACS:** 05.50.+q; 05.70.Fh; 64.60.Ht; 75.10.Hk

## INTRODUCTION

The physics of the equilibrium phase transition is now rather well understood within the framework of the equilibrium statistical physics. However, the mechanism behind the nonequilibrium or dynamic phase transition (DPT) has not yet been explored rigorously, and basic phenomenology is still undeveloped. Hence, further efforts on these challenging time-dependent problems, especially calculating the DPT points and constructing the phase diagram, should promise to be rewarding in the future. The dynamic phase transition (DPT) in nonequilibrium system at the presence of an oscillating external magnetic field has attracted much attention in recent years, theoretically [1]. Experimental evidences for the DPT have been found in magnetic systems [2], amorphous YBaCuO films [3], ferroic systems [4], cuprate superconductor [5], and polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) nanocomposites [6].

In this paper, we calculate and characterize the nature of (continuous or discontinuous) dynamic phase transitions and present the dynamic phase diagrams in the spin-2 under a sinusoidal oscillating external magnetic field by using the effective-field theory (EFT) with correlations based on the exact Van der Waerden identity for spin-2. We employ the Glauber transitions rates to construct the dynamic effective-field equation. The EFT method, without introducing mathematical complexity, can incorporate some effects of spin-spin correlations through the usage of the Van der Waerden identities and provide results that are quite superior to those obtained by using the mean-field approximation (MFA). The dynamic phase diagrams are also given within the framework of the dynamic MFA in order to investigate the spin correlation effect on the dynamic phase diagrams of the system; hence this investigation provides some qualitative insight whether the standard dynamic MFA can be applied successfully to Ising systems.

The Ising systems have been one of the most extensively studied systems in the statistical physics and the condensed-mater physics and have also been used as elementary models for variety of phenomena. That is so not only because of the relative simplicity with which approximate calculations for these models

can be carried out and tested, but also because versions and extensions of models can be applied for description of a wide class of real systems. Although the majority of studies have focused on spin-1/2, spin-1 and spin-3/2 Ising models, higher spin systems are not without interest. One of the important higher spin systems is a spin-2 Ising model and has been paid much attention for many years. The equilibrium properties of the model have been studied by well known methods in equilibrium statistical physics such as the MFA, the cluster variation method in pair approximation, the EFT, the pair approximation with the discretized path integral representation [7]. The ground state phase diagrams of the systems have also been worked out [8]. We should also mention that the spins of $\mathrm{Fe}^{\mathrm{II}}$ ions are spin-2 and it is experimentally found that these ions have anisotropy [9]. Moreover, the mixed spin (2,5/2) Ising system is the prototypical system that has been used for studying magnetic behaviors of the molecular-based magnetic materials, such as $\mathrm{N}\left(\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{C}_{4} \mathrm{H}_{9}\right)_{4} \mathrm{Fe}^{\mathrm{II}} \mathrm{Fe}^{\mathrm{III}}$ $\left(\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}\right)_{3}[10,11]$ and $\mathrm{AFe}^{\mathrm{II}} \mathrm{Fe}^{\mathrm{III}}\left(\mathrm{C}_{2} \mathrm{O}_{4}\right)_{3} \quad[A=N(n-$ $\left.\mathrm{C} n \mathrm{H} 2 n+1)_{4}\right][10,12,13]$.

A spin-2 Ising model with N spins is described by the Hamiltonian given by
$$
\mathcal{H}=-\mathrm{J}_{\mathrm{ij}} \sum_{<\mathrm{ij}>} \mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}} \mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{j}}-\mathrm{D} \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}-\mathrm{h}(\mathrm{t}) \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}, \quad(1)
$$
where $\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}$ takes the value $\pm 2, \pm 1,0$ at each site $\mathrm{i}$ of a lattice and <ij> indicates summation over all pairs of nearest neighbor sites. $J_{i j}$ represents the spin-spin interaction strength between sites i and j. For simplicity all $J_{i j}$ are taken equal to a constant $J>0$. $h(t)$ is a time dependent external field given by $h(t)=h_{0} \sin (w t)$. The Hamiltonian of this spin-2 model is also called as the spin-2 Blume-Capel model [14]. The system is in contact with an isothermal heat bath at temperature $\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{abs}}$.

The system evolves according to a Glauber-type stochastic process at a rate of $1 / \tau$ transitions per unit time. Applying the Glauber transition rates, the mean-field dynamical equation of motion for the spin-2 Ising system was found as
$$
\tau \frac{\mathrm{dm}}{\mathrm{dt}}=-\left\langle\mathrm{m}-\frac{1}{2} \frac{4 \sinh [2 \beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})]+2 \sinh [\beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})] \exp (-3 \beta \mathrm{D})}{\cosh [2 \beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})]+\cosh [\beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})] \exp (-3 \beta \mathrm{D})+\exp (-4 \beta \mathrm{D})}\right\rangle, \quad(2)
$$
where $\beta=1 / \mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{B}} \mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{abs}}, \mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{B}}$ is the Boltzmann factor. The Boltzmann constant is set at unity and $\tau=1$, the temperature $\mathrm{T}$ and the external field $\mathrm{h}$ are measured in units of $z J$ ( $z$ is the coordination number).

Now, we use the effective-field theory with correlations to obtain the effective-field dynamic equation for the spin-2 Ising system. This method was first introduced by Honmura and Kaneyoshi [15] and Kaneyoshi et al. [16], which is a more advanced method dealing with Ising systems than the MFA, because it consider more correlations. Within the framework of the EFT, one finds that
$$
\mathrm{m}=<\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}>=\left\langle\prod_{i=1}^{z}\left[\mathrm{~A}+\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{i}}+\mathrm{C}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}+\mathrm{D}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{3}+\mathrm{E}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{4}\right]\right\rangle \mathrm{f}(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})_{\mathrm{x}=0}, \quad(3)
$$
where z denotes the nearest-neighbor sites of the central site i; $\nabla=\partial / \partial x$ is a differential operator, and z=4 on the square lattice. The function f(x+h) is defined by
$$
\mathrm{f}(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})=\frac{1}{2} \frac{4 \sinh [2 \beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})]+2 \sinh [\beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})] \exp (-3 \beta \mathrm{D})}{\cosh [2 \beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})]+\cosh [\beta(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})] \exp (-3 \beta \mathrm{D})+\exp (-4 \beta \mathrm{D})}, \quad(4)
$$

Equation (3) is also exact and is valid for any lattice. If we try to exactly treat all the spin-spin correlations for that equation, the problem quickly becomes intractable. A first obvious attempt to deal with it is to ignore correlations; the decoupling approximation:
$$
\left\langle\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}} \mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}^{\prime}} \ldots \mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}^{\mathrm{n}}}\right\rangle \cong\left\langle\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}^{\prime}}\right\rangle \ldots\left\langle\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}^{\mathrm{n}}}\right\rangle, \quad(5)
$$
with $\mathrm{i} \neq \mathrm{i}^{\prime} \neq \ldots \neq \mathrm{i}^{\mathrm{n}}$ has been introduced within the EFT with correlations [15,17,18]. In fact, the approximation corresponds essentially to the Zernike approximation [18] in the bulk problem, and has been successfully applied to a great number of magnetic systems including the surface problems [15,17,18,20]. On the other hand, in the mean-field theory, all the correlations, including the self-correlations, are neglected. Based on this approximation, Eq. (3) reduces to
$$
\mathrm{m}=\left\langle\left[\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{i}}+\mathrm{C}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}+\mathrm{D}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{3}+\mathrm{E}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\right)^{4}\right]^{z}\right\rangle \mathrm{f}(\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h})_{\mathrm{x}=0}, \quad(6)
$$

Expanding Eq. (6), after some manipulations the dynamic equation of motion for the average magnetization reads
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{dt}} \mathrm{m}=-\mathrm{m}+\mathrm{a}_{0}+\mathrm{a}_{1} \mathrm{~m}_{1}+\mathrm{a}_{2} \mathrm{~m}_{2}+\mathrm{a}_{3} \mathrm{~m}_{3}+\mathrm{a}_{4} \mathrm{~m}_{4}, \quad(7)
$$

The coefficients $a_{i}(i=0$ to 4$)$ can be easily calculated employing a mathematical relation
$\exp (\alpha \nabla) f(x)=f(x+\alpha), \quad$ where $\quad \nabla=\partial / \partial x \quad$ is a differential operator.

The dynamic order parameter or dynamic magnetization as the time-averaged magnetization over a period of the oscillating magnetic field is given as
$$
\mathrm{M}=\frac{\mathrm{w}}{2 \pi} \int \mathrm{m}(\mathrm{t}) \mathrm{dt}. \quad(8)
$$

On the other hand, the hysteresis loop area is

$$\mathrm{A}=-\iint \mathrm{m}(\mathrm{t}) \mathrm{dh}=-\mathrm{h}_{0} \mathrm{w} \iint \mathrm{m}(\mathrm{t}) \cos (\mathrm{wt}) \mathrm{dt},\tag{9}$$

which corresponds to the energy loss due to the hysteresis. The dynamic correlation is calculated as

$$\mathrm{C}=\frac{\mathrm{w}}{2 \pi} \iint \mathrm{m}(\mathrm{t}) \mathrm{h}(\mathrm{t}) \mathrm{dt}=\frac{\mathrm{wh}_{0}}{2 \pi} \iint \mathrm{m}(\mathrm{t}) \sin (\mathrm{wt}) \mathrm{dt}.\tag{10}$$

We should also mention that in the numerical calculations, the hysteresis loop area A and the dynamic correlation C are also measured in units $zJ$. In the next section we will give the numerical results of these equations.

The thermal behavior of the M, A and C for the values of D/|J|=1.0, $h_0$/|J|=0.1 and w=$2\pi$, the $\circ,\bullet$ and $\boldsymbol\Delta$ lines are for M, C and A, respectively. $T_C$/ |J| is the second-order phase transition temperature from the ferromagnetic-2 (F₂) phase to the paramagnetic (P) phase.

The behaviors of M, A and C as functions of the reduced temperature for D/|J|=1.0, $h_0$/|J|= 0.1 and w = $2\pi$ are obtained by solving Eqs. (8), (9) and (10), respectively. We solve these equations by combining the numerical methods of the Adams- Moulton predictor corrector with the Romberg integration. One interesting results are plotted in Fig.1in order to illustrate the characterization of the nature (continuous or discontinuous) dynamic transitions and the calculation of the DPT points. In this figure, $T_C$/ |J| represents the second-order phase transition temperature. In Fig. 1 shows the behaviors of M, A and C as a function of the reduced temperature for D/|J|= 1.0, $h_0$/|J|= 0.1 and w= $2\pi$. In this figure, M= 2 at zero temperature, and M decreases to zero continuously as the reduced temperature increases; therefore, a second- order phase transition occurs at $T_C$/ |J|= 8.18 and the dynamic phase transition is from the ferromagnetic-2 (F₂) phase to the paramagnetic (P) phase. On the other hand, the hysteresis loop area A and the dynamic correlation C become a maximum and a minimum (negative) at the second-order phase transition temperature on the critical temperature $T_C$/|J|, respectively. Moreover, if a discontinuity or jump occurs on M, A and C, the system undergoes a dynamic first-order phase transition; hence the first-order transition temperature is the temperature where discontinuity or jump occurs. This fact has been illustrated explicitly in recent works [21].

![](./images/813213024969031681_5.jpg)

Figure 2. The dynamic phase diagram for D/|J|=-1.5 and w = $2\pi$.

We present the dynamic phase diagrams in the (T/zJ, h/zJ) plane, seen in Fig. 2. In Fig. 2, the solid and dashed lines represent the second- and first-order phase transition lines, respectively, and the dynamic tricritical point is denoted by a filled circle. The phase diagram shows F₂ + P coexistence region or phase as well as the P phase; two dynamic tricritical point (T) and one dynamic triple point (TP) appear. In order to see the influence of the correlations we have compared the present results with the results of dynamic EFT [14], namely Fig. 2 with Fig. 3.(d) in Ref. 13. The following important differences are found. (i) Two dynamic tricritical points are found instead of one. (ii) The F₂ is dominant not the F₁ phase. (iii) The F₂ + F₁ + P mixed phase does not exists. Therefore, one concludes that the dynamic EFT gives more interesting and the richer phase diagrams.
