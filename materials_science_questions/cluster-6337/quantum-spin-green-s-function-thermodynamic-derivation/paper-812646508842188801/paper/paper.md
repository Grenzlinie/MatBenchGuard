# Impurities in Ising-model lattices and probe independence of hyperfine critical exponents

T K Bergstresser† and Harvey Gould
Physics Department, Clark University, Worcester, Mass., 01610, USA

Received 3 October 1978

Abstract. The spin $-\frac{1}{2}$ Ising Hamiltonian with a single non-magnetic impurity is analysed. An exact expression in terms of perfect lattice correlation functions is found for the average value of the spin on a site neighbouring the impurity. The latter quantity is a measure of the importance of probe disturbance effects in hyperfine field measurements of critical exponents. It is shown for the case of a simple cubic lattice that the hyperfine probe does not disturb the measurement of the critical exponent $\beta$ if the temperature is in the presently experimentally accessible range close to the critical temperature. It is also found that mean-field theories overestimate the importance of probe disturbance effects.

## 1. Introduction
The advantages of hyperfine field techniques for the measurement of magnetic critical exponents are well known. For example, in a ferromagnet they permit the determination of the bulk magnetisation and associated critical exponent $\beta$ without assumptions about the magnetic equation of state near the critical temperature. Recent experiments include nuclear magnetic resonance in insulating materials such as $MnF_{2}$ (Heller and Benedek 1962, 1965 and Senturia and Benedek 1966), perturbed angular correlations (PAC) (Hohenemser et al 1976), and Mössbauer spectroscopy (Hohenemser 1975). One possible problem with hyperfine field measurements is that the probe nucleus is an impurity in the magnetic host ($Fe^{57}$ in Fe is an exception). It is possible to use a sufficiently dilute probe concentration so that impurity-impurity interactions are negligible. How- ever, since the hyperfine field is determined either by the impurity spin in the case of a magnetic impurity or the neighbouring spins in the case of a non-magnetic impurity, the probe disturbs the measurement of the bulk host properties. An analysis of many experiments (Hohenemser et al 1976) supports the hypothesis that probe-disturbance effects are small near the critical point. In the present paper we consider the problem theoretically.

Previous theoretical investigations (Jaccarino et al 1964, Lovesey and Marshall 1966, Shirley et al 1968) of the probe disturbance of the magnetisation density by a localised impurity are restricted to the mean-field approximation and are not expected to be applicable to the critical region. In this paper we model the ferromagnetic host by a spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ Ising model with nearest-neighbour interaction. The choice of the Ising model

allows us to consider the critical region and keep the calculational details to a minimum.
The generalisation of our analysis to the Heisenberg model is not straightforward. Our analysis is restricted to an investigation of an isolated, nonmagnetic, substitutional impurity at one lattice site; the method can be easily generalised to a magnetic impurity.

The impurity disturbs the local magnetic environment in a magnetically ordered host. This disturbance suggests that the local magnetisation and hyperfine field might have different temperature dependences from those in the undisturbed host. A measure of this disturbance is the behaviour of the net spin of the nearest-neighbour host atoms to the non-magnetic impurity.

In §2 we show that it is possible to derive an exact relation for the net spin of the nearest-neighbour host atoms in terms of perfect lattice correlation functions. This rela- tion is analogous to the simpler and well-known relation of the susceptibility to the two-point equilibrium averaged correlation functions. In §3 we construct Padé approxi- mants for the simple cubic perfect lattice correlation functions of interest using published results for the corresponding low-temperature series expansions. Our results for the temperature-dependence of the average value of the spin on a site neighbouring the impurity are discussed in §4. Our conclusion is that the hyperfine probe does not disturb the measurement of the bulk magnetisation critical exponent $\beta$, if the temperature is in the currently experimentally accessible range close to the critical temperature. It is shown that the measurement of other critical exponents is also not disturbed. In §5 we compare the mean-field results with our Ising model calculation and find that the mean-field theories overestimate the probe disturbance effects. A summary of our results is given in §6.

## 2. Method
We begin with the spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ Ising model with nearest-neighbour exchange interaction $J>0$. The spin variables $\sigma_{i}=\pm1$ reside on lattice sites denoted by site index $i$. The perfect lattice Hamiltonian is written as
$$
\mathscr{H}_{0}=-J \sum_{\langle i j\rangle} \sigma_{i} \sigma_{j}-H \sum_{k} \sigma_{k}\tag{2.1}
$$
where the sum $\langle i j\rangle$ is over distinct nearest-neighbour pairs. We either set $H = 0$ or take the limit $H\rightarrow0^{+}$ where appropriate.

In the imperfect lattice with one isolated (central) impurity, we denote the impurity site by subscript $c$ and the impurity site nearest-neighbours by subscripts $(1,2,\dots,q)$ where $q$ is the number of nearest-neighbours. The Ising lattice Hamiltonian with impurity is written as
$$
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{0}+\mathscr{H}^{\prime}\tag{2.2}
$$
with
$$
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}=+J^{\prime} \sigma_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\ldots+\sigma_{q}\right).\tag{2.3}
$$

For $J'\neq J$ in (2.3), $\mathscr{H}'$ describes a spin impurity with altered exchange constant $J'$. The present investigation will be restricted to $J'=J$ which decouples $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}$ from the rest of the lattice and which describes a non-magnetic impurity at the central site c.

The canonical thermal average in the perfect lattice of a spin operator $\theta$ is denoted as
$$
\langle\theta\rangle_{0}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\theta \exp \left(-\beta \mathscr{H}_{0}\right)\right] / \operatorname{Tr}\left[\exp \left(-\beta \mathscr{H}_{0}\right)\right],\tag{2.4}
$$

where $\beta=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}T$, $T$ is the absolute temperature, and $k_{\mathrm{B}}$ is Boltzmann's constant. The subscript 0 refers to the perfect lattice equilibrium average. For the imperfect lattice the canonical thermal average is written as

$$
\langle\theta\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[\theta \exp (-\beta \mathscr{H})] / \operatorname{Tr}[\exp (-\beta \mathscr{H})],\tag{2.5}
$$

or using the notation of (2.4)

$$
\langle\theta\rangle=\left\langle\theta \exp \left(-\beta \mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle_{0} /\left\langle\exp \left(-\beta \mathscr{H}^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle_{0}.\tag{2.6}
$$

The form of (2.6) follows from the fact that $\mathscr{H}'$ commutes with $\mathscr{H}_{0}$ and greatly simplifies the calculation of $\langle\theta\rangle$. In contrast $\mathscr{H}'$ and $\mathscr{H}_{0}$ do not commute in the Heisenberg model.

We introduce the additional notation

$$
h=\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\ldots+\sigma_{q}\right),\tag{2.7}
$$

and

$$
\lambda=-\beta J,\tag{2.8}
$$

so that

$$
-\beta \mathscr{H}^{\prime}=\lambda h.\tag{2.9}
$$

Equation (2.6) takes the form

$$
\langle\theta\rangle=\langle\theta \exp (\lambda h)\rangle_{0} /\langle\exp (\lambda h)\rangle_{0}.\tag{2.10}
$$

The dominant behaviour of the hyperfine field at the impurity site is determined largely by the thermal average of the net spin of the nearest-neighbour host atoms. Thus we are interested in evaluation of (2.10) with $\theta=\sigma_{1}$. We now show that it is possible to derive an exact relation for $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle$ in terms of the perfect lattice correlation functions. The identity

$$
\exp \left(\lambda \sigma_{i}\right)=\cosh \lambda+\sigma_{i} \sinh \lambda,\tag{2.11}
$$

which follows from the double-valued property of $\sigma_{i}$, and the definition (2.7) are used to write the operator $\exp (\lambda h)$ as

$$
\exp (\lambda h)=\sum_{n=0}^{q} h_{n} f_{n}(\lambda).\tag{2.12}
$$

The operators $h_{n}$ in (2.12) are sums of products of the $(q+1)$ operators $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}, \sigma_{1}, \ldots, \sigma_{q}$. The finite number of functions $f_{n}(\lambda)$ and operators $h_{n}$ are tabulated in table 1 for a three-dimensional lattice with six nearest neighbours, e.g., a simple cubic. Table 1 was derived by exhaustion of the finite number of possible values for $\exp (\lambda h)$. Other lattice symmetries are equally easy to evaluate. We use (2.12) to rewrite (2.10) in the form

$$
\langle\theta\rangle=N / D,\tag{2.13}
$$

where

$$
N=\sum_{m=0}^{q} f_{m}(\lambda)\left\langle h_{m} \theta\right\rangle_{0},\tag{2.14}
$$

and

$$
D=\sum_{p=0}^{q} f_{p}(\lambda)\left\langle h_{p}\right\rangle_{0},\tag{2.15}
$$

The quantities $\langle h_{m}\theta\rangle_{0}$ and $\langle h_{p}\rangle_{0}$ are sums of $n$-point correlation functions for the perfect lattice defined by

$$
\Gamma_{i,j,\dots,m}\equiv\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\dots\sigma_{m}\rangle_{0}.\tag{2.16}
$$

In general $n\leqslant q+l$, where $l$ is the order of $\theta$. (Our discussion is limited to $\theta=\sigma_{1},l=1$.)
Note that the problem of evaluating the thermal average of the imperfect lattice has been reduced to the evaluation of various multi-point correlation functions for the perfect lattice and the evaluation of the functions $f_{n}(\lambda)$.

The form of (2.13)-(2.15) involves some unfamiliar multi-point correlation functions.
It is possible to reduce the number of such functions needed. For example the assumption that $\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle=0$ implies a non-trivial relationship between a number of odd-point correlation functions which can be used to eliminate one correlation function. Fisher (1959, 1963) has derived a number of relations between spin correlation functions. We describe next a more convenient method to eliminate as many correlation functions as possible.

The definition of the multi-point correlation function in (2.16) is restricted to $n$ even (the two-point function is sometimes written as $\Gamma_{k}(r)=\langle\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k+r}\rangle_{0}$). The odd-point functions must be defined more carefully:

$$
\Gamma_{i\dots n}\equiv\lim_{H\rightarrow0^{+}}\langle\sigma_{i}\dots\sigma_{n}\rangle_{0},\tag{2.17}
$$

The one-point function is the reduced bulk magnetisation:

$$
\Gamma_{1}=\lim_{H\rightarrow0^{+}}\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{0}\equiv\sigma.\tag{2.18}
$$

$\Gamma$ is a function of $T$ and has the symmetry of the perfect lattice. To be specific we illustrate the method for the sc lattice. For simplicity a spin's spatial location is labelled by a single subscript:

$$
\begin{align*}
\boldsymbol{r}_{\mathrm{c}}&=(0,0,0),&\boldsymbol{r}_{1}&=(1,0,0),&\boldsymbol{r}_{2}&=(0,1,0)\\
\boldsymbol{r}_{3}&=(0,0,1),&\boldsymbol{r}_{4}&=-\boldsymbol{r}_{1},&\boldsymbol{r}_{5}&=-\boldsymbol{r}_{2},&\boldsymbol{r}_{6}&=-\boldsymbol{r}_{3}.
\end{align*}
$$

Consider the following projection operators:

$$
\theta^{6^{+}}=(\sigma_{1}+1)(\sigma_{2}+1)(\sigma_{3}+1)(\sigma_{4}+1)(\sigma_{5}+1)(\sigma_{6}+1),\tag{2.19}
$$

$$\theta^{6^{-}}=\left(\sigma_{1}-1\right)\left(\sigma_{2}-1\right)\left(\sigma_{3}-1\right)\left(\sigma_{4}-1\right)\left(\sigma_{5}-1\right)\left(\sigma_{6}-1\right).\tag{2.20}$$

These operators pick out of the perfect lattice canonical ensemble only those configura- tions having a specified spin structure in the nearest-neighbour cluster around the central spin $\sigma_{c}$. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tanh (6 \beta J) & =\left\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{c}} \theta^{6^{+}}\right\rangle_{0} /\left\langle\theta^{6^{+}}\right\rangle_{0} & & (2.21 a) \\
& =-\left\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{c}} \theta^{6^{-}}\right\rangle_{0} /\left\langle\theta^{6^{-}}\right\rangle_{0}. & & (2.21 b)
\end{aligned}
$$

We can rewrite (2.21) and make use of the rotational and translational symmetry of the SC perfect lattice and find two nontrivial relations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tanh (6 \beta J) & \left(1+12 \Gamma_{12}+3 \Gamma_{14}+12 \Gamma_{1236}+3 \Gamma_{1245}+\Gamma_{123456}\right) \\
& =\left(6 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 1}+12 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 124}+8 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 123}+6 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 12345}\right),
\end{aligned}\tag{2.22a}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tanh (6 \beta J) & \left(6 \sigma+12 \Gamma_{124}+8 \Gamma_{123}+6 \Gamma_{12345}\right) \\
& =\left(\sigma+12 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 12}+3 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 14}+12 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 1236}+3 \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 1245}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 123456}\right).
\end{aligned}\tag{2.22b}
$$

Similar projection operators can be introduced and in the same manner six independent relations are found between the odd-point correlation functions and four independent relations between the even-point functions.

Now consider (2.13)-(2.15) with $\theta=\sigma_{1}$. In this case $N$ contains only odd-point corre- lation functions and can be written in terms of nine distinct functions including $\sigma$ using the rotational and translational symmetry of the SC lattice. The structure of $N$ is such that the six relations between the odd-point functions can be used to eliminate not six but seven correlation functions from $N$. Similarly it can be shown that $D$ contains ten distinct even-point correlation functions (including the constant 1); the four relations between the even-point functions can be used to eliminate five functions from $D$. The result is

$$
\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle=N_{1} / D,\tag{2.23}
$$

with

$$
N_{1}=A_{1} \sigma+A_{2} \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 123456},\tag{2.24}
$$

$$
D=B_{1}+B_{2} \Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 1}+B_{3} \Gamma_{12}+B_{4} \Gamma_{14}+B_{5} \Gamma_{123456}.\tag{2.25}
$$

The coefficients $A$ and $B$ are functions of $\lambda$ and hence of $T$. They are well-behaved and slowly varying at the critical temperature $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ and are non-singular except near $T=0$. The values at $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ of $A$ and $B$ are given in table 2.

For the two-dimensional square lattice the projection operator technique yields the following interesting result: all of the three-, four- and five-point correlation functions that can be constructed from a central site plus nearest neighbours can be written as sums of non-singular functions times lower-point correlation functions. The result analogous to (2.24), (2.25) thus contains no $A_{2}$ and $B_{5}$ terms. Since all the lower corre- lation functions are known exactly (Kaufman and Onsager 1949 and Montroll et al 1963), the two-dimensional square Ising problem with non-magnetic impurity is solved exactly. It is not surprising to find an exact solution to this problem since the analogous problem of a two-dimensional square lattice with a plane boundary is also exactly solvable (Onsager 1944 and McCoy and Wu 1967).

It might be thought that the occurrence of only two-point correlation functions and $\sigma$ in $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle$ for the two-dimensional square lattice is a fortuitous consequence of the two- dimensional nature of the problem. However for the three-dimensional diamond lattice

all of the three-, four- and five-point correlation functions can be eliminated in favour of the lower-order functions. In this case we still do not have an exact solution to the impurity problem because we require $\sigma$ and the three two-point functions. We do not pursue the diamond lattice further since the series-expansion results for this lattice are not as well-developed as for the sc lattice. For the three-dimensional BCC lattice and especially the three-dimensional FCC lattice a number of multi-point functions remain, making these structures less convenient to analyse.

Aside from occasional references to the two-dimensional square lattice, we concen- trate the remainder of this paper on the three-dimensional sc lattice.

## 3. Correlation functions
It was shown in $\S 2$ that the evaluation of $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ requires information on various corre lation functions for the perfect lattice. We rely on the published literature for the be- haviour of the perfect lattice correlation functions in the critical region. However, this information is not sufficient since we wish to determine the behaviour of $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ for $0 \leqslant T \leqslant T_{c}$ . In the following we construct Padé approximants for the correlation func tions over this broad temperature range. Fortunately the problem of finding sufficiently accurate approximants outside the asymptotic critical region is less demanding than finding critical values of exponents and amplitudes.

We adopt the notation reduced temperature $\tau=1-T / T_{c}$ and the standard defini tions for the critical exponents $\alpha', \beta, \gamma, \gamma'$ and $\delta$ ; $T_{c}$ is the critical temperature.

We adopt the generally accepted critical exponents for the three-dimensional Ising model (Le Guillou and Zinn-Justin 1977): $\beta=0.325$ and $\alpha'=0.11$ . We take $u_{c}=$  $\exp (-4 J / k T_{c})=0.411985 \pm 14$ as in Sykes et al 1972 and as used for example by Tarko and Fisher 1975.

We require three two-point correlation functions: $\Gamma_{c 1} \equiv \Gamma_{100}, \Gamma_{12} \equiv \Gamma_{110}$ , and $\Gamma_{13} \equiv \Gamma_{200}$ . The asymptotic behaviour of these functions in the critical region iswritten as $T \to T_{c}^{-}$ 

$$
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\mathrm{c}}+E^{-\tau^{1-\alpha^{\prime}}}+\ldots. \quad (3.1)
$$

We use
$$\Gamma_{100}^{\mathrm{c}}=0.332 \pm 0.001, \quad \Gamma_{110}^{\mathrm{c}}=0.208 \pm 0.002, \quad \Gamma_{200}^{\mathrm{c}}=0.162 \pm 0.004, \quad(3.2)$$
and
$$E_{100}^{-}=3 \cdot 16 \pm 0 \cdot 18, \quad E_{110}^{\mathrm{c}}=3 \cdot 99 \pm 0 \cdot 23, \quad E_{200}^{\mathrm{c}}=4 \cdot 57 \pm 0 \cdot 26.\quad(3.3)$$

The critical values $\Gamma^{c}$ are taken from Ritchie and Fisher (1972) and the critical amplitudes $E^{-}$ from Fisher and Tarko (1975). We make limited use of the critical behaviour of thebulk magnetisation $\sigma$ :
$$\sigma=B \tau^{\beta}+\ldots\quad(3.4)$$
with $B=1.570$ (Domb 1974).

The expansion co efficients for $\Gamma_{100}$ and $\sigma$ are given in table VIII and table X of Domb (1974) to order $u^{17}$ and $u^{20}$ respectively. Our procedure is to first expand the critical behaviour of $\Gamma$ and $\sigma$, (3.2) and (3.3), in a power series about $u=0$. This series is subtracted from the series expansion for the corresponding function. A Padé approximant is constructed for the subtracted power series. Near-diagonal approximants are chosen: $[N/D] = [8/9]$ for $\Gamma_{100}$, $[5/7]$ for $\Gamma_{110}$ and $\Gamma_{200}$, and $[10/10]$ for $\sigma$. Since the Padé approximant does not go precisely to zero at $T_{\mathrm{c}}$, it is necessary to add a small term in $u^{N+D+1}$ to force it to do so. We then add the correct value at $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ to yield the final approximation. This approximation reproduces exactly the assumed value at $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ and the leading correction, and reproduces exactly all known coefficients of the power series expansion about $u=0$. This procedure is not unique; other methods were tried but yielded only small differences.

We also need two higher-point correlation functions. An inspection of table 2 and (2.24) and (2.25) reveals that they are not needed to high accuracy. Our results will not be affected by using the simple approximants:

$$
\Gamma_{123456}=(0.5 \pm 0.5) \Gamma_{100}, \tag{3.5}
$$

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c} 123456}=(0.5 \pm 0.5) \sigma. \tag{3.6}
$$

More accurate approximants could easily be constructed. Each of the above ingredients in our approximant procedure involves errors. These errors are small and are discussed in $\S 4$.

## 4. Results
As discussed in $\S 2$ the ratio $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle/\sigma$ is a measure of the influence of the hyperfine probe. The behaviour of $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle/\sigma$ in the asymptotic critical region is interesting although not directly relevant to a discussion of the experimentally accessible temperature region. In the limit $T \to T_{\mathrm{c}}^{-}$ we find the asymptotic result

$$
\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle/\sigma=(0.7986 \pm 0.0013)+(1.88 \pm 0.15) \tau^{1-\alpha'}+\dots \tag{4.1}
$$

Note that the critical value is non-zero and that the leading correction term has a positive exponent thus ensuring that $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ and $\sigma$ have the same critical exponent $\beta$.

Near $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ the most important source of error in (4.1) is the uncertainty in the critical values given in (3.2) of the nearest-neighbour correlation functions. The quoted uncertainty of the critical values taken from Ritchie and Fisher (1972) is an error estimate rather than a statistical uncertainty. The next most important source of error arises from the approximate form (3.6) for $\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}123456}$. The errors in the other critical correlation

functions are unimportant. How do we combine the individual error terms? We do not expect the errors in the two-point correlation functions to be uncorrelated, but since the term $B_2$ in Table 2 is negative and the $A_2$ term is positive we compute an RMS error as if the errors were uncorrelated. As shown in (4.1) this calculation yields the error estimate of $\pm 0.0013$ for the critical value of $\langle\sigma_1\rangle/\sigma$. (If instead we add the absolute value of all error terms, the estimated error in the critical value becomes $\pm 0.0022$.) The additional error associated with the Padé approximant is zero at $T_c$ and $T=0$ and is estimated to be smaller than the error associated with the uncertainties in the critical values of the correlation functions. The error estimate of $\pm 0.15$ for the critical amplitude of $\langle\sigma_1\rangle/\sigma$ in (4.1) arises from the uncertainties of the two-point correlation function critical amplitudes given in (3.3) and is found in the same way. The form (4.1) is expected to be valid for $\tau<10^{-3}$ for which the correction term is small.

The uncertainties in $u_c$ and $\alpha'$ have a negligible effect on our results. The exponent $\alpha'$ is difficult to calculate theoretically (Guttmann 1970 and Stanley 1971), but its precise value is not important to our final results. As an example, an earlier version of this work (Bergstresser 1975) used an analysis based on $\alpha'=0$. For $\tau\leqslant10^{-1.5}$ the present results differs negligibly from the preliminary analysis.

In order to extend our results for $\langle\sigma_1\rangle/\sigma$ to lower $T$ we require additional information on the multi-point correlation functions $\Gamma_{123456}$ and $\Gamma_{c123456}$ to that given in (3.5) and (3.6) respectively. For this purpose we have developed low-temperature expansions to order $u^{10}$ of $\Gamma_{123456}$ and $\Gamma_{c123456}$ and have constructed their Padé approximants. It is interesting to note that a direct low-temperature expansion for $\langle\sigma_1\rangle$ includes half-powers of $u$. However we did not use this direct expansion to construct a Padé approximant for $\langle\sigma_1\rangle$. The Padé approximants for $\Gamma_{123456}$ and $\Gamma_{c123456}$ were used to obtain $\langle\sigma_1\rangle/\sigma$ for the three-dimensional sc Ising lattice in the range $10^{-5}\leqslant\tau\leqslant1$ as shown in curve A of figure 1. Between $\tau=0$ and $\tau=10^{-3}$ the curve is almost horizontal and changes by only $0.3\%$. At $\tau=10^{-2}$ the change is only $2\%$. We see that our result is optimistic: probe effects do not disturb the measurement of the exponent $\beta$. We discuss the latter result more quantitatively below.

It is clear that we cannot include another term in (4.1) with its own error estimate since its error would be strongly correlated with the error in the leading correction term. However, it is of interest to know the effect of the next term. An approximant to our result shown in curve A of figure 1, accurate to $0 \cdot 1 \%$ for $\tau \leqslant 10^{-1 \cdot 5}$ is

$$

Note that the next correction term with exponent 1 is almost as important as the leading correction term with exponent $(1-\alpha')$ and partly cancels this term.

Before discussing the implications of these results for experimental measurements of exponents via hyperfine probes, we recall the reasons why the specific details of our calculation do not apply directly to current experiments. The most important limitation of our results is that they are for a sc lattice. However, for the BCC and especially the FCC lattices the asymptotic value of $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma$ in the critical region is closer to unity so that the hyperfine probe is expected to disturb the measurements even less than in the sc lattice. Our results are also restricted to the Ising rather than the Heisenberg model. The main difference between the two models is in the low-temperature regions; the fact that $\alpha'$ is negative for the Heisenberg model is not expected to be important. It should also be noted that the hyperfine field at a nonmagnetic impurity is not determined solely by the nearest-neighbour spins, and that the probe produces lattice strains and distortion of electronic structure. For radioactive probes which are diffused or melted into the lattice, the disturbance due to the original nuclear decay must diffuse away sufficiently rapidly. Other effects such as magnetostriction and thermal expansion affect not only the hyperfine probe but also the bulk magnetisation. Recall also that our result is for the nonmagnetic impurity only. With these restrictions in mind, we discuss the experimental measurement of $\beta$ based on our calculation of $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma$.

One way to discuss the present predictions of the disturbance of the hyperfine probe is to define a probe-induced change in the apparent $\beta$:

$$
\Delta \beta=-\Delta \lg \left(\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma\right) / \Delta \lg \tau. \tag{4.3}
$$

This measure of $\Delta \beta$ is only indicative since the experimental results are not analysed as in (4.3). Our results for the relative change, $\Delta \beta / \beta$, are shown in table 3. If the desired experimental accuracy in $\beta$ is $0 \cdot 01$, i.e. $\Delta \beta / \beta=0 \cdot 03$, and if the data are to be fitted to a single power law, then an inspection of table 3 indicates that data with $\tau>10^{-2}$ should not be used.

$$
\left[\frac{\left(\mathrm{d}\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \mathrm{d} H\right)}{(\mathrm{d} \sigma / \mathrm{d} H)}\right]_{T \rightarrow T_{c}^{+}, H=0^{+}}=K+O\left(|\tau|^{1-\alpha}\right), \tag{4.4}
$$

the low-temperature susceptibility:

$$
\left[\frac{\left(\mathrm{d}\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \mathrm{d} H\right)}{(\mathrm{d} \sigma / \mathrm{d} H)}\right]_{T \rightarrow T_{c}^{-}, H=0^{+}}=K+O\left(\tau^{1-\alpha^{\prime}}\right), \tag{4.5}
$$

and the critical isotherm:

$$
\left[\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle\left(T_{c}, H\right) / \sigma\left(T_{c}, H\right)\right]_{H \rightarrow 0^{+}}=K+O\left(H^{\psi}\right). \tag{4.6}
$$

The constant $K=0.7986$. The exponents for all the correction terms are positive, so that in principle the hyperfine probe does not disturb the measurement of the exponents $\alpha, \gamma$ and $\delta$. These asymptotic results do not answer the question of whether the probe disturbs the measurement in the experimentally accessible $T$ and $H$ region. It is clear from our discussion of $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma$ that it is not enough to know the amplitudes for the leading correction terms; what is needed is a calculation similar to that done for $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma$ for the experimentally accessible range of $\tau$.

The exponent $\psi$ in (4.6) is defined as the exponent of the leading singular term of the entropy on the critical isotherm (see for example Stanley 1971) but it can be shown that $\psi$ also gives the leading behaviour of the internal energy on the critical isotherm and hence of the two-point correlation functions. By scaling, it is given by $\psi=(1-\alpha) / \beta \delta$, so that its accepted value for the three-dimensional Ising model is $\simeq 0.56$. This exponent is closer to zero than is $(1-\alpha)=0.875$, so that it is likely that the hyperfine probe disturbs the measurement of $\delta$ more than of $\beta$ and $\gamma$. The exponent $\psi$ has not been measured directly nor calculated theoretically other than from the scaling relations. It is possible that a hyperfine field measurement might be the best way to measure $\psi$.

We now consider the behaviour of a perfect lattice without impurity but with an external spatially localised magnetic field acting on $\sigma_{c}$ so that $\left\langle\sigma_{c}\right\rangle=0$. In this case $\sigma_{c}$ is allowed to fluctuate about its average value of zero whereas in the case already considered $\sigma_{c}$ is specified to be zero and does not fluctuate. Our motivation for this problem is that the mean-field approximation to be discussed in $\S 5$ does not distinguish between these distinct cases. The results for the latter case are denoted by the subscript H, e.g., $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}}$. We use techniques similar to those used in $\S 2$ and find the exact result for any lattice

$$
\left\langle\sigma_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}} / \sigma=\left(1-\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}\right) /\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right), \tag{4.7}
$$

where the $\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}$ are given in (3.2). The $\tau$-dependence of $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}} / \sigma$ for the SC lattice is shown as curve C in figure 1. Its value at $\tau=0$ is $0.688 \pm 0.001$.

We close this section by giving the analogous results for the two-dimensional square lattice. The critical value for $\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma$ is

$$
\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle / \sigma=\frac{1}{2}(5-3 \sqrt{2}) /\left[-1+\sqrt{2}+\pi^{-1}\left(1-\pi^{-1}\right)(6-4 \sqrt{2})\right]=0.7749. \tag{4.8}
$$

The conclusion that the hyperfine probe does not disturb the measurement of the exponent $\beta$ is also valid for the two-dimensional square lattice. We also have at $\tau=0$

$$
\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}} / \sigma=1-\frac{1}{2} \sqrt{2}=0.2929. \tag{4.9}
$$

### 5. Comparison with mean-field theory

Although the mean-field theory results are approximate, they might be of interest since they are usually easier to evaluate than the more exact theory and thus might be more suitable for complicated situations, e.g. for complex crystal structures. It also might be possible to incorporate some of the results of a more exact theory into a mean-field context. However, our conclusion is that the mean-field theory is not useful for the present problem.

We discuss in particular the mean-field theory of Lovesey and Marshall (1966) because it provides the detailed information that is needed for comparison with our results. Their result for $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle/\sigma$ in the sc lattice can be written as
$$
\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}}/\sigma=(1-z)+F(z)^{-1}, \tag{5.1}
$$
with
$$
z=\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{c}}\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right)}, \tag{5.2}
$$
and
$$
F(z)=\frac{1}{(2 \pi)^{3}} \int_{-\pi}^{+\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{+\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{+\pi} \frac{\mathrm{d} u \mathrm{~d} v \mathrm{~d} w}{z-\frac{1}{3}(\cos u+\cos v+\cos w)}. \tag{5.3}
$$

The extended Watson integral (5.3) is tabulated in Mannari and Kawabata (1964). The results in (5.1)-(5.3) correspond to the linearised theory of Lovesey and Marshall (their approximation 1). Their partially non-linear theory (their approximation 2) yields results quantitatively similar to their linearised theory. Our evaluation of $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}}/\sigma$ is presented in curve B of figure 1. If the mean-field result were correct, then a measurement of the exponent by a hyperfine probe would be inaccurate. The mean-field prediction of the probe-induced distortion of $\beta$ is given in column 2 of table 3. Note that curve B differs from both curves A and C of figure 1 and thus the mean-field theory is not capable of distinguishing between the cases in which $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}$ is sepcified to be zero or allowed to fluctuate about average value zero. (Mean-field theory is also incapable of distinguishing between the Ising and Heisenberg Hamiltonians.) The asymptotic $T \rightarrow T_{\mathrm{c}}$ result for $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}}/\sigma$ is
$$
\left\langle\sigma_{1}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}}/\sigma=0.6595+0.5086 \sigma+\ldots. \tag{5.4}
$$

In comparison to (4.1) the mean-field result (5.4) is qualitatively correct in that the leading term is a non-zero constant close to unity, but the leading correction term has an incorrect exponent. The mean-field result for $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle/\sigma$ for lower dimensionality fails completely as is to be expected; in two dimensions $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}}/\sigma$ approaches zero logarithmically as $\tau \rightarrow 0$ rather than giving a constant value as in (4.8) and (4.9).

A successful mean-field theory usually has some input from more exact results. In the mean-field results presented in figure 1 we have implicitly used the exact value of $T_{\mathrm{c}}$, by plotting $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}}/\sigma$ as a function of $\tau$ rather than the experimentally-measured variable $T$. We must also choose between using exact values or mean-field values for the bulk magnetisation. The choice arises implicitly when we choose to plot $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle/\sigma$ rather than $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$. However, there is room for choice here. Lovesey and Marshall's theory uses the quantity $z$ as given in (5.2). One might think that using the exact value of $\sigma$ in $z$ incorporates some exactness without sacrificing computational convenience. On the other hand it is possible that using the mean-field value of $\sigma$ ensures that ambiguities due to mean-field's lack of self-consistency are not exacerbated. However, in order to

make comparison with experiment it is more consistent to use the exact $\sigma$ since the mean field $\sigma$ depends on the availability of the Hamiltonian which may not be accurately known. Curve B in figure 1 has been calculated using the exact $\sigma$ in $z$. We have also calculated $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{MF}} / \sigma$ using $\sigma^{\mathrm{MF}}$ in $z$. This result has the same asymptote at $\tau=0$ as does curve B, is higher than both curves A and C for $\tau$ close to 1 , but is much closer to curve C; it has the same asymptotic expression as (5.4) but with $\sigma$ replaced by $(\sigma^{2}-\tau)^{1/2}$. The results for $\Delta\beta$ are almost as discouraging as the calculation with the exact $\sigma$, see table 3, column 4.

It is interesting to see if the mean-field theory can be made to yield a reasonable two- point correlation function. We combine (4.7) and (5.1), recall that mean-field theory does not distinguish between $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ and $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{H}}$, and obtain

$$
\Gamma_{100}^{\mathrm{MF}}=\sigma^{2}+\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right)(z-1/F(z)).\tag{5.5}
$$

Results for other than the nearest-neighbour correlation functions can also be obtained and involve the higher-order Watson integrals tabulated in Kawabe and Mannari 1970. Regardless of what we substitute for $\sigma$ we obtain the same values for the critical correlations:

$$
\Gamma_{100}^{\mathrm{MF}, \mathrm{c}}=0 \cdot 3405, \quad \Gamma_{110}^{\mathrm{MF}, \mathrm{c}}=0 \cdot 2184, \quad \Gamma_{200}^{\mathrm{MF}, \mathrm{c}}=0 \cdot 1697.\tag{5.6}
$$

The above results compare favourably with the results in (3.2); this qualitative agree- ment for the critical correlations has been noted previously by Ritchie and Fisher (1972). However, further consideration of the correlation functions shows that the mean-field results are of limited usefulness. The low-temperature expansion for $\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}$ gives the first non-zero term beyond $u^{0}$ correctly (order $u^{3}$ ), but regardless of which combination of the exact and mean-field value of $\sigma$ is used in (5.5), the next term is incorrect. Worse still, $\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}$ has a qualitative defect: $(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}T)\Gamma_{100}^{\mathrm{MF}}$ is positive for $T$ close to $T_{\mathrm{c}}$, no matter which com bination of mean-field $\sigma$ and exact $\sigma$ is used. This incorrect result also holds for the other two-point correlation functions. The best results for $\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}$ are obtained if for the first appearance of $\sigma^{2}$ in (5.5), the exact $\sigma$ is substituted and the mean-field $\sigma$ is used in $z$. If $(1-\sigma^{2})^{\text{exact}}$ is also substituted, the resultant $\Gamma_{100}^{\text{MF}}$ is in error by no more than $\pm 0.01$ for $T$ between 0 and $T_{\mathrm{c}}$. If instead $(1-\sigma^{2})^{\mathrm{MF}}$ is used, then the range of $T$ over which $(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}T)\Gamma^{\mathrm{MF}}$ is positive is minimised, but the accuracy of $\Gamma_{100}^{\mathrm{MF}}$ is poor for a broad range of $T$.

Further efforts were made to produce a mean-field theory for $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ or for $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{H}}$ by exploiting the ambiguities of the mean-field theory in Lovesey and Marshall's derivation of their results. No improvements were found. Even if a mean-field theory can be made to yield quantitatively reasonable correlation functions, we would still need substantial input from a more exact theory in order to produce something like curve A in figure 1. For this reason we arrive at the subjective conclusion that mean-field theories are not useful in the present context.

### 6. Summary

From the theoretical point of view, the major result of this investigation is that in two and three dimensions $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ and $\langle\sigma\rangle$ have the same critical exponents $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\gamma'$, and $\delta$ as does the bulk magnetisation $\sigma$. The defect problem for the two-dimensional square lattice is shown to be exactly solvable. Useful formulae are found which relate higher- point spin correlation functions to the two-point correlation functions and $\sigma$. The results are also of interest to investigators concerned with the interpretation of measurements

of critical exponents using hyperfine probes. A consideration of the leading correction terms to $\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle$ near $T_{c}$ leads to the conclusion that a non-magnetic hyperfine probe intro duces only a small distortion into the measurement of $\beta$ in the experimentally accessible temperature range.
